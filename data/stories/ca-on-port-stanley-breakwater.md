Port Stanley Breakwater Light stands at the outer end of the west breakwater at Port Stanley, Ontario, on Lake Erie. It is a white pyramidal concrete tower that shows a green flash every five seconds.[^1][^3][^4] The harbour's older piers had lights from the 1840s, and this concrete tower stands on a newer breakwater.[^1][^3] Since 2016 the tower has belonged to the Municipality of Central Elgin.[^2][^3] The Directory says it is closed and reached only by boat.[^3]

## The place

The place was a settlement called Kettle Creek from 1812. Within ten years it had taken the name Port Stanley, in honour of a fourteenth earl of Derby.[^2] The harbour was made where a creek ran into the lake.[^2] Lighthouse Friends quotes a Legislative Assembly description of such harbours: the mouth of the creek gave no shelter from any wind except one off the land, and shingle built up quickly on the windward side of the piers.[^2]

Lighthouse Friends says a committee of the House of Assembly of Upper Canada recommended money for lights at Port Stanley, Port Burwell and the Cut at Long Point in January 1837.[^2] The Register of Historic Places says that by 1832 there was a harbour at Port Stanley, and that "by 1844 the first lighthouse was built on the harbour pier to help ensure the safety of passing ships."[^1]

## Building the light

Lighthouse Friends infers that the first light went up soon after the 1837 recommendation, and says it was improved in the harbour works of 1843 and 1844.[^2]

A replacement followed. Lighthouse Friends says a lantern with four lamps and 36 cm (14 in) reflectors was probably hoisted onto a frame by the keeper Charles Ead in 1876.[^2] In 1881 John Ellison of Port Stanley was contracted for $1,250 to build a new light at the outer end of the west breakwater.[^2] The Department of Marine's 1882 description, which Lighthouse Friends prints, gives it as a fixed white catoptric light, 12.8 m (42 ft) above the lake and visible 11 miles, on a square wooden tower 10.7 m (35 ft) high from its base to the lantern vane.[^2]

In 1905 Haney and Miller were contracted to build a new L-shaped breakwater, and F. R. Miller of Port Stanley was contracted to build a reinforced concrete tower at its outer end.[^2] The Department's annual report for the year ended 31 March 1909 says that "a reinforced steel concrete tower is in course of erection on the government breakwater," the work done by contract by Mr. F. R. Miller of Port Stanley for $3,850.[^7] Lighthouse Friends says the tower was completed in 1909.[^2] The Lighthouse Directory dates it to 1908.[^3]

The light was an acetylene beacon at first. The report for the year ended 31 March 1910 describes a "reinforced concrete gas lighted beacon, 27 feet high," on the outer end of the breakwater, and lists it as an "automatic acetylene gas beacon on breakwater, showing a fixed red light."[^8] The report for the next year says the light "has been changed from a fixed red to a white light, occulted at short intervals."[^9] Lighthouse Friends says a 25 mm (one-inch) diaphone fog signal, worked by electricity, was installed at the breakwater light in 1913, sounding a two-second blast every fifteen seconds.[^2] Only Lighthouse Friends records it, and no other source gives a date for it.

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends gives a list of head keepers for the Port Stanley light, from Richard Smith, who was in post by 1851, to A. S. Taylor, who served from 1926 until at least 1937.[^2] The sources do not say whether the breakwater tower had keepers of its own, or whether the same keepers served both.

Richard Ead kept the light from 1859 to 1869. His son Charles took charge in 1869 on his father's death, and Lighthouse Friends says Charles had ten children and kept the light until 1890.[^2] After Charles, the list gives "Mrs. Charles Ead" as head keeper from 1890 to 1907. The Department's keeper statement for the year ended 30 June 1906 names "Ead, Mrs. C." at Port Stanley.[^10] The sources do not give her first name, or say how she came to the post.

John L. Oliver was head keeper from 1907 to 1925, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^2] The Department's keeper statement for the year ended 31 March 1911 names him at Port Stanley.[^11] A. S. Taylor followed him, from 1926.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Two entries in the life-saving reports record services by the Port Stanley lifeboat. The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1902 says the schooner *Mineral State*, of Alpena, Michigan, was wrecked in a gale and heavy sea, a short way from shore and south of the harbour. Its account continues: "The coxswain, J. R. Moore, and a volunteer life-boat crew, assisted by the tug Gordon Brown succeeded in rescuing the crew of five men who had been exposed for five hours."[^12] The report says the coxswain and crew were granted money for their services.[^12] The entry gives no date.[^12] In December 1909 the lifeboat went to the assistance of the *Astahula*, which had gone ashore at Port Burwell, a separate harbour. Her crew refused to leave her, and she was eventually got off, with compensation from the company.[^13]

Lighthouse Friends also reports that in 1861 a vessel, entering the harbour in a gale, struck and destroyed the light.[^2] No other source read confirms this.

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says a shore light was set up in 1934 to form a range with the breakwater light, to guide ships to the harbour entrance. At the same time the white light on the west pier, shown from a mast, was changed to green.[^2]

The federal government owned and ran the breakwater until 2010, when it handed the pier and harbour to the municipality with a one-time payment of $13.6 million to help pay for their upkeep.[^5] The public had been kept off the pier since June 1998. Three young men died at the site that month, and Transport Canada blocked pedestrian access soon after.[^5] By mid-2015 the municipality was rebuilding the pier, after work that had started the previous fall, so that people could walk on it again; concrete barriers and steel handrails were being added.[^5]

The Municipality of Central Elgin received the lighthouse itself in 2016, with a one-time grant of $65,000 for its restoration and upkeep, and the tower was made a heritage lighthouse that year.[^2][^3][^6] Lighthouse Friends says the municipality's mayor of the day believed the paint on the tower was lead-based and would need special cleaning.[^2]

## Today

The Municipality of Central Elgin owns the tower, and it is closed to visitors.[^3] The Lighthouse Friends page still names the Coast Guard as owner, though its own account of the 2016 transfer gives the municipality.[^2] The Directory says the site is reached only by boat, and that the site and tower are closed.[^3] The 2015 news report said the breakwater would reopen to walkers that summer. The sources do not show whether it has stayed open.[^5]

The Coast Guard's list describes the light as a green flash every five seconds. It is listed as seasonal, though the structure stays in place all year.[^4] The harbour's other breakwater light, the East Breakwater light, shows a red flash every four seconds.[^4]

Visitors should not climb the tower or go past any closed barrier on the breakwater.
