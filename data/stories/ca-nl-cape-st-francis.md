Cape St. Francis Lighthouse stands on the headland that marks the southern entrance to Conception Bay, about 28 km north of St. John's, Newfoundland. The light today is a lantern on a concrete fog-signal building, put up in 1957. It shows a white flash every five seconds, and the building is fenced.[^1][^2][^3][^6]

## The place

Petitions for a light on the cape came before the House of Assembly in 1865, when John Munn and other residents of Harbour Grace asked for one.[^1] In 1871 people from Harbour Main, Bay Roberts and Carbonear, all on Conception Bay, petitioned again.[^1] A lighthouse had been established on Baccalieu Island in 1859, on the other side of the bay's entrance, but people involved in the bay's shipping wanted a companion light at Cape St. Francis.[^1]

On 25 April 1872 an Act was passed for a lighthouse "on or near Cape St Francis." The Act said the light would "tend much to the preservation of Human Life, and safety of Ships and Vessels engaged in the Fisheries and Trade," as LighthouseFriends quotes it.[^1] In February 1876 Inspector John T. Nevill argued that the coast there was bold and that no vessels had been lost there in clear weather. A powerful phonic, or sound, signal was most needed, he reasoned, with a minor light as a companion.[^1]

## Building the light

Work began in the spring of 1876.[^1][^6] A launchway at Biscayne Cove took the materials ashore, and a road ran from it to the lighthouse road.[^1] A contract went to Messrs. Cameron & Carnell.[^1]

The first light showed on 10 January 1877, a fixed red light from a fifth-order lens with its focal plane 37.5 m (123 ft) above the sea.[^1] A short square tower on the flat roof of the wooden dwelling carried the lantern, and a flat-roofed block attached to the dwelling housed the fog-alarm equipment.[^1] Later that year the coal-fired steam siren went in; A. & F. Brown of England had made it.[^1] The water supply came from a brook up the hill, through an open channel that let debris block the iron pipes, so earthen pipes were extended to the brook in 1878.[^1]

In 1911 new optical apparatus changed the light from fixed red to fixed white.[^1] In 1912 a new diaphone fog alarm was installed, giving a blast of two and a half seconds each minute.[^1]

## The keepers

Hagan became the first keeper of the light, and Soper the first engineer in charge of the fog alarm.[^1] After a larger lens and lamp were installed in 1879, complaints continued that the light was no better than before.[^1] Later, on a surprise visit at 2:55 a.m. on 13 December (LighthouseFriends prints no year), Inspector Nevill and Austin Oke found only one of the two wicks burning in the lamp.[^1] Hagan was reprimanded for negligence and suspended for a time before being reinstated.[^1]

In November 1886 Hagan lodged a formal complaint of misconduct against Soper, the engineer.[^1] The Board of Works held an inquiry.[^1] Hagan was made to leave his post, and Soper received a severe reprimand.[^1] After a short suspension Soper returned to work, now under a former assistant keeper from Baccalieu Island who had been appointed keeper.[^1]

Soper later kept the light himself, from 1901 to 1910.[^1] In 1905 Isaac C. Morris wrote up a trip out to the cape for The Evening Telegram, as LighthouseFriends quotes him.[^1] Among the sights, he wrote, "perhaps none is such a novelty as that of the Lamp."[^1] He also praised Soper, who by his "thirty years of lonely life" had "justly earned the benefits of a good pension."[^1] Soper stepped down in 1910 with a pension of $308 a year.[^1] LighthouseFriends also names William Tilley (1887 to 1890), John Moulton (1891 to 1900), William Noseworthy (1911 to at least 1912) and, later, Edward and Franklyn Noseworthy (1964 to 1968).[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The schooner Village Belle was lost at Cape St. Francis on 30 July 1872, before the light was lit.[^5] The Northern Maritime Research shipwreck list gives no further detail.[^5]

In winter the water source for the fog alarm froze, so water had to be carried in by hand and by horse.[^1] Frost often damaged the earthen pipes, and they needed repairs nearly every year.[^1]

On his 1905 trip out to the cape, Morris saw a cargo boat bound for Bell Island and icebergs drifting south on their way.[^1]

## After the keepers

The last resident keeper left in 1975.[^1] The station has had no resident keeper since.[^1][^4] The two-storey keepers' duplex that replaced the 1877 station was demolished in 1993, and a helicopter pad took its place.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends and Rowlett give 1993; the nllh3d gallery gives 1997.[^1][^2][^6] A Fisheries and Oceans Canada brochure, dated 2005, lists the station among the unstaffed lightstations of Newfoundland and Labrador.[^4]

## Today

The Coast Guard list gives the light a white flash every five seconds, from a focal height of 29.0 m (95 ft), with a range of 16 nautical miles.[^3] The nllh3d gallery gives the range as 15 nautical miles.[^6] The structure that carries the lantern is 3.3 m (11 ft) high.[^3]

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and is its site manager.[^1][^2] The grounds are open and the building is fenced. The tower is closed.[^1][^2] Rowlett describes the walk in as a 5.5 km hike from Pouch Cove, and LighthouseFriends says the last stretch past Pouch Cove is unpaved.[^1][^2]
