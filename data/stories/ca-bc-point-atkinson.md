Point Atkinson Lighthouse marks the northern side of the entrance to Burrard Inlet, on a rocky bluff in West Vancouver, British Columbia. It was the first of three lighthouses built to serve the Port of Vancouver, and the present ribbed concrete tower dates from 1912.[^10][^12]

## The place

Captain George Vancouver named the point in 1792 for a friend, Thomas Atkinson, a master in the Royal Navy.[^2] The West Vancouver Historical Society adds that the point lies in the traditional territory of the Tseleil-Waututh, Squamish, Musqueam and Sto:lo peoples, and that their name for it, Sk'iwitsut, means "turning point", for waters known for hazardous currents.[^2] After a short burst of survey work, the society says, little happened in Burrard Inlet for more than fifty years, until gold, logging and sawmilling drew ships from Asia, Russia and Europe, and the Department of Marine built a light to protect that trade.[^2] The society also counts the station among the first public works the Dominion built in return for British Columbia joining Confederation in 1871.[^2]

In 1881 the Dominion set aside 185 acres (about 75 ha) of land around the point to act as a dark backdrop to the light; LighthouseFriends describes it as virgin forest and says Lighthouse Park now covers it. LighthouseFriends gives the area as 65 ha, which does not match 185 acres.[^1][^8]

## Building the light

The first lighthouse was a wooden tower with the keeper's dwelling attached, built in 1874 by Arthur Finney of Nanaimo for a budget of $4,250.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends puts the building in the spring of 1874, while the West Vancouver Historical Society says it took two years and calls it a brick and wood-frame construction.[^1][^2] It stood 15 m (49 ft) from base to the vane on the lantern, and its light shone from about 29 m (95 ft) above the sea, visible 14 miles on a clear night.[^1][^2] The lighthouse could not go into service that year: Stone-Chance of Birmingham had sent the wrong light, and a replacement took almost a year to arrive.[^1] Edwin Woodward lit it for the first time on 17 March 1875.[^1] The original apparatus was two coal-oil lamps in a silvered-copper reflector, turned by a clockwork driven by a lead weight on a wire that the keeper had to rewind about every eight hours.[^3]

Fog was handled by hand at first. A ship's captain sounded three short blasts and the keeper carried a hand-horn outside, working its twin bellows until the vessel signalled him to stop.[^4] Lobbying by Canadian Pacific interests, who warned Ottawa of the cost of an accident in the Narrows, led to steam fog sirens being installed in a frame building about 60 m (200 ft) west of the tower.[^4] The West Vancouver Historical Society dates this to November 1888; LighthouseFriends gives 1889.[^4][^1] The keeper now had to run the signal whenever fog or smoke lay within four miles, and LighthouseFriends reports that in 1896 Walter Erwin logged 1,450 hours on it.[^1] A diaphone replaced the sirens in 1902, according to LighthouseFriends, and the society dates its type F diaphone to 12 December 1912.[^1][^4] Federal annual reports of the period show the ordinary upkeep of such a station: a new boathouse and barn costing $201 in fiscal 1900, a correction to the fog signal's timing in fiscal 1902, and a boiler re-tubed at $213 in a report catalogued as 1907.[^13][^14][^15]

The present tower was built in 1912 by the contractor W. H. Rourke to designs by William P. Anderson, Chief Engineer of the Department of Marine.[^10][^12] It is a hexagonal reinforced-concrete tower, 18 m (60 ft) tall, with six buttresses; the focal plane is 32.9 m (108 ft) above high water.[^3][^12] The Federal Heritage Buildings Review Office says the replacement "embodied federal efforts to placate CPR officials" and shipping interests in the growing port.[^12][^11] Chance Brothers of Birmingham supplied a third-order Fresnel lens, which floated on a bath of mercury and was kept turning by a clockwork the keeper wound through the night.[^3] A fourteen-room duplex for the keeper and assistant stood beside it; LighthouseFriends says a new duplex and a fog alarm building were part of the 1912 work.[^2][^1]

## The keepers

Edwin Woodward and his wife Ann came first. LighthouseFriends says Ann found the isolation too much; after the birth of their third child, who was West Vancouver's first white child, the family moved to a farm in Ontario.[^1] A plaque presented in 1967 by Woodward's grandson names that child as James Atkinson Woodward, born at the lighthouse on 25 April 1876.[^9] The sources disagree about how long Woodward stayed: the plaque and LighthouseFriends's narrative say about five years, while the keeper lists give 1875 to 1877.[^9][^1][^7] His successor, Robert Wellwood, stayed less than a year according to LighthouseFriends, though the lists give 1877 to 1880.[^1][^7]

Walter Erwin arrived from Port Moody in 1880 on the recommendation of a Member of Parliament and pre-empted land nearby; he served about three decades.[^8][^1] A Marine Branch report of the 1900s lists him by name as keeper of the "Light and Fog Whistle" at Point Atkinson.[^16] LighthouseFriends reports that the fog signal ate into his pay: he was given a $300 raise to hire an assistant, but the cheapest help he could find cost $600 a year, against a salary of $700.[^1] In 1905, it says, he fell down the tower ladder and was laid up fourteen months, hired a second assistant and fell into debt; the Department refused help for fear of setting a precedent, and he resigned in 1909.[^1] The society's page on Erwin and the plaque give his service as ending 1910, while the society's own keeper list says 1909.[^8][^9][^7] At a ceremony on the steps of City Hall in 1911 the mayor presented him with the Imperial Service Medal, "in recognition of your long and faithful service as lighthouse keeper at Point Atkinson", according to the speech the society prints.[^8][^1]

Thomas Grafton, Erwin's assistant for twenty-one years, became keeper on 1 April 1910 and saw the station rebuilt.[^1] He died on the morning of 6 October 1933. His son Lawrence's log entry reads: "Lightkeeper was killed instantly sometime before 6:00 a.m. from a dynamite blast which exploded accidently in his hand."[^1] LighthouseFriends adds that the body was recovered at 7:15 a.m. by his younger son, drifting in a submerged boat about 200 yards off the point.[^1] Lawrence, who had assisted at the station for years, applied for the post but it went to Ernest Dawe of Ballenas Island, who stayed until 1961.[^1][^7] Gordon Odlum, James Barr and Oscar Edwards followed, and in 1980 Gerald Watson became principal keeper with Donald Graham as assistant.[^1] Graham stayed until automation in 1996 and, while at the station, wrote two books on British Columbia's lighthouses and their keepers, *Keepers of the Light* (1985) and *Lights of the Inside Passage* (1986).[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1883 Erwin's wife became seriously ill on a stormy night; the society says a nurse, Susan Patterson, was brought from Moodyville by two men in a canoe, and that the poet Nora Duncan wrote of it.[^8]

The point's war came in the 1940s. The society reports that the navy built a signal station beside the tower in the late 1930s to control traffic into Vancouver, that searchlight bunkers and, in 1942, an 18-pounder gun were added, and that examination service ended in March 1944.[^5] About eighty troops lived in barracks behind the station, and the society says the keeper's family and the soldiers visited back and forth.[^5] LighthouseFriends records that the only time the station came under fire was when an instructor at another battery accidentally fired a 12-pound shell past the lighthouse.[^1]

## After the keepers

Electricity arrived in 1962, replacing the gas mantle with a 500-watt bulb.[^3] The duplex was replaced in 1966 by two separate dwellings.[^1] Fog horns were changed to air-chime horns in 1974.[^4] The Coast Guard replaced the Fresnel lens with automated prototype lights after 1985, and in June 1996 Point Atkinson and five other B.C. stations were automated.[^3] The electronic fog horns that followed were removed two years later, leaving the point silent.[^4] The original diaphone apparatus is in storage at the Vancouver Maritime Museum.[^4]

The date of the national historic site designation is not settled in the sources: the Canadian Register lists Historic Sites and Monuments Board minutes of November 1974 and October 1992 among its sources, while the West Vancouver Historical Society and LighthouseFriends give 1994.[^10][^6][^1] In 1997 the District of West Vancouver took over the station's two residences and grounds, while the lighthouse and other buildings remain with Fisheries and Oceans Canada.[^6] In 2014 the District applied for protection under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act.[^6]

## Today

The tower is still an aid to navigation, showing two white flashes every five seconds, and it is a recognized federal heritage building.[^1][^12][^17] Lighthouse Park surrounds the point; the District of West Vancouver manages the two residences and grounds, while the lighthouse itself remains with Fisheries and Oceans Canada.[^1][^6] LighthouseFriends reports the grounds open and the tower closed, and Lighthouse Park open daily from 6 a.m. to 10:30 p.m.; the trail from the car park to the point is steep.[^1] Visitors should treat the tower and the federal station as closed unless an official source says otherwise.
