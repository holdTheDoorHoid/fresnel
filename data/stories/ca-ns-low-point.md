Low Point Lighthouse stands on a barren point on the east side of the entrance to Sydney Harbour, Cape Breton, at New Waterford. A light has shown from the point since 1832. The octagonal concrete tower that stands there now dates from 1938.[^1][^3]

## The place

The Chance Heritage Trust describes the point as "both low and flat, making a light there even more critical."[^1] The point has also been called Flat Point. Government records mostly used Low Point until 1908, then Flat Point in 1909, and Low Point became the official name in 1953.[^1][^7]

Sydney Harbour was busy when the light was planned. In 1832 Robert Gammel and others asked the Nova Scotia House of Assembly for a lighthouse on Low Point to mark the entrance. About 600 vessels a year called at the harbour then.[^1]

## Building the light

The legislature approved 500 pounds for the project. When the lowest tender came in at 770 pounds, the Lieutenant Governor advanced the extra sum so that the lighthouse could be built as soon as possible.[^1] The octagonal tower, with a keeper's dwelling, was finished in November 1832. It was the ninth light the Nova Scotia government had built.[^1]

Later the lantern was changed. In 1865 the inspector John H. Kendrick found the lantern room too small, and said the light "should be second to none in the Province." A larger, twelve-sided lantern room was installed in 1877, at a cost of $1,640.73.[^1]

An inspector's note of 1901 calls the light "an exceedingly strong one," and recommends a modern quick-flashing dioptric light. The same note says he had located a site for a steam fog whistle.[^5]

The fog whistle came in 1903 and 1904. The Chief Engineer's report for 1903 says that "When the steam fog whistle on Low point is put in operation, on the first January, 1904," the fog horn at Cranberry Head would be discontinued.[^6] The Chance Heritage Trust gives the cost of the fog alarm building and its equipment as $4,600.[^1]

In 1908 the light received a new circular lantern room and a third-order double-flashing Fresnel lens.[^1] The 1909 report says that at Flat Point "The lighthouse tower deck was replaced by a new lantern platform ... to accommodate the new illuminating apparatus."[^7]

## The keepers

The first keeper, Michael McKeagney, was appointed in November 1832. The next month he failed to show the light in a heavy snowstorm, and a passing ship reported it. He did not deny the charge, and by the Trust's account he said that it was not fit for a dog to turn out that night.[^1] The Trust's keeper list gives the name as George McKeagney.[^1]

Robert McNab took over the next June and tended the light until 1865. His appointment letter from the Commissioners set out the work: "The lamps to be trimmed at least twice during each night," and "A journal of all particular circumstances to be kept." His pay was "one hundred pounds per annum," and he was to "have an assistant on the spot."[^1] In 1857 McNab told the inspector, William Condon, that nineteen years had passed since any repairs of consequence had been made to the station.[^1]

John G. Peters kept the light from 1865 to 1910.[^1] After him came a long run of keepers. In 1979 James H. Jobe became the only keeper on the point, and he stayed until the station was de-staffed in October 1988.[^1] The Trust's keeper list gives 1978 as the start of his term.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A gale in 1833 blew the lead roof off the lantern room, and the light went out for a time, until a new iron lantern room and lamp were received.[^1] That winter temperatures often fell to twenty below zero. The oil in the lamps kept congealing, so the keeper used pans of hot coals to keep the light going, which damaged the lamps.[^1]

On 16 November 1909 the schooner Myrtle F. Hopkins "went ashore on Livingston shoals, near Lowpoint, during a heavy gale." The tug went to her assistance, but "owing to a very heavy sea running, nothing could be done."[^8] The entry gives the place as "Lowpoint"; it is probably this point, though the report does not say so.

On 1 May 1909 the tug C. M. Winch went to the aid of the schooner Ronald L. Smith, which was ashore at Flat Point.[^9]

Erosion has been an issue for some time. Shore-protection work in 1915 cost $3,223.76.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1938 the present concrete tower was built. The Chance Heritage Trust says the lantern room and lens of the old wooden tower went into it.[^1] The register instead describes an iron lantern "recovered from the previous lighttower which had stood on the site since 1832," and calls the lens "original."[^3] The sources disagree on which lantern came from which tower.[^1][^3] The Trust also records that in October 1984 a rotating airport beacon replaced the Fresnel lens in the lantern room.[^1] The register lists the lens system among the features to protect.[^3]

In 1953 an assistant keeper's dwelling went up, and commercial power reached the station.[^1] In 1962 a second assistant keeper's dwelling was added when a diaphone foghorn was set up on the point. In 1970 an electric horn replaced the diaphone, and the second assistant's post was dropped.[^1]

The station was de-staffed in October 1988.[^1] On 19 October 2006 the tower was recognised as a federal heritage building. The register notes that the site "has been continuously occupied by a light since 1832."[^3]

The Low Point Lighthouse Society was formed to work for the tower's restoration. It won $75,000 in July 2015 in the National Trust's crowdfunding competition, and contractors resurfaced the tower in 2017–2018.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard's list gives the light as a white flash every five seconds ("Fl W 5s"), 25.8 m above the water, with a range of 18 nautical miles. It operates day and night. The tower is an octagonal white tower with a red top, 20.8 m high.[^4] The Trust's data sheet gives the tower as 22 m (72 ft).[^1]

LighthouseFriends gives the Canadian Coast Guard as owner, with the grounds open and the tower closed.[^2] The Trust gives the same picture of access: the site is open and the tower closed.[^1] It also says the lighthouse is endangered by erosion of the cape it marks, and that major funding is needed for erosion control.[^1]
