The Sapelo Island Light is a brick tower, 65 feet (19.8 m) high, at the southern end of Sapelo Island on the Georgia coast.[^1] It was built about 1820 to serve the port of Darien, on the Altamaha River,[^85] and the state's National Register nomination calls it the oldest surviving coastal lighthouse in Georgia.[^2] Its light has been dark since 1905, but the tower still stands as a daymark, and the nomination says that when it was written in 1997 the tower still worked unofficially as a navigational landmark.[^3][^4][^5]

## The place

The Altamaha is the largest river on Georgia's coastal plain. The small city of Darien, about ten miles inland, was its port, and cotton, rice, timber and naval stores went out through it.[^6] The lighthouse stands on a tract of higher ground at the south end of the island. Tidal creeks separate it from the rest of Sapelo, and its highest ground lies about 20 feet (6 m) above mean sea level.[^7] For most of its history the lighthouse island was reached by water only.[^8] Today a causeway connects it to Sapelo Island proper, presumably built after the 1950 sale.[^9]

The government first needed land. In 1808 Thomas Spalding's five acres at the south end of the island were ceded to the United States for a lighthouse.[^10] A deed dated 15 July 1816 records that Spalding sold them to the Treasury's Lighthouse Establishment for one dollar.[^11]

## Building the light

On 14 September 1819 the government signed a contract with Winslow Lewis of Boston for a brick tower 65 feet (20 m) tall, topped by a 15-foot (4.6 m) iron lantern.[^12] The contract called for a round tower "to be built of stone or hard brick," and the price was $14,500.[^13][^14] A keeper's dwelling was part of the same contract.[^15] A second contract, dated 13 January 1820, had Lewis fit the lantern with "fifteen of the Patent Lamps and reflectors, each sixteen inches," on a revolving iron frame.[^16] LighthouseFriends gives the reflectors as fifteen inches, so the two accounts differ on their size.[^17] The light was activated in 1820.[^18]

Lewis was the builder, not a keeper, and the tower he built is still standing.[^12] The brick walls are several feet thick at the bottom and narrow to two feet (0.6 m) at the top.[^19] In 1822 the government built a wooden range beacon on Wolf Island, across Doboy Sound.[^20] A fourth-order Fresnel lens went into the lantern in 1854.[^21] In 1877 an iron range beacon went up 660 feet (200 m) east of the tower, and it still stands.[^22] The nomination's site description puts it at about 600 feet (180 m).[^84]

## The keepers

The first keeper, William Brow, received $400 a year and resigned after about ten months, LighthouseFriends says.[^23] Jeremiah Lester, from 1821 to 1825, was the first keeper to stay more than a year.[^24] The archive's roster gives the head keepers and first assistants by year.[^25]

Alexander Hazzard was appointed head keeper in 1853 at $600 a year.[^26] He served until early 1862.[^27] The Confederates who held the island then removed the Fresnel lens and destroyed the reflector system, but left the rest of the facility intact.[^28] The nomination quotes the Light-House Board's report for 1868, which says the light was "relighted for the first time on the evening of April 15, 1868."[^29] Hazzard is on the roster again for 1868 to 1869.[^25] In 1869 the tower was painted with wide red and white bands, a change the nomination dates to that year.[^30] LighthouseFriends places the striped daymark about four years before the 1854 lens, which would put it around 1850.[^18]

The keeping passed to one family for most of the next sixty years, LighthouseFriends says.[^18] The nomination calls it a local tradition, begun in 1873 when Irish-born James Cromley was appointed keeper at $600 a year.[^31] LighthouseFriends says he was 74 when he took the post and that he made boots for the crews of vessels anchored nearby.[^32][^33] He held the post until his death in 1889.[^34] His son William G. Cromley kept the light from 1890 until 1900.[^35] James Cromley Jr. became head keeper on 5 March 1900, with his brother Daniel as assistant, and the two were on duty when the new steel tower began in 1905.[^36][^37]

The nomination says there were four Cromley brothers on the station, and that Robert, the only one to marry and have a family, became assistant keeper in 1912 and later head keeper for many years.[^38][^39] The archive's roster has Robert H. Cromley as first assistant from 1912 and as head keeper from 1926 to 1933. It also lists a Robert H. Cromley as first assistant from 1890 to 1895, a stint the nomination does not mention. LighthouseFriends lists the same stint, and neither source says whether it was the same man who became assistant in 1912.[^40][^54]

In all, the roster lists 44 names for the station from 1820 to 1933: 23 head keeper stints, 20 first-assistant stints and one contractor, Winslow Lewis.[^25]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Light-House Board's report for 1899 says a gale on 2 October 1898 put out the lights of the Sapelo range, and that they were relit on 13 October.[^41] Its entry for Sapelo says the storm of October 1898 "was particularly severe along the coast from Sapelo to Fernandina." At Sapelo, "the water covered the floor of the second story of the dwelling" and the top of the oil house's roof.[^42][^43] LighthouseFriends adds that one gable end of the keeper's house was undermined and washed away, and that the interior was gutted.[^44] The Board reported that "the dwelling was practically rebuilt, the oil house was repaired, and a boat house was provided."[^45] The nomination says that for several hours the tide covered the lower 18 feet (5.5 m) of the tower.[^46]

LighthouseFriends reports that William G. Cromley and his family took refuge in the tower during the storm and lost most of their possessions. It adds that the Treasury sent Congress a list of his losses, "which totaled $626 and included a $125 organ."[^47][^48]

The sea kept encroaching on the site. The Board's report for 1900 said "the sea is rapidly encroaching on the site," and asked for a new site and a combined tower and dwelling. The 1901 report repeated the request, at a cost "not exceeding $40,000."[^49][^50] The 1901 report says spring tides sometimes reach the piazza of the keeper's dwelling and the base of the light tower.[^51] Congress appropriated $40,000 in June 1902.[^52]

In January 1914 Robert Cromley, then assistant keeper, "picked up at sea a motor boat belonging to a private individual, which had been stolen," according to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin.[^53] In February 1928, LighthouseFriends says, Cromley used his binoculars to spot three men stranded on Wolf Island. They had been flying toward New York in a seaplane when fog forced them down.[^54][^55]

## After the keepers

In 1904 the Cromley family sold the 182-acre lighthouse island to the government, according to a bracketed passage in the nomination.[^56] The new light was a 100-foot (30 m) steel tower built a few hundred feet north of the brick one, and it was activated on 18 September 1905.[^57][^58] The brick tower was deactivated that year and never lit again while the government ran the light.[^3] In 1913 the steel tower was converted to incandescent oil-vapor lighting.[^59]

Shipping through Darien declined, and activity at the south end of the island declined with it.[^60] The Lighthouse Service deactivated the station in 1933.[^61] Robert H. Cromley was retired as the last keeper under a revocable license dated 4 May 1933.[^62] The steel tower was dismantled in 1938. The nomination says its parts went to Southport, North Carolina, while LighthouseFriends says they went to South Fox Island, in Lake Michigan.[^63][^64]

In 1950 the government sold the tract, then measured at 195 acres, to R. J. Reynolds Jr.[^65] The nomination says the State of Georgia took full title from the Reynolds heirs in 1992.[^66] LighthouseFriends instead says the state and NOAA bought the southern 206 acres in 1991.[^67] The two accounts disagree on the date and the buyers. Before restoration, the archaeologist Morgan Ray Crook carried out a small-scale study around the tower in January 1997.[^68]

LighthouseFriends reports that the tower, oil house and cistern were restored in 1998 at a cost of $490,000.[^69] The work rebuilt a spiral stair of 77 steps and painted the tower with six stripes. On 6 September 1998 Governor Zell Miller switched on a light in the lantern room.[^70][^71] In 2011, LighthouseFriends says, archaeologists unearthed the remains of the keeper's house that the 1898 storm had undermined.[^72]

## Today

The tower stands on state land inside the Sapelo Island National Estuarine Research Reserve.[^66][^73] The Reserve's visitor center is on the mainland, next to the ferry dock at Meridian, and it gives information about the Reserve's public and group tours on the island.[^74][^75] LighthouseFriends says a tour of the island takes in the 1820 lighthouse, the foundation of the 1905 tower and its oil house.[^76] LighthouseFriends says the 1998 stair was rebuilt to let visitors climb the tower, and its visitor section lists the grounds and tower as open.[^77][^86] This story does not confirm whether climbing is allowed now, so check with the Reserve before planning a climb. Around the tower are the four walls of the c.1890 oil house, a brick cistern, the 1877 range beacon, the footings of the 1905 tower and the foundation of a fortification from 1898.[^78][^79][^80][^81]

While the light had a keeper, the island was a place for outings. The nomination says that from the 1880s the lighthouse was the venue for social outings, as the local newspaper reported, and that the lighthouse island was "a favorite place for Sunday afternoon parties and picnics," helped by the keeper's hospitality.[^82][^83]
