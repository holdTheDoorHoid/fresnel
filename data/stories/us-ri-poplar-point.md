Poplar Point Light stands at the southern side of the entrance to Wickford Harbor, in North Kingstown, Rhode Island. It was built in 1831 and is the oldest surviving lighthouse in Rhode Island on its original site.[^4] Its tower still rises through the roof of a house, which is private property.[^1] The light was discontinued in 1882 and sold in 1894.[^2][^4][^6]

## The place

The light occupies a site of about an acre and a half at the southern side of the harbour entrance.[^2] A plaque set in the driveway records a Revolutionary War post: "On this point during the Revolution stood a cannon," guarding the Newtown area, now Wickford, "against enemy excursions on the main." The plaque says the post's rangers had a charter that was "granted April 17, 1777."[^3]

Congress approved $3,000 for a navigational beacon at Wickford Harbor on 3 March 1831.[^6] LighthouseFriends says Poplar Point, at the southern entrance to the harbour, was picked as the site.[^6] LighthouseFriends suggests the light did little for ships in the West Passage of Narragansett Bay. It stood in a shallow bay, about two miles from the channel's centre.[^6]

## Building the light

Three acres were bought from Thomas Albro for $300, LighthouseFriends says, and Charles Allen of Kingston was hired to build the lighthouse for $1,889. Winslow Lewis supplied the lamps and reflectors for $375.[^6] The nomination gives the cost of the tower and dwelling as $1,888, and names the same builder.[^4] The two sums differ by a dollar. The archive also lists Charles Allen and John Stevens as contractors, without dates.[^7]

The tower was wooden and octagonal. The nomination says it rises "through the dwelling's roof at the north end," as "a three-story wooden octagonal light tower sided with clapboards."[^2] The nomination says the walls of the keeper's dwelling are of cut stone, now covered with shingles. LighthouseFriends describes a one-storey rubblestone dwelling, so the sources disagree on the stone and on the number of storeys.[^2][^6]

LighthouseFriends describes the way up to the tower.[^6] Access ran through a small bedroom with no window, whose unlined walls let cold and snow in during winter. The tower's interior was at first left unfinished, with its studs exposed.[^6]

In 1838 Lieutenant George M. Bache reported on the station. LighthouseFriends quotes his report: the light was "elevated forty-eight feet above high-water mark," with "eight lamps with concave reflectors," and "a portion of the silver has been rubbed from their concave surface."[^6]

LighthouseFriends says a sixth-order steamer lens and a single Argand lamp replaced the eight lamps in 1855.[^6] The archive lists the original optic as eight lamps with 14.5-inch (37 cm) reflectors, "later 5th order Fresnel," and gives 1856 as the year the original lens was installed. The two dates differ by a year.[^7]

## The keepers

The first keeper was Samuel Thomas, Jr., who LighthouseFriends says was appointed in November 1831 at a salary of $350 a year.[^6] The archive lists him as Samuel Thomas, from 1831 to 1849.[^7] He was followed by James Reynolds (1849 to 1854), Abram B. Green (1854 to 1859), Samuel A. Spink (1859 to 1861) and John Hull (1861 to 1874).[^7]

Henry F. Sherman had served at Poplar Point since 1874. The archive gives his stint as ending in 1882, and LighthouseFriends says it ended in 1885.[^6][^7] LighthouseFriends also says Sherman was moved to Wickford Harbor Lighthouse when that light was activated on 1 November 1882.[^6]

The archive lists Nathaniel Dodge as keeper from 1885 to 1893.[^7] LighthouseFriends agrees, and adds Edward Andrews, from 1893 to 1930.[^6][^7] Both lists name keepers after the light was discontinued in 1882, and the sources do not explain why.

## After the keepers

In 1878 the Lighthouse Board cited the need for an additional navigational aid to mark the entrance to Wickford Harbor.[^4] It proposed a light on Old Gay Rock, "500 yards north of Poplar Point."[^4] LighthouseFriends says Congress in 1880 provided $45,000 for a light on Old Gay Rock.[^6]

The Wickford Harbor light was activated on 1 November 1882, and the nomination says Poplar Point "was discontinued after fifty one years of service."[^4][^6] The nomination ends its period of significance in 1882, "when the light was deactivated and no longer served as part of a system of navigation aids."[^5]

The government kept the light until 1894.[^4] The nomination says it was sold at public auction to Albert R. Sherman "of Pawtucket," who remodelled it as a residence.[^4] LighthouseFriends gives his home as Pawtuxet, Rhode Island, and the price as $4,000, paid on 15 October 1894.[^6]

Sherman commissioned the architectural firm of Straight and McKenzie to design the additions. The nomination says "two shingled wings" were added in 1894, and that "shingles were applied to the outside of the original dwelling at that time."[^2][^4] The result, in the nomination's words, is "a rambling, shingled structure laid out in the shape of a Y."[^2] The lens and lamp have been removed.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the house has been used as a summer home and as a year-round home.[^6]

## Today

The house is a private residence. The nomination lists access as "yes, restricted," and the archive lists it as not open to the public.[^1][^7] The archive also records that the light is no longer an active aid to navigation, and that it is a private aid.[^7]

The light was lit again. LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard has given permission for a relight, with a flashing light in place of the fixed light first used, and that the relight took place on Christmas night 2025.[^6] The archive lists the current optic as a 1905 Swedish Fresnel lens with a six-second white flash. That does not match the nomination's statement that the lens had been removed.[^2][^7]

LighthouseFriends recommends viewing the light from the water, or from the breakwater at Sauga Point across the mouth of the harbour. It is privately owned, and the grounds, dwelling and tower are closed.[^6] Please do not go onto the grounds.
