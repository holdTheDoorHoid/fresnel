Cleveland East Ledge Light is a concrete Art Moderne house with a tower on top, sitting on a steel caisson in open water about two miles off the shore of Buzzards Bay. It was the last traditional lighthouse built in Massachusetts, and it was finished with a war on.[^1][^2]

## The place

The light stands on the eastern side of the southern entrance to the channel that leads to the Cape Cod Canal, on a ledge roughly two miles from the nearest shore, in twenty-one feet of water.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the ledge takes its name from President Grover Cleveland, who after his 1892 election made Grey Gables, in Bourne, his summer White House and often fished in the bay near the ledge.[^2]

Decades before the lighthouse, a buoy marked the ledge. The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1915, lists a gas-lighted buoy established on Cleveland Ledge among the works at the approaches to the Cape Cod Canal, where $50,000 had been appropriated by the act of 1 August 1914.[^5][^2] The canal itself was later widened and deepened: between 1935 and 1940 about $21 million went into making it 480 feet wide and 32 feet deep, which LighthouseFriends links to the case for a permanent light on the ledge.[^2]

## Building the light

The contract, worth $198,851, went to the J. F. Fitzgerald Construction Company of Boston. The steel caisson was fabricated at the Thames shipyard in New London, Connecticut, and towed to the ledge by commercial tugs, with the Coast Guard vessel *Pequot* helping, in a single nineteen-hour operation on 7 October 1940.[^2] The National Register nomination gives a different figure for the whole station, $250,000, and does not say what it includes.[^1]

The finished design stacks a round, eight-faced lower storey and a cross-shaped upper storey on the caisson's deck, with a cylindrical concrete tower rising another fifty feet. Below the deck were the engine room, fuel tanks and four water tanks holding 4,800 gallons. The lantern originally held a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^2][^3] The nomination describes the style as Art Moderne and says the simple railings of the steel main deck recall the bridge of a 1930s luxury liner.[^1]

War interrupted the work. The nomination records that the station was transferred to the government on 18 September 1941 in a partly finished state, with a temporary light installed, and that it survived a violent storm.[^1] LighthouseFriends says materials grew scarce after the United States entered the war and that the light did not enter service until 1 June 1943.[^2]

## The keepers

This was a Coast Guard station from the start, not a civilian Lighthouse Service one. The US Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the keeper registers, lists Edgar M. Wallace as the first principal keeper, 1942 to 1943, and then a quick succession of men, many of them listed for a single year.[^3] A Coast Guard crew served alongside them, and the archive names dozens of its members over the years.[^3]

An article written in December 1967 described the interior as "spit and polish spotless" and warm from a large oil furnace; the four men then on duty had a lounge with a small television, a gallery, a lavatory and sleeping quarters. They worked two weeks on and had one week off, and supplies came up in a sling from the Coast Guard patrol boat out of Woods Hole, which called weekly.[^2] LighthouseFriends relates that, not long after the loss of the submarine USS *Thresher* in April 1963, the crew received a call from the Corps of Engineers warning that a nuclear submarine was heading straight for the light; they could see its red and green lights, and it turned away just short.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The test came in September 1944, about fifteen months after the light was lit. LighthouseFriends tells it this way: the wind drove waves in from the southeast, then shifted soon after midnight to the southwest and took out a glass-block skylight on that side, and tons of water poured in. Lieutenant Olie P. Swenson and his eight men bailed from the engine room with pails, then built a barricade of mattresses, oil drums and boards over the hole, with water about two inches below the tops of the batteries.[^2] They lost radio and telephone but kept the light burning.[^2] LighthouseFriends also quotes a newspaper saying every man was surprised and gratified that the "hitherto untried cement and steel structure" had held.[^2] Swenson appears in the archive's list as principal keeper in 1944.[^3]

The same storm sank the Vineyard Lightship; LighthouseFriends says twelve of her crew were lost.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1978 an underwater cable brought power to the station, it was automated, and the Coast Guard crew was removed that September. The building was sealed.[^2][^1] A 1982 entry in the National Register nomination notes the Coast Guard's plan to fit glass-block windows for security and maintenance.[^1] The nomination, stamped May 1987, found that the station met National Register criteria A and C as an example of caisson construction and as the last lighthouse of traditional form built in the state.[^1] The Coast Guard did three weeks of renovation in 1990, and very little afterward, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2]

Under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, the government offered the light to public bodies and non-profits in 2007. One group began an application and withdrew in 2009; a second notice went out on 1 June 2010; and with no qualifying takers the General Services Administration put the lighthouse up for public auction in October 2010.[^2] The bidding ended on 14 December 2010 at $190,000, bought by a private bidder.[^2][^4]

## Today

The light is privately owned and the tower is closed.[^2][^3] It remains an active aid to navigation: a 190-millimetre optic flashing white every ten seconds, and an automated horn that can sound every fifteen seconds in poor visibility.[^2] LighthouseFriends advises viewing it from the water.[^2]
