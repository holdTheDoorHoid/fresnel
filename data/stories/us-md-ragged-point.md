Ragged Point Light was a screw-pile light on the Potomac River, where a shoal runs out from the shore at a short turning point in the river.[^2][^12] It was first lit on 15 March 1910.[^12] In July 1962 its wooden house was dismantled, and a skeletal tower carrying an automated light was put up on the same foundation.[^12][^11]

## The place

The Light-House Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1896 described the hazard in plain terms: "This shoal makes off from the west bank of the river at a short turning point."[^2] The Board wanted a light and a fog signal there, at an estimated $20,000.[^12] It repeated the request year after year. By 1905 the recommendation was one "made in the Board's last nine annual reports."[^12]

The reports did not agree on the state. The 1896 report, and the Board's 1901 index, call the station Ragged Point, Virginia.[^2][^4] From 1907 the Board's own reports call it Maryland,[^12][^13] and by 1918 the Commissioner's list reads "Ragged Point, Md."[^5] A Chesapeake Chapter page places the light at Coles Point, on the Virginia shore.[^14] The sources do not settle which state the light was in. This account uses Maryland, as the later reports do, and leaves the dispute open.

## Building the light

By 1901 the cost of materials and labour had risen, and the appropriations table of the Board's report carries the station at $30,000.[^11][^3] The act of 20 June 1906 authorised the station, and $15,000 was appropriated on 30 June 1906.[^12] A further $15,000 came on 4 March 1907, and the Board's report for that year asked for about $15,000 more.[^11][^13] Congress added $5,000 in 1908.[^12] After the 1907 appropriation the Board said the total was "not sufficient to establish a suitable light and fog-signal."[^12]

The metal work was delivered to the light-house depot at Lazaretto Point, Maryland, and the contractor finished the wooden house on 26 February 1910.[^12] The Board's account of the first lighting, as Chance Heritage Trust excerpts it, says the light was "shown for the first time on March 15, 1910."[^12] It was a fourth-order lens, flashing white every ten seconds, in a two-storey wooden house on seven wrought-iron piles.[^12] The fog bell was on the roof, on the channel side, and was "struck a double blow every ten seconds to sound a warning to vessels."[^12] The total cost was $34,223.97.[^12]

## The keepers

The roster that the US Lighthouse Society's archive prints for Ragged Point opens with Alexander B. Curtis, listed for 1910, then Horace C. Groom (1910 to 1912) and Patrick Reedy (1912 to 1915).[^1] Charles A. Larsen was principal keeper from 1915 to 1918, and Carl Geiling from 1919 to 1927.[^1]

Geiling won a Lighthouse Service efficiency flag.[^10] The Chesapeake Chapter dates the award to 1923,[^11] and the Bulletin's index files it under his name.[^10] The Chapter also records that Larsen had received an inspector's efficiency star in 1918.[^11]

The roster and the Bulletin disagree on one name. The archive lists James S. Downs as first assistant from 1918 to 1927, and then as principal keeper until 1930.[^1] The Bulletin of 1919 names the assistant as James A. Downs.[^7] Neither source says which initial is right.

Joseph L. Mayor was principal keeper from 1937 to 1943, after serving as first assistant from 1933 to 1937. Harvey E. Mayor, who has the same surname, is listed as principal keeper from 1943, and the archive's record runs to at least 1948.[^1] The archive does not say whether the two men were related.

The Bulletin's index for 1924 to 1929 also lists that the Ragged Point light station was used in aerial navigation,[^15] and its index for 1930 to 1935 lists a mantle burner tested at the station.[^16]

In 1926 the Bulletin printed letters from the keepers of three lights in the fifth district, Ragged Point among them.[^9] A wealthy woman of New York had sent 12 radio sets to the superintendent of the Baltimore lighthouse district, for distribution among its tenders, light vessels and shore stations.[^8] The Bulletin said the sets were meant "to afford relief from the loneliness" of men "cut off from all but few human contacts."[^8] One of the letters reported that "the radio we received is in excellent working order."[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the winter of 1917 to 1918 ice floes damaged the screw-pile lights of the Chesapeake and the Potomac.[^12] The Commissioner's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1919 says "ice floes wrought an unprecedented amount of damage to screw-pile lighthouses and light stations."[^6] Ragged Point received 600 tons of riprap to act as an ice breaker.[^6] The work ran from 10 November 1918 to 15 April 1919.[^6][^12]

On 28 November 1918 a barge loaded with riprap for the station broke her moorings in a storm and sprang a leak. The Bulletin records that Keeper Charles A. Larsen and his assistant, James A. Downs, of Ragged Point Light Station, "rendered assistance to the captain of the barge L. B. Adams."[^7][^11]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1951.[^12][^13] In July 1962 the wooden house came down. The Chapter calls Ragged Point one of the first lighthouses to be dismantled,[^14] and a skeletal tower with an automated light went up on the existing screw-pile foundation.[^12][^11]

## Today

LighthouseFriends describes a modern light shown from a tower on the screw-pile foundation.[^13] Chance Heritage Trust describes the light as located offshore, with the site and tower listed as closed.[^12] The USLHS archive's record gives "Open to Public: No."[^1] No source describes a visit. The Chapter says the light can be seen from the shore at the marina at Ragged Point in Virginia.[^14] The sources do not say who owns the structure today.
