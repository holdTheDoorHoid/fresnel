The Lake St. Clair light is a steel tower on a steel-and-concrete foundation, near the middle of the lake, roughly seven miles from St. Clair Shores.[^1] It was commissioned in 1941 to replace a radio-controlled lightship that had marked the channel for decades. The Coast Guard owns it, and its tower is closed to visitors.[^1][^9]

## The place

Lake St. Clair averages only about eleven feet (3.4 m) in depth, so its shipping channels had to be dredged and then marked.[^1] In its report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1891, the Light-House Board said the passage across the Grosse Pointe Flats "is now marked by a light-ship at the north end," with a pile-cluster beacon at the head of the Detroit River.[^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says the federal government took over the marking of the channel in 1887, when a lightship numbered LV-10 replaced a privately built one. LV-10 was a wooden scow built in 1878 that had been used in building Stannard Rock lighthouse before it was converted into a lightship.[^1] The Board's reports call it the Grosse Pointe light-vessel No. 10.[^4] In 1894 the report says that it "received some repairs to her hull before being put on her station in the spring."[^4]

In its 1894 report the Board said the channel "should be lighted at each end by lights placed on cribs." When this was done, it wrote, "the light-vessel now in use at this point could be discontinued."[^3]

Lightship No. 75 was placed on the lake in August 1902, according to the Lake Carriers' Association's 1941 description, which LighthouseFriends quotes.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the ship was moved farther north and renamed Lake St. Clair in 1911.[^1] In 1935 it was rebuilt inside and fitted with automatic and remote-controlled apparatus. LighthouseFriends calls it "the first radio-controlled lightship in the United States."[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1936 named the Lake St. Clair ship as one of the two lightships that would remain in service on the Great Lakes, and said that "the latter of which is automatic and remote controlled."[^5] The 1938 Bulletin describes the radio-controlled ship as having "no propelling power" and as "regularly occupying a station."[^7]

The lighthouse replaced that ship.[^1] Its foundation was erected in 1938 as a Public Works Administration project.[^1] The bottom was so soft that an outer timber crib was needed to hold the riprap in place, according to the Lake Carriers' Association's 1941 report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it.[^1] The station was placed in commission on June 18, 1941.[^1][^9] Its light is 52 feet (16 m) above the lake, shown through a 375-millimetre lens with an electric incandescent lamp.[^1][^9] The fog signal is a compressed-air diaphragm horn, controlled by radio from the Lake St. Clair Flats light station about eight miles away.[^1]

## The keepers

When the lightship went back on station after its 1935 rebuild, the Lake Carriers' account says it "had no crew," and its signals were controlled by radio from a nearby station.[^1]

Before that, the sources name one member of the lightship's crew. LighthouseFriends says Joseph Desjardins served aboard the Grosse Pointe lightship for sixteen years and was dismissed in 1909.[^1] The Detroit Times of 20 February 1909 spells the name DesJardins and calls him the mate of the Grosse Pointe lightship. It reported that he had celebrated Christmas Eve while the ship lay moored at the dock for the winter. An inspector in the lighthouse service reported him "unfit for duty," and he was separated from the government pay roll.[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The same Times article recalled that DesJardins had once been a hero in "saving six people from drowning when a yacht capsized" near the lightship, several years before. The article does not give a date for that rescue.[^8]

The Bulletin's January 1937 issue reported that "extreme cold weather at the beginning of December" had brought extensive ice to the Great Lakes, "particularly in the channels of Lake St. Clair and Detroit River."[^6] It said that it "became necessary to accelerate the relief of buoys and other floating aids to navigation." The report does not name this station.[^6]

## After the keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record gives "Keeper's Quarters?: No".[^9]

The fog signal was changed from a diaphragm horn to a bell in 1954.[^1] A remote weather station on the lighthouse operated for several years, and was disestablished on May 16, 2011.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light, and its tower is closed. LighthouseFriends says it is best viewed from the water.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record reads "Open to Public: No" and "Active Aid to Navigation?: No".[^9]
