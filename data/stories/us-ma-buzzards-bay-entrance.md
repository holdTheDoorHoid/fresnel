Buzzards Bay Entrance Light stands at the southern entrance to Buzzards Bay, Massachusetts, on a steel tower that was the first of five staffed "Texas Tower" lights built off the Atlantic coast. It went into service on 1 November 1961, in place of the lightships that had marked the entrance before it.[^1][^2] The tower was replaced in 1996 or 1997 by a smaller structure, and an automated tower marks the spot today.[^1][^2]

## The place

Lightships had marked the area before the tower.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1891 lists a "Hen and Chickens Light-Vessel No. 2, entrance to Buzzards Bay." It records that her crew's hammocks were replaced with berths and that she was given a new main boom.[^3]

Later sources give other Hen and Chickens numbers. A 1914 Bulletin personnel notice names a "Hen and Chickens Shoal Light Vessel No. 42, Mass.",[^9] and a 1939 notice names a "Hen and Chickens Lightship No. 86" without giving a state.[^10] The sources do not say whether these were the vessel the Board listed as No. 2.

In 1893 the Board's report on Hen and Chickens No. 2 says the vessel "leaks somewhat about the stem" and will be brought in for repairs to her copper.[^5]

The Cape Cod Canal made it necessary to light the entrances. The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1918 that "On completion of the Cape Cod Canal, the dredged channel in Buzzards Bay leading to same, and the breakwater at the eastern Sandwich entrance, it became necessary to light these two entrances."[^7] At the Buzzards Bay entrance to the dredged channel, the Service placed three acetylene gas and bell buoys and three acetylene gas buoys.[^8] LighthouseFriends says the tower was set up to help mark the southern approach to the canal.[^1]

## Building the light

According to LighthouseFriends, the Perini Corporation built the tower at a cost of $950,000, and four steel legs were driven to bedrock 268 feet (82 m) below the surface of the water, which is 61 feet (19 m) deep.[^1] The archive's record gives the tower as steel, on steel piles filled with concrete, with a skeletal shape.[^2]

The deck house held living quarters for a crew of seven, along with a kitchen, a recreation room and space for machinery. Its roof was a helicopter landing pad.[^1] The light stood 117 feet (36 m) above the water, with a foghorn and a radiobeacon.[^1]

Weather complicated the commissioning on 1 November 1961. LighthouseFriends says high seas, raised by winds of 30 miles an hour, kept the Coast Guard cutter *Frederick Lee* from positioning alongside the tower. A forty-foot (12 m) patrol boat from the Cuttyhunk Island lifeboat station carried the visitors out instead.[^1] Rear Admiral Chester L. Harding came by Coast Guard helicopter.[^1] The station was commissioned at 11:28, and the Buzzards Bay lightship was relieved of duty.[^1]

## The keepers

Ralph O. Morris was the first officer in charge. LighthouseFriends says he was placed in charge of the Stonehorse Shoal lightship only weeks later, and Lionel F. Crossman took over his duties.[^1]

The archive lists nine principal keepers for the tower: Ralph O. Morris (1961), Lionel F. Crossman (1961 to 1962), Jack B. Netherwood (1962), Robert A. Buotte (1962 to 1964), Joseph F. Burgess (1964 to 1965), Lawrence R. Carr (1965 to 1966), Robert A. Cole (1966), Elias J. Martinez (1966 to 1968) and Gerald F. Pickett (1968 to at least 1970).[^2] The archive also lists 46 Coast Guard names for the station between 1961 and 1971, most with spans of a year or two.[^2]

The archive files one more name under this place: Henry R. Holt, a lamp lighter, from at least 1919 to at least 1921. The archive does not say which light he tended, and the record does not place him at the tower.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Board's report for fiscal 1891 records buoy damage from an October gale in 1890. "During October a severe gale caused much damage to the buoys along the coast." In the same report, a whistling buoy off Hen and Chickens Reef "went adrift and came on shore near Cuttyhunk light-station badly damaged."[^4]

Ice did damage in 1917. The Commissioner's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1917 says that "Ice conditions during February, 1917, were unusually severe in the North Atlantic coast districts, and many aids to navigation, as well as lighthouse vessels, were damaged, particularly in Buzzards Bay."[^6]

## After the keepers

The archive's Coast Guard lines run to at least 1971.[^2] LighthouseFriends says an automated tower marks the location today.[^1]

The archive records the 1961 tower as discontinued in 1994. It says the tower was dismantled in 1996 or 1997 and replaced with a smaller structure.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that a second tower replaced the first in 1996.[^1] The two sources differ by a year on the replacement, and this story does not choose between them.

## Today

An automated tower marks the entrance, in place of the 1961 tower.[^1] The archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^2] The tower stands at the entrance to the bay, and LighthouseFriends describes visitors only at the 1961 commissioning, who came by cutter, patrol boat and helicopter.[^1]
