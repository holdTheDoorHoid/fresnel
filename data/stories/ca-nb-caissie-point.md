Caissie Point Lighthouse is a square, tapered wooden tower on the southeastern shore of New Brunswick, in the village of Cap-de-Caissie, built in 1872 to mark the approach to Shediac Bay on the Northumberland Strait.[^1][^2]

## The place

Shediac was the northern end of the European and North American Railway, and from its wharf at Pointe-du-Chêne ferries ran to Prince Edward Island and Nova Scotia.[^1] In his 1871 report, the Marine Agent at Saint John, John H. Harding, called a light at Caissie Point (spelled "Cassie's Point" in the early reports) of "first importance" to the trade of the strait and of Shediac, where steamers also called to discharge.[^1] Parks Canada's heritage statement likewise ranks it among the most significant lights built on the Northumberland Strait, because of the number of vessels loading and unloading cargo at Shediac.[^3]

## Building the light

The tower was under construction when Harding wrote in 1871.[^1] It is a square, tapered, wood-frame tower, 13.9 m (46 ft) high, shingled, with an octagonal metal lantern under a red roof, and with the keeper's dwelling attached to it originally.[^2][^3][^1] The light began operating on 30 August 1872, showing a flashing white light from two banks of lamps, each backed by an 18-inch reflector; the apparatus turned once in three minutes, giving a flash every ninety seconds.[^1] Masters of steamers trading to the railway terminus at Shediac praised the new light, according to the Lighthouse Friends history, which draws on the Department of Marine's annual reports.[^1]

The federal heritage review of the tower describes its wooden platform as designed so that the tower could occasionally be moved to suit shifting channels.[^4]

## The keepers

Charles P. LeBlanc was the first keeper, at an annual salary of $240. In 1875 his household was himself, his wife and two children; a year later the family numbered five, and the inspector noted that a fence was needed to keep cattle away from the premises.[^1] Inspectors praised him often: Lighthouse Friends quotes "very attentive, and keeps the building in good order."[^1]

How long he served is unclear. The same page says in its text that he served thirty-eight years, to 1901, and in its list of keepers that he served from 1872 to 1909. The Department of Marine's keeper tables for 1903 and 1904 both still list him at Cassie Point, appointed 4 May 1872, which is consistent with the longer span.[^1][^5][^6] After him, Lighthouse Friends lists Placide Legere (1909 to 1913), Alfred Gallant (1914 to 1936) and Edelbert Babineau (1936 to 1963).[^1] The department's list for the New Brunswick agency in the fiscal year ended March 1915 shows Alfred Gallant at Cassie point, appointed 28 January 1914, which agrees with that date.[^7]

The station was given a boat in 1878 and, in 1900, a manual foghorn to be sounded whenever the fog signals of vessels nearby were heard.[^1][^8] In the fiscal year ended March 1915 the department spent $766.08 at Cassie point on providing and installing a flashing long-focus reflector, work it said would be completed the following year.[^9]

## After the keepers

The record gives no date for the end of resident keepers. The last keeper Lighthouse Friends names ended in 1963.[^1] The tower was recognised on 5 September 1991 as a federal heritage building and on 21 March 2022 as a heritage lighthouse under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act.[^10][^2] The original keeper's dwelling has been moved a short distance to an adjacent lot.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse. The register lists its current function as a navigational aid, and Lighthouse Friends describes it as active with a pattern of flash, eclipse, flash, longer eclipse.[^1][^2] The register and Lighthouse Friends give no more recent status than this. It stands at Lighthouse Drive in Cap-de-Caissie, surrounded by private property: it can be viewed from the road, but the tower and grounds are closed.[^1][^2]
