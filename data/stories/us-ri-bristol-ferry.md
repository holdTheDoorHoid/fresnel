Bristol Ferry Light stands on the southern tip of Bristol Point, on the north side of the entrance to Mount Hope Bay, in Rhode Island. A two-story brick tower was attached to the front of the keeper's house in 1855.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society gives 1927 as the year the light was discontinued, but the nomination dates the removal of its lantern to 1928.[^2][^1] The house has been private since the lighthouse was sold in 1929, and the Mount Hope Bridge was built directly above it in 1928 and 1929.[^1]

## The place

The strait between Bristol and Portsmouth "was increasingly busy in the early 1800s," with traffic to and from the textile mills of Fall River.[^3] In 1846 the Bay State Steamboat Company set up a small lighted beacon at Ferry Point.[^3] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive gives 1846 as the year the station was established.[^2] The light took its name from a Bristol-to-Portsmouth ferry that operated from a landing a few yards west of it.[^1] The nomination says the light "served to guide ships en route to the textile mills of Fall River."[^1]

The early beacon proved unreliable.[^3] Benjamin Brayton, master of the steamer *Empire State*, asked for a more substantial structure in November 1853.[^3] William Brown, master of the 320-foot (98 m) steamer *Bay State*, wrote of the mariners who "have to pass through this strait on dark and stormy nights."[^3]

## Building the light

Congress set aside $1,500 on 3 August 1854 for a station that combined a lighthouse with a dwelling.[^3] The land was acquired on 9 October 1854, and the tower and dwelling were completed the following year.[^1] The station paid $100 to George Pearse for the land.[^3] The one-and-a-half-story brick dwelling had a square 28-foot (9 m) tower at its southern end.[^3]

According to New England Lighthouses (Jeremy D'Entremont), the wooden lantern held a sixth-order Fresnel lens, first exhibited on 4 October 1855.[^3] The U.S. Lighthouse Society archive gives the original optic as a fifth-order Fresnel, so the two sources disagree.[^2]

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1895 lists the light among lights discontinued that year: a sixth-order light, discontinued on 30 October 1894 and reestablished on 5 November 1894. The report gives no reason.[^6] The guide says the light was upgraded to a fifth-order lens in 1902.[^3]

The 1918 changes are recorded in two versions. The nomination dates the removal of the wooden lantern, a six-foot (1.8 m) rise in the tower, and a new cast-iron lantern to 1918.[^1] The guide dates them to 1916 and says the iron lantern came from the Rondout Lighthouse on the Hudson River.[^3]

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1896 notes that about 125 running feet (38 m) of fence was built at the station and the old fence repaired.[^7]

## The keepers

George Pearse was the first keeper for a short time, and in December 1855 Henry Diman of Bristol was appointed.[^3][^2] Diman died about eight months later and was replaced by his widow, Elizabeth Diman.[^3] The archive lists Elizabeth Diman as principal keeper for 1856 to 1857.[^2] She too died the following February.[^3] The guide says Daniel W. Coggeshall, another Bristol native, succeeded her and stayed four years.[^3]

Charles Sanford followed, from 1861 to 1870, and "stayed for almost a decade," according to the guide.[^2][^3] The guide says of the station's keepers that "Turnover of keepers was frequent."[^3] The roster also lists George T. Gladding, James W. Waldron, William Dunwell and Edward P. Hoxie (the guide's log spells it Hoxsie).[^2][^3]

Edward Sherman was keeper for the longest stretch. The archive gives 1886 to 1916, and the guide gives 1886 to 1914.[^2][^3] The guide's keeper logs, as it quotes them, show Sherman at the light in 1887, 1891, 1903 and 1911.[^3] Arthur J. Baldwin is listed on the roster for 1916 to 1925.[^2] A log entry for 28 June 1915 mentions a "Keeper Baldwin" during an inspector's visit. The sources do not say whether he was the Arthur J. Baldwin on the roster, who is listed from 1916.[^3][^2] Leo R. Roode kept the light from 1925 to 1927.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1 October 1925 names him keeper of Bristol Ferry Lighthouse.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The guide says that on 8 September 1869 a hurricane struck the area. The keeper's dwelling flooded at the height of the storm, and the Sanford family had to leave until it passed.[^3]

The guide's keeper log entries, as it quotes them, record that on 19 March 1887 the schooner *Fairdeal* got ashore on the point in front of the light and "remained 2 1/2 hours."[^3] On 2 August 1903 Sherman rescued two men from an overturned boat, and on 14 August 1911 he went to the rescue of two men in an overturned canoe.[^3]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1918 reports that during the northeast storm of 13 and 14 December 1917 the sea "seriously threatened" the old station. Water covered the first floor of the buildings to a depth of two or three inches (5 to 8 cm), and the lower floor of the tower at its northeast corner "settled considerably."[^4]

On the evening of 25 August 1925 Roode rendered assistance to three boys whose canoe had capsized near the lighthouse.[^5]

## After the keepers

In 1928 the lantern was removed from the tower, and the light was replaced by an automatic beacon on a new steel skeletal tower across Ferry Road.[^1][^3] The Mount Hope Bridge was completed in 1929, and the guide says it cost $5 million and "rendered it irrelevant as an aid to navigation."[^3]

The lighthouse, its outbuildings and most of the land were sold in 1929, and it has been a private residence since.[^1] The Lighthouse Service kept the skeletal tower until 1934, when it was taken down and replaced by the Bristol Harbor Light.[^1] The guide says the property was rented for some years to students from Roger Williams University. The building was in deplorable shape when it was sold in 1991 to a private couple.[^3] After the sale, the new owners put in a new lantern, and a light for show, coming on for two hours each evening on a timer, was added.[^3]

## Today

The nomination lists the house as a private residence, with access "restricted."[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^2] The guide, with photographs from March 2025, says the house is managed as an inn: "Overnight accommodations are now offered through airbnb."[^3] The house and grounds are private, so the station is not open to visitors.[^1][^2]
