The Cuckolds are two low rock islets at the ocean mouth of Boothbay Harbor, about three-eighths of a mile south of Cape Newagen, rising roughly 15 feet (4.6 m) above high water and washed over by the sea in heavy storms.[^1] The station on the eastern rock began in 1892 as a fog signal, and in 1907 a small wooden light tower was set on top of the brick signal house.[^1]

## The place

Boothbay Harbor was a busy fishing port in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and the Cuckolds sat in the west side of its entrance channel.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for 1890, as quoted in the National Register nomination, called the rocks "much dreaded by mariners in thick weather" and put the number of vessels that ran into the bay for refuge at three to four thousand.[^1] The Board said it had received "numerous petitions" for a fog signal.[^1]

A wooden tripod day mark already stood on the rocks. The National Register nomination says it existed by 1888, when Maine deeded the island to the United States; New England Lighthouses gives 1874 for the tripod.[^1][^2] The nomination dates the deed to 30 June 1890. Congress authorised a fog signal that August but made no appropriation, and then voted $25,000 on 3 March 1891.[^1]

## Building the light

The fog signal station was finished on 16 November 1892 at a cost of $24,750, machinery included.[^1] Because the ledge floods in storms, the builders raised the signal house on a semicircular granite pier 36 feet (11 m) across and 12 feet (3.7 m) high, with a brick building above it; the nomination calls this the only fog signal building known to have been built to this design.[^1] The materials came mainly by the lighthouse tender *Myrtle*, and the work had begun at the Light-House Board's shop in Portland in January 1892 before moving to the rock three months later.[^3] Attached to the signal house was a wood-framed double dwelling, bolted to the ledge, each half with a kitchen, pantry and sitting room below and two bedrooms above.[^1]

The Light-House Board reported that the Daboll trumpet, fitted in duplicate, "was put in operation for the first time December 15, 1892."[^4] In the fiscal year ended June 1894 it ran about 1,009 hours.[^5] The reports are not consistent about its size: that year's table of hours calls it a first-class trumpet, while the signal tests in the same volume describe it as a third-class trumpet.[^5] A 1,000-pound bell was added in 1895 to sound while air pressure built up for the trumpet.[^6] In 1902 the old hot-air machinery was replaced by oil engines, and in 1904 the signal ran 1,220 hours.[^1]

The light followed in 1907. The nomination says the tower was added "due to increased fishing vessel traffic in the bay"; there was no room for a separate tower on the rock, so it was built on the roof of the fog signal house.[^1] Its fourth-order Fresnel lens showed a double white flash every six seconds.[^1]

## The keepers

Edward H. Pierce was the first head keeper, from 1892 to 1898, with Wolcott H. Marr as his assistant.[^7] The Marr name runs through the station's first quarter-century: Clarence E. Marr was assistant from 1896 and head keeper from 1898 to 1899, and Preston L. Marr served as assistant from 1898 and then as head keeper from 1907 to 1919.[^7] Fred T. Robinson followed (1919 to 1925), then Edward D. Elliot (1925 to 1934); the nomination records that Justin A. Foss became keeper on 8 March 1934, with H. E. Seavey as his assistant.[^7][^1] The archive's roster stops with John Sitton, head keeper in 1972 to 1974.[^7] These are the archive's year spans, taken from the keeper registers, and some names and dates may be only approximate.

This was a station where keepers' families lived on the rock. A Portland newspaper describing the station in January 1896 said that "two keepers and their families" lived on the larger islet.[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

**The Aurora, January 1896.** On the night of Saturday 4 January 1896, the 85-ton British schooner *Aurora*, bound from Harborville, Nova Scotia, to Boston with a deck load of wood, went ashore on the smaller of the two islets in fog and a rough sea at about half past six.[^8] The Daily Kennebec Journal had a short report within days, saying the crew were safe.[^9] The Portland Daily Press told the full story from the crew who reached that city. By their account the tide rose and the schooner fell over on her beam ends; the deck load went overboard, and the men took refuge on top of the cabin house with the thermometer below zero and the waves breaking over them.[^8] For four hours, until midnight, they stayed there, until the men on the other islet saw their light or heard their foghorn.[^8] The keepers came out in their dory, but the sea was too rough to take anyone off in it. According to the paper they rowed to a neighbouring island for larger boats and the help of fishermen, then took the men off one at a time, backing up to the main rigging and waiting while each man "would watch his chance and spring from the rigging into the boat."[^8] The sailors stayed at the station until Tuesday because the sea was too rough to reach the mainland, and the paper put the temperature on the Cuckolds as low as 18 below.[^8] The Light-House Board's report of 1900 records that Congress, by an act of 28 March 1900, allowed keepers C. E. Marr and E. H. Pierce to accept silver watches awarded by the Dominion of Canada for rescuing the captain and crew of the *Aurora* on 4 January 1896.[^10] New England Lighthouses says two lobstermen from Cape Newagen helped.[^2]

**Later rescues.** The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded assistant keeper Ernest V. Talbot rescuing a party of seven in a motor boat lost in the fog in August 1917.[^11] Its 1923 volume reports that keeper Robinson and Talbot rescued the master and crew of the schooner *Barbara W.* when she went on the rocks near the station on 28 November.[^12] LighthouseFriends dates that rescue to 1922 and says the keepers signalled for a Coast Guard vessel and looked after six men until it came.[^3] In September 1925 Robinson saved several people from a disabled vessel that was drifting out to sea.[^1] In 1930 the Bulletin noted that Elliot had gone to the help of a disabled boat carrying seven people and towed it to safe harbour.[^13]

**The Great Gale, January 1933.** In the northeaster of 27 and 28 January 1933, the bulkhead that protected the station was torn away and much of the contents of the assistant keeper's dwelling was destroyed; the Department of Commerce reimbursed the assistant for his losses, a list that included his radio and a Hawaiian guitar.[^1] The head keeper told the Rockland *Courier-Gazette*, as LighthouseFriends quotes him, that the seas had driven the bulkhead into the east side of the house and broken through the dining-room wall, flooding the rooms.[^3]

**A Coast Guard night.** Larson Alley, a Coast Guardsman at the Cuckolds in 1969, later described a gale that came up the coast when the crew could not reach the helicopter pad on the smaller outcrop because the tide was high. They rode it out in the tower, after leaving a house with a foot of water in it; he summed it up as a long night.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard automated the light in 1974, according to the US Lighthouse Society's archive and LighthouseFriends; the National Register nomination says 1975.[^7][^3][^1] The keeper's dwelling and boathouse did not survive for long. The nomination says a blizzard in 1978 destroyed the dwelling; New England Lighthouses says Coast Guard sources date its demolition to 1977, and LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard demolished the buildings because of vandalism.[^1][^2][^3] The fourth-order lens was removed in 1980 and is now at the Maine Lighthouse Museum in Rockland.[^3] The nomination, written earlier, places it at the Shore Village Museum there.[^1]

In 2004 the Coast Guard declared the station excess, and a local nonprofit, the Cuckolds Fog Signal and Light Station Council, applied for it under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act; LighthouseFriends reports that it was awarded the deed in April 2006, New England Lighthouses says May 2006, and a 2020 *Boothbay Register* article describes the Council as holding a lease from the National Park Service.[^3][^2][^14] The Council rebuilt the boathouse in 2010 and the keeper's dwelling in 2011 and 2012, and in June 2014 opened the dwelling as the Inn at Cuckolds Lighthouse, run by resident hosts, who the source says also helped mariners in trouble.[^3] The inn closed abruptly on 31 July 2019.[^3]

## Today

The light is still an active aid to navigation, flashing twice every six seconds from 59 feet (18 m) above high water through an LED optic.[^7][^16] The 2002 nomination notes that the island can be reached only by boat or helicopter.[^1] In 2020 a new group, Friends of Cuckolds Light Station, formed to take over the lease from the National Park Service, and in August 2021 it was still waiting for the Park Service to act on its application, hoping to open the island by appointment.[^14][^15] The sources consulted do not say how that application ended, so the station should be treated as closed to the public unless a current announcement says otherwise. The light and signal house can be seen from excursion boats out of Boothbay Harbor and from the public landing at Cape Newagen.[^2]
