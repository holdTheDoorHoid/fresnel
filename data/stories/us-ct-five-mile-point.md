Five Mile Point Light has marked the entrance to New Haven Harbor since 1805. Its brownstone tower, the second on the site, has stood there since the 1840s, on a point about five miles (8 km) from the center of New Haven that gave the light its name.[^1][^2] The tower is inside Lighthouse Point Park, in New Haven.[^1]

## The place

The National Register nomination says the light marked the eastern side of the harbor entrance, where the harbor opens onto Long Island Sound. The mouth of the harbor was about four miles (6.4 km) wide, and the nomination says that left the port's berthing and loading areas open to wind and waves.[^1] A dangerous ledge lay to the light's southwest.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes Southwest Ledge as lying in the middle of the harbor's shipping channel, under only seven and a half feet (2.3 m) of water at low tide.[^2]

The point had seen fighting before there was a light on it.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that in July 1779 British troops tried to land there on their way to New Haven, that American defenders waited for them, and that the British officer Watkins was killed in the fight. It says the British also burned the house and buildings of the landowner, Amos Morris.[^2]

## Building the light

The nomination says the government completed the first lighthouse in 1805 to guide the China traders, sealers and packets that used the port.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Morris sold the tract to the government for $100, and that Jonathan Finch was appointed keeper on 19 December 1804.[^2] That first tower was a wooden, octagonal structure 30 feet (9.1 m) tall, with eight lamps set in 13-inch (33 cm) reflectors.[^2]

The sources do not agree on the keeper's house or where it stood. The nomination describes a brick dwelling built during the War of 1812, when an earlier light was already on the site, standing about 30 feet (9 m) east of the tower.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a two-and-a-half-story keeper's dwelling was added in 1835, and places it just north of the tower.[^2]

The second tower is the one that stands today, and here the sources part company on dates. The nomination says the new brownstone tower was placed in service in 1845. Its stone came from the East Haven quarry of Jabez Potter, and the contract went to the New Haven builder Marcus Bassett.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a new tower was recommended in 1845, and that Bassett finished it in 1847 for $10,000, with its light 97 feet (30 m) above the water.[^2] The nomination gives the tower's height as about 80 feet (24 m). LighthouseFriends gives 65 feet (20 m).[^1][^2]

The lamps were replaced by a fourth-order Fresnel lens in 1855, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The cast-iron lantern with its conical roof "was probably installed in the late 1860s or early 1870s," the nomination says, when American lighthouse technology began to use prefabricated iron components.[^1]

LighthouseFriends quotes the Light-House Board's report for 1868, which listed the station's needs. The tower needed new windows. The dwelling and oil vault stood where surface water collected, and at times the oil vault held as much as eight inches (20 cm) of water.[^2] A fog bell worked by a caloric engine was installed in 1860. After an 1870 appropriation, repairs were made, and a striking apparatus replaced the engine.[^2]

## The keepers

The roster compiled by the U.S. Lighthouse Society from the National Archives' keeper registers begins with Jonathan Finch, listed as "Jonathan Finch (1804-1821)".[^3] His son Milton followed, and LighthouseFriends says Milton died three years later at the age of 35.[^2][^3]

In 1806 Finch advertised in the *Connecticut Journal*, from the light itself, that he had a good stock of liquor and could accommodate parties with lobsters, blackfish and clams. LighthouseFriends quotes the advertisement.[^2]

Elihu Ives kept the light from 1824 to 1846, according to the roster, and LighthouseFriends credits his years with the building of the second tower.[^3][^2]

The roster lists Sarah A. Bradley as a first assistant keeper from 1868 to 1869, and Elizabeth M. Thompson from 1869 to 1871. LighthouseFriends calls the second "Mrs. E.M. Thompson."[^3][^2] Neither source says how either came to the post or how either was related to the principal keepers.[^3][^2]

Elizur Thompson kept the light twice, from 1861 to 1867 and again from 1869 to 1877, with Charles W. Bradley in between.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he left in 1867 after reports of gold in Alaska and returned two years later with no money.[^2] He then kept the Southwest Ledge light until 1881. He stayed on at Five Mile Point as custodian of the property, paid one dollar a year, until his death in 1898.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Southwest Ledge light replaced Five Mile Point on 1 January 1877, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The nomination says the government decommissioned the old tower when the ledge light went into service in 1877.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1897 describes the old station as "abandoned in 1877 upon the establishment of the Southwest Ledge light-station," and records its transfer to the War Department on 13 November 1896.[^4]

LighthouseFriends says the War Department leased the property to Albert Widmann, who for a small fee let visitors climb the tower. When the lease ended in 1922, it says, the land went to the State of Connecticut and the buildings to the city of New Haven.[^2] The nomination gives a different date for the city's acquisition: it says the property "remained as federal property until the city acquired the property in 1924 for Lighthouse Point Park."[^1] The park opened to the public in 1949, and the tower was renovated in 1986, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The lighthouse and its keeper's dwelling were listed on the National Register in 1990.[^1]

## Today

The tower stands in Lighthouse Point Park, which the city owns.[^1] The park also has an early 20th-century carousel, a ranger station and a bathing pavilion, the nomination says.[^1] In 1990 the keeper's dwelling was a private residence for New Haven Recreation Department staff, and its interior was not accessible.[^1]

LighthouseFriends lists the grounds as open and the dwelling and tower as closed, and gives the East Shore Ranger Station as the contact for tours of the lighthouse.[^2] The tower is closed to climbing, so visitors should go to the grounds and check with the ranger station before expecting to go inside.
