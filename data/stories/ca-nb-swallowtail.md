Swallowtail Lighthouse stands at the end of a rocky peninsula north of North Head, on Grand Manan Island, New Brunswick.[^1] Its octagonal wooden tower was first lit on 7 July 1860.[^2] The register says it is believed to be the seventh-oldest octagonal wooden tower standing in Canada.[^3] It remains an active aid to navigation, owned by the Village of Grand Manan.[^4]

## The place

The register describes the light as closely associated with aids for regional and international shipping at the entrance to the Bay of Fundy.[^3] The British ship Lord Ashburton, sailing from Toulon for Saint John, was lost on Grand Manan in January 1857.[^5] The New-York Tribune printed an Eastport dispatch on 27 January that gave the date as the 10th of the month, and said that eight men of the twenty-nine aboard were saved.[^5] LighthouseFriends gives the date as 19 January at 1 a.m., places the wreck on the island's northern shore, and says seven other crew members were saved.[^2] The sources differ on the date, and on how many were saved.

LighthouseFriends reports a New Brunswick House of Assembly recommendation for a light on the northern head of Grand Manan.[^2] In June 1859 the contractor, John P. McKay of Saint John, and I. Woodward, superintendent of lighthouses in the Bay of Fundy, chose a spot on the Swallow's Tail for the buildings.[^2] A Department report of 1875, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, describes the site as a high, almost sheer cliff, with no approach from the land except across a deep gorge.[^2]

## Building the light

McKay built the tower in 1859, but the lantern room was not finished until the following spring.[^2] The tower is an "octagonal heavy hewn-timber construction covered with wood shingles", with eight facets for most of its height and seven near the top.[^3]

Jonathan Kent, the first keeper, lit it on 7 July 1860 with nine lamps in twenty-inch reflectors, covering three-quarters of a circle. An extra lamp and reflector were added to light five-sixths of a circle, for vessels heading to the western part of Long Island Bay.[^2] A bridge to the headland was built in 1861.[^2] In 1887 the reflectors were replaced by a lens, so the optic changed from catoptric to dioptric.[^2]

The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1909 records that the fourth-order fixed white light was replaced with an occulting white light, visible for 4 seconds and eclipsed for 2 seconds.[^6] LighthouseFriends instead dates a fourth-order lens and the occulting signal to 1907, along with a Chance vapour installation.[^2] The sources do not agree on the year. The same report says the tramway was repaired that year.[^6]

G. N. Breen completed a wooden building for a fog bell in 1914, at a cost of $974. In 1920 it was moved from the tip of the peninsula to stand beside the tower.[^2] A new keeper's dwelling was built in 1958, and the old one was demolished.[^2]

## The keepers

Jonathan Kent kept the light until 1873. His son John W. Kent took over, and LighthouseFriends says he began on a salary of $400 a year.[^2]

An 1877 Department report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, praised the station as a model for the Department.[^2]

LighthouseFriends gives George T. Dalzell's service as 1893 to 1912.[^2] The Department's keepers' table for the year ended 30 June 1905 lists "Dalzell, Geo. Y" at Swallow Tail, appointed 18 March 1893.[^7] The table's OCR text gives the middle initial as Y, where LighthouseFriends has T. The two sources do not agree on his middle initial.[^2][^7]

George A. Lahey served from 1912 to 1936.[^2] In August 1936, with her husband away at Southwest Head Lighthouse, Elodie Foster was tending the light.[^2] LighthouseFriends lists Thomas P. Foster from 1936 to at least 1937, and L.E. Foster for 1936 alone. It does not say which Foster was at Southwest Head.[^2] Wallace Lahey (1916) and R.S. Lahey (1917) appear with a single year each, and LighthouseFriends gives no role for either.[^2]

Grimmer A. Ingersoll kept the light from 1960 until 1986, when it was automated.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends reports that the Saxby Gale struck on 4 October 1869, when the station was in first-rate condition.[^2] The gale destroyed the station's boat and washed away a large part of the landing slip.[^2] The foundation was significantly damaged, and a stone wall had to be built beneath the tower.[^2] The keeper's dwelling was shaken so hard that two chains were run over its roof and fixed to the rock on each side.[^2]

In August 1936 Elodie Foster accidentally overfilled the light, and her clothes caught fire. Her son climbed the tower quickly and put out the flames in the lantern room, but she died the next day.[^2]

At a 2010 anniversary celebration, a keeper's daughter recalled that during the Groundhog Gale of 1976 she and her father had to crawl across a wooden bridge to reach the dwelling.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Directory gives the transfer of ownership to the Village of Grand Manan as 2012.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives a different sequence: the property went to the Village of North Head in 1994 and then to the Village of Grand Manan in 1996.[^2] The sources differ on the date.

In March 2008 the village council announced that it would sell the keeper's dwelling, because upkeep was too expensive. After a public meeting, the council rescinded the motion, and the Swallowtail Keepers Society was formed to rejuvenate the lighthouse.[^2] On 2 November 2009 the village signed a twenty-year lease with the Keepers Society, which could then apply for grant money to maintain the site.[^2]

The Society held a celebration at the lighthouse on 7 July 2010, 150 years to the day after the light was first lit.[^2] The tower was opened in 2015 after further restoration.[^1] On 10 June 2019 a Coast Guard helicopter lifted off the lantern room that had sat on top of the tower since about 1970, and set a new aluminum lantern room in its place. The new lantern holds a fourth-order Fresnel lens lit by LED.[^2] The restoration was completed in August 2024.[^1]

## Today

The light is still an active aid to navigation.[^4][^8] The Coast Guard list gives its characteristic as "Oc W 6s", with a focal height of 37.1 m and a range of 12 nautical miles, and describes the tower as a "White octagonal tower with red top".[^8]

The Village of Grand Manan owns the site, and the Swallowtail Keepers Society manages it.[^4] Visitors can climb the 54 steps to the top of the tower. Three floors of museum displays are inside, and admission is $2 per person.[^9] Opening times differ: the Lighthouse Directory says the tower is open daily in summer, while LighthouseFriends describes occasional tours, so check before visiting.[^1][^2] The ferry from Blacks Harbour passes the lighthouse as it arrives.[^1]
