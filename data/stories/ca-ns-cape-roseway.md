Cape Roseway light stands on the southeast point of McNutt's Island, where the entrance to Shelburne Harbour opens on Nova Scotia's southwest shore. A light has shown from the point since 1788, when the province built a stone tower there. The tower standing now is concrete, put up in 1961 after a lightning fire in 1959.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

In the spring of 1783 more than 5,000 settlers from New York and neighbouring colonies sailed north to Port Roseway, and the settlement that grew there was soon renamed Shelburne.[^1] For a brief time it was the fourth-largest city in North America, according to the Nova Scotia Archives.[^4] The government stopped handing out provisions in 1787, and many settlers left. By the 1820s about 300 people remained.[^1]

The light was meant for the approach to that harbour. In 1787 a member of the House of Assembly moved a grant of £500 for a lighthouse to guide ships in.[^1] McNutt's Island sits across the harbour mouth, and the tower went on the granite bluffs at its southeast point.[^1]

## Building the light

The first tower was an octagonal building of local stone, about 23.5 m (77 ft) high, with walls 1.8 m thick at the base.[^1][^2] The Society's essay gives its height as 28 m (92 ft), while the detail table on the same page gives 23.5 m (77 ft).[^2] Lighthouse Friends calls it the second lighthouse the province built, after Sambro; the Society counts it the third lighthouse in Canada.[^1][^2] The Archives say it went up in 1788 in response to local demands for the protection of shipping.[^4] The first keeper, Alexander Cocken, was appointed that year.[^1]

The keeper's house was a problem for decades. In 1795 Cocken asked the House for a kitchen, a cellar and a storehouse, and for a road across the island. His residence then had just two small rooms, and the only landing place was about 5 km (3 miles) away, at the opposite end of the island.[^1] In 1833 the commissioners of lighthouses, as Lighthouse Friends quotes them, called the keeper's house a jumble of old sheds in a state of ruin. A new house was built the next year.[^1]

An inspection in 1857, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, found the lantern and lamps worn out, through no fault of the keeper. The inspector fitted nine new catoptric lamps in the lantern and three in a bow window lower on the tower. The station showed two lights, one from each.[^1]

A square fog-alarm building went up in 1883 to house a steam fog trumpet, with a brick tank holding 6,400 gallons of water.[^1] The Society dates the fog signal itself to 1884.[^2] Later reports of the Marine Department show the machinery being kept up. In the report labelled 1900, a new reed box and six new reeds were fitted to the fog trumpet.[^6] In the report labelled 1909, a fog-horn engine recently removed from Cranberry Island was repaired and installed here.[^7]

In 1919 a third-order lens went into a new lantern room on top of the tower, and the upper light changed from fixed to flashing, which let the lower light go.[^1] The Society gives 1922 for a third-order dioptric lens and an air diaphone.[^2]

## The keepers

Alexander Cocken was one of the Port Roseway Associates. Lighthouse Friends lists him as arriving in 1783 with his wife and three servants.[^1] In a petition to the Assembly he said he had been paid only £115 and had fallen into debt. The House awarded him £50 more and set his monthly pay at six pounds five shillings.[^1]

The family's dates do not agree. Lighthouse Friends' roster has the father to 1812, but its narrative says he died in 1821.[^1] Yarbrough's blog, which cites the son's letter in the Archives, gives 1812.[^5] His son, Alexander Hood Cocken, took over. In a letter of 1 June 1860 to Joseph Howe, he asked to retire on a pension after forty-eight years at the light, as the blog describes it.[^5] To add to his pay he ran a school for boys, who lodged at the lighthouse during term. As more men turned to fishing, an assistant cost him nearly half his wages.[^1]

In the late 1860s a report noted that the keeper was unfit for his duties, and an assistant was sent to the station for two months.[^1]

The Society's roster gives Otis Orchard as head keeper from 1920 to 1956. Lighthouse Friends gives Beaumont Decker from 1920 to 1944, then Orchard from 1944 to 1953.[^1][^2] A 1950 feature in *The Standard*, as the Society quotes it, described the light as tended by Orchard, then 62, and his assistant Nelson Goulden, who worked six-hour shifts.[^2]

Bernice Goodick's account differs from the roster. Her father, who had been Orchard's assistant for a few months, became keeper when Orchard retired, and kept the light until June 1956.[^2] She told the Society that the station had no electricity. Kerosene had to be carried up, she thought, nine sets of stairs, and the lamp's pressure had to be pumped every four hours.[^2] Harry Van Buskirk, head keeper from 1966 to 1983, and Robert Croft, who served until 1986, were the last keepers before automation.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Orchard recalled four shipwrecks on the island in his twenty years as keeper: the *Nellie Viola*, the *Governor Marshall*, a government examination boat, and the two-masted *Good Luck*. The Society prints his recollections from the 1950 feature.[^2]

The *Good Luck* ran aground on the night of 2 January 1937, in a blizzard, and sank within five minutes. Her captain and crew rode ashore on her cargo of laths. In Orchard's time, the crews of all the ships that were wrecked survived.[^2]

Lightning struck the stone tower in 1959, and the fire that followed destroyed its heavy oak timbers, which gave support inside.[^1][^2] The Coast Guard judged the tower beyond economical repair and had it demolished.[^2] A temporary light was set on the roof of the fog-alarm building in 1960 while the old tower came down.[^2] Cameron Contracting Ltd. was paid $24,382 to build the concrete tower, which the Society dates to 1961.[^1][^2]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1986, and the station became unmanned.[^1][^2] The houses have stood empty since then.[^2] The fog horn was taken out of service in 1989, and the radio beacon was discontinued.[^2] In 1992 the light was given an electric lantern and began to flash white every ten seconds.[^2]

The Society writes that the dwellings and the old fog-alarm building are on what it calls the Canadian Lighthouse Doomsday List. It says the station should be refurbished and maintained, since it is one of the few in Nova Scotia that remains relatively intact.[^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the station, and the light still works.[^1][^3] The Coast Guard's list gives a white octagonal tower with a red top, flashing once a second and dark for nine, with a focal height of 33.1 m and a nominal range of 10 nautical miles.[^3] The list gives the structure as 17.3 m tall, while the Society gives the tower as 14.6 m (48 ft). The two heights do not agree, and the sources do not say why.[^2][^3]

The station is reached by boat only. Lighthouse Friends reports that the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1][^2] Little is left of the 1788 station. A 1988 federal heritage review, as the Society quotes it, found very little physical evidence of the station's history, perhaps only some granite blocks near the cliff edge, dumped there when the old tower was demolished.[^2] Also standing in 1988 were the 1960 replacement tower, the houses and sheds, and a steel tower once used for a radio transmitter.[^2]
