Georges Island Light is the front light of the Halifax Harbour Inner range. It stands on the west side of Georges Island, a fortified island in Halifax Harbour, Nova Scotia, and has shown from the island since 15 January 1876.[^1][^10] The octagonal concrete tower there now dates from 1917, after the second lighthouse burned in 1916.[^1][^8][^9]

## The place

Georges Island became home to a battery of cannons in 1750, a year after Halifax was founded.[^1] Prince Edward, who became commandant of Halifax in 1794, had a fort built on the island four years later. He named it Fort Charlotte after his mother.[^1] The island served as a prison for large numbers of Acadians during the Acadian Deportation of 1755 to 1763.[^1] Its forts are now part of the Halifax Defence Complex, and Parks Canada describes the island as an Acadian internment camp and a prison.[^11]

Merchants engaged in navigation asked for a lighthouse on the island in 1857.[^1] A beacon there had the lowest priority among that year's approved works, and the allocated funds were insufficient to build it.[^1] In 1866 S. Cunard & Co. and the Superintendent of Lighthouses for Nova Scotia petitioned for a harbour light.[^1] The light was to guide vessels into and out of the harbour through the channel west of the island.[^1]

## Building the light

Work began in 1875, and the light was placed in operation on 15 January 1876.[^1] Thomas Halliwell built the square wooden tower for $1,300.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes the Department's description, which gives the building a height of 6.4 m (21 ft), painted drab, with the top of the lantern black.[^1] NSLPS, however, describes the first tower as 10.7 m (35 ft) high.[^8] Two fixed white lights stood 6.1 m (20 ft) apart, one above the other, the upper one 15.2 m (50 ft) above high water. The lamps were three flat-wick burners with 43 cm (17 in) reflectors and one B lamp with a 30 cm (12 in) reflector.[^1]

In 1889 the fog bell from Meagher's Beach was taken to the island and mounted on the west side of the lighthouse, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] NSLPS dates the transfer to 1899 in its text, though its table gives 1889.[^8]

In the spring of 1903 the two white lights were discontinued. A single revolving red light replaced them, shown from a polygonal iron lantern on a square wooden tower beside the keeper's dwelling.[^2] That tower stood 10.7 m (35 ft) from its base to the lantern vane.[^1]

On 31 March 1903 a light was shown from the Dartmouth Exhibition building, whose tower was raised to 21.3 m (70 ft), in line with the island's revolving red light. The pair led vessels up the harbour from Mauger's Beach.[^3] By 1920 the rear light was a black pyramidal skeletal tower about 100 m from the exhibition building.[^1]

The Department's report for fiscal 1913–14 records a fourth-order occulting dioptric lens, showing red, in place of the revolving catoptric light.[^4] LighthouseFriends says a fourth-order Fresnel lens was mounted in 1913.[^1] NSLPS says the light was group occulting white until 1922, when a fourth-order dioptric lens was installed.[^8] The sources do not agree on the lens or its colour.

Fire destroyed the second lighthouse in 1916.[^1][^9] NSLPS says a temporary red occulting light was shown afterwards.[^8] LighthouseFriends says a provisional light shone until the new tower was built in 1917.[^1]

The new tower is octagonal and of concrete, with a cast-iron lantern room of 2.4 m (8 ft).[^1] It was erected in 1917, and the brick keeper's dwelling about 61 m (200 ft) away was also built that year.[^1][^8][^9] NSLPS's table gives 1919 as the year the present light was first lit, though its text says only that it was lit "early in January".[^8]

## The keepers

Robert Ross, a sapper in the Royal Engineers, was appointed keeper on 18 January 1876 at $150 a year.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives his service as 1876 to 1919.[^1] The Department's keeper table for fiscal 1905–06 lists a Ross, Robert against George Island Light and Fog Bell.[^12]

After Ross, S. B. Ross (1919 to 1920) and J. Ross (1920) are listed briefly. Wallace H. Nolan kept the light from 1920 to 1946.[^1] Earl Edwards and Joseph Bedgood are listed for 1946 with no other detail.[^1]

Victor Matthews kept the light from 1946 to 1964, and David Barkhouse from 1964 until the station was automated in 1972.[^1][^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 29 December 1909 a vessel stranded on Georges Island.[^5] At the formal investigation held at Halifax on 14 February 1910, the master was found not to blame and was exonerated. The mate was censured and fined $10.[^5]

On 23 March 1917 the Sarah Radcliffe, registered at London, stranded on the north side of the island.[^6] The inquiry at Halifax on 1 April 1917 found the master and officers exonerated of all blame. The first and second officers were cautioned to be more careful.[^6]

On 6 December 1917 the Mont-Blanc and the Imo collided in Halifax harbour. The explosion that followed caused great devastation in the harbour and the city, and about fifteen hundred lives were lost.[^7] The sources read do not tie the explosion to the island or its light.

## After the keepers

The island was declared a National Historic Site in 1965.[^1] Commercial power reached the station in 1970, and two years later the light was automated.[^1] A red stripe was painted on the tower in 1973.[^1][^8] NSLPS says an army anti-aircraft battery moved onto the island in 1943.[^8]

In March 2009 the federal government designated $3.5 million for water, sewer and electrical services on the island. In August 2019 it announced $3.6 million for visitor facilities, so that the island could open to visitors in 2020.[^1]

## Today

Georges Island is a National Historic Site, managed by Parks Canada.[^11] Visitors reach it by the ferry from Halifax, or by private boat, canoe or kayak.[^11] When the ferry is cancelled in heavy rain or unsafe conditions, the island is closed.[^11]

The lighthouse is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard, which operates it. The grounds and the tower are closed to visitors.[^1][^8][^9] The Coast Guard's list gives the front light as fixed white, from a white octagonal tower with a red vertical stripe and a red upper portion. The tower is 16.4 m high, and the focal height is 17.7 m.[^10] Rowlett gives the focal plane as 17.5 m.[^9]

The closest views come from Pier 21 on the Halifax waterfront.[^1][^9]
