Prospect Point Light stands under the bluff at Prospect Point in Stanley Park, Vancouver, just west of the southern end of the Lions Gate Bridge.[^2] It is a white tower with a red band at the top, owned by the Canadian Coast Guard.[^1][^3] The sources give three different years for the first light shown there, 1888, 1898 and about 1899, and the present tower dates from 1948.[^2][^3][^10]

## The place

Ships entering Vancouver harbour had to round Prospect Point, and the marker at the point says that ships in the Narrows, where the tide runs hard, depended on the lighthouse's warnings to avoid its rocks.[^5][^10]

LighthouseFriends adds that before the Cleveland Dam was built in 1954, the uncontrolled Capilano River churned tons of silt and rock into Burrard Inlet near First Narrows, shifting where the shallows lay. Ships going through the Narrows kept as close to Prospect Point, opposite the river mouth, as they could.[^2]

On 25 July 1888 the steamship *Beaver* ran aground just off the point. LighthouseFriends gives that date and says her drunken crew caused the grounding.[^2][^10] The marker gives only the year, and says the grounding followed years of shipping mishaps caused by rocks and strong tidal currents.[^10]

## Building the light

On 1 October 1888, just four days after Stanley Park opened, a light was established at Prospect Point.[^2] Its fixed white light shone from a square lantern room on the roof of the keeper's dwelling, with the focal plane 9.4 m (31 ft) above the water. A mechanical fog bell hung under a gable on the seaward side.[^2] The dwelling had two tiny rooms, and water came in and went out of the cellar with the tides. In March 1889 the marine agent inspected the house and ordered six barrels of cement so that the keeper could pour a concrete cellar floor when the tide allowed.[^2]

The later sources put the first light later. The marker says eleven years passed between the grounding and the construction of the original lighthouse, a wooden building with a lantern and fog bell, which would place it around 1899.[^10] Rowlett's directory gives 1898 as the year the station was established.[^3] A keepers' table in the Department's report for the fiscal year ending 31 March 1910 gives "Jno. Grove" an appointment date of 7 July 1898.[^8] The table prints station names and keepers in separate columns, so this story matches that entry to Prospect Point by its row. The date does not say when the light first shone. The sources do not agree, and this story does not choose between them.

For the fiscal year ending 31 March 1911 the light had been changed. The Department's report for that year, printed in 1912, says the light "has been changed from fixed white to an occulting white light", visible six seconds and eclipsed three seconds in turn. Its apparatus was a fifth-order dioptric lens, with petroleum vapour burned under an incandescent mantle.[^4]

The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1906, printed in 1907, records an additional room built onto the dwelling at Prospect Point, with the roof repaired, at a cost of $242.85.[^6] LighthouseFriends dates the extra room to 1904, when the keeper's oldest son was thirteen.[^2] The two dates differ, and this story gives both.

## The keepers

John Grove is the only keeper the sources name at Prospect Point. LighthouseFriends gives him as keeper from 1888 to 1926, and the marker names him as the keeper, without dates.[^2][^10] The Department's keepers' list printed with its 1907 report places "Grove, John" against Prospect Point.[^7] LighthouseFriends says the dwelling became the home of Grove, his wife and their two sons.[^2]

The marker says that Grove lit the lantern at sunset and put it out at sunrise. He also recorded inbound traffic, cleaned the lights and fog signals, and rewound the weights of the fog alarms every half-hour.[^10]

From 1909 he also worked the semaphore masts on a bluff about 46 m (150 ft) above the light, which signalled incoming traffic.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the Department gave him $55 a month to pay assistants, but the lowest wage he could pay a man willing to do the semaphore work was $62 a month, so Grove carried most of the work himself.[^2] When he asked whether his daughter-in-law could work as an assistant, he was told that the regulations called for competent men.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 17 July 1904 the steamship *Princess Victoria*, of 1,943 gross tons and on a voyage from Victoria, struck the rocks about a ship's length east of the lighthouse as she rounded Prospect Point into Vancouver harbour. Her starboard tail shaft was bent and a bracket twisted. She was repaired and back in service by 27 July.[^5]

In dense fog, LighthouseFriends says, the bell at Prospect Point was the only aid mariners had to guide them through the narrow channel into Vancouver's inner harbour.[^2] A light and fog bell on the Capilano shoals across the Narrows followed in 1908.[^2] In 1900 the bell's signal had been changed to one stroke every twenty seconds, and the keeper had to wind its weights every half-hour.[^2][^10]

## After the keepers

On 1 November 1922 the Prospect Point signal station was taken over from the Department and came under the control of the port authorities, according to the Department's report for the fiscal year 1922-23.[^9]

In 1926, LighthouseFriends says, Grove was told that the light would be electrified and run from the signal station, so his work as keeper would end. The marine agent wrote that he was prepared to recommend that Grove be allowed to stay in his dwelling.[^2] The marker says the lighthouse was converted to electricity that year.[^10]

The old lighthouse stood on after 1938, when the Lions Gate Bridge was built to link the bluff with North Vancouver.[^2] The present light was established in 1948. Its tower is a square, tapering structure of reinforced concrete, 8 m (27 ft) high with no lantern, built by J.C. Dill for $7,712.[^2][^3]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light, and the Vancouver Board of Parks and Recreation manages the site as part of Stanley Park.[^3] The Coast Guard list gives the light as isophase red, Iso R 2s, with a nominal range of 10 nautical miles.[^1] Rowlett gives the focal plane as 11.5 m (38 ft) and describes the light as red, one second on and one second off.[^3]

The grounds are open and the tower is closed. Visitors can reach the point on foot along the Stanley Park seawall from the park's parking areas.[^2][^3] The tower is closed to the public and should not be climbed.
