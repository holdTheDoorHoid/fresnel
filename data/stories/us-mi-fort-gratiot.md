At Port Huron, Michigan, where Lake Huron empties into the St. Clair River, a white brick tower rises beside the water. The Port Huron Museums bill it as Michigan's oldest lighthouse, and the first light in the state was built close by.[^1][^3]

## The place

The light takes its name from Fort Gratiot, a military post established in 1814 to guard the juncture of Lake Huron and the St. Clair River and named for General Charles Gratiot.[^3] On 3 March 1823 Congress provided $3,500 for a lighthouse near the fort, in what was then Michigan Territory; a further $5,000 followed in April 1824.[^4] Putnam's 1917 survey of the American service calls the Fort Gratiot light the first to mark the passage through the St. Clair and Detroit Rivers.[^5]

## Building the light

The first tower was contracted to Winslow Lewis, who subcontracted the work to Daniel Warren of Rochester, New York; it was finished in August 1825 at a cost of $5,762.83.[^4] The Friends of the Fort Gratiot Light Station give its dimensions as 32 feet (9.8 m) high, 18 feet across at the base and 9 feet at the top, standing near the base of the present second span of the Blue Water Bridge.[^3] The keeper, George McDougall, complained about it early: a former Detroit lawyer, he found the stairs so steep that he had to go up sideways, and the trap door into the lantern a squeeze.[^4] By the summer of 1828 he reported cracks and a lean to the east. The tower toppled in November 1828, after a September storm had eaten away the shore.[^4][^3]

Congress appropriated $8,000 in March 1829, and Lucius Lyon, later one of Michigan's first senators, won a $4,445 contract for a tower and dwelling, built farther north.[^4][^3] Sources differ on its original height: the Friends' history gives the plan as a 65-foot (20 m) tower, and Lighthouse Friends says it stood 69 feet (21 m).[^3][^4] The Port Huron Museums also give 65 feet originally.[^2] In 1862 the tower was raised to 82 feet (25 m); the join is still visible.[^3]

A fourth-order Fresnel lens ordered from France arrived in January 1857, according to the Friends of the light station; the US Lighthouse Society archive lists the original optic as fifth order, a disagreement this history cannot settle.[^3][^6] In 1867, Lighthouse Friends reports, the fixed lens was exchanged for a revolving third-order lens because of the many lights at nearby railway depots.[^4] An eight-inch steam fog whistle was authorised in March 1871, and a brick duplex for the keepers and their families was funded in 1874.[^4] The Lighthouse Board's reports from 1875 to 1882 called the old tower leaky and asked for a new one; the request for $25,000 was not granted.[^4]

## The keepers

McDougall kept the light from 1825 until his death in 1842.[^7] Lighthouse Friends says he was a bachelor who, troubled by gout and other infirmities, had to hire a helper, and that when an inspecting officer visited in 1838 he found several lantern panes broken, which McDougall blamed on gulls.[^4]

After McDougall the roster turns over quickly, often with a head keeper staying only a year or two.[^7] One family stands out: John Sinclair kept the light from 1869 to 1881, and a John Sinclair Jr. (the archive does not state the relationship) served as first assistant from 1872 to 1881 and then as head keeper for a year.[^7]

Lighthouse Friends calls Frank E. Kimball the longest-serving keeper, and says he had been head keeper of Port Austin Reef Lighthouse from 1883 to 1894 before coming to Fort Gratiot.[^4] The archive dates his tenure as head keeper here to 1894-1929.[^7] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded that he entered the service on 10 April 1882, had been keeper of Fort Gratiot for the past 35 years, and retired on 1 September 1929 at 70, after more than 47 years' service.[^8] Lighthouse Friends adds that the station won the district's efficiency flag in 1918, and the Bulletin's index lists an efficiency flag for the station in its 1918-23 volume.[^4][^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The first recorded disaster is the loss of the original tower in 1828 (above). The sources used here name no wrecks or rescues at the station. The 1913 Great Lakes storm of 8-10 November damaged the Fort Gratiot light station, with other Michigan lights, to the extent of about $4,000 in total, according to the Lighthouse Service.[^10]

In the Light-House Board's 1895 report the 8-inch steam whistle ran some 205 hours and burned about 10 tons of coal, and its blasts had been changed that April to 3 seconds separated by 17 seconds of silence.[^11] In its 1927 notice the Bureau explained that the station's steam whistle sounded for fog on the lake and its air diaphone for fog in the river, and said a new arrangement was meant to lessen the confusion that existed.[^12]

## After the keepers

The light was electrified in March 1927, and in the 1930s a single keeper's dwelling was added (1933) and an electric oscillator replaced the steam whistle (1934).[^4] The Coast Guard maintained the station from the 1930s until it moved into a new station next door in 2004.[^3] Declared excess in 2004, the light was offered under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act; the city of Port Huron declined the deed over restoration costs of about $4 million, and the deed passed to St. Clair County in September 2010.[^4] A restoration followed, and the station reopened on 19 May 2012 after being closed since 2008.[^4]

## Today

The light was entered on the National Register of Historic Places in 1976, and the Lighthouse Society's archive lists it as an active aid to navigation.[^13][^6] St. Clair County Parks owns the station, and Port Huron Museums run its tours at 2802 Omar Street.[^3][^14] For 2026 the museum lists it open Saturdays and Sundays, 10 a.m. to 5 p.m., from 12 September to 20 December. The tower climb, 94 iron steps, is available only on a guided tour and not in severe weather.[^14]
