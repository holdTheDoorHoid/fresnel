The Anse-à-la-Cabane Lighthouse stands on a red sandstone cliff on the south shore of Île du Havre-Aubert, in the Îles de la Madeleine (Magdalen Islands) of the Gulf of St. Lawrence.[^3][^4] Its hexagonal wooden tower was first lit in 1871, and it was deactivated in December 2011.[^4][^5] The Quebec register lists Amherst Lighttower among its other names.[^3]

## The place

The islands are more than eighty kilometres from the nearest land, and LighthouseFriends says more than 200 vessels were wrecked on them between 1855 and 1880.[^4] The Quebec register says that between 1870 and 1874 the Department of Marine and Fisheries built four lights on the islands' coasts to secure the sea corridor of the Gulf, where wrecks were frequent (translation).[^3]

Île du Havre-Aubert, which LighthouseFriends calls Amherst Island in English, is the largest and southernmost island of the group.[^4] The islands' first light, on Rocher aux Oiseaux, the northernmost island, was established in 1870. LighthouseFriends says Amherst Island was a natural choice for the second.[^4]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says Carrol and Sinclair won a $4,600 contract to build "the fifty-four-foot, wooden lighthouse on Amherst Island." E. Chanteloup was paid $1,155 for the lantern room and $772.34 for the lighting apparatus, and the total cost came to $6,828.01.[^4] The Quebec register describes a hexagonal wooden tower with a cast-iron lantern.[^3] The Canadian register describes its hexagonal plan and slim, tapered mass.[^1] Its French text gives the tower as 17.1 m.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends and Rowlett give 16.5 m (54 ft).[^4][^5] The Route des phares page describes the tower as hexagonal, but one sentence of it calls the tower an octagonal example.[^6]

William Cormier was the first keeper. LighthouseFriends says he activated the light for the first time on 30 June 1871, at a salary of $300 a year, and that mariners were to know it by its alternating red and white flashes.[^4] The Department's statement for the year ended 30 June 1901 lists "Cormier, Wm" at Amherst Island, appointed 26 April 1871 at $300 a year.[^7] The register notes that after 1871 this hexagonal form was dropped for a cheaper square tower, and that this light is "probably one of the last of its type in Canada" (translation).[^2]

In 1874 four wire stays were sent to the island to steady the tower.[^4] In 1878 an inspection found two lamps and reflectors in very bad order. Cormier explained that the lamps had caught fire and burned the reflectors.[^4] In 1889 flat-wick lamps replaced the circular-wick ones in the lantern room.[^4]

In 1890 two officials, William P. Anderson of the Marine Department and William H. Smith of the Board of Examiners of Masters and Mates, inspected the islands.[^4] As LighthouseFriends quotes their report, they thought the lights were badly placed. The Amherst Island light stood on a low south point, while a higher cape to the south-west cut into its view.[^4]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends gives three keepers: William Cormier (1871 to 1911), Charles Cormier (1911 to 1950) and Edmond Boudreau (1950 to 1970).[^4] The Quebec register lists the same three as people associated with the light. It also says that, by tradition, William's son Charles took over in 1912, a year later than LighthouseFriends gives.[^3] The sources do not settle the date.

The keeper's house was rebuilt in 1951.[^4] The original house was demolished the following year.[^2][^4] The new one is a square house with a pavilion roof.[^2] The register says the oil shed dates from the time the light was built.[^2]

Boudreau, the last keeper, is credited with two changes that made the work easier.[^4] According to LighthouseFriends, he reportedly cut a hole in the first-floor ceiling so that the weight turning the lens could drop another twelve feet between windings, which gave him an extra hour's sleep. With his electrician brother's help he wired the light so it could be switched on from his bedroom.[^4]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1970, the year Boudreau's keeping ended.[^3][^4] It was deactivated in December 2011.[^4][^5] On 17 January 2006 the municipality of Les Îles-de-la-Madeleine cited the light as a heritage property. The protection covers the outside of the tower, the keeper's house and its outbuildings.[^3] The Quebec register says the keeper's house was sold to a private owner in 1971.[^3]

LighthouseFriends says the light was nominated as a heritage lighthouse in 2012, and that the result, which would allow its transfer from the Department of Fisheries and Oceans to a local organization, was due by June 2015.[^4] Rowlett's directory says that "sometime in 2012-2014" the lighthouse and keeper's house were moved northwest across the road, to escape erosion of the cliff edge.[^5]

In September 2003 the light was opened to the public for three days during Journées de la culture, in an event run by a heritage group and a local nautical centre.[^4] LighthouseFriends says more than 600 visitors climbed the tower, and that the open days were meant to generate interest in the municipality acquiring it.[^4]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light, according to LighthouseFriends. Rowlett's directory gives the Coast Guard as owner and site manager, with the site open and the tower closed.[^4][^5] The Route des phares lists the manager as "privé" (private) on a page that is undated.[^6] The sources disagree on who runs the site now, and the record does not settle it. The Quebec register says the tower is not open to visitors during the summer season.[^3] LighthouseFriends reports that the keeper's house has been sold into private hands, so access to the property is not allowed.[^4]

The Quebec register's description comes from a 2009 to 2010 inventory and calls the light the oldest in the archipelago still in operation. The Route des phares page says the same. LighthouseFriends and Rowlett's directory record its deactivation in December 2011.[^3][^6][^4][^5]
