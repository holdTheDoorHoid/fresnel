Puffin Island Light Station is a staffed light and fog signal on a small island about 400 metres off Greenspond, on the northwest side of Bonavista Bay in Newfoundland and Labrador. Its first light, in a granite tower, was shown on 1 March 1873. The granite tower was replaced around 1950, and Fisheries and Oceans Canada's list of staffed lightstations, on a page modified in 2019, names the station.[^1][^2][^3][^5]

## The place

Greenspond is one of the oldest continuously inhabited outports in Newfoundland. A local history says it was settled in the 1690s and grew into a major trading centre.[^4] Its first settlers were fishermen from Bonavista, and LighthouseFriends notes that for nearly two centuries the town was known as the "Capital of the North."[^1] Its main harbour had a hazardous entrance and room for only a few vessels.[^1]

In 1859 J.T. Oakley and others of Greenspond petitioned the government for a light on the island. In 1866 the House of Assembly asked for an inquiry, and Robert Oke, Inspector of Lighthouses, took up the matter.[^1] Oke reported, as LighthouseFriends quotes him, that the island lay "South of the entrance to Greenspond Harbor" and was "easy of access except in bad weather."[^1] He judged that it would work as a harbour light, not a coastal one, because of outlying islands.[^1] The local history gives the purpose plainly: the government put up the light "To insure the safety of the steamer into Greenspond after nightfall."[^4]

## Building the light

The work did not begin until 1872, two years after John T. Nevill had succeeded Oke as inspector.[^1] The contractors, Smith and Haw, built a granite tower attached to a keeper's dwelling, with stone quarried on the island. Nevill's 1872 report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says that "the granite was quarried on the spot."[^1] The cost was more than $10,000.[^1]

The lens was a fourth-order Chance Brothers Fresnel lens, with a lantern room provided by David and Thomas Stevenson of Edinburgh.[^1] The fixed red light, at a focal plane of 25.9 m (85 ft), was first shown on 1 March 1873.[^1] Robert Oakley, the first keeper, had begun to draw his salary in late 1872.[^1] Repointing the granite was the most frequent job, needed in 1880, 1884, 1887, 1890, 1896 and 1913.[^1]

In 1889 Nevill, as LighthouseFriends quotes him, recommended an outside kitchen, because "the steam from the existing kitchen" was rising into the lantern room and "condensing on the glass of the lantern."[^1]

In 1912 the light was changed from a fixed red to an occulting white light.[^1] A diaphone fog alarm was added in 1914, and an extension to the dwelling followed in 1915.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the granite tower was taken down in 1950 and replaced by a frame dwelling and a square wooden pyramidal tower. The Lighthouse Directory dates the present station to 1951, so the sources differ by a year.[^1][^5]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends lists the keepers. Robert Kelland Oakley served from 1872 to 1874, and Fred William Oakley from 1875 to 1897.[^1] Fred Oakley, son of the first keeper, was in charge in 1889, and he had complained that the granite lighthouse was too small for his family.[^1] The later keepers on the LighthouseFriends list are Nathan Saunders (1898 to 1900), Frederick William Oakley (1901 to 1904), Walter Jennings (1905 to 1908), Joseph Butler (1909 to at least 1912), Edward Wheeler (from at least 1927), and Alex Herron (1941 to 1963). It also names Albert Wakeley, with no dates, and Tom Bragg (1968 to at least 1987).[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the fall of 1887, gales and heavy seas left the station's water supply too salty to use. It was the first time that had happened since the light was established, and fresh water had to be brought by boat.[^1]

In 1927 Edward Wheeler was on duty. LighthouseFriends describes a stormy June evening when he heard two gunshots from a schooner of about thirty tons, some 30 m from him at the landing slip. He rowed out and learned that the schooner had been caught in heavy fog and had already hit rocks four times. He brought her to safe anchorage inside Greenspond harbour before returning to his light. The account says he never learned the schooner's name, or the names of the twenty-eight people aboard whom he had helped save.[^1]

LighthouseFriends also records that a keeper drowned in 2001, shortly after leaving the island by kayak. This story does not repeat his name.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard's list gives the station's name as Puffin Island Light Station and Fog Signal. The Fisheries and Oceans Canada list of staffed lightstations also names Puffin Island.[^2][^3] The Coast Guard refurbished and repainted the lighthouse in 2015, according to the Lighthouse Directory.[^5]

LighthouseFriends says that in 2009 the Canadian Coast Guard announced plans to de-staff the staffed lighthouses in Newfoundland and Labrador and British Columbia. A Senate committee later recommended that the Coast Guard halt that plan and consider staffing case by case.[^1] In April 2012, according to LighthouseFriends (which cites CBC News), a polar bear came near the station, and the two keepers went for shelter.[^1]

The station is a staffed lightstation and the keepers' home and workplace.[^1][^5] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower closed, and that Greenspond is the place to view the light. Rowlett says the site is open and the tower closed, reached only by boat, with a good view from Greenspond.[^1][^5] Do not land on the island uninvited; view the station from Greenspond.
