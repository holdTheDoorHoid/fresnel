Smith Point Light is a white brick house on an iron drum, standing in open water on the west side of Chesapeake Bay about two and a half miles east-southeast of the point that guards the southern side of the Potomac River's mouth.[^1] It is the latest of the lights to mark this shoal, and it was built after ice carried away the screwpile lighthouse that stood here before it.[^2]

## The place

Smith Point, in Northumberland County, Virginia, has been marked since the early nineteenth century. Congress authorised a lighthouse on the point in 1801 and a light was first shown in 1802. Because the first tower stood too close to an eroding bluff, it was rebuilt farther inland in 1807 and moved inland again in 1828, and a lightship was stationed off the point from 1821.[^1] The Virginia Department of Historic Resources counts "no less than five lighthouses and two lightships" at the place over its history; LighthouseFriends counts four lighthouses and several lightships.[^3][^4]

The land tower was discontinued in 1859, and the lightship stationed off the point since 1821 was destroyed by Confederate forces in 1861.[^1] In 1868 a screwpile lighthouse, a house on iron legs screwed into the shoal, was lit offshore.[^1] In the winter of 1892-93 the Lighthouse Board reported that it was "much damaged by ice" and that "the keepers abandoned the station when the danger seemed imminent." The light and fog signal were out from 20 to 31 January 1893.[^5] The Board said the damage would be repaired; in September 1893 one of the cast-iron struts broken by the ice was renewed.[^6] LighthouseFriends adds that the keepers were dismissed for leaving; the Board's report does not say so.[^4]

On 14 February 1895 the ice took the whole structure. The Board reported that the lighthouse "was carried away by ice on February 14, 1895," and that the lens and fog-bell apparatus, some oil and a few small articles were recovered from the wreck.[^7] A New York paper of 18 February, reporting from Baltimore, told how it had gone. The screwpile had broken away the week before and drifted down the bay with the ice, and a steamer's captain had seen it off the Great Wicomico River, heeled over and half submerged. The paper said its keepers had left the day before. The steamer's first mate and crew walked across the ice, broke into the lantern, and lowered the lamp and its fittings to the ice for the ship, and the material was brought to Baltimore.[^8] A light-vessel was stationed in its place.[^9]

## Building the light

Congress acted within three weeks. An act of 2 March 1895 gave $25,000 and allowed contracts up to $80,000 for re-establishing the light. Borings were made and the Board settled on a kind of structure.[^7] The choice was a caisson light: a cast-iron drum filled with concrete, sunk into the shoal, with the keepers' house on top. LighthouseFriends says the massive foundation was chosen to withstand the ice floes.[^4]

The contracting took two rounds. The plans for the metalwork were finished in December 1895 and the contract let. The first bids for putting the structure up were thrown out because the lowest bidder was "a defaulting contractor on other light-house work."[^10] Congress added $41,000 on 11 June 1896.[^10] Bids were opened again on 30 July, and the lowest was accepted.[^11] The work followed the plans of Wolf Trap Light, built three years earlier.[^3][^1]

The base was built in Baltimore. Work on the wooden caisson began at a Baltimore dock in September 1896.[^12] It was launched on 17 November 1896, and by March it had two sections of the iron cylinder on top. The Board noted that no effort was made to hurry, because towing the tall structure down the bay before settled weather would have been hazardous.[^11] It left on 7 April 1897 and, after waiting out the weather at the mouth of the Great Wicomico River, reached the site ten days later. It grounded on the afternoon of 17 April, and about 150 tons of riprap were put around it at once to stop scour. Within a few hours it had settled about 2 feet and leaned 4 degrees from upright.[^11]

Sinking the caisson with compressed air took until 22 May, when it reached 15 feet 5 inches into the shoal. In the last 3 feet of sand the workmen met sulphuretted hydrogen gas, which "seriously affected the eyes of the workmen and caused some delay."[^11] Then the contractor's own steamer, moored alongside, broke eight plates in the sixth course of the cylinder by its motion, and about twenty days went on replacing them.[^11] By 1 July 1897 the substructure was practically complete.[^11]

The finished station is a cylinder 30 feet (9 m) across and 44 feet 9 inches (13.6 m) high, topped by a two-storey octagonal brick dwelling with a square tower rising 30 feet (9 m) above the cylinder.[^12] The lighthouse was accepted on 5 August 1897.[^12][^3] It showed a white flash every 30 seconds from a fourth-order Fresnel lens, with a red sector over the shoals off the point; LighthouseFriends says the lens was the one salvaged from the wrecked screwpile.[^12][^4] The records disagree about the year of the light: the Historic Structures text and the state register give 1897, the US Lighthouse Society's archive gives 1898.[^3][^12][^13] A clockwork fog bell was installed in December 1897.[^12]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the keepers' registers, lists James B. Williams as head keeper from 1897 to 1913, and a stream of first and second assistants after him.[^13] LighthouseFriends also lists him as head keeper of the earlier screwpile from 1889 to 1895, which would mean the man who was at the station when it was lost came back to run the new one.[^4] The sources do not say whether he was there on the night the ice took it.

The archive's roster shows how a career could run inside one station. William A. Gray appears as second assistant from 1926, first assistant from 1931, and head keeper from 1939 to 1942. Robert W. Fulcher appears as second assistant in 1920, first assistant from 1921, and head keeper from 1931 to 1939.[^13] Miles Hudgins, who was second assistant in 1912, was first assistant and then head keeper in 1913.[^13] Other long runs were Horace C. Groom (1920-1931) and, according to LighthouseFriends, Nelson S. Atherton, who held the head's post from 1946 until 1965; the archive's list shows no head keeper between 1946 and 1965.[^13][^4]

By the 1930s the station had three boats, including a 22-foot motor boat, and radio equipment.[^12][^4] LighthouseFriends says the nearest community was Sunnybank, on the Little Wicomico River, about four and a half miles away.[^4] The Bulletin for 1938 reports that Lewis R. Carman, then second assistant keeper at Smith Point, had built a scale model of Holland Island Bar Lighthouse and presented it to the Bureau of Lighthouses in Washington.[^14]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A Daboll trumpet, an air-driven horn, replaced the fog bell on 10 February 1900, and in the months to June it ran about 63 hours.[^15][^16] The trumpet was damaged in the winter of 1901: the Board records it disabled, a bell struck by machinery on 26 January, and the trumpet sounding again and the bell dropped on 4 February.[^17] The Historic Structures text instead has the old trumpet "blown away" on 5 February 1901 and a replacement fitted in April; the sources do not agree on what happened to it.[^12] A diaphone, a type of air-powered fog signal, was fitted in 1936, according to the Historic Structures text. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1924 already lists "Smiths POint" among stations whose fog signal was changed "from reed horn to an air diaphone." The sources do not reconcile the two dates.[^12][^18]

The keepers answered calls from boats near the station. The Bulletin for 1913 commended first assistant W. A. Crockett and second assistant Miles Hudgins for rescuing two men in danger of drowning.[^19] On 31 August 1914 Hudgins, by then head keeper, and assistant James M. Ellis helped two men in a disabled motor boat.[^20] The Bulletin for 1921 reports that Groom towed a disabled motor boat picked up near the station on 27 August to a machine shop.[^21]

In 1914 the Service noted that the bottom around the Smith Point, Wolf Trap and Newport News lights was scouring out in a saucer shape, and proposed four radial groynes of riprap stone to hold the ground around the tower.[^22]

## After the keepers

The Bulletin for 1920 reports automatic wireless sending outfits set up at Wolf Trap, Windmill Point and Smith Point in Chesapeake Bay.[^23] The station remained manned until 1971, when a roughly three-mile submarine power cable was laid to the station and Coast Guard personnel were taken off.[^12][^4] A small emergency lamp, powered by batteries, was to take over if the cable failed.[^4]

In the 1980s the cable was damaged, and the Coast Guard considered closing the light rather than replacing it. LighthouseFriends reports a quick public outcry, and the cable was replaced in 1988.[^4] A Coast Guard crew did a major overhaul in 1991: new mortar, paint inside and out, new windows, and a sealed roof.[^12][^4] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 2 December 2002 and on the Virginia Landmarks Register in 2003.[^3]

## Today

The station is private and reachable only by boat, and the US Lighthouse Society's archive lists it as not open to the public.[^13][^12] In 2005 the General Services Administration offered it for sale at online auction after no public body took it under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, and a private bidder won it for $170,000.[^4] A real-estate listing says he worked on it from 2006 to 2009, adding a kitchen and new windows and roof.[^24] The Coast Guard keeps the light as an aid to navigation and has a right of access to maintain it; the light is now solar-powered, and the owner is responsible for the historic parts of the building.[^25] It was put on the market in April 2024 at $450,000, and the listing was still reported active in 2026.[^24][^25] Who owns it today is not confirmed by these sources. The light can be seen from the water, but the structure is not open to visitors.
