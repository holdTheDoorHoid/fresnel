The Burlington Canal Main Lighthouse is a white limestone tower, 16.7 m (55 ft) tall, on the south side of the cut that joins Lake Ontario to Hamilton Harbour.[^1][^3] It is a relative of the Imperial towers of Lake Huron and Georgian Bay, and it now stands beside the lift bridge and the Skyway, long after its light went out.[^2][^3]

## The place

A low sandy bar known as the Beach separates Burlington Bay (renamed Hamilton Harbour in 1919) from the western end of Lake Ontario.[^3][^4] A canal through it was proposed in 1823, and the lighthouse helped guide vessels into the bay, which grew into a major industrial port.[^3][^4][^2] The sources differ on the digging. One has work beginning in 1826 and the canal opening in 1832; another has the Lieutenant Governor opening the waterway on 1 July 1826, with the work finished and lit only in the 1832 season.[^4][^3]

## Building the light

The first lighthouse was a wooden octagonal tower, 54 feet (16 m) tall, contracted to John L. Williams in 1837, with part of the canal tolls used to pay for it.[^3] Parks Canada also dates the first light to 1837; a Hamilton Public Library history and the lighthouse group's own site say 1838.[^2][^5][^4] A wooden front range light followed on the pier in 1845.[^3]

The wood was a hazard. Steamers rubbed the wooden piers as they passed, and sparks from their funnels set the piers alight; George Thompson, the ferryman who was made keeper in 1861, had to douse the fires more than once.[^3] On 18 July 1856 the steamship *Ranger* set a pier on fire. Thompson's diary records: "Large lighthouse caught fire by a spark from the steamer Ranger. Burnt to the ground with ferry house, lightkeeper’s house and my own dwelling house."[^3] Until a new house was built he lived in a shanty and carried on.[^3]

The replacement was meant to resist fire. A red brick keeper's dwelling went up in 1857, and John Brown, who had built the Imperial towers, began the limestone tower's foundation that year, at a cost of $10,479.98.[^3][^4] Its date is given variously: Parks Canada says built in 1857, while the library history calls it the permanent lighthouse of 1858.[^2][^5] Its height is 60 feet in Lighthouse Friends and 55 feet in the lighthouse group's account and Parks Canada's.[^3][^4][^1] Brown used coal oil rather than whale oil, said to be a first in Canada, and the whalers were angry.[^3][^4]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists M. Homer from 1832, William Nicholson from 1837, John Davidson from at least 1849 and George Thomson from 1861; the lighthouse group, by contrast, calls Thompson the first light-keeper, so the early order is uncertain.[^3][^4] Thompson left his own account of winters with the new oil: "I had much trouble in warming the coal oil in the pier and lighthouse."[^5] He wrapped the lamps in flannel and rope yarn.[^5] Poor health forced his retirement in 1875, and he died in 1879.[^3]

Captain Thomas Campbell took over at $300 a year and stayed until 1905.[^3] An 1876 description gives the light as six lamps with six 20-inch reflectors on two iron frames, one lamp facing Hamilton and five facing out.[^3] One October night in 1900, as Campbell waited in fog at the pier for the steamer *Macassa*, a duck broke through a pane of the pierhead lantern and knocked over the lamp. Campbell put out the small fire, and the newspaper account said he meant to have the duck stuffed.[^3]

Thomas Lundy followed in 1905, A. W. Cripps in 1924 and Carl J. Van Cleaf in 1941, with more names in the 1960s.[^3] Pete Coletti was keeper when the stone tower was decommissioned in 1961 by Lighthouse Friends' account, though its roster dates his term 1968 to 1987; the lighthouse group says he lived in the cottage 23 years.[^3][^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A storm in early December 1907 heavily damaged the piers at the canal entrance, and temporary pole lights were set on the north pier while the south pier was rebuilt.[^3] The library history adds that a powerful storm in 1958 let water into the tower, after which timber planks bedded in concrete strengthened its foundation.[^5]

## After the keepers

The main light got a new lantern and apparatus in 1891.[^3] The Department of Marine reported that a fourth-order dioptric apparatus burning petroleum vapour under a mantle had replaced the old reflectors by 1911-12.[^6] The lighthouse group, however, describes a third-order Fresnel lens, now at the Joseph Brant Museum, and the sources do not resolve the difference.[^4]

The keeper's cottage was moved to make room for a swing bridge, in 1896 by one account and about 1897 by another.[^3][^4] The stone tower was deactivated in 1961 and replaced by a light on the railway bridge, though the library history says it ceased operating in 1968.[^3][^5] Automation came in 1987 by one account and 1990 by another, and the cottage has been vacant since.[^3][^4]

## Today

Residents began a preservation effort in 2003 and became the Beach Canal Lighthouse Group.[^3] The tower was designated a heritage lighthouse in December 2021, with ownership to pass to the Hamilton Oshawa Port Authority and a plan to move it about 100 metres for public access.[^3][^1] The cottage is boarded up.[^4] Lighthouse Friends reports the grounds open and the tower closed; treat the tower as closed to visitors.[^3]
