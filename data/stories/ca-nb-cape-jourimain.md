The Cape Jourimain Lighthouse is an eight-sided wooden tower, 15.5 m (51 ft) tall, on the northern tip of Jourimain Island in Westmorland County, New Brunswick, beside the narrowest part of Northumberland Strait. It was lit in 1870, was kept by one family for most of its working life, and now stands in the grounds of the Cape Jourimain Nature Centre beside the Confederation Bridge that ended its service.[^1][^3]

## The place

Cape Jourimain is the northern end of the Jourimain Islands, a few kilometres northwest of Cape Tormentine. The islands are joined to each other and to the mainland by sand bars, and mariners counted them a hazard because they lie where the strait between New Brunswick and Prince Edward Island is narrowest.[^1] Parks Canada's statement of significance adds that the surrounding waters are shallow and hold rocky shoals, and that as shipwrecks mounted the government answered with a series of lights along the strait.[^2]

In August 1868 John Page, Chief Engineer of Public Works, looked at Indian Point, Cape Tormentine and Cape Jourimain and chose Cape Jourimain. LighthouseFriends, drawing on the Department of Marine's reports, quotes his reasoning that it was the extreme point of land for vessels passing in either direction.[^1]

## Building the light

Page recommended a tower about 12 m (40 ft) high and a separate keeper's dwelling. His estimate was $8,500 for the pair in stone or $3,500 in wood; Parliament voted $3,500, and the work went to the Department of Public Works in New Brunswick for a contract price of $2,974.[^1] The buildings were finished in 1869, but the lighting apparatus had not arrived by the end of that year, and Lewis Wells was paid $20 to watch the empty buildings until the keeper was appointed.[^1]

John Bent became the first keeper on 1 April 1870 at $200 a year. On 15 May 1870 he showed a temporary light of three lamps, and the intended apparatus, three lamps in 23-inch reflectors, went into service on 7 June.[^1] The National Trust for Canada gives the year of building as 1870, while the nature centre says 1869; the account drawn from the Department's reports puts completion of the structures in 1869 and the first light in 1870.[^1][^3][^4]

The original lantern, only four and a half feet across, was judged too cramped for the keeper to pass around the lamps and trim them, and a new lantern room was fitted in 1876 with seven reflector lamps. The heat of seven lamps cracked panes of glass, so the number was cut to five.[^1] On 15 June 1878 the fixed white light became a flashing white one, turned by a revolving apparatus of four lamps that flashed every ten seconds, and the keeper's salary rose from $250 to $300 for the added work.[^1] In 1895 Milton Walsh, the Marine Department's foreman on Prince Edward Island, replaced the tower's old sills and lower floor, rebuilt the foundations from the old stone, and anchored each of the octagonal posts with iron.[^1] The tower was moved to a new site in 1910 by day labour at a cost of $400.54, and a fourth-order dioptric lens was installed in 1914.[^1]

## The keepers

John Bent drowned on 14 June 1875, with three others, returning in the station's sailboat from Summerside, Prince Edward Island. Silas Ross, who had a family of four, filled in until Arthur W. Bent was hired. The station's boat was recovered, but repairing it cost too much, so the Department sent the boat formerly used at the Quaco light, with a marine telescope and flag.[^1]

Arthur W. Bent kept the light until 1901, and A. J. Percy Bent followed him. The Department of Marine's staff table of light-keepers for the fiscal year ended June 1905 lists "Bent, A. J. Percy" at Jourimain, appointed 25 January 1901, and the next report's table repeats the entry.[^1][^6] LighthouseFriends lists Arthur Welsley Bent from 1938 and Merrill William Trenholm from 1949, with Trenholm recorded at least to 1966.[^1] The nature centre says John Bent was the first of four generations of Bents to serve and that the family may be the longest-serving lightkeeping family in Canada, a belief it states cautiously.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Department of Marine's report for the year ended 30 June 1900 records awards of $10 to each of the fishermen who rescued the shipwrecked crew of the schooner *Lady Speedwell* of Halifax at Jourimain Island, Cape Tormentine, on 10 May 1899.[^5] The report does not say whether the keeper took part.

## After the keepers

It was automated in 1970 and decommissioned in 1997 with the opening of the Confederation Bridge.[^1] The nature centre dates the closing to the end of the Cape Tormentine ferry service as well.[^3]

It was recognised as a Federal Heritage Building in 1991.[^1] In 2010 the Coast Guard was still responsible for it, shoreline erosion threatened it, and the nature centre's director said the centre could not afford to maintain it; that May the Department of Fisheries and Oceans also declared nearly all its lighthouses, this one included, surplus.[^1][^4] In 2015 it was recognised as a heritage lighthouse under the Lighthouse Heritage Protection Act and ownership passed to the Cape Jourimain Nature Centre. In summer 2016 Mervil Rushton Building Movers moved it back from the eroding shore.[^1] The nature centre counts three moves in all since 1869 and describes a renovation that replaced the shingles, windows and frames and added a deck.[^3] It opened to the public in July 2019.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse belongs to the Cape Jourimain Nature Centre. The centre's website says a shuttle runs from the interpretation centre to the lighthouse and that the tower was closed until June 2027, although LighthouseFriends describes it as open in season; admission to the centre's trails is charged, and the Lighthouse Trail loop passes the tower.[^1][^3] Check the centre's current hours before visiting.
