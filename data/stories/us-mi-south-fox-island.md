South Fox Island Light stands near the southern tip of South Fox Island, one of the two Fox Islands in northern Lake Michigan, between Beaver Island and the Manitou Islands.[^1][^2][^4] Congress paid for a light there in 1867.[^3][^4] Keepers tended it for ninety-one years, until it was automated in 1958.[^2][^4] The brick tower of 1867 still stands on the site, beside a skeletal tower that was taken down on Sapelo Island, Georgia, and rebuilt on South Fox in 1934.[^4][^5]

## The place

South Fox Island lies in Leelanau County, Michigan.[^1] Vessels sailing north from Chicago to the Straits of Mackinac could cross Lake Michigan between South Fox and North Manitou islands.[^2] LighthouseFriends says a light there would let them make the crossing at night and would mark the sheltered anchorage in the island's lee.[^2]

The hazard was shoals in the passage between the islands.[^2][^6] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 says vessels in the passage kept "near the South Fox Island light or off the north end of Manitou" because of "the outlying shoals south of the Fox."[^6] The same report recommended a steam fog signal for the station.[^6] LighthouseFriends quotes the Board's report for 1866 as saying that a light with a fog signal, at the island's southeast end, had long been needed.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $18,000 for the lighthouse on 2 March 1867.[^3][^4] The tower went up in the summer and fall of 1867, built of Milwaukee "Cream City" brick as a square tower at one end of a one-and-a-half-story keeper's dwelling.[^2] The sources give its height two ways. FILA's history says 45 feet (13.7 m) from the base to the top of the ventilator.[^4] The U.S. Lighthouse Society record gives 39 feet (11.9 m) from the base to the centre of the lantern.[^1]

The lantern held a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^2][^3] Keeper Henry J. Roe first lit it on 1 November 1867.[^4] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 lists the light as "Fourth order, fixed red," varied by "a red flash every two minutes."[^10] In 1916 the illuminant was changed from oil to incandescent oil vapour.[^15][^4]

The fog signal came twenty-eight years after the light. The Board recommended it in its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891, at a cost not exceeding $5,500.[^6] Its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1893 gave the authorised cost as "not to exceed $5,600", and said no appropriation had yet been made.[^7] The reports give the cost differently, and they do not explain the difference.[^6][^7] Congress appropriated $5,500 on 2 March 1895.[^8] A 10-inch steam fog signal went into operation on 10 December 1895, on duplicate marine boilers with Crosby automatic signals.[^9]

Other works followed. A brick oil house held kerosene, which replaced lard oil in the lamp.[^4] In 1880 Keeper Willis Warner built a board fence 320 feet (98 m) long against drifting sand and snow.[^4][^3] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 records materials delivered for some 600 feet (183 m) of walkway, and says "The work was done by the keeper."[^19] Terry Pepper's page dates the walkways to 1892.[^3] A five-room dwelling for two keepers went up in 1898, and a larger red brick dwelling in 1910.[^4][^2] The light was electrified in 1929, and the steam fog signal was replaced by a Tyfon air signal.[^4]

The skeletal tower came later. It was built in 1905 on Sapelo Island, Georgia. FILA's history calls it cast iron, and its station page calls it steel.[^4][^5] It was taken down in 1933 and shipped to South Fox Island.[^4] In 1934 workers from Northport reassembled it on the southern tip of the island, beside the fog building.[^4][^3][^2]

## The keepers

Henry J. Roe kept the light from 1867 to 1871.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster then lists William Bruin (1871 to 1876), Willis S. Warner (1876 to 1882), William T. Lewis (1882 to 1885) and Joseph Fountain (1885 to 1891).[^1]

Lewis G. Bourissau was an assistant from 1885 and principal keeper from 1891 to 1915, the longest principal term on the roster.[^1] Terry Pepper spells his name Bourisseau, and the Bulletin prints Lewis Bourissau.[^3][^14]

James McCormick was principal keeper from 1915 to 1923 on the roster. LighthouseFriends carries him to 1924.[^1][^2] He is named in most of the rescue accounts below.[^2][^13][^16][^17]

William F. Green was a first assistant, and from 1924 to 1934 the principal keeper, according to the roster. LighthouseFriends runs his principal term to 1940.[^1][^2] The keepers named in the Bulletin's 1914 account were Bourissau, Green, Oscar E. Dame and Frank L. Moore.[^14] In June 1916 they were McCormick, Green, Dame and Ray H. Buttars.[^16][^20]

The Bulletin's October 1922 account is at odds with the roster. It names Emil C. Johnson as first assistant and Dame as second. The roster has Dame as first assistant from 1921 to 1928 and does not list Johnson at all.[^1][^17]

The U.S. Lighthouse Society roster ends in 1934.[^1] LighthouseFriends names Frederick W. Leslie as principal keeper from 1940 to 1946, and Allen P. Cain from 1948 to 1958. FILA calls Cain the last official keeper, and he left the island when the light was automated.[^2][^4] LighthouseFriends, citing a 2002 newspaper article about the logbook, quotes an entry for the opening day of the 1953 season, 27 March: "Arrived at Station by Charlevoix Picket Boat at 12 noon, put lights and water in operation. NW wind."[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 the fog signal ran 581 hours and burned about 42 cords of wood and 43 tons of coal.[^11] In the year ended 30 June 1900 it ran 277 hours on about five tons of coal and 41 cords of wood.[^12]

On 2 December 1910 a Calumet paper printed a list of Lake losses that included the steamer Vega, "stranded on South Fox island, loss $119,000." The item does not say where on the island she grounded, or whether the light had any part in it.[^18]

On 8 July 1914 the Bulletin reports that four keepers of the station "towed a disabled launch with five persons on board to wharf."[^14] In June 1916 the Bulletin says McCormick, Green, Dame and Buttars helped transport a sick woman to medical attention.[^16] LighthouseFriends says that same year McCormick and an assistant pulled a disabled boat with three men off the island and towed it to a fishing tug.[^2]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report, in the bound volume covering the fiscal years 1915 to 1922, credits McCormick, L. A. Vannatter, Dame and N. A. Nelson under its section on saving life and property. They towed a fishing tug from the island to Northport, hauled the wrecked fishing tug Emma S. Olson to shore, and helped keep the launch Aida from going ashore in a storm.[^13][^2]

On 24 October 1922 the launch of the lighthouse tender Hyacinth, under First Officer George K. Brown, was towing motor boat No. 63 to the station when it "was caught in the breakers and wrecked." LighthouseFriends says the first officer drowned.[^17][^2] The Bulletin says the station's keepers "made heroic effort to reach those in distress."[^17]

## After the keepers

The fog signal was discontinued in 1954. The light was automated in 1958, the last crew left in 1959, and the fourth-order lens from the lantern went to Old Presque Isle Light on Lake Huron.[^4][^2]

The automatic light was shut down in 1968. FILA's history adds: "Electronic navigation has rendered it obsolete."[^4] FILA's station page says the station was decommissioned in 1969.[^5]

The southern 115 acres passed to the state in 1971.[^4] FILA's history says the U.S. Department of the Interior transferred them to the Michigan Department of Natural Resources. FILA's station page says the State of Michigan bought the station from Interior.[^4][^5] In 1984 the DNR engaged the Traverse Bay Area Vocational Center to clean up and secure the station, and members of the Michigan Youth Corps did the work.[^4]

FILA's history dates the Fox Island Education Association to 2000, and the Fox Island Lighthouse Association to 2004.[^4] LighthouseFriends dates the education association to 2002, and says the group took its present name when it applied for non-profit status in 2005.[^2] LighthouseFriends says it received $16,666 from a state lighthouse assistance program in 2009.[^2] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 26 August 2021.[^5]

## Today

The 115 acres at the south end are state land.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the Michigan Department of Natural Resources owns the lighthouses.[^2] Terry Pepper's page, last modified in 2003, said about two-thirds of the island belonged to a private developer, and that the island could only be reached by private vessels suitable for safe offshore cruising.[^3]

LighthouseFriends says the lights can only be seen by boat or from the air, and that the grounds are open while the dwellings and towers are closed.[^2] FILA's station page gives the height of the skeletal tower as 104 feet (31.7 m).[^5]

LighthouseFriends says the association began a Camper Keeper Program in 2015. It lets people camp on the island for several weeks while helping with maintenance and restoration.[^2] Check with the association before planning a visit.
