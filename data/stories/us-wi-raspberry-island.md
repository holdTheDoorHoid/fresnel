Raspberry Island Light stands on a clay bluff at the southwest end of an island in the Apostle Islands of Lake Superior, off the Bayfield peninsula in Wisconsin.[^1][^3] It was built to guide vessels through the west channel into the harbours of Bayfield and La Pointe,[^2] and the National Park Service now calls it the "Showplace of the Apostle Islands."[^1]

## The place

The Apostle Islands had two approaches. The Michigan Island light of 1857 marked both channels into La Pointe and Bayfield for boats coming from the east, while the Raspberry Island light was built to guide vessels through the "west channel."[^2] According to the National Park Service, the light was built at the urging of Henry Rice, the St. Paul politician who founded Bayfield, and in 1859 President Buchanan signed an order reserving the whole island for lighthouse use.[^1] Congress appropriated $6,000 for the light on 3 March 1859, a LighthouseFriends history reports.[^3] The same history says the island took its name from nearby Raspberry Bay, where the Raspberry River empties.[^3]

Not everyone thought the light did its job. When a light on Sand Island was later proposed, the National Register nomination says, mariners claimed that the Raspberry Island light was not clearly visible to vessels approaching the islands from the west.[^2]

## Building the light

Construction began about two years after the island was reserved, and the building was nearly ready by the end of 1862. What held up the opening was the lens, made in France, which took months to reach the lake. The Park Service says it was installed in mid-July 1863, when the station officially began operation.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives 20 July 1863 as the first night the light was shown, with a fifth-order fixed Fresnel lens.[^3] The US Lighthouse Society archive dates the station and its lens to 1862,[^4] so the records differ by a year; the difference seems to be between the finished building and the lit lamp, though no source says so in as many words.

The first building was a boxy two-bedroom house with a shed on one side for the kitchen. A short tower carrying the lantern rose from the middle of the roof.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that by 1867 the fixed white light was being varied by a white flash every ninety seconds, made by three flash panels on a revolving iron frame; the keeper had to wind up the weights that drove it every four hours. The same history says lard oil gave way to kerosene in 1880 and that a brick oil house for the more volatile fuel was not built until 1901.[^3] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 records another change at the opening of navigation, from a flash every 90 seconds to one every minute.[^5]

Shipping on Lake Superior grew, and pilots asked for a fog signal. The district inspector and engineer wrote in January 1902 that traffic through the passage between the island and the Wisconsin mainland was "very considerable" and increasing, and in 1902 a tramway went in to haul materials up the forty-foot bluff.[^3] The tender *Amaranth* brought the boilers and workmen on 4 June 1903.[^3] Keeper Charles Hendrickson wrote in the station log that the steam whistle, once timed, would sound in thick weather with blasts of three seconds "separated by silent intervals of 17 seconds."[^3] The new machinery needed more people, and a second assistant keeper was authorised.[^1]

The little dwelling could not hold three keepers' families. In 1906 it was rebuilt into a double house, at a cost the Park Service gives as $7,500, with the head keeper in one half and the two assistants in the other.[^6][^3] That building is the one standing today.[^6] A diaphone, driven by compressed air from an oil engine, replaced the steam whistle in 1932, and the light was electrified in 1941.[^3]

## The keepers

The first keeper, Andrew Cramer, held the post only in 1863.[^4] LighthouseFriends reports that the local superintendent recommended his removal that October because he had hired another man to run the light while he himself stayed on Madeline Island. William Herbert took charge a few weeks later.[^3] The US Lighthouse Society roster lists seventeen principal keepers between 1863 and 1947.[^4]

The Park Service notes that a married keeper could rely on his wife for help, and here two women were paid for it. It writes that Annie Larsen, wife of keeper Louis Larsen, was appointed assistant keeper and paid for her work during their eleven years on the island, and that Mary Snow, wife of Seth Snow, had a similar arrangement.[^1] The archive's roster spells them Lewis Larson and Annie M. Larson (1869-1880) and Mary E. Snow (1880-1882).[^4]

Francis Jacker took over in 1885 and left his large family at their farm.[^4][^1] His log, quoted by the Park Service, said that watching the revolving machinery "during the long nights of the fall when frequent windings are required, is exhausting," and that in an emergency "no assistance is available on the island."[^1] The Park Service says he hired his son Edward as assistant keeper,[^1] and in December 1887 a newspaper reported that the Secretary of the Treasury had appointed Edward Jacker assistant keeper at the station.[^7]

In 1913 Keeper Alexander McLean and his two assistants carried twenty sacks of coal to Bayfield in the station's boat for his home there, having said it was bought from the Service. An investigation found otherwise and he was demoted. Officials were more lenient with him than with another keeper who lost his post over a similar act, because of his twenty years of service and clean record; he chose to take the open post of second assistant so that he could keep his garden, at a lower salary.[^3] His wife Cecelia told a Detroit newspaper in 1931, "I hate lighthouses," and said that families had to leave the islands by 15 October and so kept two homes.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In September 1887 Keeper Jacker's light went out. A steamer's master, J. C. Thompson, complained to the Treasury Secretary that it was dark on the night of 13 September, and Jacker had a reason. LighthouseFriends says a westerly gale rose that morning, that he took the station sailboat toward a safer anchorage, was blown past the end of the island and drifted to Oak Island, where the boat was heavily damaged, and that on the 16th a passing Native American rescued him after nearly three days without food or fire; his family, arriving on the 14th, had kept the light burning for two nights.[^3] The Park Service tells it differently: it has his wife arrive, find him missing and organise a search, and says the near disaster persuaded his superiors that the station needed an assistant.[^1] LighthouseFriends adds that the post had been abolished at Raspberry Island in 1882 and was now reinstated.[^3]

Crossing to the mainland at the season's end was dangerous too. On 3 December 1896 the Evening Star of Washington carried a dispatch from Bayfield: keepers John Eddy and John D. McMartin had started from the island for the mainland on Saturday in a sailboat and were driven aground by wind and ice floes. After four days on the lake and on uninhabited islands they reached the shore. Eddy had frozen both hands and feet, McMartin his ears, and they had lived on flour and water baked in a wash basin in a fish shanty.[^8]

The station also helped others. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded an event of 28 June 1929 in which Herbert Winfield, whom the Bulletin calls the keeper (the archive roster lists him as first assistant, 1923 to 1930), stood by a disabled Coast Guard motor boat with the station boat and took a lifeboat in tow for several miles while repairs were made on the motor; the motor boat had been trying to help the wrecked steamer *Ralph Budd*.[^9] The 1932 volume of the Bulletin reports that when the York Island Shoal lighted buoy went missing on 27 May, the Raspberry Island keeper searched the islands with his launch in vain, and two men who flew over in an airplane saw it submerged beneath the clear water.[^10]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard had run the station since 1939 and discouraged families from living there during the navigation season. In 1947 the light and fog signal were fully automated and the keepers left. The last head keeper was Earl Seseman, who closed the station that October with a Coast Guardsman.[^11][^4][^3] In 1957 the Coast Guard took the light out of the tower and put a free-standing automatic beacon at the edge of the bluff.[^11] The fifth-order lens was kept in storage for a couple of years, then given to the founders of the Madeline Island Museum, where it has been on display since about 1960.[^3]

The empty buildings were leased to Ellerbee Architects, a Minneapolis firm, as a retreat; the cultural landscape inventory dates the lease 1958 to 1975.[^11] Apostle Islands National Lakeshore was created in 1970, and the Park Service ended the lease in 1975. In 1982 the park historian rebuilt the keepers' gardens from enlarged photographs.[^3]

The Lake Superior shore took its own toll. A $1.4 million shoreline project in 2002 and 2003 gave the bluff rock walls, better drainage and a replanted slope to keep the fog signal building and lighthouse from sliding into the lake.[^12] A $1.3 million restoration of the lighthouse followed, dated 2005 to 2007 by the Park Service and 2005 to 2006, with the station reopening in 2007, by LighthouseFriends: new roof, foundation repairs, restored windows and repaired walls. The south half was returned to its early-1920s appearance, in the time of Keeper Lee Benton, and the north half is used for park housing.[^12][^3]

## Today

The station belongs to the National Park Service and is part of Apostle Islands National Lakeshore. It can be reached only by water. In summer, rangers give tours of the tower,[^1] and a small dock below the lighthouse is open to visiting boaters on a first-come basis.[^12] Check the park for current tour times and boat service before planning a trip. The original lens is on display at the Madeline Island Museum.[^3]

Sources disagree on whether the station is still a lit aid. A 1999 Park Service inventory says the Coast Guard continues to maintain an automated beacon on the bluff,[^11] while the US Lighthouse Society archive lists it as no longer an active aid.[^4]
