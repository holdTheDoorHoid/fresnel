Robbins Reef Light is a brown-and-white cast-iron tower standing on a granite pier in Upper New York Bay, about two miles west of Bayonne, New Jersey, where it marks a submerged ledge beside the main ship channel.[^1] It is also called Kate's Light, after Katherine Walker, its best-known keeper, who held the post from 1894 until 1919.[^1]

## The place

The reef is a dangerous, submerged ledge on the western side of the bay's main north-south channel, a short way northeast of the mouth of the Kill Van Kull, the waterway between Bayonne and Staten Island.[^1] The National Register nomination says several ships were wrecked on it before a light was built in 1839, and others afterward.[^1] A newspaper feature of 1903 added that the Staten Island ferry ran close to the reef and that the channel used by ocean liners lay only half a mile to the east.[^5]

The same feature put the nearest land, the Staten Island shore, about two and a half miles away.[^5] The nomination says the light stands about two miles west of Bayonne, and Kate Walker herself later spoke of rowing home from "the docks, a mile away".[^1][^4] The sources give different distances.

## Building the light

Congress authorised the light and funded it with $50,000 in 1837.[^1] The nomination dates the station to 1839, as does the Lighthouse Society's archive; a National Park Service lesson and the current owners' website say it was built in 1838.[^1][^2][^12][^13] The work began with a granite pier, cut and laid with interlocking blocks and fitted to the ledge. The nomination describes it as octagonal and 48 feet (14.6 m) across, though its own description section gives about 45 feet (13.7 m) for the pier today.[^1] On top went a four-story octagonal granite tower that held the keeper's quarters.[^1] The nomination calls the pier the oldest surviving example of its design in the United States.[^1] Its lantern held nine Argand lamps with 21-inch reflectors, and a fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced them in 1855.[^1]

By 1875 the Lighthouse Board's depot at Tompkinsville on Staten Island was using Robbins Reef as a place for lighting experiments, and trials there of mineral oil and kerosene in place of colza and lard oil helped persuade the Board in 1878 to begin changing lamps over to mineral oil.[^1] In 1880, when business interests sought a grant of part of the reef for manufacturing, the Board found that it held no title to the land under the light; it applied for and obtained a circle of submerged land 700 feet (213 m) across.[^1]

By 1883 the granite tower had become decrepit, in the nomination's word. It was torn down that year and a local Notice to Mariners of 8 March 1883 said Lightship No. 25 would mark the reef until another tower could be built.[^1] The replacement was a prefabricated "sparkplug" tower of cast iron. The nomination explains that towers of this design were cast in parts, fitted together on shore, numbered, taken apart and shipped, so that once a foundation stood, a tower could go up in a few days.[^1] The new light was first shown on 10 July 1883 from a fourth-order lens, flashing white every 6 seconds and visible 13.25 miles.[^1] It stood on the old pier. No assistant was assigned.[^1]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the National Archives' keeper registers, lists Isaac Johnson as the first principal keeper, from 1839 to 1841.[^2] It has 32 principal-keeper entries up to 1967, among them Charles G. Clarke (1853 to 1861 and again 1862 to 1864) and C. A. Blydenburgh, the first keeper of the iron tower, who took the post in 1883 at $600 a year and resigned at the end of 1885.[^2][^1]

His successor was John Walker, born in Sweden, who had been an assistant keeper at Sandy Hook.[^1] There he met Katherine, a German immigrant with a child, and she came with him to the reef, although Lighthouse Board rules barred women and children from offshore stations. The nomination says the reef's nearness to land let the Board make an exception.[^1] The National Park Service lesson gives 1885 for her arrival and quotes her recollection of it: "the sight of water, which ever way I looked, made me lonesome. I refused to unpack my trunks at first".[^12] Two newspaper features, of 1903 and 1918, call her husband Jacob rather than John; the Lighthouse Society's archive and the nomination say John.[^3][^4][^5][^1]

John Walker died of pneumonia in 1890.[^1] In 1918 Kate told a Sun reporter that he was taken to the Smith Infirmary on Staten Island and that she "could not leave the light to be with him"; the news came by boat at night, while she was tending the lamp.[^3][^4] According to the nomination, several men were offered the post and none took it, so she applied, over objections from various parties. She was made acting keeper in June 1894 and keeper a year later.[^1] The Lighthouse Society's archive lists her as principal keeper from 1890 to 1919, while the nomination dates her appointment to 1894 and 1895.[^2][^1] The Park Service lesson says Kate was in effect the principal keeper throughout the interval.[^12] Her son Jacob Walker became assistant keeper in 1896 at $450.[^1]

She was under five feet tall: the 1918 feature gave her height as four feet ten.[^3] The house she kept was the tower itself. A 1903 account describes round rooms, one to a floor, growing smaller as the walls tapered upward. The first floor served as kitchen, dining room, parlor and living room together; the walls were painted white and the floors scrubbed; scarlet geraniums stood in the deep window ledges, and washing hung on the balcony.[^5] In 1905 the New York Times quoted her on the routine, as reproduced in the Park Service lesson: the lamp needed winding every five hours, she wound it every three, and "In 19 years that light has never disappointed sailors who have depended upon it."[^12] In 1909 Harper's Weekly sent three reporters. She told them: "Just keep the light burning and the fog bell wound up and the siren ready all the time."[^1]

Kate retired in 1919; the nomination says at age 73, though the birth year of 1848 given by the Noble Maritime Collection would make her about 70.[^1][^14] She died in 1931.[^14] Jacob Walker was keeper in 1919 and 1920, followed by Frank Niblett to 1923 and Harry C. Bell. Adelard Bussiere is listed from 1926 to 1943 and Herman F. Westgate from 1946 to 1962.[^2] In 1921 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that Frank S. Niblett, keeper of Robbins Reef, had recently rescued two men from drowning near Swinburne Island on his way home; the archive spells his middle initial K.[^9][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Kate estimated that she saved fifty people over the years: "I suppose I've saved fifty persons. I don't know the names of any."[^4] She said rescued people asked her not to mention it, and that she kept no record but the date and the number saved.[^4]

She described two hard winters in her 1918 interview. In one the bay filled with ice. With the lighthouse surrounded, she packed bedding, food and warm clothes for the children, ready to step onto a floe if the tower were ground to pieces. The floe held for three days, and blocks of ice drifted in the bay for three months that spring.[^4] On another occasion a trip home from the docks took three hours; a ferryboat threw a line and towed her boat as near as it dared, and the spray froze on her as she climbed the ladder.[^4] On a Christmas two years before the article, a gale and sleet drove against the windows. She started the foghorn, spent the night alone, and at 3 a.m. two scows jammed against the lighthouse and went off with the help of its light.[^4]

The Lighthouse Service recorded other incidents. On 30 October 1921 a lighthouse tender went to help the ferryboat Mayor Gaynor, with 400 passengers, which had run ashore on Robbins Reef, and helped a police steamer tow it to its slip at St. George.[^10] On 18 June 1930 the liner President Roosevelt went aground off the reef at 7:35 a.m.; the keeper reported that the fog signal was running at the time.[^11]

## Fog signals

The station's first fog signal was a bell that someone had to ring by hand in heavy weather.[^1] In 1893 a blower siren driven by a Priestman engine was added; the nomination says the engine gave way to a Hornsby-Akroyd oil engine three years later, and a larger siren trumpet followed in 1898.[^1] The Lighthouse Board's reports give the siren's running time: about 267 hours in the year ended June 1894, 336 in the year ended June 1899 and 338 in the year ended June 1900.[^6][^7][^8] An air siren came in 1929, sounding three seconds on and three off, and a Nautophone in 1933, three seconds on and seven off. The bell remained as a backup.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1939 the Coast Guard took over the Lighthouse Service.[^1] In November 1964 the New York Times reported that Robbins Reef and Romer Shoal would be automated, by armored submarine cable, by 1 July 1965. A March 1965 follow-up described a crew of four, three on duty and one ashore. They kept eight-hour watches, served a few weeks to a month, and then had four or five days of shore leave.[^1] By 1968 the Light List showed the station unmanned.[^1] The owners' website says Coast Guard crews lived there until 1966, when the windows were sealed and the light went on solar power.[^13] The archive's roster runs to 1967.[^2] The log kept in the tower records that the fourth-order lens was taken out on 30 April 1986 and replaced with a 300 mm optic on a 35-watt solar panel, and the fog signal was discontinued the same day.[^1]

The station is listed in the National Register of Historic Places.[^14] The Foundation's website says that in 2010 the government de-accessioned the lighthouse and offered it to non-profit groups, that the Noble Maritime Museum, on Staten Island, bought it for $1, and that the Noble passed it on to the Foundation in 2021.[^13] The Noble Maritime Collection's own page gives different dates, saying the federal government declared the light surplus in 2009 and awarded it to the Noble in 2011, and it still calls the Noble the steward.[^14]

## Today

The light still flashes green every 6 seconds, as an automated aid to navigation.[^1] The Lighthouse Society's archive lists it as not open to the public, and the nomination says the only access is by boat.[^2][^1] The Foundation says it is restoring the tower and hopes to open it to visitors; it is not open now.[^13] In 1996 the Coast Guard named a 175-foot (53 m) buoy tender Katherine Walker in her honor.[^1]
