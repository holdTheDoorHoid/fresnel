Nobska Light stands on a rocky point at the entrance to Woods Hole Harbor in Falmouth, Massachusetts, where Buzzards Bay meets Vineyard Sound.[^1] A light has been shown there since 1828, when the first one was set on the roof of a keeper's house, although the US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1829 as the year the station was established.[^1][^2][^3] The iron tower that stands now went up in 1876, and the Society's record still lists the light as an active aid to navigation.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The 1981 Massachusetts form for the station, written for a group nomination to the National Register, describes a major navigational aid "located at the entrance to Woods Hole Harbor" in Falmouth. It says the light covers "Hedge Fence and L'Hommedieu Shoal" and gives its strategic position as the "junction of Vineyard and Nantucket Sounds with Buzzard's Bay."[^1]

Watching traffic was part of the work. Throughout much of the nineteenth century, keepers were required to track maritime traffic in a Journal of Vessels and submit it monthly.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that on one day in 1864 the keeper, Frederick Ray, counted 188 vessels, 175 of them schooners.[^2] A fog bell worked by machinery was added in 1875.[^2]

## Building the light

The first light cost $2,949.30 to build in 1828, according to LighthouseFriends, and its lantern held ten lamps with fourteen-inch reflectors.[^2] The 1981 form records the light as set on top of the keeper's dwelling, 80 feet (24 m) above the water.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls the design "a poor one": the weight of the iron lantern put severe stress on the dwelling's roof, and several tenants complained that when it rained the family had to move their beds to avoid the leaks.[^2]

A fifth-order lens went into the old light in 1856, LighthouseFriends says, and moved to the new tower in 1876. A larger fourth-order lens took its place in December 1887.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1876 as the year the original lens was installed, and gives a fourth-order Fresnel as the optic now in use.[^2][^3] The 1981 form describes a "4th order Fresnel lens (with a cotten cover)."[^1] The sources disagree on when the fourth-order lens came.

In 1875 the Lighthouse Board said the station needed rebuilding, and in 1876 a cast-iron tower lined with brick went up with a new wood-frame dwelling.[^2] The 1981 form says the complex dates "mainly from 1876."[^1] The brick oil house and paint lockers were built the same year.[^1][^3]

Later work came in order. In the Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1892, a stone wall five feet (1.5 m) thick was built to protect "the site of the bell tower."[^4] A covered way from the dwelling to the tower followed in the year to June 1899, when the Board also recorded a new cistern.[^5] In the year to June 1900 a new house was built for the fog bell's striking machine and the bell tower was strengthened.[^6] A first-class Daboll trumpet, a fog signal, went into operation on the point in 1910, after a dispute with summer residents over the plan.[^2] The 1981 form gives a second keeper's house as built in 1900, which it calls the "second keepers house."[^1] LighthouseFriends mentions a second dwelling added for George Cameron when he became first assistant, a post the roster dates to 1910.[^2][^3] The sources do not reconcile the two dates.

In 1937 a 125-foot (38 m) steel radiobeacon tower went up at the station, and over the next decade the fog signal changed from a reed horn to a diaphragm, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The 1981 form dates the steel tower to 1948.[^1] Here too the sources disagree.

## The keepers

Peter Daggett kept the light from 1828 to 1849.[^3] LighthouseFriends says some of his papers are in the hands of the Falmouth Historical Society, and prints a December report in which he complained that he had "a Great Deal truble to Mak the Oil burn."[^2] In a report of 1 November 1838, Lieutenant Edward W. Carpender, U.S. Navy, praised Daggett's "neatness and reputation."[^2] A newspaper article, quoted by LighthouseFriends, praised his service and then reported that in the summer of 1849 he had been removed "for no other reason than because he is a democrat."[^2] Keepers' posts were political appointments at the time.[^2]

Frederick Ray kept the light from 1861 to 1874.[^3] Oliver A. Nickerson followed, from 1874 to 1911, the longest service of any keeper at Nobska.[^2][^3] His daughter Florence was the station's designated "observer," who watched the water by day for passing ships. A 1908 article, quoted by LighthouseFriends, described her as "shrewd and kindly."[^2]

George I. Cameron was first assistant from 1910 and became head keeper when Nickerson died in 1911, serving to 1913.[^3][^2] George L. Lyon kept the light from 1913 to 1929.[^3] John M. Scharff was first assistant from 1925 and head keeper from 1929 to 1955.[^3]

The roster and LighthouseFriends disagree about the years after 1968. The roster lists Joseph G. Hindley as first assistant from 1956 to 1973, and names Donald W. Selfe as head keeper from 1967 to 1969.[^3] LighthouseFriends says Hindley was head keeper from 1968 and retired on 3 November 1972 after forty-four years, and that he was believed to be the last civilian keeper in New England.[^2] The roster's later head keepers run from Osborne E. Hallett through Donald W. Selfe, William D. Lambert, Tracy Clarke and Gary Williams to Charles Tebo, who held the post in 1985, the year the light was automated.[^3]

In January 1925, the Lighthouse Service Bulletin says, keepers in the second and third districts were to submit data on the eclipse of the sun on January 24, and Nobska Point is among the lights it names.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In August 1911 the Boston-bound steamer Bunker Hill, carrying more than 300 passengers, ran aground on a clear, calm night in an area LighthouseFriends describes as hazardous even with the light and fog signal.[^2] A passenger quoted there said the steamer had not come within 100 feet of the light. LighthouseFriends says the keeper summoned help and every passenger was taken off safely.[^2]

In August 1935 keeper John M. Scharff and assistant keeper Waldo Leighton rescued a man who had been thrown into the water from a sailboat in a windstorm, the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports.[^8]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1985, and the station's last keeper left. The keeper's house became the residence of the Coast Guard commander at Woods Hole, LighthouseFriends says.[^2][^1]

The Friends of Nobska Light was incorporated in 2015 to raise money for the property. In March 2016 the Falmouth selectmen approved a Coast Guard license making the town steward, and the town approved $264,000 for restoration at its April 2017 town meeting.[^2] The General Services Administration offered the lighthouse at no cost in May 2023 to eligible federal, state and local agencies and non-profit corporations, and in June 2024 the Friends opened the restored tower and keeper's dwelling to the public.[^2] The Friends received the deed from the Coast Guard on July 21, 2025.[^2][^9]

## Today

The Friends of Nobska Light own the station.[^9] The Society's record still names the Coast Guard as owner, licensed to the Friends.[^3] Their website lists tower tours and the museum and gift shop for the 2026 season, which ran from June 2 to October 6 on Tuesdays, Wednesdays and Thursdays, with twilight tours on Wednesdays, and it says the season is now closed. Private tours can be arranged on request.[^9] The Friends plan a Nobska Light Maritime Museum,[^9] and LighthouseFriends reported in 2017 that the keeper's dwelling was to become a maritime museum.[^2] The Society's record gives the current optic as a fourth-order Fresnel and lists the light as an active aid to navigation.[^3] Climb the tower only on one of the Friends' scheduled tours, and check their website before you go.
