Allan's Island Lighthouse marks the entrance to the harbour at Lamaline, on the southern shore of the Burin Peninsula in Newfoundland and Labrador. It is a white tower with a red top, standing on the southern end of the island that shelters the harbour.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Shoals and islets lie just off the harbour at Lamaline.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the community may have taken its name from them, and traces the name to a French term meaning malignant.[^1] The settlement is believed to date from the late 1700s.[^1] The first official census of Newfoundland, in 1836, counted 263 people there.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1877 a Mr. Ayre presented a petition to the Newfoundland House of Assembly.[^1] It came from Josiah Darrel and other people of Lamaline, asking for a grant "to erect a Beacon Light at Lamaline."[^1] Work on a lighthouse on Allan's Island began in 1878.[^1] Inspector John T. Nevill, who visited the following year, judged the site a good one.[^1]

The date of the first light is not settled. LighthouseFriends gives 1879.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory gives 1880 as the year the station was established.[^2]

The first tower was a wooden octagon, its sides painted alternately red and white.[^1] Its fixed white light came from an eighth-order dioptric lens, with its focal plane 19.5 m (64 ft) above the water.[^1] In 1883 work began on a small structure beside the tower, with a room for the keeper to sleep in.[^1]

The light was upgraded to a fourth-order dioptric light in 1911.[^1] In 1912 two range lights on poles were set up on the north side of the harbour, the front one 6.6 m high on the shore and the rear one 146 m inland.[^1] The octagonal tower came down in 1916, after an iron tower and a new dwelling were built, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory says the iron tower came in 1920.[^2]

A fog alarm, run by compressed air from an oil engine, began in 1920.[^1] It sounded two blasts every ninety seconds.[^1] The pole range lights were replaced by two enclosed square, pyramidal towers in 1926, according to LighthouseFriends, or in 1927, according to the Lighthouse Directory.[^1][^2] In 1953 a 9 m (30 ft) square pyramidal wooden tower went up beside a double dwelling for the keepers.[^1][^2] The present tower was erected around 2004.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

John Messervey kept the light until he died in September 1885, and John Fleming was appointed in his place.[^1] In 1890 Nevill found the light "not as good as that of similar lamps and lenses elsewhere."[^1] He decided that Fleming was falling short in his duties, and warned him to take more care.[^1]

Fleming kept the light until 1900.[^1] William Cochrane followed, from 1901 to 1912.[^1] LighthouseFriends also names a William Cake, without dates.[^1] Ernest McCarthy kept the light from 1915 to 1945, Adolph McCarthy from 1945 to 1951, and Ches Maddigan from the early 1950s to 1964.[^1] Lawrence Slaney is the last keeper the list names, from 1964.[^1] J. Hillier, Jr., was the first keeper of the range lights, in 1912.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard's list gives the light a white flash every four seconds, from 19.5 m above the water, with a range of 16 nautical miles.[^3] The tower is 11.3 m (37 ft) high.[^3] The fog horn sounds a three-second blast, followed by 27 seconds of silence.[^3]

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse.[^1] A Fisheries and Oceans Canada brochure, dated 2005, lists Lamaline (Allan's Island) among the staffed lightstations, and says 23 of the province's 55 major lightstations are staffed.[^4] Because the station is staffed, the keepers' home is a working place.[^4] The grounds are open, the tower is closed, and the road reaches the station across a causeway to the island.[^1][^2]
