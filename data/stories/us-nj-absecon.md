Absecon Light stands at the northern end of Absecon Island, now the north end of Atlantic City, New Jersey.[^1] Its brick and iron tower, 171 feet (52 m) tall, was built to guide ships clear of the shoals off Absecon Inlet.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends calls it the tallest lighthouse in New Jersey, and its first-order Fresnel lens was first lit on 15 January 1857.[^1][^2]

## The place

The shoals were the reason for the light.[^2] In 1854 Congress appropriated $35,000 for a coast light to guide navigators clear of Absecon and Brigantine Shoals.[^2] The idea was older than that. Dr. Jonathan Pitney arrived on Absecon Island in 1820 and encouraged his friends and patients to vacation there.[^2] In 1830 he petitioned the government for a lighthouse.[^2] Congress approved $5,000 in 1837, but Commander E. A. F. Vallette, sent to look at the site, judged that a light was not justified.[^2] A bar across the inlet's mouth meant ships could keep well offshore, and the work was cancelled.[^2]

Pitney had teamed with a Philadelphia civil engineer to link the island to Philadelphia by rail, and the first train arrived on 5 July 1854.[^2] The tourists followed.[^2] The lot near the inlet was bought from the Camden & Atlantic Land Company for $520.[^2]

The sea was already moving the shore.[^2] The tower first stood 1,300 feet (396 m) from the water.[^2] By 1864 the encroaching sea was causing alarm, and regular measurements of the shoreline began.[^2] Seven short jetties went in during 1876 and a long one in 1878, and in 1884 a small part of the lighthouse lot was used to build a life-saving station.[^2]

## Building the light

Work began in June 1855 under a succession of officers of the Army's Topographical Engineering Corps: Maj. Hartman Bache, Lt. George Meade and Lt. Col. William F. Raynolds.[^2] Meade complained that the appropriation was "entirely inadequate for a work of the magnitude now being erected," and that there were no wharves for landing materials.[^2]

While the foundation trench was being dug, water from the adjoining marshes flooded in.[^2] A steam pump able to remove 12,000 gallons an hour had to be bought.[^2] Meade asked for a further $17,436.62 to finish the work, and the money was provided on 18 August 1856.[^2]

The sources give different dates for the building. The 1970 National Register nomination says the tower was erected in 1856 and was built under the direction of an officer who later commanded Union forces.[^1] The Lighthouse Society's record gives 1857 as the year the tower was established.[^3] The tower used 598,634 bricks and has a spiral staircase of 228 steps.[^2] The nomination describes a circular tower 27 feet (8 m) across at the base, with a 12-sided pyramid roof.[^1]

The tower's height is also given two ways. The nomination and LighthouseFriends say 171 feet, while the Society's record gives 159 feet from the base to the lantern.[^1][^2][^3]

LighthouseFriends says the lens was a first-order Fresnel lens made in 1854 by L. Sautter & Cie of Paris, and that it was first lit on 15 January 1857.[^2] The nomination says the light was fired by mineral oil and intensified by a first-order Fresnel lens.[^1] The head keeper's dwelling was joined to the tower by a 30-foot (9 m) enclosed passage.[^2] The second dwelling, for the assistants, stood on the far side of the tower.[^2]

## The keepers

Daniel L. Scull was the first head keeper, from 1856 to 1861.[^3] The Society's roster names six head keepers in all, and a long line of assistants.[^3] William Hardwick served as second assistant from 1924 until the light closed in 1933.[^3]

Abraham G. Wolf was the fourth head keeper, serving from 1873 to 1896.[^2][^3] Kate W. Wolf is listed as first assistant keeper from 1874 to 1884.[^3] The roster does not say how she was related to the head keeper of the same surname, if at all.[^3]

Knud Hanson arrived in 1896 as second assistant and became head keeper in 1914.[^2] He retired at 66, though he stayed on as a caretaker for a while.[^2]

The Lighthouse Service asked why three keepers were needed at a station with no revolving lens and no fog signal.[^2] An inspector who visited in 1912 was asked whether it would be wise to cut visiting hours and a keeper.[^2] He said no.[^2] He allowed that the tours were a bother at times, but they were good publicity for the Service, and more than 10,000 people a year were signing the visitors' book.[^2]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded the visitors by the quarter.[^4][^5][^6] 10,339 people registered at the station in the three months to 30 September 1912,[^4] and 8,500 in the quarter to 30 September 1913.[^5] In the quarter to 30 September 1922, 10,500 persons registered, or about 117 a day, and the Bulletin said the station was visited more than any other lighthouse in the United States.[^6] The Service also ordered the greenhouse removed, and the ornamental plantings that had got an early start in it were lost.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Board wrote that, in the ten months since the light was first shown, "there have been no wrecks in its vicinity."[^2]

LighthouseFriends prints the entry that Keeper Abraham G. Wolf made in the station log at 9:52 p.m. on 30 August 1886.[^2] He was out on the gallery at the top of the tower, and the entry reads "Felt a shock of earthquake while on top of tower."[^2] He called his wife on a telephone of his own making that ran from the tower to the dwelling kitchen, and she had not felt it.[^2] When the assistant came to relieve him, the assistant suggested the entry be struck out.[^2] The next morning a Philadelphia paper carried an account of the earthquake, and the entry stayed in the log.[^2]

LighthouseFriends dates the entry to 30 August.[^2] The US Geological Survey dates the Charleston, South Carolina, earthquake of 1886 to 31 August, a day after the entry, and no source here says whether the two events are the same.[^11]

The Board's 1893 report records a bell buoy dated 16 December 1892 for the wreck of the Floriday off the Absecon Life-Saving Station, the wreck having been removed.[^8]

## After the keepers

In its report for 1895 the Board recorded that several people were erecting buildings on light-house land.[^9] A written protest was served on each of them.[^9] The Board's report for 1896 to 1897 records the tower as repainted, and the grounds, once loose drifting sand, as grassed over.[^10]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists the light among those whose illuminant changed in July 1925, from incandescent oil vapor to electric incandescent.[^7]

The light was discontinued in 1933, and the nomination says it was decommissioned that year.[^1][^2] On 11 July 1933 a new light was exhibited from a steel tower on the boardwalk near New Hampshire Avenue.[^2] Keeper Knud Hanson retired.[^2]

The two keepers' dwellings were demolished in the 1940s.[^2] In 1946 Atlantic City agreed to take control of the lighthouse, which LighthouseFriends credits with saving the tower from demolition.[^2] Atlantic City ran the lighthouse until 1966, when the property went to the State of New Jersey.[^2]

According to LighthouseFriends, the Atlantic City Coastal Museum began opening the lighthouse to visitors in 1979.[^2] The 1970 nomination already describes "a small museum in a new building erected at the base," open during the tourist season, so the sources differ on when visitors were first admitted.[^1]

A 1997 campaign raised $2.75 million to rebuild the keeper's dwelling as it appeared after 1903, and to restore the tower and lens.[^2] The nearly finished dwelling burned on 6 July 1998, but the tower was not damaged.[^2] The complete station reopened to the public in October 2001.[^2]

## Today

New Jersey owns the lighthouse.[^2] The nomination names the state's Department of Environmental Protection as owner.[^1][^2]

The grounds, the keeper's dwelling and the tower are open.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the museum is in the keeper's dwelling and that visitors can climb the tower.[^2] It lists hours of 10 a.m. to 5 p.m. daily in July and August, and 11 a.m. to 4 p.m., Thursday through Monday, for the rest of the year.[^2] Check those hours before a visit.[^2]

In 2024 the lighthouse received a $750,000 Save America's Treasures grant for interior and exterior restoration.[^2] Water had got into the top and the bottom of the tower, causing the brick to deteriorate and the metal to corrode.[^2]

The Society's record still lists the light as an active aid to navigation, and gives its discontinuance as "1933-1997."[^3] LighthouseFriends says the light was deactivated years ago, though it kept its Fresnel lens.[^2]
