Straitsmouth Island Light stands on an island southeast of the entrance to Rockport Harbor, off the northeast end of Cape Ann, Massachusetts.[^1][^3] The National Register nomination dates a brick tower there to 1835, but the archive and the Light-House Board's reports date the tower standing now to 1896.[^1][^2][^6] The light belongs to the Town of Rockport.[^2] The island it stands on is a bird sanctuary, and the archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^2][^3]

## The place

Rockport's growing granite trade and its fishing business made a lighthouse necessary, LighthouseFriends says, to guide ships to Pigeon Cove Pier, where the stone was loaded. It chose Straitsmouth Island as the ideal site at the northeastern end of Sandy Bay.[^3]

The hazard was the island's low eastern end and Alden's Ledge. In 1842 the inspector I. W. P. Lewis complained that the first light misled strangers, who could come ashore on the eastern part of the island or strike Alden's Ledge, where there was no buoy or other mark.[^3]

The land was bought before the tower went up. On 28 November 1834 Aaron and Solomon Poole sold the site to the federal government for $600.[^1]

## Building the light

The money came first. LighthouseFriends says $5,000 was appropriated on 30 June 1834, and that $4,091.29 of it was spent on a nineteen-foot (5.8 m) brick tower and a brick keeper's dwelling.[^3] The National Register nomination dates a brick tower, a Gothic Revival keeper's house and an oil house to 1835.[^1]

The first light did not last. LighthouseFriends quotes the inspector Lewis's verdict on the tower, made in 1842: "a specimen of contract work of the worst kind."[^3] It reports that he found the mortar poor, the woodwork rotten, the tower leaky, and four of the six lamps out of plumb.[^3] Keeper John Davis agreed with Lewis that the contractor had built on the spot more convenient for him, rather than the intended one.[^3] In April 1843 Davis proposed moving the light eighty-seven yards (about 80 m). The new spot would put the tower farther from the dwelling, and Davis argued that this should not count against the lives and property at risk.[^3]

LighthouseFriends says it would be more than seven years before a replacement was built.[^3] Construction began in 1851 on a new octagonal stone tower, twenty-four feet (7.3 m) tall, on the island's northeastern tip. It received a sixth-order Fresnel lens in 1857.[^3][^2]

The keeper stayed in the original dwelling until 1877, when LighthouseFriends says a new six-room wood-frame house was finished.[^3] The archive gives 1878 for the keeper's quarters.[^2] The nomination puts the surviving Gothic house in 1835.[^1] The sources disagree, and this story does not settle the date.

The keeper walked to the tower along a raised wooden footbridge.[^3] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 records that the footbridge from the dwelling to the tower was rebuilt that year.[^4]

In 1896 the tower itself was rebuilt. In May the light was shown from a temporary wooden tower about 45 feet (14 m) away.[^5][^3] On 30 July the light moved into the new tower, 45 feet (14 m) from the temporary one.[^7] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1896 says the tower and work-room were "rebuilt on the same foundations."[^6] LighthouseFriends describes the new tower as thirty-seven feet (11 m) tall, cylindrical and brick, set on the foundation of the 1851 tower.[^3] The archive gives its height as 37 feet (11.3 m) to the center of the lantern.[^2]

In the fiscal year 1900, some 120 running feet (37 m) of stone and gravel walk was laid, and the boathouse was moved onto stone piers.[^8] LighthouseFriends dates the oil house to 1905, when a building was put up to hold the kerosene then in use.[^3] The nomination lists an oil house from 1835, built of stone.[^1] The two sources disagree, and this story does not settle which date is right.[^1][^3]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists eighteen principal keeper's stints between 1835 and 1933.[^2] Benjamin W. Andrews is listed first, for 1835 to 1840. LighthouseFriends says he died on the island in 1840, at the age of forty-four.[^3] The roster also lists a second Benjamin W. Andrews for 1840 to 1841, whom LighthouseFriends calls Benjamin W. Andrews, Jr.[^2][^3]

John Davis came next, for 1841 to 1849. LighthouseFriends says his salary at appointment was $350 a year.[^3][^2] The 1842 inspection and the 1843 letter described above both fall in his time.[^3]

Henry T. Lowe, Ebenezer Pool Jr., Sylvester Pearce and John S. Wheeler followed, each for between one and seven years, through 1866.[^2] Nehemiah Knowlton kept the light from 1866 to 1879.[^2] Frank H. Dennis, Walter S. Rogers and Walter S. Thompson held it between 1879 and 1893.[^2] Thomas W. Newcomb served from 1893 to 1913, the longest single stint on the roster.[^2]

Henry W. Newcomb held the light for 1913 alone. Thomas J. Creed followed from 1913 to 1918, John E. H. Cook from 1918 to 1923, George F. Woodman Jr. from 1923 to 1928, and Hoyt P. Smith from 1928 to 1933, the last keeper the roster names.[^2] The sources say little about the keepers' families.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources give few dated events on the island. LighthouseFriends reports that the storm known as the Perfect Storm, in October 1991, destroyed the entry house to the tower.[^3]

The Life-Saving Service also kept a station nearby. Its 1900 station table lists a station called Gap Cove, one-half mile west of Straitsmouth light.[^10] By 1909 the Service's annual report refers to "the Straitsmouth life-saving station" in a casualty narrative, as the station within whose official jurisdiction the casualty happened.[^11] The two names probably belong to one station, but the sources do not say so outright.

## After the keepers

The sources disagree on when the light was automated. The archive gives 1967.[^2] LighthouseFriends says it was automated in the early 1930s, when a local man was licensed to live in the keeper's dwelling.[^3] The roster's last keeper is listed until 1933.[^2]

In 1931 the light was changed from white to its present green, flashing every six seconds.[^3]

In 1941 Glenn Wilson of New York bought the island, all but the 1.8-acre light station, for $3,050.[^1][^3] For a short time after the Second World War a restaurant ran on the island.[^3] The island was later sold to another owner. In 1960 that owner's stepson was allowed to live in the keeper's house, provided he did not disturb the gulls or change the character of the house.[^3] The island went to the Massachusetts Audubon Society as a sanctuary after that owner's death in 1967.[^3]

An electric foghorn was installed on the active tower in 1974, and the abandoned house was quickly deteriorating.[^3] The nomination records a 1982 painting of the tower with new windows and a new door, and a conversion to solar power in 1985.[^1]

The Coast Guard declared the light surplus in July 2008.[^3] On 16 July 2010 it handed the keys and deed to the light, and its 1.8 acres, to Rockport's selectmen.[^3] Mass Audubon spent more than $180,000 between 2010 and 2013 rehabilitating the keeper's dwelling. The Thacher Island Association restored the oil house in 2010 and the lighthouse in 2013, signed a thirty-year lease on the dwelling and oil house in 2014, and saw restoration of the lighthouse and keeper's house completed in 2018, along with a dock for visitors.[^3]

## Today

The light is an active aid to navigation, solar powered, and it belongs to the Town of Rockport.[^2][^3] The station is on the National Register of Historic Places as number 87001487.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the Thacher Island Town Committee would manage it, and the Thacher Island Association would raise money for its upkeep.[^3] Seasonal caretakers live on the island in one-week shifts.[^3]

The island itself is private land. It belongs to the Massachusetts Audubon Society and is used as a bird sanctuary.[^3] The archive records the station as not open to the public.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the best view is from the water, and it reports summer trips to the island on Tuesdays run by the Thacher and Straitsmouth Islands Association.[^3] Check the current arrangements before you go.
