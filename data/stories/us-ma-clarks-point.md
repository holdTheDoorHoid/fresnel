Clarks Point Light stands at the southern entrance to New Bedford Harbor, on its western side. Local merchants paid for its first beacon in 1797, rather than wait for the government to do it.[^2] Its lantern now sits on top of Fort Taber, a granite fort on the point, which is now a city park.[^8][^2]

## The place

New Bedford was a whaling port, and the ships that sailed from Buzzards Bay into the harbor needed a mark at its mouth.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the merchants built their beacon to protect ships and cargo on that passage, and that the lucrative whaling trade made them willing to pay for it.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says a plot at the end of the point was deeded for the light on 16 June 1800. On 29 July 1800, it adds, Congress voted to accept financial responsibility for the beacon and to appoint a keeper.[^2] On 24 September 1857 the federal government bought the Edward Wing Howland farm on the point, so a granite fort could be built.[^2] LighthouseFriends notes that the fort came to be called "Fort Taber" unofficially, though the U.S. Army never recognized that name.[^2] As the fort grew, it began to block the light's beam, and its construction progressively blocked the view of Clarks Point from approaching ships.[^2][^8]

## Building the light

The merchants' wooden beacon of 1797 burned after about a year of use.[^2] A replacement was first lit on 12 October 1799.[^2] Ricketson, writing in 1858, says the wooden light was raised with a feast of a hundred-gallon try-pot of chowder, and that "no one became intoxicated on this occasion."[^1] LighthouseFriends puts the 100-gallon pot of chowder at the raising of the 1804 stone tower's frame instead, so the two accounts disagree about which raising it was.[^2]

On 16 March 1804 Congress committed $2,500 to replace the beacon with a stone tower of 38 feet (11.6 m).[^2] LighthouseFriends says it showed a fixed white light with a focal plane of 50 feet (15 m); the New England Lighthouses history gives 52 feet above the sea for the light.[^2][^3] That history describes the tower as an octagonal rubblestone tower, 38 feet tall, completed in 1804. The tower was raised 4 feet (1.2 m) and given a new octagonal iron lantern in 1818.[^3]

Lieutenant Edward W. Carpender inspected the light in 1838. LighthouseFriends quotes his report: "Two of the chimneys, or tube-glasses, were broken, and some of the others so much smoked as to show that they had been carelessly trimmed." The keeper lived in his own house, because the government had provided no dwelling.[^2]

A fifth-order Fresnel lens had been installed at Clarks Point in 1856, and in 1865 the old lantern room was replaced with one of larger panes of glass.[^2] In 1869 the lantern and its apparatus were moved to a rectangular wooden tower on the fort. The light was first shown from there on 15 June 1869, 68 feet (21 m) above the sea.[^3][^8]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster names eight principal keepers, from David Allen (1800-1803) to Amos C. Baker, Jr. (1880-1898).[^4] LighthouseFriends also lists Joseph Howland (1803 to 1813) before Benjamin Butler, though he is not on the roster.[^2] The roster prints David Wilber's stint as 1815-1919; LighthouseFriends gives 1815 to 1819.[^4][^2]

Cornelius Howland kept the light from 1819 to 1835. The New England Lighthouses history says he died at 77, and that his son Edward became keeper in early 1835. The roster calls the son Edward H. Howland; the history calls him Edward W.[^3][^4]

Henry M. Smith was keeper from 1843 to 1872, according to the roster; the New England Lighthouses keeper list gives 1843 to 1873.[^4][^3] The New England Lighthouses history says he was a woodcarver of figureheads for New Bedford's whaling fleet.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he used his woodworking skills to help complete a needed keeper's dwelling.[^2]

The Board's annual reports also recorded repairs. In its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892, the Light-House Board noted that "The barn was rebuilt," and that a scuttle was put in the roof of the dwelling.[^5]

The roster has Amos C. Baker from 1873 to 1880, and his son Amos C. Baker, Jr., from 1880 to 1898.[^4] The 1929 Lighthouse Service Bulletin dates the father's service from 1872 to 1879, "the year of his death," and calls the family's service "a total service in three generations of this family of 70 years."[^7] LighthouseFriends gives the father's death as 7 January 1881, so the sources disagree.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Ricketson says the first wooden light was burnt during a severe thunderstorm at night. He gives two possible causes: lightning, or, as some supposed, a sea-fowl that flew through the glass and overturned the lamps.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the fire to about a year after the beacon was built.[^2] The sources cited here record no wreck or rescue at the light.

## After the keepers

The Light-House Board's report for fiscal 1895 records an appropriation for a light and fog signal on or near Butler Flat, at the entrance to the lower harbor, "to take the place of Clark Point and Fairhaven bridge lights."[^6] The Clark Point light was extinguished on 30 April 1898, after the Butler Flats light was completed. Amos C. Baker, Jr., moved to Butler Flats.[^2][^3][^7] He stayed there until his death in 1911, according to the Bulletin.[^7] The stone tower stood near the fort until it was demolished in 1906.[^2][^3]

The light on the fort then fell into neglect.[^8] A 2001 Coast Guard account says it "stood dark for nearly 80 years," and that the light and Fort Taber were designated a National Register district in 1973.[^8] It was briefly relit for the bicentennial in 1976, but vandals burned and nearly destroyed it after its early 1970s renovation.[^8][^2] In 1997 the light was given "a new lease on life," and the Coast Guard account says the city committed $7 million to renovate the light and build a park at the fort.[^8] The light was relit on 15 June 2001, the 132nd anniversary of its first lighting.[^2]

## Today

LighthouseFriends lists the lighthouse as owned by the City of New Bedford. The grounds are open, but the lighthouse and the fort are closed.[^2] It has been inactive since 2016, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The park is reached by taking Route 18 south into New Bedford, which becomes Water Street and then West Rodney French Boulevard, and continuing to the southern end of the peninsula.[^2]
