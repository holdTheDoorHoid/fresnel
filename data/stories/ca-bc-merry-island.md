Merry Island sits at the southeast entrance to Welcome Pass on British Columbia's Sunshine Coast, where vessels travelling between Vancouver and points to the north pass on their way through the Welcome Pass route, past a light on the island's low southeast point.[^2][^1]

## The place

The island takes its name from a racehorse. According to Lighthouse Friends, Captain George Richards, surveying the coast in HMS *Plumper* in 1860, named Merry Island, Thormanby Island, Epsom Point and Derby Point after the Epsom Derby winner Thormanby, owned by James Merry.[^2] (The Sechelt Community Archives, which dates the Derby to 1865, disagrees on the year.[^5]) Welcome Pass runs between Thormanby Island and the mainland, and Merry Island lies at its southern entrance.[^2] The Department of Marine described the 1903 light as standing on the low point forming the island's southeast extremity.[^1]

The Canadian Register of Historic Places says the station has served heavy traffic since 1903, directing vessels coming from the north, from Alaska and farther away, through the Strait of Georgia into the Port of Vancouver.[^3] The case for a light was made locally: Lighthouse Friends reports that in 1901 the Vancouver Ship Masters Association petitioned Ottawa for a lighthouse and fog alarm on Merry Island.[^2]

## Building the light

Work began in May 1903, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^2] The Department of Marine's report describes the result: a rectangular wooden dwelling with a mansard roof, painted white, with an octagonal wooden lantern on its roof, 35 feet (10.7 m) from base to ventilator.[^1] From 6 November 1903 a temporary fixed white light was to be shown from 57 feet (17 m) above high water, visible 6 miles, until the permanent apparatus was ready. The works were done by day labour under Mr. P. G. Fenton of Vancouver.[^1]

Some modern summaries give 1902 as the founding year; the Department of Marine's own report, the Register of Historic Places and Lighthouse Friends all point to 1903.[^1][^2][^3]

A mechanical fog alarm followed in the 1920s. Until then the keeper had a hand foghorn, provided in 1903, to sound in answer to a ship's signal. In 1922 the Canadian Navigators' Federation's Pacific Division secretary complained it was inadequate, and Keeper Franklin was asked to count passing vessels; Lighthouse Friends says he counted 220 in daylight hours in a single month.[^2] The Department of Marine reported that the new fog alarm building and machinery were finished and that the alarm would begin operating about the middle of April 1924.[^6] A radio station was set up on the island at the same time, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^2]

## The keepers

William Thomas Franklin was the first keeper. The Department of Marine's 1906 table of light-keepers lists "Franklin, Wm. Thos" at Merry Island, appointed 8 January 1904 at $360 a year, a date a few months after the first light.[^7] Lighthouse Friends says his salary was $360 a year and that he and his wife Mary Ann homesteaded the rest of the island, raising sheep, turkeys, chickens and ducks.[^2] The Sechelt Community Archives adds that the couple kept a guest book for visitors, and that Franklin was credited with rescuing many sailors and fishermen from rough water.[^5] Lighthouse Friends says he retired in 1932 after thirty years but stayed on the island for several years to look after his small farm; the Sechelt archives' caption says he kept the light until 1933.[^2][^5]

Lighthouse Friends lists Jonathon Fleming and then Ellie Joseph LeClerc as the next keepers.[^2] When LeClerc lay dying in hospital, he wrote to the marine agent to ask for help for his family; Lighthouse Friends says the agent responded in 1939 by appointing his wife, Helen LeClerc, keeper. Born in British Columbia to German parents, she came under close scrutiny from the island's few residents when the Second World War began, and Lighthouse Friends says she was accused of being a German Nazi; she retired in 1945 through ill health.[^2] William Charles Copeland is next on Lighthouse Friends' list, and a Sechelt Archives caption says the Copeland family took over the lighthouse duties after Franklin retired.[^2][^5]

George Potts kept the light from 1951 to 1966 with, at first, no assistant. In 1958 he was treated for total exhaustion; Lighthouse Friends lists six weather reports a day among his duties and reproduces his own account: he was "on the go from daylight to 9 PM every day", and in one winter period went 14 days without taking his clothes off and got only 10 hours' sleep. An assistant's dwelling was finally built in 1958 and Fred Julian became the first permanent assistant.[^2] Without commercial power the Potts family ran a small generator for a television and kerosene for the refrigerator.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 22 March 1927 the station's radio operator, Gerald Pike, was refuelling an engine to charge the radio batteries when a spark from his pipe ignited the gasoline. Lighthouse Friends, drawing on a police report, says he ran burning to the keeper's house; Franklin's mother-in-law doused him, then summoned Franklin with the hand horn, and Franklin returned in time to save the buildings. Franklin signalled an SOS and the tug *Leroy* took Pike to Vancouver, where he died.[^2]

## After the keepers

The present lighthouse, a square one-storey building with a 40-foot (12 m) tower at one corner, was built in 1966, the year electricity and telephone cables were laid across Welcome Pass, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^2] Parks Canada's statement of significance calls it a concrete block replacement of a type built between the 1940s and 1960s, with an integrated fog alarm building, two fog horns protruding from the tower and two red maple leaves sculpted in relief.[^3] The Canadian Coast Guard List of Lights describes a white square tower with a red top.[^4]

## Today

The light flashes white every 15 seconds, 17.6 m above the water, with a range of 17 nautical miles.[^4] Lighthouse Friends lists Don Richards as keeper from 1987 to 2021 and Mike Schulz from 2021 to at least 2024, so the station was still staffed at last report.[^2] Access is restricted: Lighthouse Friends says the lighthouse is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard and the grounds and tower are closed; it is best seen from the water, and a distant view is possible from Redrooffs Road on the mainland. The portion of the island Franklin once owned was offered for sale in 2001.[^2]
