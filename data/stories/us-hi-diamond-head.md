Diamond Head Light stands at the foot of the old volcano that overlooks Waikiki, on the southeast coast of Oʻahu. It was first lit in 1899, the last primary light the Hawaiian government built, and the concrete tower standing there now dates from 1918.[^1][^2] The light was automated in 1924, according to the Coast Guard's history, or 1923 according to LighthouseFriends.[^1][^2]

## The place

For ships coming to Honolulu from the American West Coast, Diamond Head was the landmark that guided them in.[^2] Its name comes from clear calcite crystals that sailors took for diamonds in the 1820s.[^2] A lookout stood on the seaward slope before the light was built.[^2] LighthouseFriends says it was established in 1878, when John Charles Petersen, a Swedish-born mariner, became its first watchman.[^2] Petersen, known as Diamond Head Charlie, served for thirty years until his death in 1907.[^2] The Coast Guard's history dates a wooden lookout tower to 1856, so the sources disagree on when the lookout began.[^1]

Two wrecks were widely cited as reasons for a light.[^2] The Coast Guard's history says the steamer Miowera "ran aground at Diamond Head," which prompted the Hawaiian legislature to recommend a lighthouse.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the grounding to the night of 2 October 1893 and says it took six weeks to free her.[^2] In 1897 the China ran aground near the crater, and the Coast Guard's history says that wreck is what finally got an iron tower under way.[^1][^2]

## Building the light

Captain James King, minister of the interior, had been pressing the legislature for a light on Diamond Head for several years.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the 4 December 1897 Pacific Commercial Advertiser reported that steps were finally being taken, with a forty-foot (12 m) metal tower ordered, and that Charles Peterson, the lookout, would look after the light as well as his own work.[^2]

The Coast Guard's history says the 40-foot iron frame was constructed at the Honolulu Iron Works "but never installed."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the original structure was a forty-foot iron framework tower built by the Honolulu Iron Works. The Senate committee that inspected the new light on 31 March 1898 described it as iron above a concrete foundation, supported on four galvanized iron pipes.[^2][^3] The lens was a third-order Fresnel "built by Barbier and Benard."[^1][^2] Some legislators argued that the frame "was wholly insufficient for strength."[^3] The committee's report, printed in the Pacific Commercial Advertiser on 12 August 1898, said that "a lighthouse should be a permanent institution that can be relied upon."[^3][^2] It recommended a stone and cement tower built up around the iron, and the original appropriation of $10,000 was not enough to cover it.[^3][^4] The Hawaiian Gazette printed the same day's story, with the Senate debate "on the new Diamond Head lighthouse."[^4]

LighthouseFriends says E. B. Thomas was contracted in January 1899 to build the masonry tower for $1,170, using coral rock quarried on Oʻahu.[^2] The masonry was left with a circular hole about halfway up, so that Charlie could see toward Barbers Point.[^2] The light was first lit on 1 July 1899.[^2][^1]

## The keepers

Captain A. Christian was the first official keeper.[^2] LighthouseFriends calls him an old sailor who knew the duties of a lighthouse keeper.[^2] On 23 December 1899 he was stricken with paralysis and blindness at the lighthouse. He died of a cerebral hemorrhage after being taken to Queen's Hospital.[^2] Neil C. Neilsen is listed as keeper from 1899 to 1904.[^9] LighthouseFriends reports that in January 1901 a judge fined Diamond Head Charlie $50 after he struck the keeper in the face with a club. Charlie was dismissed as lookout and replaced, and he got the job back five months later.[^2]

John M. Kaukaliu was an assistant from 1899 to 1904 and then head keeper until 1916.[^9] The Coast Guard's history ends his service in 1914.[^1] The 1912 Lighthouse Service Bulletin awarded the efficiency pennant to the station, and lists "John Kaukaliu" for it.[^8] LighthouseFriends says he earned seventy-five dollars a month and, since no keeper's dwelling had been provided, lived in a private house a quarter mile away.[^2] On 6 October 1916 he was found paralyzed at the top of the tower. He was carried down on a stretcher and died eight days later.[^2] LighthouseFriends lists the Honolulu Star-Bulletin of 7 October 1916 among its sources.[^2]

The keepers after him were Owen H. Wayson, briefly in 1916, Robert I. Reid from 1916 to 1920, and Alexander D. Toomey from 1920 to 1923.[^9] The Coast Guard's history gives Reid 1915 to 1917 and 1918 to 1919, and Toomey 1919 to 1924. It also names a keeper, Charles K. Akana, in 1924.[^1] Toomey moved to Makapuu Point Light after a couple of years at Diamond Head, LighthouseFriends says.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Miowera and the China are the two groundings the sources tie to the station's story. Both happened before the light existed.[^1][^2]

By 1916 the tower had growing cracks, and LighthouseFriends says an inspection that year found they were compromising its integrity.[^2] Funds were allocated for a new light the following year.[^1] LighthouseFriends says scaffolding went up around the old tower, and the lantern room was moved onto a new metal framework, so the light could keep running while the old tower was taken down.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1904 the Lighthouse Board took control of the Hawaiian lights.[^2] The Coast Guard's history says that year a floor was added to the tower and telephone lines were installed.[^1] The light was given an incandescent oil vapor system in 1910.[^1] The Commissioner's report for 1915 lists Diamond Head among the important coast lights changed "from fixed to flashing or occulting."[^5]

The new concrete tower was completed in 1918 at a cost of $6,109, and the characteristic was changed from fixed to group occulting.[^1] The Coast Guard's history places that change in 1918, while the 1915 report already lists it. The sources disagree on the year.[^1][^5]

The keeper's dwelling came next. An act approved 19 July 1919 appropriated $50,000 for keepers' dwellings, and the Commissioner's report for 1920 says $5,000 was allotted to this station.[^6] The report for 1922 describes it as a frame bungalow, 28 by 46 feet (8.5 by 14 m), on a moss rock foundation.[^7]

In 1939 the Coast Guard took over the light station, along with the rest of the nation's lighthouses.[^1][^2] During the Second World War a Coast Guard radio station was housed in the keeper's dwelling, and a small structure was built on the seaward side of the tower.[^2] After the war the dwelling was remodeled, and LighthouseFriends says it has since been home to the commanders of the Fourteenth Coast Guard District. Rear Admiral Benjamin Engel moved into it in 1967.[^2] The light was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1980.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by the Coast Guard.[^2] The grounds, dwelling and tower are closed, and the Society's record says the light is not open to the public.[^2][^9]

LighthouseFriends says good, distant views of the grounds can be had from the crater rim at Diamond Head State Monument, and that obstructed views are possible from the street and the area near the lighthouse.[^2]

The light still shows a third-order Fresnel lens.[^9] LighthouseFriends also says the tower serves as one end of the finish line for the biennial Transpac yacht race, and that members of the Transpacific Yacht Club are allowed to use it as a lookout.[^2]
