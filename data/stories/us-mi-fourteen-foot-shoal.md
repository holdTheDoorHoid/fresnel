Fourteen Foot Shoal Light marks a shoal in the Straits of Mackinac, about a mile (1.6 km) northwest of Cheboygan Point, Michigan, where the water over a hard gravel bottom is only fourteen feet (4.3 m) deep.[^2][^4] It was first exhibited in 1930, from a concrete and steel station built on a timber crib sunk on the shoal.[^1][^2][^3] The station was designed to be run by radio from the Poe Reef light four or five miles away, depending on the source, and the sources disagree on whether a keeper was ever assigned to it.[^2][^3][^5]

## The place

The shoal lies in the South Channel, the passage between the northern end of the Lower Peninsula and the south side of Bois Blanc Island.[^3] Before the light, a can buoy marked the danger, and Pepper says it had long been placed there.[^2] On 15 April 1925 an acetylene gas buoy replaced the can buoy, and the Lighthouse Service's bulletin that year lists gas buoys established at Fourteen-Foot Shoal in the Straits of Mackinac.[^2][^3][^4]

The older Cheboygan light, built in 1851 to guide mariners through the South Channel, was the other mark for that passage. Pepper describes it as "the seventy-five year old Cheboygan Light" when the new station was planned, and the new station was meant to let it be discontinued.[^2][^3][^6] LighthouseFriends says the Service believed a fog signal out on the shoal would prevent some strandings near Cheboygan Point. The 1930 Bulletin says a steamer's stranding there was caused, no doubt, by mariners being unable to hear or properly locate the fog signal on shore.[^3][^5]

## Building the light

Congress made an initial appropriation of $78,000 in early 1927, and work began that summer.[^3] The Bulletin for 1929 lists the project as a light station on the shoal and two gas buoys, and says these would allow the Cheboygan light to be discontinued.[^6]

The crib was built onshore in July 1928 at a station on the Cheboygan pier, eased down wooden ways into the water, and towed out the following month by the Lighthouse Tender *Aspen*.[^2][^3] On the shoal it was sunk by filling its empty pockets. Pepper says they were filled with rock and gravel. LighthouseFriends says rock and cement, and quotes the Commissioner's report on mass concrete in the outer pockets.[^2][^3] Pepper says wooden forms were built up around the crib and filled with concrete, and a steel house went up on the deck.[^2] By 3 October 1929 the lantern room was on the tower.[^3]

The Commissioner's report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, gives the light as an electric one shown from a fourth-order lens in a lantern 51 feet (15.5 m) above the water, and the total cost as $73,000.[^3] Pepper describes a cable laid from the Cheboygan municipal power plant to the station, and a pair of duplicate diaphone fog signals that sounded one second of blast followed by fourteen seconds of silence.[^2] The 1930 Bulletin gives the mass of the Fourteen Foot Shoal structure as about 6,000 tons, and compares it with the weight of the Poe Reef station, somewhat over 9,000 tons.[^8]

The station was placed in commission at the opening of navigation in 1930. LighthouseFriends gives 18 April 1930 as the day it went into service, and the Bulletin records that the Cheboygan Point light was discontinued that season as the new station was placed in commission.[^2][^3][^5]

## The keepers

The sources disagree. Pepper says the light was unmanned from the day it was commissioned, that the keepers at Poe Reef controlled and maintained its equipment, and that "there were no keepers assigned here."[^2] The 1930 Bulletin says the Fourteen Foot Shoal Light Station was "provided with one keeper" and could request help from Poe Reef at any time, and that three of the four keepers for the two stations were usually on duty at Poe Reef.[^5] LighthouseFriends says a single keeper stayed at the station until the opening of navigation in 1935.[^3] The archive's roster lists no keepers for the station.[^1]

No keeper is named in the sources cited here.

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends quotes the Lake Carriers' Association's report for 1935 as saying the single keeper was withdrawn at the opening of navigation that year, and that the light ran the whole season with nobody in attendance.[^3] In 1956 a 200 mm drum lens lantern replaced the fourth-order lens, according to LighthouseFriends. The archive records a 250 mm optic as current.[^1][^3]

In 2002 the crew of the Coast Guard cutter *Mackinaw* repainted and refurbished the lighthouse.[^3] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 27 July 2005.[^7]

In 2012 the light was made available, under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, to eligible agencies and non-profit groups. No group took it on, and the General Services Administration put the structure up for online auction on 25 July 2017. Five bidders took part, and the auction closed on 30 September 2017 with a high bid of $133,333. LighthouseFriends notes that the bottomlands under the light belong to the State of Michigan.[^3]

## Today

The archive lists the light as active and not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends' visitor notes say the Coast Guard owns the lighthouse and that its tower is closed, yet its own history reports the 2017 auction. No source cited here names the buyer or says whether the sale closed.[^3] The light is best seen from a boat, and eastbound lighthouse cruises from Mackinaw City pass it.[^2][^3]
