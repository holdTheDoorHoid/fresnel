The Faro de Punta Borinquen, Punta Borinquen Light in English, stands on a bluff at the northwest corner of Puerto Rico, near Aguadilla.[^1][^2] Spain completed its first tower there in 1889.[^4] An earthquake and tsunami wrecked it in 1918,[^8] and the light in use today is its replacement, put into service on 31 August 1920.[^13]

## The place

LighthouseFriends says that the opening of the Panama Canal in 1914 made the light a key landmark for ships on the route from Europe to Panama.[^2] The National Register nomination says the light helps guard north-south shipping into the "historically famous Mona Passage."[^1]

The first light was a fourth-order light.[^4] The Light-House Board's 1901 report describes it as flashing "alternately red and white," with thirty seconds between flashes, shown 66 feet (20 m) above the level of the sea and visible 12 miles.[^4]

Getting to the station was hard. Boats could land near it only in the best conditions, because it sat on an open roadstead where the trade-wind swell pounded the shore. Stores had to be landed at Aguadilla and carried five miles (8 km) over a rough trail.[^2] The old tower also had a view problem. A 1915 Commissioner of Lighthouses report says the tower stood at the foot of a bluff 230 feet (70 m) high, and that "plantations of high trees obscure the light" toward the harbor of Aguadilla.[^5]

## Building the light

The first light was built by Spain.[^4] The Light-House Board's 1901 report says it "was finished in 1889 by the Spanish, at a cost of 30,870 pesos."[^4] LighthouseFriends dates its first lighting to 15 September 1889, under the Spanish government.[^2] The nomination says the old structure "closely resembled Arecibo Light in design although it was much smaller."[^1]

The sources describe the old buildings differently. The 1901 report puts the octagonal tower "in front of the center of a square one-story" dwelling.[^4] The nomination says the thirteen-room dwelling "was built around the octagonal tower,"[^1] and LighthouseFriends says the dwelling was centered around the tower.[^2] This story gives both descriptions and does not choose between them.

The sources also disagree on the first lens. The nomination describes "a 3 1/2 order lens built by Barbier & Bernard,"[^1] but the 1901 report calls the light fourth order, and LighthouseFriends says the lens was.[^4][^2] This story follows the report.

Money for a replacement was requested for years before it came. The Commissioner's 1915 report asked for $85,000 to move and rebuild the station, and said that "the present tower is in bad state of repair, the top part being considered unsafe and the foundation insecure."[^5] An act of 28 August 1916 authorized the work but made no appropriation.[^6] The 1915 and 1916 reports both say that, "with the opening of the Panama Canal," the light was "quite insufficient."[^5][^6] Congress passed the appropriation on 12 June 1917.[^7] The Commissioner's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1918 says that "No work has been done or expenditures made under this appropriation" up to that date.[^7]

Preparations for the replacement went ahead in 1918. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that the bid for the metal work had been accepted.[^11] The 1920 report describes the work as "the reconstruction of Point Borinquen Light Station on a new site," and says a contract had been let for "a concrete tower and dwelling for two keepers."[^9] The tower was reinforced concrete and stood apart from the dwelling, which had quarters for two keepers.[^2] A third-order lens was ordered from the Macbeth Company of New York, because the earthquake had destroyed the first lens.[^2]

The Bulletin records that the new station "went into commission on August 31, 1920."[^13] It says the station was "reestablished in a new location" a short distance northeast of the old site.[^14] The old light had stood at the foot of a bluff 230 feet (70 m) high, which obscured the light to the northeast.[^14] The nomination dates the new light to 1922.[^1] This story follows the Bulletin's 1920 date, which LighthouseFriends also gives.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive's roster begins in 1901 with two men. Francisco Capo is listed as principal keeper "at least 1901-at least 1905," and Jose Ducheny as first assistant, "at least 1901-at least 1907."[^3] Later principal keepers on the archive's list include Gustav Larsen (at least 1907 to at least 1911) and Ramon Romero (at least 1917 to at least 1921).[^3]

The 1913 Bulletin names two keepers at this station: "Jose Velez, keeper" and "Jose Carrero, assistant keeper."[^12] The archive lists a Jose F. Carrero as first assistant "at least 1911-at least 1917."[^3] It also lists Jose Escalera Velez as first assistant from 1909 and as principal keeper from at least 1913.[^3] The Bulletin gives no middle name for its Velez, and the sources do not say whether these are the same men, so this story does not link them.

Francisco Cavallieri became an assistant in 1918, listed in the archive as "Fancisco Cavallieri (at least 1918-)."[^3] His part in the 1918 earthquake is in the next section.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 24 February 1913, according to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin, "Jose Velez, keeper" and "Jose Carrero, assistant keeper," of Point Borinquen Light Station, furnished provisions and assistance to two men from the sloop *San Miguel*. The men had been swamped while landing and had been without food for two days.[^12]

The 1918 earthquake was the most damaging event the sources record. LighthouseFriends gives the time as 10:14 a.m. on 11 October 1918, when a magnitude 7.5 earthquake struck Puerto Rico.[^2] The Service's November 1918 bulletin says that nearly every light station on the island sent in earthquake reports, but that the damage was minor except at Point Borinquen and Point Jiguero.[^10] It says the Point Borinquen tower had been "shored up temporarily" but was considered unsafe, so a temporary light would be shown on a wooden tower while new towers and dwellings were built.[^10]

LighthouseFriends, citing a report by the keeper's great-granddaughter, describes the morning of the quake. Keeper Francisco Cavallieri was living at the station with his wife and three children. According to that account, his wife had a sense that something was wrong just minutes before the tremor. When he would not leave, she took the children inland, and he followed. Moments later the tidal wave reached the station.[^2] The quake shattered the tower, and a keeper later measured the flood at about fifteen feet (4.6 m) near the lighthouse.[^2] A strong aftershock followed on 24 October, and the light was shown from a temporary wooden structure until the new station could be built.[^2]

The Commissioner's 1919 report says that lighthouse property "was not seriously damaged" in the earthquake, except at Point Borinquen and Point Jiguero, which it calls "practically wrecked."[^8]

## After the keepers

The new station got its own road. LighthouseFriends says a right of way was bought in 1924, and that the road built on it was about 2,000 feet (610 m) long.[^2]

Earthquakes kept affecting the light. On 23 May 1922 a severe earthquake broke the mantle of its incandescent-oil-vapor lamp.[^2] In 1925 an earthquake put the light out, and the keepers had it back in operation in about twelve minutes. In 1927 the lamp mantle broke, and the light was promptly restored.[^2]

The lens was replaced in 1947. The nomination says that "In 1947 the light was changed for an airway beacon for surface and air navigation."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the lantern room was removed and a thirty-six-inch double-headed beacon replaced the Fresnel lens.[^2]

The light's automation came after a false start. LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard tried to automate it, but the relays could not carry the load, so manual operation continued.[^2] When the officer in charge left in December 1977, switching of the light passed to the nearby air station.[^2]

## Today

The National Register nomination classifies the station as "Occupied. Restricted access."[^1] The station is Coast Guard property.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the grounds and tower are closed to visitors, that the light is still controlled by Air Station Borinquen, that the dwelling is used as guest housing, and that the beacon is now automated.[^2] Visitors should stay off the grounds.
