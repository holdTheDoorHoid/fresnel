Isle Royale Light stands on Menagerie Island, at the entrance to Siskiwit Bay on the south shore of Isle Royale, in Lake Superior.[^1] It was built in 1875 to light the passage into the bay, which the National Park Service describes as hazardous to approach.[^1] It had two keepers until May 1914, when the light was changed to automatic acetylene, and today it can be reached only by private boat.[^1][^5]

## The place

Copper mining came back to Isle Royale in 1873, and ships returned to its rocky harbors.[^1] The Island Mine, about two miles northwest of the head of Siskiwit Bay on the island's southwest shore, was opened in the 1873 boom, and mining interests asked for a lighthouse to help mark the way to a long pier in the bay.[^7] The Park Service says the light was sited on Menagerie Island "at the entrance to Siskiwit Bay, where the profitable Island Mine Company was located," and that it also warned Lake Superior traffic of the existence of the island's south shore.[^1]

The island had a light before this one.[^7] LighthouseFriends says Rock Harbor Light, near the east end, was built in 1855 and was lit in two spells, from 1856 to 1859 and from 1874 to 1879.[^7] The Park Service calls the Menagerie light "the island's second lighthouse."[^1]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends reports that Congress appropriated $20,000 for a lighthouse on Isle Royale on 3 March 1873.[^7] Construction waited for the opening of navigation in 1875 while the site was debated, and Menagerie Island was chosen.[^7]

The tower is an octagon of red sandstone, double-walled and 61 feet (19 m) high.[^1] The Park Service gives its focal plane as 75 feet (23 m) above the mean low water of Lake Superior, and says the lantern is of the fourth order.[^1] The original fourth-order Fresnel lens and later light systems have since been replaced.[^1] The outer wall was whitewashed, which LighthouseFriends says made a more distinctive daymark.[^1][^7]

LighthouseFriends dates the first light to 20 September 1875, with William Stevens as head keeper and his wife, Mary Ann Stevens, as first assistant.[^7] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster lists the same two people as the first keepers.[^2]

## The keepers

The Society's roster has eighteen lines for the station, from 1875 to 1914.[^2] William Stevens was head keeper from 1875 to 1878, and Mary Ann Stevens was first assistant in the same years.[^2] The sources do not say where either of them came from.[^2]

In 1878 Stevens swapped stations with John H. Malone, who had been keeper at the Portage Lake Ship Canal light.[^7] Malone was head keeper at Isle Royale from 1878 to 1910, the longest tenure on the roster.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he married Julia Shea on 13 July 1880; she was nineteen.[^7] The Park Service says he and his wife brought up to 11 children on the island.[^1] LighthouseFriends counts a dozen children born during their time there, one of whom died at birth.[^7]

Supply deliveries were infrequent, so the family supplemented them: the Park Service says they kept cows, took seagull eggs, fished, snared hares and kept a shallow garden on the rock.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that the island's residents collected over a thousand eggs in May 1886, during the nesting season.[^7] The Park Service says several of the family learned to play musical instruments, and that the Malones kept track of bird species for the Audubon Society.[^1]

The 1925 Lighthouse Service Bulletin tells of a keeper who is not named.[^4] He raised a family of 12 children at Isle Royal Lighthouse, "on a lonely rock on the north side of Lake Superior."[^4] The Bulletin says he had helped build the light and applied for the post as a bachelor.[^4] The superintendent told him the post needed a married man, and he married on the mainland without delay.[^4] LighthouseFriends ties the story to Malone, who married in 1880, but its own account has Malone arriving in 1878, after the light was built.[^7] The Bulletin does not name the keeper, so the identification rests on LighthouseFriends alone.[^7][^4]

The Bulletin's obituary says Malone entered the service in 1877, retired in 1918 after 41 years, and died on 8 December 1928.[^6] It gives his years at Isle Royale as 1878 to 1910.[^6] LighthouseFriends says he left in 1910 for the Pipe Island light on the St. Marys River.[^7]

When Malone left in 1910, his son John A. Malone, an assistant there since 1900, became head keeper and served until 1912.[^1][^2] Klaas (Klass) L. Hamringa was head keeper from 1912 to 1913, and Lee E. Benton was listed for 1914.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Malones kept a station log, and LighthouseFriends quotes several entries.[^7] For 16 October 1880 the keeper recorded hail, snow, rain and a tempest, and the loss of a boat, the boathouse ways and the dock.[^7] He wrote: "I don't believe a cat could get from the dwelling to the boat house."[^7]

The steamer Algoma, bound for Port Arthur, passed the Sault canal at noon on Friday, 6 November 1885, and that night she was caught by a gale and heavy snow halfway across Lake Superior.[^8] At about 4:40 a.m. on 7 November she struck the rocks of Mott Island, near the east end of Isle Royale, and less than two hours later she broke in two.[^8][^7] The wreck was not at the station, but the keeper logged it.[^7] Malone noted the Algoma in the station log before closing the light for the 1885 season.[^7] In June 1886 he recorded four of the Algoma's life preservers on the beach abreast the station.[^7]

The sources disagree on the toll. An early report in the Springfield weekly Republican of 13 November 1885 said, "It is reported that 100 lives were lost."[^9] A Saginaw paper's headline of 11 November, as the Park Service reprints it, reads "Forty-Eight Persons Drowned."[^8] LighthouseFriends gives forty-six dead and fourteen survivors, picked up by the steamer Athabasca.[^7]

The 1929 Bulletin describes an earlier outage, which it does not date.[^5] The light was out for some days until the keeper from Grand Marais, Minnesota, reached the island by commercial steamer, the quickest help the circumstances allowed.[^5]

## After the keepers

The light was changed to automatic acetylene in May 1914, and the keepers' service ended with it.[^5] LighthouseFriends dates the change to 29 May 1914.[^7] The 1914 Bulletin lists the light among those changed from a fixed to a flashing character, and from oil to acetylene.[^3]

The Park Service gives two other years for automation: 1913 in its summary and 1939 in its history.[^1] The 1929 Bulletin and LighthouseFriends both give 1914.[^5][^7]

The Bulletin ties the stand-by light to that outage.[^5] To guard against a repeat, stand-by acetylene equipment was installed in October 1928.[^5] The main gas supply was six 180-cubic-foot tanks in the base of the tower, with two spare tanks in a small shelter outside.[^5] The stand-by light was a burner in a 375-millimeter cut-glass lantern.[^5] LighthouseFriends dates a battery lighting system to 1941 and a solar light to 1993.[^7]

## Today

The present light is a 12-volt solar-powered acrylic optic.[^1] The Park Service says the station can be reached only by private boat.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard owns the light, that the grounds are open and that the tower is closed.[^7]

LighthouseFriends says Isle Royale and about 450 smaller islands became a national park in 1940.[^7] The Park Service installed a new roof on the keeper's dwelling in 1986, and reports that the kitchen floor has collapsed, though the other interior finishes have very high integrity.[^1]
