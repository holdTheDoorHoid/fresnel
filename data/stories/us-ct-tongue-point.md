Tongue Point Light is a black cast-iron tower, 31 feet (9.4 m) high, on the western side of the entrance to Bridgeport Harbor, Connecticut.[^3] Its nickname, "The Bug," perhaps refers to its short profile.[^1] It was built on a breakwater in 1894 and 1895, moved to a new foundation in 1919, and in January 2020 was still an active aid to navigation.[^1][^10]

## The place

The light stands at the eastern tip of Tongue, or Wells, Point, a point of land that extends into the harbor from the western shore.[^1] Bridgeport's harbor development lagged behind that of neighboring cities with better natural harbors until the 1840s.[^1] "With the advent of the railroad era," Bridgeport businessmen won federal contracts to dredge a ship channel and build breakwaters.[^1] In 1891 a breakwater created a harbor of refuge there.[^1]

The breakwater's end was a hazard at night. Lighthouse Friends says the rocky mass became a dangerous obstacle once the sun went down.[^3] The Lighthouse Board asked for a beacon light on the breakwater, and Congress appropriated $2,000 on 15 February 1893.[^3][^4] The Board's 1893 report says it had been supposed that the breakwater would serve as a foundation: "This, as it now appears, is impracticable."[^4]

## Building the light

A temporary light went onto the breakwater first, on a cylindrical pier topped by a wooden pyramid, with a staff and cask above that. Its focal plane was 24 feet (7.3 m) above the water, and it was placed in operation on 31 January 1894.[^3] The Board's list of lights established that year includes it.[^5]

The permanent tower needed a special foundation. Congress added $2,500 on 18 August 1894 to build that foundation.[^4][^6]

The tower was completed. About 1 March 1895 the temporary lantern was removed and a lens lantern was set up in the tower.[^7] The nomination says Lieutenant Colonel D. P. Heap of the Lighthouse Board supervised the installation of a 31-foot (9.4 m) conical cast-iron tower. That design was a standard one for the Board from 1873 to about 1910.[^1] Two stories hold storage below and a watchroom above, with no living quarters.[^1]

Sources disagree on the order of the first lens. The nomination describes a sixth-order lens lantern established in 1895.[^1] Its 1989 amendment calls the original lantern of 1894 fourth order.[^1]

The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1897 planned a fog signal, with sleeping quarters for a keeper, at the inner end of the east breakwater, at a cost of $1,200.[^8] In 1897 Congress appropriated $1,200 for an electrically operated gong fog signal.[^1] The gong was discontinued for repair on 30 March 1898. The nomination says a 160-pound fog bell took its place in March 1899, struck every eight seconds. Lighthouse Friends gives the 1899 bell as roughly 100 pounds, struck every eight seconds, and describes a bell tried in 1898 that was "inefficient and unreliable."[^3]

In 1919 the breakwater was demolished as a hazard to shipping. The tower was moved to a new reinforced-concrete foundation, which the nomination puts 275 feet closer to shore. Lighthouse Friends says it was moved back roughly 300 feet.[^1][^3]

## The keepers

S. Adolphus McNeil kept the light from 1895 to 1904.[^2] Lighthouse Friends calls him Stephen A. McNeil. It says he first tended both this light and the Bridgeport Harbor light. As his health failed, he paid an assistant out of his own wages to tend the harbor light and kept this one himself. In 1901 the Lighthouse Board learned of the arrangement. It hired another keeper for the harbor light and left McNeil at the breakwater.[^3]

Keeping this light meant a daily trip out along the breakwater.[^3] A plank walk was installed in 1900.[^1][^3] The nomination says a small-boat wharf was built because the keeper had to row out to do his daily tasks.[^1] In 1906 the Board's annual report recorded that the keeper had put up a small shanty at his own expense, in which he slept nightly "so as to start the fog signal at short notice."[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates the shanty to 1902, four years earlier.[^3] A keeper's dwelling and garden was proposed in 1904, and $5,000 was requested for it, but the dwelling was never built.[^1][^3] Lighthouse Friends dates the request to 1905.[^3]

McNeil died in 1904.[^3] His wife, Flora, took over his duties, and Lighthouse Friends says she became Bridgeport's only female keeper.[^3] The archive lists her as principal keeper from 1904 to 1919.[^2] In May 1914 a Norwich newspaper reported that she was receiving $48 a month as keeper, succeeding her husband.[^12]

In a 1913 newspaper article, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, Flora McNeil described the winter trips out on the plank. After rain and a freeze the plank was "a glare of ice," and in snowstorms it was "scarcely possible to keep your footing."[^3]

The archive's only later name is Frank Evans, lamplighter from 1939 to 1948.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A storm on 24 October 1917 damaged the breakwater light, along with several other lights in the Third District.[^9] No wreck or rescue is recorded at this light in the sources cited here.

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the light was automated in 1954.[^3] In 1967 the Coast Guard proposed discontinuing it and taking down the tower. Boat owners and yacht clubs protested, and the plan was dropped.[^3][^1]

The nomination's 1989 amendment records that the fog bell had been removed by then.[^1] The classic Fresnel lens gave way to a 155 mm modern optic in 1988, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^3] The 1989 amendment instead describes a plastic Fresnel-type lens.[^1]

The light was listed on the National Register on 29 May 1990.[^1] In 2004 the light was offered for transfer under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, and no applications came in.[^3] In March 2007 the offer was withdrawn, over fears that a privately owned light beside a power plant would be a security risk.[^3] In July 2019 the Coast Guard, under the 2000 lighthouse preservation act, determined the light was "excess to (its) needs" and offered it. In January 2020 the city announced an application for ownership.[^10][^11]

## Today

Lighthouse Friends says the Coast Guard owns the light and that the tower is closed.[^3] The archive's place record gives the light as not an active aid to navigation, which disagrees with the January 2020 reports.[^2][^10] Lighthouse Friends describes a green light flashing every four seconds.[^3] The Coast Guard will continue to maintain it as an active aid even if the city is granted ownership.[^10]

The land beneath the light is owned by PSEG. PSEG also owns the gas-fired power plant nearby, and the Connecticut Post reports that this complicates public access.[^10]

The newest source used for this story is from January 2020. It describes Bridgeport's application and does not say who holds the light now.[^10]
