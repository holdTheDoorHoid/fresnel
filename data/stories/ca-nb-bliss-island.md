Bliss Island Light stands on the southwest point of Bliss Island, in the Bay of Fundy, where it marks the entrance to Bliss Harbour.[^1][^2] The light was first lit on 1 December 1871.[^1][^4] The light shown today is a red flash from a small skeleton tower beside a concrete tower of 1964 that Rowlett says has been inactive since 2016.[^2][^3]

## The place

LighthouseFriends tells how Samuel Bliss, a merchant of Greenfield, Massachusetts, was banished under the Proscription Act of 1778 for his loyalty to the Crown. After service with the British army he was rewarded in 1784 with land at L'Etang and later with a large nearby island that is still known as Bliss Island.[^1]

LighthouseFriends argues that Bliss Island was a natural site for a second light in the Passamaquoddy approaches. The Head Harbour light on Campobello Island, established in 1828, marked the southern side of the northern entrance to Passamaquoddy Bay. Bliss Island lies about 8 km (5 miles) to the northeast, with open water between, and a light there could also mark the anchorages of Bliss Harbour and L'Etang Harbour.[^1]

## Building the light

The first tower was square and wooden, painted white. Its fixed red light came from five flat-wick lamps with reflectors behind them.[^1][^4] Jarvis Clarke was its keeper at $200 a year.[^1]

In 1872 the station received a signal gun, which LighthouseFriends says had been used at Point Lepreau, to be fired in fog. A fog trumpet was also sounded for steamers' whistles.[^1] A letter of November 1872 that LighthouseFriends quotes says the gun "has been promptly fired" and the trumpet sounded in foggy weather, to the benefit of a Saint John steamer.[^1]

The Marine Department paid $250 in 1874 for the land under the light and an adjacent lot. In 1876 James Donohue was paid $745 to build a dwelling near the tower, and a bridge crossed the gully between the two.[^1]

The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1900 says the lighthouse "has been painted inside and out, this year."[^5]

The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1901 says the light "will be changed on January 15, 1902, from fixed red to fixed white".[^6] That is a plan in the future tense, so the report does not show that the change was made on that date. LighthouseFriends and the Chance Heritage Trust both give January 1901 for the change.[^1][^4] The sources disagree on the date, and this story does not choose between them.

In 1903 the tower was raised and the wooden lantern replaced by an octagonal iron lantern. The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1903 gives the height from base to vane as 40 feet (about 12 m). It says the light became an occulting white light, ten seconds on and five off, from a fifth-order dioptric lens. In its words, "These improvements, which were made under the direction of our local agent, cost $1,248.43."[^7]

The present concrete building, a one-storey fog-alarm house with a square tower rising from one corner, dates from 1964 according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] Rowlett gives the concrete tower as 12 m (38 ft).[^3] The Chance Heritage Trust suspects the switch from wood to concrete came in 1903, but says it could not confirm the date.[^4]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends names the keepers in order. Jarvis Clarke kept the light until July 1880, when he died. In 1878 he had asked the Department for medicine, because members of his family were "not very healthy".[^1]

Hugh Maloney followed Clarke and kept the light to 1883. Cornelius McNichol kept it to 1891, then Burton McNichol to 1897, then Joseph E. McGee to 1900.[^1] The Department's keeper table for 1900 appears to list a McLeod against Bliss Island. The names are read from separate columns of the OCR text, so that match is by position.[^8] LighthouseFriends names James H. McLeod as keeper from 1900 to 1919.[^1]

After McLeod, LighthouseFriends names Mrs. Ester McLeod for 1920, A. Henderson from 1921 to 1924, and F. K. Spinney from 1925 to at least 1937. It names Frederick Frye as the last keeper it lists, to 1964, without a start date.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the year ended 31 March 1924 the Department's report says the masts of the schooner Centenial, which were a menace to navigation, were removed at Bliss Island by the CGS Laurentian.[^9]

## After the keepers

The sources do not say when the light was automated. Rowlett says the concrete tower has been inactive since 2016, and that the active light, a red flash every four seconds, is on a 7.5 m (25 ft) square skeleton tower beside it.[^3] The Coast Guard list describes the same skeleton tower, with a focal height of 12.5 m (41 ft) and a range of 7 nautical miles.[^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light.[^1] The island lies well offshore and can only be reached by boat.[^1][^4] LighthouseFriends says the light is best seen from the water, and gives the grounds as open with the tower closed.[^1] Rowlett says the site is open and the tower closed.[^3] The Coast Guard list gives the active light as a red flash every four seconds, year round.[^2]
