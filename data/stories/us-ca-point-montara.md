Point Montara began as a foggy-weather horn, not a light: a steam whistle on a 70-foot (21 m) bluff south of San Francisco's Golden Gate, put to work in 1875 to warn steamers off a reef-strewn stretch of the San Mateo County coast.[^1] The short steel tower that stands there today was not built for it: LighthouseFriends says the tower first served at Mayo Beach on Cape Cod before it was moved west and erected here in 1928.[^2][^1]

## The place

Point Montara is the seaward end of a spur running out from Montara Mountain. The National Register nomination describes cliffs about 60 feet high, shoreline outcroppings "waiting to snag the unwary vessel," and a coast where mariners were told to take extreme care inside the 30-fathom line.[^1] The tower stands on the bluff in San Mateo County, California.[^1]

The nomination names two losses that pushed Congress to act: the steamer *Colorado*, stranded on an offshore ledge in 1868, and the ship *Acuelo*, gutted below Montara Mountain in November 1872 with its coal, iron and grain.[^1] LighthouseFriends tells the same two stories with more detail and spells the second ship *Aculeo*. It gives the *Colorado*'s grounding as 9 November 1868 and says she floated free with her passengers and mail, after which the ledge, earlier called Uncle Sam, was renamed Colorado Reef. It dates the *Aculeo*'s loss to 17 October 1872, which differs from the nomination's "November".[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends adds that a signal here would roughly halve the gap between the existing fog signals at Pigeon Point and Point Bonita.[^2]

## Building the light

According to LighthouseFriends, Congress appropriated $15,000 for a fog signal in March 1873, and work began in October 1874.[^2] The two-story keeper's dwelling, in the Victorian Gothic style, was finished the next February, and the 12-inch steam whistle went into service on 1 March 1875.[^2] The nomination agrees that the station was founded in 1875, as a fog-signal station only, and it calls the first fog-signal building, dated 1874, "dilapidated" by the time it was replaced.[^1] The duplex dwelling of 1875 is still standing.[^1]

For its first 25 years there was no light at all. The Light-House Board's report lists a red "lens-lantern" light shown at the station from 26 November 1900, and describes it as set on a post 300 feet west by south of the signal house.[^4][^5] A new fog-signal building went up in 1902, and the nomination describes it as a standard design for the California coast, with a central chamber for the machinery and a watch room and tack room in the wings.[^1]

The light was improved on 15 June 1912, when a fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lantern, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] LighthouseFriends adds that the light's characteristic changed that year from a fixed red light to a white flash, and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1912 lists Point Montara among the lights whose illuminant was changed.[^2][^6] Sources disagree on what the lens sat in: the nomination says a small wood-frame structure, LighthouseFriends a white pyramidal skeletal iron tower.[^1][^2]

The present tower dates from 1928. The nomination calls it the only bolted, metal-sided lighthouse in California, made of steel panels that could be prefabricated far away and assembled quickly on site.[^1] Its history was uncertain until recently. The National Archives reports that researcher Colleen MacNeney found correspondence showing that the tower had stood at Mayo Beach in Wellfleet, Massachusetts, and travelled to Yerba Buena Island in San Francisco Bay before being erected at Montara, all between 1922 and 1928.[^7] LighthouseFriends gives the Mayo Beach service as 1881 to 1922, and says the tower went west in 1925.[^2] The Hostelling International account calls it cast iron, whereas the nomination says steel.[^8][^1]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists 17 principal keepers from Henry T. Holbrook (1875 to 1878) to Ottley W. Austin, documented from 1967 to at least 1969.[^3] Two long terms stand out: Henry Hall served as principal keeper from 1895 to 1908, and Patrick J. Dempsey held the post from 1908 to 1928, after serving as first assistant from 1902.[^3]

Herbert H. Luff first appears as first assistant keeper from 1892 to 1901. The archive records him back as principal keeper from 1928 to 1933, and LighthouseFriends says he held head-keeper posts at Ano Nuevo, Point Loma, Yerba Buena and Punta Gorda in the years between.[^3][^2]

Staffing was thin by about 1922. In an estimate for improvements, a Commissioner of Lighthouses report described Point Montara as "one of the most important in the approach to San Francisco Harbor," with dense fogs through the summer, "often lasting several days at a time," and only two keepers, who shared one two-story dwelling. It asked for a third keeper and a second set of quarters.[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Light-House Board's reports give the fog signal's work in hours. In its report for 1893, the twin 12-inch steam whistles ran about 531 hours and burned about 63 cords of wood.[^10] By the report of 1900 the fuel was counted in coal: the whistles ran about 1,035 hours and used about 93 tons.[^11]

A report from 1896 or 1897 noted that the sounding board behind the whistles was too small and "much of the sound passing landward over it." A new one, 12 feet high, was fastened over three-quarters of the length of the roof.[^12] In 1900 the Board bought a new hydraulic ram to improve the water supply, and rebuilt the bridge on the government road to the station.[^13] The report for 1901 records that the reservation fence, destroyed by storm, was rebuilt.[^5]

The fog signal did not end the wrecks. LighthouseFriends reports that, four years to the day after the *Aculeo*, the Welsh ship *Rydal Hall* struck Frenchman's Reef in fog, that 21 of her 30 crew survived and that she lay on the rocks for almost a month, and that the captain of the schooner *Ada May* mistook the Montara signal for the one at Point Bonita and ran ashore. These accounts rest on that single source.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1919 the Lighthouse Service replaced the old steam boilers, engines and 12-inch whistles with gas-engine compressors and an air diaphone that began sounding on 24 January 1919. The light was changed from incandescent oil vapor to electric on 14 March, raising its candlepower from 2,900 to 25,000.[^14] The nomination notes a duplex for assistant keepers built in 1961 and two concrete bunkers left from a small Naval operation south of the station in the Second World War.[^1]

The nomination says the Fresnel lens was replaced by an automated beacon in 1970, and LighthouseFriends also says the light was automated that year.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends dates the loss of the Fresnel lens, for a ten-inch rotating aerobeacon, to 1962, so the sources disagree.[^2] The lens is now with the San Mateo County Historical Society, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2]

In the 1970s California planned youth hostels at disused light stations, and Point Montara was among them. LighthouseFriends says the restoration drew on the state, American Youth Hostels staff and volunteers, the California Coastal Conservancy and local businesses, and the hostel opened on 15 July 1980.[^2] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 3 September 1991, with the period of significance later amended to 1875 to 1940.[^1]

## Today

The tower is Coast Guard property and is closed to visitors, while California State Parks manages the grounds, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The keeper's quarters house the staff of a Hostelling International hostel, and the former fog-signal building contains guest rooms and a common room.[^8][^2] Hostelling International offers rooms there, and LighthouseFriends says the hostel asks visitors to stop by its office before visiting the grounds.[^15][^2] The light is still an active aid to navigation.[^3]
