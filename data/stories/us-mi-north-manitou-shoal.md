North Manitou Shoal Light Station, known locally as "The Crib", is a square steel lighthouse set on a concrete-and-stone crib in Lake Michigan, roughly midway between the southern end of North Manitou Island and the Leelanau County mainland.[^1] It was a Public Works Administration project, finished in 1935, and it kept a Lighthouse Service and then Coast Guard crew until 1980.[^1][^2]

## The place

The Manitou Passage is the channel between the Manitou Islands and the mainland shore. In 1909, the Lighthouse Board noted that a shoal had developed southeast of North Manitou Island in recent years, and asked for a lightship on its easterly end to mark the six-mile-wide channel between Dimmick's Point and Pyramid Point on the mainland.[^1] The lightship that had been used at White Shoal was moved to North Manitou Shoal when White Shoal Lighthouse was finished in 1910.[^1] Three lightships served the station: LV-56 from 1910 to 1926, LV-89 from 1927 to 1933, and LV-103 from 1934 to 1935.[^1]

The lightship was often forced off station by ice in early spring and late fall. In 1923 the Lighthouse Service proposed replacing it with a fixed station on a crib, which would also let the fog signal on North Manitou Island be discontinued.[^1] The island light on Dimmick's Point had been lit in 1898, and its fog signal had begun running in November 1896.[^1] Glenn Furst, whose stepfather Ernest G. Hutzler was made head keeper of the island light in 1919, later wrote in a memoir about the fog months: "I remember one spring when the fog signal never stopped sounding for more than twenty days."[^1]

## Building the light

Funds came from the Public Works Administration, and construction began in the early fall of 1933.[^1] The Lighthouse Service's own description, as quoted by LighthouseFriends, says the crib stands in 22 feet (6.7 m) of water on a bottom of hard sand and coarse stone. The crib is 65 feet (19.8 m) square and 22 feet (6.7 m) deep and filled with stone, and it is enclosed by steel sheet piling driven 24 feet (7.3 m) into the lake bottom.[^1] A hollow concrete pier rises 20 feet (6.1 m) above the water and holds the heating plant, coal and oil storage and laundry.[^1] Above it sits a two-storey steel building 37 feet (11.3 m) square, topped by a three-storey tower.[^1] The same description puts the focal plane at 79 feet (24 m) above the water, gives the station's cost as $152,186, and says the second floor held a living and watch room, a kitchen and four bedrooms.[^1]

Power came from diesel generators with storage batteries, and two horns faced up and down the channel, with a radiobeacon synchronised to the sound signal.[^1] The Lighthouse Service intended the station to replace both the lightship and the island light.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported it the first of five new Great Lakes offshore stations to be finished, put in operation on May 1, 1935.[^3]

The records disagree about the optic. LighthouseFriends, quoting the Lighthouse Service, describes a third-order lantern holding a four-panel, 36-inch revolving lens with a 1,000-watt lamp.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society archive lists the original optic as fourth order, installed in 1935.[^2]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society archive, drawing on the National Archives' keeper registers, names John A. Renehan as the first principal keeper, from 1935 to at least 1940, with John C. McDonald as first assistant (1935 to at least 1942) and Jerry P. Conley as second assistant (1935 to 1942).[^2] Michael Nawrocki is listed as principal keeper in 1943 and 1944.[^2] Melvin N. Nelson's entry ends in 1955, and George T. Gauthier is listed from 1958 to 1967.[^2] George S. Machulis appears as principal keeper in the archive ("at least 1958") and in LighthouseFriends, which dates his term to before 1958; the sources do not settle it.[^1][^2] Later principal keepers were Joseph F. Kraynak, Wesley E. Davis, Charles O. Russell and, from 1970, Terry G. Harring.[^2]

From about 1960 the archive lists dozens of Coast Guard crew members, most for a year or less.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the station was typically manned by three people, each serving two weeks followed by a week off.[^1] To pass the time, it says, they watched television, read, played board games and chatted by radio with passing ship captains; one coastguardsman practised rappelling from the lantern gallery to the concrete deck.[^1]

## After the keepers

The station was automated in 1980, when it was one of just a handful of staffed lights on the Great Lakes and, LighthouseFriends says, the last offshore light to lose its crews.[^1][^2] The four-sided lens is now on display at the Cannery Boat Museum in Glen Haven, part of Sleeping Bear Dunes National Lakeshore.[^1] In December 2000 citizens from Glen Arbor, Maple City and Walled Lake met to form a preservation society, which gave up after concluding that restoration would be very costly.[^1] The light was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2005.[^4]

In May 2015 the Coast Guard declared the lighthouse excess, and it was offered to eligible organisations under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000. No new custodian was found, and the General Services Administration ran an online auction that began on July 15, 2016, with an opening bid of $25,000.[^1] The auction ended on September 27, 2016, with a high bid of $73,000 from North Manitou Light Keepers, Inc.[^1] The nonprofit says the structure had stood vacant and uncared for until then.[^4] LighthouseFriends reports that the group spent roughly two million dollars on rehabilitation and opened the restored structure for its first public tour on June 19, 2021.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is privately owned by North Manitou Light Keepers, a nonprofit, and remains an active aid to navigation with automated solar-powered equipment maintained by the Coast Guard.[^4] It stands eight miles off Leland and cannot be reached except by boat. The Keepers run ticketed, guided tours from Leland Harbor; the 2027 schedule had not yet been published when this was written, and private tours can be arranged.[^5] Visitors board from a moving boat through a water-level door, and there are six flights of stairs plus an inclined ladder to the lens room. Weather can cancel a landing, in which case guests may view the Crib from the boat.[^5] Do not try to land on the structure outside a tour.
