South Pass Range Rear is the tall iron tower on Gordon's Island, at the mouth of the Mississippi River in Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana. It is the rear light of the range that guided ships into South Pass.[^1][^2] The first light on the island was lit in 1832, and the present tower has had no resident keepers since 1971.[^2]

## The place

South Pass is one of the entrances from the Gulf into the Mississippi, where the river water mixes with the water of the gulf.[^2] In the 1870s Port Eads was a construction camp, where James Eads put his experimental jetties in place. The jetties scoured the entrance channel to a depth of thirty feet (9 m).[^2] The island is named after the customs collector at New Orleans.[^2]

The range came later. In 1886 a beacon on four wooden piles was set in the river to line up with the tower, so that ships could pass between the mud lumps about 1,000 feet (300 m) outside the jetties.[^2] The Board's report for 1894 calls the rear mark the "South Pass rear beacon."[^3]

## Building the light

On 3 March 1831 Congress appropriated $40,000 for lighthouses at South Pass and Southwest Pass, and Winslow Lewis was awarded a $19,150 contract to build them.[^2] Lewis had planned to drive piles into the soft ground, but Martin Gordon persuaded him to float the tower on a cross-hatched grid of squared timbers.[^2] Keeper Henry Heistand first lit the lamps on 15 May 1832.[^2] The Society's record gives 1831 as the year the station was established.[^1]

In 1839 logs carried by the current struck the keeper's dwelling and knocked it off its foundation. In 1841 a storm erased all signs of the station.[^2] The replacement was an octagonal wooden tower, built so that it could be moved if erosion threatened. It was completed in June 1842, and within five years it had decayed so badly that another was needed.[^2] The third tower, a wooden octagon 54 feet (16 m) high with a dwelling built into its base, went up in 1848, and a Fresnel lens was installed in 1859.[^2]

Confederate forces removed the lens and oil soon after the Civil War began. After Union forces took the station, a third-order revolving Fresnel lens returned to service in the fall of 1862.[^2]

By 1868 the Board wanted a first-order light at South Pass. It described the wooden tower, surrounded by swamp cane, as "readily set on fire by malicious or careless persons," in the words LighthouseFriends quotes from its justification.[^2]

Congress provided $50,000 on 3 March 1879 for a first-order lighthouse at South Pass.[^2] The new tower was a hexagonal iron structure first intended for Trinity Shoal, with an iron-clad dwelling in its base. It went into operation on 25 August 1881, and the old wooden tower was torn down afterwards.[^2] The Society's record gives the light's height as 108 feet (33 m) above mean high water.[^1]

## The keepers

The Society's roster lists 162 stints at the station, from 1832 to 1945.[^1] Henry Heistand was the first head keeper, from 1832 to 1835, and William R. Knight followed until 1843.[^1] Richard A. Fitzgerald served as head keeper from 1881 to 1905, the longest head-keeper term on the roster.[^1]

The roster also lists three assistants: Louisa Ripps, first assistant from 1868 to 1869; Margaret Mitchell, second assistant from 1871 to 1873; and Abbie Kipp, first assistant from 1875 to 1879.[^1] The archive does not say whether any of them was a woman, or whether they were related to the head keepers who share their surnames. It lists one first assistant only as Narcissa, from 1853. The archive gives no more about any of them.

In 1900 the Board reported that the tower's color confused vessels trying to make the passes, because it was like the tower at Southwest Pass. The tower was "sealed, scraped, and painted white."[^4] Leonard F. Edgecombe was head keeper from 1910 to 1926.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The storm of 1 October 1893 damaged the rear beacon, and the Board's report for the year to June 1894 records that the station was put into repair.[^3]

On 3 August 1913 a squall brought lightning down on the oil house at the South Pass light station, at 12:30 in the afternoon. The wooden cases around the mineral-oil cans caught fire, but the fire was put out before the oil ignited.[^5]

In October 1916, during a hurricane, the lighthouse sheltered seventy-five people in its rooms through the night.[^6]

The Bulletin printed a list of commendations for the hurricane of 13 to 14 September 1919. It named Leonard F. Edgecombe, keeper, and S. Coludrovich, assistant keeper, of the South Pass Range Rear Light Station, for "rendering assistance to and caring for a large number of residents in the vicinity of the respective stations, who took refuge in the station dwellings."[^7]

## After the keepers

In 1894 the Board placed a lightship about a mile off the jetties to help mariners find the pass. That was a separate station, which served until 1912. Another lightship, LV-102, was anchored off the pass from 1918 until that station was discontinued in 1933.[^2]

The Bulletin's 1921 issue lists a new dwelling completed at the South Pass Range Rear Light Station.[^8] LighthouseFriends says a new six-room dwelling was completed in 1921 for the keeper of the jetty lights, near the fog-signal building.[^2]

About 1945 the first-order lens was replaced by a DCB-224 optic.[^2] The tower lost its resident keepers in 1971.[^2]

## Today

The tower remains an active aid to navigation, owned by the Coast Guard, and it is not open to the public.[^2][^1] No visiting arrangements are recorded.
