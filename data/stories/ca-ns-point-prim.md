Prim Point, also written Point Prim, is a rocky headland on the west side of Digby Gut, the narrow gap through which the Annapolis Basin empties into the Bay of Fundy, in the Municipality of the District of Digby, Nova Scotia. A light has been shown here since 1804.[^1][^2][^6]

## The place

The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society, quoting the federal Sailing Directions, describes Digby Gut as a deep-water passage 0.4 mile wide between high, steep shores, and the Canadian Coast Guard's list of lights places Prim Point at the west side of the entrance to Annapolis Basin.[^6][^3] In 1801 a petition signed by Richard Hill and other inhabitants of Digby asked the House of Assembly of Nova Scotia for a lighthouse at the entrance. A committee recommended that the province provide up to £250 if the inhabitants of Annapolis County raised £100 themselves. The light was finally lit in 1804, after a further £77 was provided to finish it; it was the third lighthouse built by the province, after Sambro Island and Cape Roseway.[^1]

The traffic grew with the steamers. According to Lighthouse Friends, the steamship *Empress*, built in 1865, began regular runs between Saint John, New Brunswick, and Digby, and the route carried more passengers after train service reached Annapolis and Halifax in 1869.[^1]

## Building the light

The sources do not agree on how many towers the point has had. Lighthouse Friends counts the present one as the fourth, as does Tourism Nova Scotia; the Canadian Register of Historic Places calls it the third on the site.[^1][^7][^2] The sequence, as Lighthouse Friends gives it, runs as follows.

The first lighthouse lasted four years and burned in 1808, and no replacement came until 1817, when the province gave £400 and the county's inhabitants subscribed another £100. That second light was a white dwelling with red vertical stripes on its north and two adjoining sides, showing its light from a bay window at the north end.[^1] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society's list dates the early light differently, reporting an earlier light "destroyed by fire in 1817" and calling the 1817 building the first of three.[^6]

That building burned too. The Department of Marine and Fisheries reported, in a passage Lighthouse Friends reproduces, that on the night of 16 March "the lighthouse at Point Prim, at the entrance to Digby Gut, was entirely destroyed by fire", supposed to have started in the small, confined lantern.[^1] Lighthouse Friends places the report in 1873, and the Preservation Society's list dates the fire 16 March 1873.[^6] A temporary light was shown from a small lantern on top of the engine house, a few yards from the old site.[^1]

A square wooden tower with sloping sides, built into the north end of a one-and-a-half-storey dwelling, replaced it in 1874 at a cost of $3,410.45. The lantern was a twelve-sided iron room holding thirteen lamps with reflectors in two tiers.[^1]

## The keepers

Two families kept the light for most of the years from 1817 to 1963. Richard Bragg, who came from England with his family, was appointed to the 1817 lighthouse and served until his death in 1839; his son Frederick then took charge and stayed until 1871.[^1] (The Preservation Society's list names a "Bray, Frederick" with a date of 1857 and no more, probably the same man spelled differently, though neither source says so.[^6])

Captain William Ellis became keeper in March 1875. The Department's list of keepers for the year ended March 1910, read by column position, shows W. E. Ellis at "Pointe Prim Light, Fog Alarm, Digby" with the year 1875, so he was still in charge after thirty-five years.[^5] His son Leander succeeded him in 1912, and Leander's daughter Janet Wilson carried the family's run on until her husband, Frank Wilson, died in 1963.[^1] Carman Frankland came as keeper in June 1963, and Edward Murphy followed in 1964 and is listed to 1987.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

**The fog whistle.** A steam fog whistle began sounding at Prim Point on 29 December 1871, an eight-second blast each minute in the Bay of Fundy's frequent fogs.[^1] Five years later, at six in the evening of 19 June 1876, the smokestack through the fog-alarm building's roof caught fire and the whole building was destroyed. Only the boiler could be saved. An engine and pump meant for Cape Sable were diverted to Prim Point and running by 31 July, and a new engine house was in service in February 1877.[^1]

**Lightning.** Days after the fire, lightning struck the lighthouse twice. The Department's 1876 report, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, says it struck first on the west side of the tower, "tearing off the laths and plaster in the room below the lantern", and then at the south-east corner where dwelling joins tower, where it "burst off the lathing and plaster and destroyed some of the studding."[^1]

**The *Princess Louise*, 1883.** The new government steamship *Princess Louise* was being towed to Halifax by the *Newfield*. The Department's report, as transcribed on the Islands History website, says that at 10 p.m. on 2 December the wind "suddenly shifted to the east and rapidly backed into the north-east, with a blinding snow storm"; at 3:30 the next morning the tow line parted and the ship "drifted on the rocks near Point Prim, becoming a total wreck." Ten people were aboard and two were saved.[^4] A monument to the dead stands along the shore, with an inscription naming the officers of the *Newfield* as its donors.[^4]

**The *Westport*, 1910.** Keeper Ellis saw the steamer *Westport* entering the Gut with her cargo apparently on fire and telephoned for help. According to Lighthouse Friends the lifeboat *Daring* took off her nine passengers, and the Digby fire department helped put out the blaze.[^1] The Canadian Register also links the station with the wreck of the steamer *Agnes G. Donahue*, without giving details.[^2]

The fog signal itself was moved in 1889 closer to the cliff edge, because the lighthouse and high ground interrupted its sound.[^1] In its report for the year ended 31 March 1910, the Department of Marine and Fisheries recorded that a revolving catoptric light had replaced the fixed white light, with "the flashes attaining their greatest brilliancy every 15 seconds", burning petroleum vapour under an incandescent mantle.[^5] Lighthouse Friends dates the change to 1910.[^1] A new fog-alarm building with a diaphone plant followed in 1911, and the light and fog signal were electrified in 1930.[^1]

## After the keepers

On 13 May 1964 a bulldozer pushed the third lighthouse over the bluff, and a contractor demolished the other buildings and built the present tower.[^1] It is a square concrete tower rising from one corner of a one-storey flat-roofed fog-alarm building, and it keeps the red vertical stripes the station has long worn.[^1][^2] The Preservation Society's list says an assistant keeper drowned while tending lobster traps in 1976 and gives automation as "by 1987"; Lighthouse Friends lists Murphy as keeper until 1987.[^6][^1] The station record held by this atlas gives 1984 for automation, and the sources here do not settle the date.

The Department of Fisheries and Oceans handed the property to the Municipality of the District of Digby in June 2016, after an offer signed in September 2014. The lighthouse had been declared a heritage lighthouse in February 2015, and a plaque was unveiled on 14 September 2019.[^1] A non-profit, the Friends of Point Prim, was set up to look after it.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard's list of lights gives the light as isophase white, 6 seconds, 24.8 m above the water, with a range of 13 nautical miles, shown from a white rectangular tower with red vertical stripes, 13.9 m tall.[^3] The heritage statement notes that it is the only structure visible from the water along this stretch of coast, and that its fog alarm can be heard 9 km from the centre of Digby.[^2]

The municipality owns the site. Tourism Nova Scotia lists interpretive panels and a short walking trail at 1430 Lighthouse Road; the grounds are open, and the tower is not open to the public.[^7][^1]
