Grand Marais Light stands at the outer end of a breakwater that shelters the harbor at Grand Marais, on Minnesota's North Shore of Lake Superior, in Cook County.[^1] Congress appropriated money for the light in 1885.[^2] Since then a succession of towers has stood on the site, and sources still disagree about the year the lamp was first shown.[^1][^4]

## The place

The harbor came before the light. The harbor project was started in 1879, the breakwater was finished in 1883, and the harbor was dredged to sixteen feet (4.9 m), which LighthouseFriends calls the first harbor of refuge on the north shore.[^1] In 1878 a memorial from the Minnesota legislature asked for a light at Grand Marais. A Senate committee denied the request, citing a Lighthouse Board report that found the light inexpedient until the harbor work was finished.[^1]

## Building the light

On 3 March 1885 Congress passed an act that the Board's 1893 report quotes as providing money "for completing the construction of a light house at Grand Marais, Minn."[^2] The St. Paul Daily Globe's anniversary number, dated 25 December 1885, reported that "The government erected a lighthouse at Grand Marais during the past season."[^3]

LighthouseFriends gives the sequence. Materials came in July 1885 on the steam tender *Warrington*. The first tower was a square, pyramidal wooden tower with an octagonal lantern, finished on 21 August, and the light and fog bell began operating on 1 September 1885, with Joseph Mayhew as first keeper.[^1] The Cook County Historical Society dates the first lighthouse to 1884, one year earlier than the other sources.[^4]

The keeper's dwelling was the problem. In 1893 the Lighthouse Board reported that $8,409.17 of the 1885 appropriation was still in the Treasury, but accounting rulings barred its use for the purchase of a site and the erection of a keeper's dwelling, and the station had no government land on which to build. The Board asked Congress to allow the balance for that purpose.[^2] LighthouseFriends says Congress acted in 1895, a site was obtained that year, two bidders declined to sign contracts, and the house was finished in October 1896.[^1] The Cook County Historical Society says the house was built in 1896 by the federal government for the keeper and his family.[^4]

Later towers followed in order. A breakwater on the west side of the harbor entrance was completed in 1901, and a post carrying a lantern was placed at its end in 1902. A square, pyramidal metal tower reached the station in 1904, and its light was first shown on 27 August 1904.[^1] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' 1915 list of lights burning other than liquid oil names the station "Grand Marais West Breakwater, Minn."[^8] LighthouseFriends describes a steel tower moved sixty yards (55 m) east along the eastern breakwater on 1 November 1920, and says plans to return it to the end of that breakwater followed a War Department permit in October 1922.[^1] The two accounts of which breakwater the light stood on are not reconciled here.

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists four principal keepers.[^5] Joseph E. Mayhew is listed from 1886 to 1902, though the archive prints two lines for 1886. LighthouseFriends counts him as first keeper from the 1885 lighting.[^1][^5] John Woods followed him in 1902 and is listed until 1921.[^5] In April 1919 the government tender *Amaranth* called at the port "supplying the lighthouse for the season."[^7] Emmanuel Luick is listed from 1921 to 1937, and William A. Brooks from 1937, "at least" to 1946.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends records that storm-tossed seas breached the base of the exposed tower in November 1886, and that a storm in October 1887 damaged it again. After the 1887 repairs a plank protection went up on the east side of the tower.[^1]

The worst storm the local paper described came on Monday, 10 November 1919. The Cook County News-Herald, in its issue of 12 November, reported that "The worst storm for many years struck the North Shore Monday evening."[^6] The paper reported that "The lighthouse was nearly wrecked, the sea tearing a hole in the south and north walls," and that Keeper Woods "had the light burning last night." Woods told the paper he did not think the old lighthouse would be repaired, though it might be patched for the fall and a new one built in the spring.[^6]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends dates the change to an electric fog siren to 4 October 1923, and an alteration of its blast pattern to 1926.[^1] In 1925 the Lighthouse Service changed Grand Marais from oil gas to acetylene and altered its characteristic from fixed to flashing.[^9] In 1935, with Public Works Administration funds, the station was equipped for remote control. It gained a duplicate horn fog signal and an electric light with a lamp changer, and its control circuits ran to the keepers' quarters in the village about 1,000 feet (305 m) away.[^10]

## Today

LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, that the grounds are open and the tower is closed, and that visitors can park at the end of Broadway Street and walk the breakwater to see the light.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists the station as not open to the public, and marks it as not an active aid to navigation. That status has not been checked against a current Light List.[^5] The tower is closed and is not for climbing.

The keeper's house on shore is the main museum of the Cook County Historical Society. The society acquired it in 1966, and it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1978. It displays the Fresnel lens from the lighthouse.[^4]
