Cardona Island Light is a small stone tower on a six-acre key at the western side of the entrance to Ponce Harbor, on Puerto Rico's south shore.[^1][^2] The Spanish finished it in 1889,[^3] the United States acquired Puerto Rico's lighthouses as a result of the Spanish-American War,[^4] and since March 2021 the Coast Guard has condemned the lighthouse and told the public to stay away.[^8]

## The place

The nomination calls the island "a small 6 acre key west of Ponce Harbor," and says the light guides ships into the port.[^1] In the nineteenth century, the nomination says, the port "exported millions of pounds of sugar particularly to the United States."[^1]

The approach had a shoal that needed marking. The Lighthouse Board's annual report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 says that during the year a buoy was placed off the entrance of Ponce Harbor to mark Cardona Island Shoal.[^11]

## Building the light

The Lighthouse Board's annual report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 describes the building history.[^3] The tower "was commenced in 1887 and was finished in 1889 by the Spanish, at a cost of 11,760 pesos."[^3] The same report gives the light as a "sixth-order fixed red light" 46 feet (14 m) above high water, shown from a cylindrical tower "in the center of the front of a one-story flat- roof dwelling which is blue with white trimmings."[^3]

The nomination describes the dwelling as "designed of stone and brick for one 2nd class keeper."[^1] It gives the tower as 33 feet (10 m).[^1] The 1901 report gives it as 31 feet (9.4 m) high from its base.[^3] The two figures are close but not the same, and this story does not choose between them.

The sources also differ on the original lens by a year. The nomination calls it "an 1888, 6th order fixed red lenticular Sautter, Lemonnier & Cie. lens";[^1] the Coast Guard's light list gives the original sixth-order lens as 1889.[^4] The same list says the tower and dwelling had changed to white by 16 February 1925.[^4] LighthouseFriends reports a reinforced-concrete oil house finished in early 1914 at a cost of $370.72.[^9]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists six principal keepers for the station, and one laborer, in entries running from 1901 to 1921.[^2] Robustiano Reguera is listed from at least 1901, and Arturo Castro from at least 1903 to at least 1911.[^2] Manuel Perez Castillo is listed for 1913 to 1914, and Simeon Martin for 1914 to 1915.[^2] Ramon Romero is listed from 1916, and Felix R. Giraldet from at least 1917 to at least 1921.[^2] Elenterio Echavarria is listed as a laborer from at least 1913 to at least 1915.[^2]

The archive's dates are its own. "At least" marks a span the record documents, not a day someone was appointed. Neither the archive nor LighthouseFriends says where these men came from, what the work was like for them, or what became of them after they left the island.[^2][^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In July 1898 the light was relit.[^9] The Army's report on its operations in Puerto Rico, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says that "light-house on Cardona Island was relighted on the evening of the 29th (of July, 1898)."[^9]

In September 1921 a hurricane passed over Porto Rico.[^5] The Bulletin of 1 October 1921 names Cardona Island among the ninth-district stations damaged, along with Cape Rojo.[^5] For the group as a whole it reports four boats lost and many station buildings damaged. It gives no separate account for Cardona Island.[^5]

## After the keepers

In 1922 the light changed.[^6] A Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists Cardona Island among the changes from oil to acetylene and from fixed red to flashing white.[^6] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for fiscal 1922, which ended on 30 June 1922, gives the cost: "Cardona Island Light Station, changed from oil to acetylene, $1,025."[^7] The Coast Guard's light list gives the new flash as one every three seconds from 25 May 1922, and a four-second flash from 14 July 1937.[^4]

The same list says the light was "Extinguished due to war, 1942," and that it was "Relighted on 10 November 1943."[^4] The name changed twice: to Isla Cardona Light on 1 November 1953, and to Cayo Cardona Light on 13 June 1958.[^4] The light went to electric power on 11 April 1962.[^4] The nomination says that in 1962 it "was electrified and, apparently, unmanned."[^1]

Two other sources give dates that do not match. A Puerto Rico architectural-drawings archive's record, whose description was taken from a Facebook page, says the station was "closed in 1950," a date the Coast Guard list does not show.[^10] The same record says the sixth-order lens was in use "until 1978 when it was substituted."[^10] LighthouseFriends says the original lens "was still in use in the late 1970s" and that it is now on display in the Coast Guard Museum in San Juan.[^9] The nomination, by contrast, calls the 1888 lens "still in use."[^1] The sources do not agree on where the lens is, and this story does not settle it.

The Park Service's NPGallery record gives the nomination's "Date Published" as 22 October 1981.[^12] The Puerto Rico drawings archive says the light was included in the National Register of Historic Places in 1981.[^10]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the lighthouse.[^9] The nomination, published in 1981, classed the station "Unoccupied. Unrestricted access."[^1] Since 2021 the Coast Guard has barred entry. The 2020 earthquake and seismic activity in Puerto Rico left the facilities with "significant damage," and on 22 March 2021 the Coast Guard "revoked the certificate of occupancy and officially condemned the facility."[^8] It said it would fence the lighthouse and post warning signs "approximately 50 feet away from the structure," and warned that "visiting Cayo Cardona with the purpose of entering the lighthouse is prohibited and extremely unsafe."[^8] It also said "the lighthouse tower could collapse without notice."[^8]

The island is reached only by private boat.[^9] The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^2] LighthouseFriends notes that a distant view of the lighthouse is possible from the observation tower at La Guancha boardwalk in Ponce.[^9]
