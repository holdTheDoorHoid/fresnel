Tybee Island Light stands at the north end of Tybee Island, where the Savannah River meets the Atlantic.[^1][^9] The first tower on the site was finished in 1736 to mark the river entrance, and the sources disagree on what it was built of.[^1][^9] The brick tower there now was rebuilt after the Civil War and first showed its light on 1 October 1867.[^1][^9] Today the light station is a museum owned by the Tybee Island Historical Society, and visitors can go up the tower on posted hours.[^9][^10]

## The place

The light marked the mouth of the river that led to Savannah.[^9] The National Register nomination calls the site a "historically strategic maritime location at the gateway to the Savannah," and in the Civil War Union ships tried to blockade "the entrance to Tybee Roads."[^1]

LighthouseFriends describes the first tower as a wooden structure of about 90 feet (27 m), ordered built by General Oglethorpe and finished in 1736 as a daymark for ships entering the river.[^1][^9] The nomination dates the first lighthouse to 1735 and 1736, "under the direction of Noble Jones of Wormsloe," and says it was made of brickwork and cedar piles and reportedly rose to about 90 feet (27 m).[^1] That tower was destroyed by a storm, and the nomination says it was "not formally rebuilt until 1742."[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the collapse to 1741.[^9]

The site became federal land in 1791.[^1] The nomination describes "a five-acre tract of land (465 feet square)," about 142 m on each side, ceded to the United States "by an Act approved December 15, 1791."[^1]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says a third, brick tower went up farther from the shore in 1773.[^9] The nomination says the base of the present tower is "reportedly part of the third lighthouse built on Tybee Island in 1791," and that the lighthouse was rebuilt inland in 1791.[^1] The two sources give different dates for the same base, and neither settles it.

On the night of 8 November 1792 a fire damaged the top of the tower and the lantern room.[^9] LighthouseFriends quotes a customs inspector, Jesse Tay, who was at the light and wrote about it the next day. The lamp trimmer went up at about two in the morning and "discovered the lanthorn in flames."[^9] President Washington, LighthouseFriends says, directed that repairs use a "plain stair case" instead of a more expensive hanging staircase.[^9]

A 50-foot (15 m) front beacon went up in 1822 to make a range with the main tower.[^9] In 1857 the nomination says the reflectors were replaced by a first-order Fresnel lens.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the main tower received a second-order lens that year and the front light a fourth-order lens.[^9] The sources disagree on the order of the 1857 lens. Both place a first-order Fresnel lens in the rebuilt tower of 1867, and LighthouseFriends says that lens still lights the tower.[^1][^9]

The Civil War reached the light in 1862.[^1] The nomination says the Montgomery Guards, a Confederate raiding party, attacked the lighthouse, and that "the lighthouse was burned but only partially destroyed."[^1] LighthouseFriends places the Confederate withdrawal in 1861, when the troops took out the lens and set the tower on fire.[^9] The two sources give 1861 and 1862 for the same burning. A Savannah newspaper of 10 June 1889 says the tower was "blown up by the confederate forces" as they abandoned the island.[^11] That account differs from the nomination's.[^1]

According to LighthouseFriends, Congress appropriated $20,000 for the rebuilding on 7 April 1866, and another $34,443 the following year.[^9] Federal troops who came to the island brought cholera with them, LighthouseFriends says. The foreman and four workers died, and a replacement crew finished the job.[^9] Only the bottom 60 feet (18 m) of the old tower could be saved, and 94 feet (29 m) more was built on it, bringing the total height to 154 feet (47 m).[^9] The nomination gives 145 feet (44 m) above ground.[^1]

Storms and budgets followed. A hurricane in 1871 left five faces of the octagonal tower dangerously cracked, and the Lighthouse Board requested $50,000 for a new tower.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the money to replace the main light was never provided.[^9] In 1877 Congress allocated $3,000 to replace the frame beacon with an iron tower.[^9] A new residence for the head keeper was built in 1881, and in 1884 the lamp was switched from lard oil to kerosene. A first assistant's dwelling had to be rebuilt in 1885 after a fire.[^9]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster for the main tower has 180 named lines, from 1791 to "at least 1971," and four more for the front beacon.[^2][^3] The roster's one contractor line has no dates and is not counted.

The first name on the roster is Ichabod Higgins, printed as principal keeper for 1791 to 1792 and again for 1795 to 1800.[^2]

Women appear in the roster's assistant posts in the 1860s and 1870s.[^2] Mary L. Cohen is printed as second assistant for 1869 to 1870, Emma A. Manchester as second assistant in 1870, and Bridget Comer as second assistant in 1873. Revennah G. Adams is printed as second assistant in 1875 and as first assistant for 1875 to 1876. "Mrs. Francis Sickel" is printed as first assistant from 1870 to 1873.[^2] The roster gives only their posts and years, and the sources read for this station say nothing more about them.

A Savannah newspaper visited the light in June 1889.[^11] The Morning News reported that "It requires three keepers to tend the light," and that "Each stands watch four hours."[^11] Its head keeper, "Keeper Jacobs," had seen service on "a government lightship before he took charge of Tybee light," and the paper names "Peter Rasmusson, first assistant, and John L. Evans, second assistant."[^11] The paper says the head keeper had been at his post for six years.[^11] The roster has Peter Jacob at Tybee from 1884 as first assistant and as head keeper from 1888.[^2] The roster prints Peter Jacob (1888 to 1900), Peter Rasmusson (1888 to 1895) and John Simmons Evans (1888 to 1895) in the same posts.[^2] The spellings differ, and the match rests on post and year alone.

The 1889 feature describes the routine in detail.[^11] The lamp, the paper says, used "about five gallons of oil" every night and was "kept burning until sunrise." The climb was "the 187 spiral steps" from the base to the last landing.[^11] A 1897 feature describes "seven long flights of winding iron stairs" to the top.[^12] The 1889 writer also listed what the keepers heard on watch: "no sounds except those of the storm which vibrate the tall tower" and "the roar of the surf on the shore."[^11]

Frederick H. Bruggeman was head keeper from 1914 to 1931.[^2] He had been transferred to Hunting Island in 1913, and in early 1914 he and Franz Traugott, then head keeper at Tybee, won permission to swap stations.[^9][^2] Three days after the United States declared war on Germany on 6 April 1917, the commander of Fort Screven wrote to his superiors that Bruggeman was "of German descent, and of possibly German affiliations."[^9] LighthouseFriends says the local inspector nonetheless felt the keeper could be trusted, "due to his years of faithful service." A military guard was stationed in the lighthouse at all times during the war.[^9] Bruggeman retired in 1931 at seventy, after seventeen years at Tybee.[^9]

The front range had its own assistants. The roster lists Arthur A. Burn as first assistant at the Tybee Range Front in 1913,[^3] and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin names him as the keeper who went to the rescue in 1916, described below.[^7]

By 1933 the station had one keeper.[^9] George B. Jackson had been first assistant from 1931 to 1933 and then head keeper until his death in 1948.[^2][^9]

The roster also prints Coast Guard personnel from about 1950 to 1971 with no position given.[^2] This story names none of them.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends quotes the Board's 1887 report on an earthquake in August 1886.[^9] The lens was displaced and fittings on its upper ring were broken, and the Board said the damage was repaired without delay.[^9]

On 27 August 1893 a gale overturned the front beacon, and the Board's report says it was "reestablished September 6, 1893."[^5] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 says the second assistant keeper's dwelling was "almost entirely rebuilt" in that year, and that the front beacon "was reerected and bolted to it with iron rods."[^6]

The 1893 report records that "lamps were substituted for Hains lamps" at the light and beacon.[^4]

On 13 February 1916, in a severe gale, Arthur A. Burn, first assistant keeper of the Tybee Range Front, "rescued three soldiers from Fort Screven," whom he "found in a small boat" and who were in danger of being drowned.[^7]

## After the keepers

Electricity reached the light in 1933, replacing kerosene, and the staff was cut to one keeper.[^9] The Coast Guard took control of the tower in 1939 and stayed in the station buildings until 1987, when it moved to a newer facility on Cockspur Island.[^9]

The Board noted in 1901 that gunfire from the battery was bringing down the plaster in the principal keeper's dwelling.[^9] Battery Garland, part of Fort Screven, had been built in 1898 and 1899.[^9]

On the night of 30 November 1933, the master of relief lightship No. 109, H. T. Brett, reported that Tybee Light "was visible a distance of 34 miles" that night.[^8]

The Tybee Island Historical Society carried out a major restoration in 1998, after twelve years of fundraising.[^9] In 2002 the Society received ownership of the station under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, and LighthouseFriends counts Tybee among the first lights transferred under the act.[^9] The head keeper's house was restored in 2000 and 2001, and the first assistant's dwelling in 2003 and 2004. The second assistant's house became a lecture hall, art gallery and audio-visual space between 2005 and 2008.[^9]

## Today

The light station is open to the public on posted hours. The Society's visitor page says the site is closed on Tuesdays and that the lighthouse closes at 5 p.m.[^10] The lighthouse and catwalk "may close without warning due to adverse weather conditions."[^10] A ticket covers the whole light station and the Tybee Museum at Battery Garland.[^10]
