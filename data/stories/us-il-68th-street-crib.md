The 68th Street Crib is a city water-intake crib about two miles out in Lake Michigan, off the eastern end of 68th Street in Chicago.[^1] Its red skeleton tower still carries a light.[^7] In January 1909 a fire on a temporary crib on the same tunnel system killed dozens of men.[^1][^5] The permanent crib built beside it that year, the Edward F. Dunne Crib, stands 50 feet (15 m) away.[^1]

## The place

Chicago drew its water from Lake Michigan through tunnels that ran out from intake cribs offshore.[^1] The city hired Ellis Chesbrough in 1861 to improve its water supply and sewers.[^1] His plan called for a tunnel from a pumping station to an intake crib two miles out.[^1] LighthouseFriends places that pumping station five miles inland, and it calls the intake crib the Two Mile Crib.[^1] Preservation Chicago describes the pumping station as on the shore.[^6] The contract for the tunnel was signed in October 1863, and the tunnel was completed on 30 November 1866.[^1] The 68th Street Crib was built later, in 1892.[^1]

The 68th Street Crib originally supplied two tunnels.[^1] The larger, 20 feet (6 m) across, fed the Jardine water purification plant, which LighthouseFriends says was the largest purification plant in the world at that time. The smaller, 10 feet (3 m) across, fed the South Water plant.[^1]

## Building the light

The crib's warning light was shown from "a four-sided pyramidal skeletal tower topped by a lantern room," and a fog bell was tolled every twelve seconds when needed.[^1] The Coast Guard's 2025 list gives the same bell interval and describes a silver-gray lantern on a red skeleton tower.[^7]

The earliest records of the light are thin. A Light-House Board report of 1893 says that a light at the northwest end of Chicago's outer breakwater "was moved to the Emergency Intake Waterworks Crib on October 19, 1892."[^8] The report does not say which crib that was, and this story does not assume it was this one.

In 1908 work began on a second, temporary crib, the Intermediate Crib, on the 68th Street tunnel system.[^1][^5]

The permanent Edward F. Dunne Crib was built in 1909. It is named for Edward Fitzsimmons Dunne, the mayor of Chicago when its plans were approved. It is a circular crib 110 feet (34 m) across that stands in 32 feet (10 m) of water. A steel footbridge links it to the 68th Street Crib, which allowed one set of keepers to serve both cribs.[^1]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends names no keeper of the 68th Street Crib.[^1] In December 1964 the Chicago Tribune printed an article about the crew at the Dunne crib, which LighthouseFriends reprints.[^1] Robert Melone, the man in charge of the Dunne crib, said there usually were two or three men in the crew, depending on the weather. The article says the crew spent every other week on the crib.[^1] In winter, the article says, the crew's trouble was ice. They kept it clear with pike poles and, when it began to collect too rapidly, with dynamite.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the morning of 20 January 1909 fire broke out on the Intermediate Crib, a temporary wooden structure in use while a tunnel was being built.[^5] LighthouseFriends dates the fire to 30 January 1909.[^1] The Richmond, Washington and Los Angeles papers of 20 to 22 January, and the Tribune's 2020 retrospective, all date it to 20 January, so this story uses that date.[^2][^3][^4][^5]

The Tribune's own account from the next day, as its 2020 retrospective quotes it, called the crib "made of wood, made strong enough to withstand the waves and ice, but in no sense fireproof."[^5] The retrospective says many of those who escaped were not fully dressed, because the fire had struck while they were sleeping.[^5]

Estimates of the crew differed. The Richmond paper of 20 January said "some seventy-five men were employed" at the crib and in the tunnel.[^2] The Washington Herald said a "force of eighty-eight men" lived there and worked in shifts.[^3] The 1909 Tribune, as the 2020 retrospective quotes it, gave eighty as the usual number, and George W. Jackson, the tunnel company's president, furnished a list of seventy-nine names.[^5] The Los Angeles Herald quoted the tunnel union's president as saying there were "about 175 men" working at the crib.[^4]

The newspapers also disagree on where the burning crib stood. The Richmond paper placed it "about two miles out," the Washington Herald "a mile and a half off Seventy-third street," and the 2020 retrospective about 1.5 miles from the shore, near 71st Street.[^2][^3][^5]

The counts of the dead changed from day to day. The Richmond paper's first wire said "between twenty-five and thirty-five men are believed to have been burned to death or drowned," and one survivor placed the death list at thirty-six.[^2] The Washington Herald of 21 January gave "forty-six workmen" as having lost their lives.[^3] On 22 January the Los Angeles Herald reported 47 bodies at the city morgue, a coroner who thought the list might reach 66, and a union president who feared it might reach 100.[^4] The Tribune's report of 21 January gave 47 bodies recovered and 48 men rescued, as the 2020 retrospective quotes it.[^5]

The coroner's jury later put the number of dead at 60, and some estimates went as high as 70.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the fire claimed seventy of the one hundred men working on the crib.[^1] The sources do not agree on the number.

The tug T. T. Morford was moored at the 68th Street crib.[^2] The Richmond paper reported that it "went to the rescue" and saved twenty-six men.[^2] Where the Richmond paper gives twenty-six, the 2020 retrospective says the tug collected "dozens of men" from the lake.[^5] The retrospective says Capt. E. A. Johnson and the Morford's crew came over from the nearby 68th Street crib when they saw the fire, breaking through four-inch ice floes.[^5]

The 1909 Tribune, as quoted in 2020, described the scene in the crib: "Naked and half clad men fell over each other and fought to get on the outside of the crib."[^5] Some who jumped from the second-story window reached blocks of ice, where they had a few minutes of safety. Others struck the water and needed many failed tries to climb out onto the ice.[^5]

The cause was never settled. The jury could not say what caused the fire, and it cleared the company and the city of criminal carelessness.[^5] Theories ranged from gasoline used against bedbugs to a smoker in the sleeping rooms, and to a torch dropped near dynamite.[^5] Some workers claimed there were three tons of dynamite in the crib. The company denied it and said there were 800 pounds.[^5] The dynamite did not go off, and the fire started, the retrospective says, "without any explosion."[^5]

The dead were buried on the same day, in a single grave at Mount Greenwood Cemetery.[^5] The plaque there reads "In memory of crib fire" and notes "45 unknown men," where the Tribune had counted 47 bodies.[^5]

## After the keepers

In 1923 the navigational aids on the 68th Street Crib were electrified, and LighthouseFriends says they were judged enough for both cribs.[^1] For many years the city kept isolated crews on the cribs around the clock. The cribs still in use are staffed remotely.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the final crew of keepers was withdrawn in 1990.[^1] In 2002 security systems with motion detectors and video cameras, linked to the police, were installed on all the intake cribs.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard's 2025 Light List still carries the light as LLNR 19870. It shows a red flash every three seconds, and a bell that strikes once every twelve seconds.[^7]

The Chicago Department of Water Management owns the lighthouse, LighthouseFriends reports. Its tower is closed, and the best views would come from a boat or plane. Vessels are kept out of a buoyed zone around each crib.[^1]
