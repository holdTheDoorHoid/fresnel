Flowerpot Island Lighthouse stands on Castle Bluff, at the north-east point of Flowerpot Island, off the tip of the Bruce Peninsula in Georgian Bay. The Department of Marine had it built in 1897 to increase the safety of "large and fast steamers entering Georgian Bay,"[^1] and today it is part of Fathom Five National Marine Park.[^1][^2]

## The place

The island takes its name from its flowerpot-shaped sea stacks, which are narrow at the base and wider up top.[^1] The light's height above the water owed more to the cliff than to the tower. LighthouseFriends explains that the light's "lofty focal plane was mostly due to the cliff, known as Castle Bluff, on which the lighthouse was perched." The tower itself was "just over thirty feet tall."[^1]

## Building the light

In 1897 the Department announced that "a lighthouse and fog bell were being established on the northeast point of Flowerpot Island."[^1] J. C. Kennedy of Owen Sound was paid $1,137 to build it, and a temporary light was shown from a lens lantern on a pole while the work went on.[^1] The lighthouse was a square wooden dwelling with a square wooden lantern on its roof. It went into service in the late autumn of 1897 as a fixed white dioptric light of the seventh order, 26.8 m (88 ft) above the surrounding water.[^1]

Tenders for a keeper's house were invited in the fall of 1898, but the offers were considered too expensive and "the project was deferred."[^1] LighthouseFriends says a two-storey keeper's dwelling was built on the shore in the summer of 1900, by day labour, at a cost of $1,397.[^1] The heritage register dates the lightkeeper's dwelling to 1901,[^2] and the Lighthouse Directory dates the principal keeper's house to 1901 as well.[^7] The 1900 and 1901 dates disagree, and the sources read here do not settle which is right.

The fog bell moved onto a detached platform at the opening of navigation in 1902.[^1] The departmental report for 1903–04 says the bell, "referred to in the last year's report as disabled in August, 1902, was again put in operation in September."[^3] That report covers the fiscal year that ended 30 June 1904, so its September is September 1903. LighthouseFriends says the bell came back into use about a month after the August breakdown, which would put the return in September 1902.[^1] The two accounts differ by a year, and this story cannot settle which is right.

A fog alarm was established at the station later. The report for 1909–10, which covers the fiscal year that ended in March 1910, says "a fog-alarm was established at this light station," with a 4-h.p. standard diaphone plant bought from the Canadian Fog Signal Company for $2,400.[^5] A rectangular wooden fog-alarm building was "erected by day's labour," and with its machinery it "cost $2,010.65."[^5] LighthouseFriends dates the building to 1908,[^1] so the two sources disagree on when the fog alarm came.

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends lists the head keepers from 1897 to 1987: Donald Smith (1897–1903), John Parker Jr. (1903–1904), Hector Currie (1904–1907), John Parker (1907–1909), Daniel Smith (1909–1912), William J. Spears (1912–1937), Bill Spears (1937), Hugh Rumley (1937 or 1938 to 1949), Audrey Coultis (1950–1982) and John Freethy (1982–1987).[^1] Two of these names turn up in the departmental keepers' tables. The 1906–07 table has "Currie, Hector" against Flowerpot Island, appointed 18 August 1904 at $300,[^4] and the 1911–12 table has "D. Smith" against it, appointed 6 October 1909 at $260.[^6]

Donald Smith was the first keeper and was paid $300 a season.[^1] Smith and his two sons are credited with a rescue in 1900, described below. William Spears and his wife raised eight children on the island during the twenty-five years they served there.[^1] When Spears died in 1937, his son Bill "took care of the lighthouse for a few months" before Hugh Rumley was placed in charge.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In mid-October 1900, LighthouseFriends reports, the schooner Marion L. Breck, "bound from Owen Sound to Blind River with a cargo of bricks," ran aground on a shoal while seeking shelter from a storm in the lee of Bear's Rump Island. Keeper Donald Smith witnessed the drama but could not reach the crew in his small sailboat. The crew were "rescued two days later by Keeper Smith and his two sons."[^1]

On 15 October 1909, LighthouseFriends reports, the Canadian Pacific steamer Athabasca "grounded on Flowerpot Island." No one was hurt, and two tugs from Owen Sound freed her. At a marine board of inquiry, the captain "admitted the accident was due to the helmsman confusing his orders." The steamer's crew had said the keeper was responsible because the foghorn was not sounding. Keeper Daniel Smith, LighthouseFriends says, blamed the crew, saying the fog horn had not needed to run in the snowfall.[^1] LighthouseFriends cites two Buffalo Evening News reports from October and November 1909 for its account.[^1]

Bill Spears later recalled, as LighthouseFriends prints it: "Never heard thunderstorms as bad as at Flowerpot."[^1]

## After the keepers

A second dwelling for an assistant keeper went up in 1959, and a boathouse in 1963.[^2] A pyramidal steel tower "topped by a light was put in place in 1968," and the following year the old lighthouse, "no longer needed, was burned and pushed over the cliff."[^1][^7] The register describes the change as part of "the late 20th-century shift away from staffed lighthouses and their replacement with low-maintenance automated structures."[^2]

The station was de-staffed after the 1987 season. The buildings sat vacant until Friends of Fathom Five reached an agreement with the Coast Guard and began restoration work in 1996.[^1] A Lightkeeper's Host Program started in 1998, and volunteers now live at the station and interpret it for visitors.[^1][^2] In 2012 red and white dayboards replaced the all-white ones. The new dayboards make the tower look square, but the tower underneath is still the pyramidal one of 1968.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and LighthouseFriends says the site is managed by the Friends of the Bruce District Parks Association.[^1] The island is part of Fathom Five National Marine Park. Passenger ferry service runs from early May to mid-October, with tours from Tobermory.[^1][^2] One of the keeper's dwellings is a museum, and volunteers live in the other. Visitors can take a 1.5 km hike from the island's dock to the light.[^1] The grounds are open and the tower is closed to visitors.[^1]
