Chrome Island's two range lights stand on a small sandstone island off the southern tip of Denman Island, and together they mark the southern entrance to Baynes Sound on British Columbia's Strait of Georgia.[^1] The island was first labelled Yellow Rock on navigational maps around 1860,[^1] and the Department of Marine's annual report for 1907 still calls it Yellow Island.[^2] The sources disagree on when the name changed. CoastView gives 1923,[^3] and Lighthouse Friends gives about 1940.[^1]

## The place

Baynes Sound lies between Denman Island and Vancouver Island.[^3] The light was built to serve the coal trade.[^1] In 1887 the Union Colliery Company laid track linking Deep Bay, Fanny Bay, Buckley Bay and Union Bay, and coal suppliers petitioned for a lighthouse at the sound's entrance.[^3][^1]

The island's sandstone also carries carvings.[^1] Lighthouse Friends calls them the widest variety of petroglyphs on the West Coast.[^1] Some scholars speculate that they record battles of the Pentlatch people, and others think they hold a religious meaning.[^1] The builders blasted many of them away when work began in 1890.[^1]

## Building the light

The first station was a white dwelling joined to a square tower with a red lantern.[^1] A contract of $4,070 went to J. A. Brittancourt of Nanaimo, who failed to carry out the work according to specifications, and the Department of Marine had to finish the job itself.[^1] The light was completed in 1890 and first lit on New Year's Day 1891 by keeper Tom H. Piercy.[^1][^3] Its tower stood 17 m (55 ft) to the vane on the lantern, and its three white flashes could be seen for 27 km (17 miles), about 15 nautical miles.[^1][^3]

In 1898 the first light was taken apart.[^1] Its lantern and apparatus went to Egg Island, the upper tower came down, and the lower part was roofed over as a dwelling.[^1] Two range lights began showing on 16 July 1898.[^1] The back light was a wooden pyramidal tower, 8.5 m (28 ft) from base to vane.[^1] The front light, 88 m (290 ft) west of it, stood 6 m (20 ft) from base to vane.[^1] Seen in line, the two marked the centre of a channel seven fathoms deep.[^1]

The fog signal came later.[^2] The Department's report for the year ended 31 March 1907 says a fog-alarm building for a diaphone was under construction at Yellow Island, by day labour, and had cost $1,200 to that date.[^2] The next report says the building was finished and the diaphone installed.[^4] The diaphone came from the Canadian Fog Signal Company of Toronto for $1,900, and the building and machinery together cost $3,347.68.[^4]

By 1920, Lighthouse Friends says, mariners were complaining that the fixed light could be mistaken for a farmhouse or a ship at anchor.[^1] A steel tower with a revolving light was begun in March 1922, and its light flashed every six seconds.[^1] In 1989 the skeletal tower was brought down and replaced by the present round fibreglass tower, whose lantern shows a white light every five seconds.[^1]

## The keepers

Tom H. Piercy kept the light from 1891 to 1898 and raised ten children on a rock of less than 0.8 hectares (two acres).[^1] One day his son Henry, aged eight, found a piece of metal half hidden in the dirt, and struck it.[^1] The explosion of the blasting cap tore off his thumb and mangled the rest of his hand.[^1] The keeper, who could not leave the station without written permission, bound the boy's hand and rowed him across to Denman Island, where he was set ashore and given directions to a doctor about 10 km (six miles) away.[^1]

Keepers followed quickly. William McDonagh served from 1898 to 1901, Walter Gordon from 1901 to 1906, and John Doney from 1906 to 1914.[^1] The Department's keeper list for 1906 names a John Doney at Yellow Island.[^5]

G. Allan Couldery kept the light from 1922 to 1939. The dwelling he lived in was infested with beetles and other pests, which dropped into the food on the stove and into the beds, and his complaint led to a new house in 1928.[^1] The roster below gives the full run of keepers, from Piercy to at least 2024.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 16 December 1900 the steamer Alpha of Vancouver, bound from Victoria for coal at Comox and then for Japan, stranded in a strong gale and fog on Yellow Island and was a total loss.[^6] The Department's list of casualties says that the master and eight of the crew drowned, including three engineers.[^6]

A wire report printed in the *Wheeling Daily Intelligencer* on 19 December 1900 names the dead, among them the managing owner, the master, the purser and a stowaway. It says that one crewman swam a line to the lighthouse on Yellow Island.[^7]

Lighthouse Friends tells the story differently.[^1] Quartermaster Anderson swam a rope ashore, and about twenty-five crew came in along the line.[^1] The owner, the captain and five others stayed aboard and clung to the mainmast until it snapped, and seven of them died.[^1] Keeper McDonagh, it says, sheltered and fed the survivors until they could be taken back to Vancouver.[^1]

The accounts do not agree on the death toll. The Department's list gives nine dead, the master and eight of the crew.[^6] The wire report names eight dead, including two engineers.[^7] Lighthouse Friends says seven died.[^1]

In 2009, as winds rose and waves swamped a couple's boat near the island, keeper Roger Williamson spotted them and hailed them in.[^1] With his wife Leslie, who was assistant keeper, he helped the couple dry off, fed them dinner, and kept them sheltered for eight hours while the seas calmed.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard's list of lights describes the two range lights. The front light is a fixed yellow light 14 m above the water, shown from a cylindrical tower with a red daymark and a white stripe. The rear light, in a white cylindrical tower, flashes every five seconds from 21.9 m above the water, and the list adds that a yellow light is visible in line with the range.[^8]

The station is staffed.[^1] Fisheries and Oceans Canada lists Chrome Island as an active lightstation in its sea-surface sampling, from 1961 to the present.[^9] The programme's daily sea-surface temperature and salinity observations have been made at several locations on the coast of British Columbia, most of them lighthouses.[^10]

Access is limited. Lighthouse Friends says a good view of the lighthouse can be had from an overlook in Boyle Point Provincial Park, and that the grounds are open while the tower is closed.[^1] The station is a working Coast Guard lightstation, so do not land on the island and do not try to climb the tower. In 2000 an archaeological study was carried out on the island, and the island is now a protected archaeological site, featuring petroglyphs, human bones and a shell midden.[^1]

The anchor of the Alpha was recovered in 1973 and set on a cement block near the Denman ferry landing.[^1] Lighthouse Friends also reports that the Coast Guard has repeatedly threatened to de-staff the light. Public outcry from mariners and floatplane operators has so far kept that from happening.[^1]
