Plum Island Light stands on the western tip of Plum Island, off the end of Long Island's North Fork, where the island's bluff faces the narrow, fast-running channel called Plum Gut. A light was first lit here in 1827 (sources differ on whether it was a stone tower), and a granite keeper's house with an iron tower on its front has stood since about 1870; the Coast Guard turned the light off in 1978.[^1][^2]

## The place

The light sits on three acres at the west end of the island, bought from Richard Jerome for $90 in 1826. It marks the east side of Plum Gut, which Lighthouse Friends describes as a mile-wide entrance to Long Island Sound with extremely strong tidal currents; Orient Point lies on the opposite shore.[^2] Ted Webb, a Southold local historian who guides lighthouse cruises from Orient Point, calls the Gut "a very treacherous body of water" with a rocky bottom that shoals quickly.[^11]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $4,000 on 18 May 1826, and the first light was lit in 1827. Lighthouse Friends describes it as a 35-foot (11 m) octagonal tower of rough stone, lit by ten lamps with reflectors in two groups of five on rotating copper tables, with an eight-room dwelling for the keeper.[^2] Webb tells it differently, as a light fixture on a pole rather than a tower, and this account has not been settled here.[^11] The 1868 Light-House Board report, as quoted by Lighthouse Friends, speaks of "the tower, built in 1827".[^2] In 1856 a fourth-order Fresnel lens, giving a white flash every thirty seconds, replaced the lamps.[^2]

The tower was already leaking by 1850. After an inspector's visit that summer, when Sineus M. Conkling kept the light, the inspector found the dwelling in fair order but said the "Tower of the light-house is leaky", the lantern was rusty and dirty, and the keeper, "a small consumer of oil", had promised to do better.[^2] The Board's 1868 report judged both tower and dwelling in bad condition, with "the masonry is soft and crumbling", and proposed rebuilding.[^2]

Funds came on 3 March 1869. The new light went into service in 1870 and reused the old lens.[^2] It is a two-story granite dwelling with a white cast-iron tower and a black lantern room attached to the front of the roof, together with a fog bell in a wooden tower, struck by a Stevens apparatus. The old dwelling stood until 1885.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's archive dates the present tower to 1869 and the keeper's quarters to 1870.[^1]

## The keepers

The first keeper, Sineus Conkling, held the post from 1827 to 1837, and the roster shows the Conkling name on the station until 1853: Henry Conkling, James M. Conkling, then Sineus M. Conkling.[^1] William Booth followed until 1861 (Lighthouse Friends spells it Boothe).[^1][^2]

William Whiting Wetmore (1806-1892) took charge in 1861. The archive records that he was dismissed or removed in 1868; Austin B. Booth held the post for a year; and Wetmore returned in 1869 and stayed until he died in 1892.[^4][^1] Lighthouse Friends calls him a noted angler. It says John Jacob Astor stopped at the island and Wetmore fitted him out with bait and lines, and that three years later President Grover Cleveland stopped to get his advice on catching bluefish. It adds that at eighty-five he was reportedly the oldest keeper in the country.[^2]

His son William Walker Wetmore (1839-1909) served as first assistant from 1871 to 1892 and then as head keeper until 1903.[^3] His obituary in the Suffolk Times said he was "well known to mariners".[^3] Lighthouse Friends reports that in October 1900 a granddaughter of Keeper Wetmore, Blanche, found matches and set her clothes alight; her father rowed to Orient Point for a doctor, who could not save her.[^2] The Wetmores kept the light for most of four decades.[^1]

Later head keepers included William S. Parks (1905-1911), George Ehrhardt (1911-1913) and William M. Chapel (1913-1931).[^1] Keepers bought food and supplies at the commissary of Fort Terry, the Army post on the island, until 1916, when Chapel was told he could no longer; he had to take his small boat two miles to Orient Point or twelve to New London, until the Lighthouse Board intervened and access mostly returned.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year 1917 (July 1916 to June 1917), p. 55, lists Chapel as having rescued the captain and wife of the tug S. L. Hommedieu, adrift in an open boat without oars after the tug sank.[^10] Lighthouse Friends dates the rescue to 1917.[^2] In 1924, the same source says, Chapel pulled Sidney Smith Jr. from the water after three and a half hours following the explosion of the Smith family's motorboat; his brother Harry's body washed ashore on Plum Island.[^2]

A lookout tower was mounted on the lantern room for part of the Second World War.[^2]

## After the keepers

Light-House Board reports show the station kept up through the 1890s: a new fog bell was substituted for the old one, a new fog-bell tower was built, and a timber-crib landing wharf was completed.[^7][^8][^9] The archive's roster shows Coast Guard crews at the station from 1951 to 1978.[^1] Tony Tuliano, one of a four-man crew in 1977, had to switch the electrified light on and off each day and wind up a 100-pound weight that turned the lens.[^2] The Coast Guard removed its people in 1978 and replaced the light with a flashing white light on a 14-foot brick shed.[^2] Ownership passed to the US Department of Agriculture in 1981.[^6]

The dismantled station deteriorated. In 1994 the lens and its clockwork were taken to the East End Seaport Museum in Greenport.[^2] In 1997 erosion dropped the generator house into the water; from 2000 the group East End Lighthouses had 15,000 tons of rock placed to hold the bank.[^2] The lighthouse is listed on the National Register of Historic Places.[^6] A 2019 report said the Department of Homeland Security and the Army Corps of Engineers were paying for $1.5 million of work on rusted ironwork, the roof and the interior.[^5]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by the Department of Homeland Security. The island, home to a federal animal disease laboratory, and the lighthouse are closed to the public, and are expected to stay closed after the stabilization work.[^2][^6] It can be seen from the water, for example from the Cross Sound Ferry or excursion boats from Orient Point.[^2] The lens is on loan to the East End Seaport Museum in Greenport.[^11]
