Battery Point Light stands on a rock islet beside the point at the north side of Crescent City harbor's entrance.[^7][^8] Its stone keeper's house and central brick tower were built in 1856 and first shown on December 10 that year.[^1][^6][^7] The federal light was discontinued in 1965 and became a private aid to navigation again in 1982. The house is now a museum run by the Del Norte County Historical Society, and volunteers live there.[^1][^6]

## The place

Crescent City was an isolated town in the 1850s, and its harbor, shaped like a crescent, was its route to the sea.[^1] In January 1855 the California legislature urged its congressional delegation to win money for lights at Trinidad and at Crescent City.[^1]

The point took its name from a battery of three brass cannons, according to LighthouseFriends and Peggy Coons, the lighthouse's curator in 1964.[^7][^8] LighthouseFriends says the cannons were salvaged after the ship *America* burned in the harbor in 1855, and Coons calls it the 1855 wreck of the *America*. The cannons were often fired on Fourth of July celebrations and have since disappeared, LighthouseFriends says.[^7][^8]

Before the Crescent City pier was built, the point was a peninsula joined to the mainland by sand. After the outer breakwater went up, the sand no longer rebuilt the connection, and the point became an island except at low tide.[^8]

The federal reservation was ten acres in all: land on the mainland, the islet with the light, and the reef of rock connecting them. The Light-House Board's annual report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1897 said the mainland part "was of no use to the Light-House Establishment," and that squatters occupied it.[^3]

In March 1880 the *Sacramento Daily Record-Union* reported that Senator Farley was pressing federal officials to hand the reservation to the city, describing a tract of about eleven acres that the Lighthouse Board did not need.[^5] The paper's figure differs from the Board's ten acres. The Treasury officials were willing, on conditions, to return the land to the public domain for a town site, but the Interior Department's law officer ruled that no conditions could go into a town-site patent, and the question went back to the Lighthouse Board.[^5] Senator Farley opposed a public auction, the paper said, because speculators could buy the harbor's key and push out the settlers who had already improved the land.[^5]

In 1875 the Lighthouse Board judged the station to be in a dilapidated condition. As LighthouseFriends quotes the report, the Board wanted a first-order light at Point St. George before the Crescent City station was discontinued.[^7]

## Building the light

The sources disagree about the appropriation. The National Register nomination says Congress voted $15,000 on March 3, 1855; LighthouseFriends says it came in May 1855.[^1][^7]

Construction began in 1856. The Light House Service built a Cape Cod dwelling with a tower rising through its center.[^1] The granite was shipped in, and the timber was milled locally.[^1] The nomination says the central tower stands 77 feet (23.5 m) above mean high water. Coons gives the lighthouse as 74 feet (22.6 m) above mean sea level.[^1][^8]

The light was first shown on December 10, 1856, with a fourth-order Fresnel lens in the lantern room.[^6][^7] LighthouseFriends says the first official keeper arrived fifteen days after the light was first lit, so a temporary keeper ran it until then.[^7]

The lamps changed over the next decades. Mineral oil replaced lard oil in 1881, and new fourth-order lamps went in in 1898.[^1] The Board's 1899 list gives Crescent City as a fourth-order fixed white light, varied by a white flash every ninety seconds.[^4] In 1880 the dwelling was painted light buff and the tower white, and in 1887 the whole lighthouse was painted white.[^1][^7]

In 1904 three water tanks went up, and in 1907 an oil house was built.[^1] In May 1907 a new four-panel fourth-order lens changed the light to a white flash every fifteen seconds.[^1][^7]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive prints a keeper register for the station: seventeen entries from 1857 to 1953, for sixteen people, one of them a woman who served as first assistant.[^2]

Theophilus Magruder came first. LighthouseFriends says his salary was $1,000 a year, reduced by forty percent on September 1, 1859, and that he resigned a few weeks later.[^7] The register gives him the years 1857 to 1859.[^2]

Dugald S. Sartwell served twice, from 1859 to 1860 and again from 1860 to 1862. The register also lists Charles Edwards for 1860 alone, a year that overlaps both of Sartwell's stints.[^2] George W. Emery followed, from 1862 to 1866, and then John Henessey, to 1875.[^2]

John H. Jeffrey became keeper in 1875 and stayed until 1914, the longest tenure in the register.[^2][^7] LighthouseFriends says he and his wife, Nellie, served nearly forty years at the station and raised four children there.[^7] The register lists Nellie as first assistant from 1880 to 1882, and LighthouseFriends says she worked as an assistant for part of the time her husband was keeper.[^2][^7] Their son George joined the service in 1894 on St. George Reef and kept Oakland Harbor light from 1903 to 1914, according to LighthouseFriends.[^7]

Jeffrey's successors served shorter terms. Charles C. Bruehl kept the light from 1914 to 1916, and John E. Lind from 1916 to 1930.[^2] LighthouseFriends says Lind reached the mandatory retirement age of seventy in 1928, took a two-year extension, and retired in 1930, giving his service as thirty-seven years, nine months and twenty-nine days.[^7]

The post then went through a few changes. John O. Becker kept the light from 1930 to 1933. The register lists Cheney V. Dunbar as principal keeper from 1933 to 1936, but LighthouseFriends says the Service replaced the keeper with a lamplighter in 1933 to save money, and that the keeper's post was restored in 1936 with the appointment of Joseph R. Marhoffer.[^2][^7] Marhoffer served to 1939, James E. Simonson to 1942, Frederick C. Saunders to 1945, and John Hollenbeck to 1946. Wayne R. Piland, from 1946 to 1953, is the last principal keeper in the register.[^2]

In March 1964 Peggy and Clarence Coons were the resident curators at the lighthouse. Coons wrote that they had lived on the island since 1962.[^8] The Historical Society's page calls the people living there in 1964 resident keepers.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1879, while Jeffrey was in charge, a giant wave struck the wooden lean-to kitchen on the west side of the lighthouse. The chimney fell, the stove went over and started a fire, and the family drew water to fight it until a second wave swamped the island and put the fire out, LighthouseFriends reports.[^7] The same account says, without a date, that one wave broke three panes of glass in the lantern room and left water in the tower.[^7]

An earthquake in Alaska on March 27, 1964 sent tsunami waves south. They reached Crescent City about midnight, LighthouseFriends says, with crests of up to twenty feet.[^7] Coons wrote that she got up a little before midnight and, at the window, saw that all the rocks around the island had disappeared under the water.[^8] The Coons couple ran outside and headed for the highest point overlooking the town. She describes the loud blast of breaking glass and splintering wood as the first wave hit, and says the third wave, much larger than the second, reached the south end of town and started a fire.[^8]

"We glanced at the tower: the light was still flashing," Coons wrote.[^8] The Historical Society says the lighthouse itself was not damaged, and LighthouseFriends calls the damage in Crescent City the worst tsunami damage on the lower forty-eight's west coast.[^6][^7]

## After the keepers

An automatic light was installed in July 1953, and on November 1 that year the government leased the lighthouse to Del Norte County.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a modern 375 mm lens replaced the fourth-order Fresnel lens at that time. The Historical Society's page says the Coast Guard kept a fifth-order Drumm lens in service until 1965.[^6] The sources do not agree on which lens the automatic light used.

The Historical Society dates the Coast Guard's takeover of the lighthouse to 1936.[^6] The National Register nomination dates it to July 1939, when the Coast Guard took over from the defunct lighthouse service.[^1]

The light continued until about 1965, when a light at the end of the new harbor jetty replaced it.[^1] The Historical Society's page says the light was decommissioned that year.[^6] In 1969 the federal government formally transferred the property to the county for historical monument purposes. It was then leased to the Del Norte County Historical Society, which maintains it as a museum.[^1]

The state recognized the station as State Historical Landmark No. 951 on October 4, 1982.[^1] On December 10, 1982, Battery Point Lighthouse became a private aid to navigation again, with a 1,000-watt beacon.[^1][^6]

## Today

The property belongs to Del Norte County. The Historical Society operates the museum, and the light is a private aid to navigation.[^1][^6]

The island is staffed. Volunteer keepers live there, and the Historical Society's page says the station is "still staffed by volunteer keepers."[^6] Guided tours take visitors into the keepers' quarters and up the tower. The Society lists them as running from 10 a.m. to 4 p.m. in both seasons, tide permitting.[^6]

The island can be reached on foot only at low tide. The Society warns of the crossing, which it says is about two hundred feet (61 m), and of sneaker waves on the rocks. Coons, writing in 1964, gives the distance as three hundred yards (274 m).[^6][^8] The Society advises extreme care whenever wave action may cover the crossing.[^6]
