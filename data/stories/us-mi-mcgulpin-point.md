McGulpin Point Light is a brick lighthouse on the Michigan shore of the Straits of Mackinac, about two miles west of Fort Michilimackinac, in what is now Mackinaw City.[^1][^2] It was lit in 1869 and turned off in 1906.[^2] Emmet County has owned it since 2008 and has opened it to visitors since 2009, with summer tours.[^2][^8]

## The place

Lake Michigan and Lake Huron meet in the Straits. The Waugoshance Light marked the western entry, and the Bois Blanc Island light the eastern.[^2] The county history says the middle of the Straits had no aid to navigation, which made night passage and passage in poor visibility dangerous.[^2]

Congress appropriated $6,000 for a station in August 1854, the county history says.[^2] That money lapsed unspent. Clear title to the land was hard to obtain, and no construction followed for more than a decade.[^2] The Board asked again in 1864, and Congress appropriated $20,000 on 26 July 1866, according to the county.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives the date as 28 July, and says condemnation proceedings under Michigan law had to be started in 1867 to get title to the land.[^7] The sources agree on the sum and differ on the day.

LighthouseFriends says the point is named for John McGulpin, who came to North America around 1760 as an officer in the British army. A deed to the land, which had passed to his son Patrick, was signed by President James Madison in 1811.[^7] The county history names a different first owner. It says John McAlpine and his Native American wife lived on the point in the 1760s, and that his son Patrick McGulpin held the first recorded deed in Emmet County, dated 1811.[^2] The two accounts do not agree on the first owner's name.

The county history also gives Anishinaabek oral tradition: after the Odawa expelled an earlier people from northern Michigan, they moved into Emmet County and settled first at McGulpin Point.[^2] That is tradition as the county tells it, not a dated record.

## Building the light

Work began in 1868 or in early 1869, depending on the source.[^2][^7] LighthouseFriends says an early close of navigation cut short the 1868 season, and that the light was first exhibited on 18 June 1869. The total cost, land included, was $19,782.74.[^7]

The county history says the station copied the plan used at Chambers Island and Eagle Bluff, a style sometimes called Norman Gothic.[^2] The keepers' dwelling and tower were built of Cream City brick, and the tower was capped with a cast-iron lantern and a third-and-a-half order Fresnel lens.[^2] The first floor held a parlor, a kitchen and two bedrooms.[^2]

LighthouseFriends gives the focal plane as 102 feet (31 m) above the lake. It says most of that height comes from the hill the lighthouse stands on, and that the tower itself is about forty feet (12 m) tall.[^7] The archive gives the height of the light as 38 feet (11.6 m).[^1] The archive also gives 1892 as the year the original lens was installed, which does not fit the county's account of a lantern and lens on the 1869 tower. The sources are not reconciled.[^1][^2]

Later work appears in the Lighthouse Board's repair lists. In the fiscal year to June 1896 a well 27 feet (8.2 m) deep was drilled, and in the year to June 1897 some 3,600 bricks were bought for an oil house.[^4] In the year to June 1899 a storm house was built at the front entrance to the keeper's dwelling.[^5]

## The keepers

The archive lists five principal keepers at McGulpin Point between 1869 and 1906.[^1] LighthouseFriends names Charles Louisignau as the first keeper, while the archive lists "Charles Louisignau (1869-1875)" and gives Gabriel Allotte for 1869 alone.[^1][^7] In 1875 Louisignau swapped posts with Thomas P. Dunn of St. Helena Island. Dunn died in 1879, and Gabriel Bourissau took the post for a few months before he went to Little Sable Point Lighthouse.[^7]

James Davenport kept the light for 27 years, from 1879 until the station closed in 1906.[^2] Before McGulpin he had served at Waugoshance and Little Point Sable.[^2] His family lived at the station through each navigation season. They moved to Mackinaw City for the winter so the children could get to school.[^2]

Davenport reported to the district inspector in Milwaukee. The county history says he wrote every week during the winter, and sometimes more often, on ice conditions in the Straits.[^2][^3] It suggests the inspector used those reports to judge when navigation would open on the lakes.[^2] In a March 1890 letter he wrote that "The ice is good and solid," and closed: "This station is in good order."[^2]

In March 1891 his wife and child died, and he missed a weekly report. He wrote to the inspector: "You will see by this report that I did not report to you last week. My wife and child died last week and I could not go up to the light house to report to you as required."[^2] In December 1893 Davenport had gone to Mackinaw City that day, leaving his nine children alone at the station.[^2] He was transferred to Mission Point after McGulpin closed and served there until his retirement in 1917. He died in Mackinaw City on 18 March 1932, at the age of eighty-five.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 5 December 1893 the wooden propeller Waldo A. Avery caught fire in the Straits, the county history says.[^2][^3] It says the captain steered the burning ship toward the light, and that trips to the beached vessel in the station's small skiff brought all seventeen crew ashore.[^2]

A Grand Rapids paper of 11 December 1893 printed a Mackinaw City dispatch, dated 10 December, reporting that what was left of the corn cargo of the burned steamer had been bought.[^6] The paper confirms the burning and the cargo sale. The county history's text matches Terry Pepper's article, so the rescue account is one source, not two.[^2][^3]

## After the keepers

In 1889 Congress authorized a light at Old Mackinac Point, on the condition that McGulpin Point be discontinued. LighthouseFriends says the Board objected, and the condition was dropped when $20,000 was provided for Old Mackinac Point in 1891.[^7] The county history dates the new Old Mackinac Point light and fog signal station to 1892.[^2]

The county history says the Board decided McGulpin no longer served a critical role. The new light at Old Mackinac Point could be seen throughout the Straits, while McGulpin was effectively visible only from the west.[^2] The Board authorized discontinuance on 12 November 1906. Davenport showed the light for the last time at the close of navigation on 15 December that year. The station was boarded up, and its lantern and lens were removed.[^2]

The Board approved an auction in May 1907. The highest bid was $875, but the district inspector thought the property was worth at least twice that, and the bid was rejected.[^2] The property passed into private ownership in July 1913, when Sam Smith, a Mackinaw City businessman, bought it for $1,425.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes that sale as an auction.[^7] A private owner held the lighthouse from 1935 until at least 1965.[^7]

The Peppler family put the property on the market in 2005, and in 2008 Emmet County bought it for $710,000.[^7][^2] Soon after the purchase, the county formed a historical commission to plan the restoration and interpretation of the lighthouse.[^2]

## Today

Emmet County owns the lighthouse and about ten acres, with 336 feet (102 m) of shoreline on the Straits.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives 11.5 acres for the adjoining property in its account of the 2005 listing.[^7] The county reopened the lighthouse with a celebration on 30 May 2009.[^2] It is open for tours from mid-May to mid-October, with an admission of $3 per person.[^8]

Visitors who climb into the lantern room get a wide view of the Straits, LighthouseFriends says.[^7] It also reports that a replica lantern room was placed atop the tower on 23 April 2009.[^7] The county says the lens and lantern were removed when the station closed, and that the eventual disposition of both is undetermined.[^2]

The archive's record describes the light as a private aid to navigation, and not an active one.[^1]
