Browns Point Light stands on a point on the eastern side of the entrance to Commencement Bay, the water approach to the port of Tacoma, in Pierce County, Washington.[^1][^2] A lens lantern on a post was first lit there in December 1887.[^1] The wooden tower that replaced it in 1903 gave way in 1933 to the concrete tower that stands on the point now, 34 feet (10.4 m) high.[^2]

## The place

The water there was busy and often foggy. LighthouseFriends says fog was a regular problem for shipping in the area.[^1] In 1895 the Light-House Board asked Congress for a fog signal at the point, which its report calls "Point Brown." The Board wrote that a fog signal was "urgently required" for "the numerous water crafts plying between Tacoma and points down the sound," and it estimated that a bell struck by machinery, with its buildings and dwellings, would cost about $6,000.[^3]

The point had been part of a reservation. The National Register form says the Browns Point area "became part of the 23,000 acre Puyallup Indian reservation, established by the Treaty of Medicine Creek in 1854," and that it "remained reservation property until the late 19th century, when some of the Puyallup lands began to be sold."[^2] LighthouseFriends names the owner of the point at the time of the first light as Joe Douette.[^1]

The point has carried several names. LighthouseFriends says Lieutenant Charles T. Wilkes, who surveyed Puget Sound in 1841, named it Point Harris. In 1877 Lieutenant Ambrose Wyckoff marked it Point Brown on his charts, a name locals used to honor an early settler.[^1] The National Register form says Captain George Vancouver and his party are believed to have been the first Europeans to come ashore at Browns Point, in May 1792.[^2] In 1934 the Browns Point Garden Club placed a commemorative tablet on the lighthouse. Its inscription recalls the day Vancouver and his associates dined at the point.[^1]

The light was important to the Mosquito Fleet. The National Register form calls the fleet "the hundreds of boats that constituted the short-haul transport system for goods and people."[^2]

## Building the light

The first light was a lens lantern on a post, not a tower. The National Register form describes it as "a fixed white light lens lantern placed on a white post 12 feet above sea level."[^2] LighthouseFriends dates its first showing to 12 December 1887, the same day a similar beacon was lit at Point Robinson.[^1]

Congress acted in 1900. An act of 6 June 1900 "appropriated $6,000 to establish a light and fog-signal at Browns Point." The Board reported that the price asked for the proposed site was excessive, so "proceedings for the condemnation were commenced."[^4] The sources do not agree on what was paid for the land. The National Register form says that "two property owners received $1,500 each for the land needed to build a lighthouse." LighthouseFriends says the condemnation paid $3,000 to the owner, and that Congress had to appropriate another $3,200 in June 1902 before work could start.[^2][^1]

The only bid came in over the money available. LighthouseFriends says the bid of $6,090 exceeded the funds on hand, so the bidder offered $5,300, and a contract was signed on 5 March 1903.[^1] The National Register form says the new wooden tower was completed in October 1903 "under the direction of the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers."[^2] LighthouseFriends gives the first lighting as 26 October 1903.[^1]

The fog bell has a disputed date. The National Register form says it was cast in Philadelphia in 1852.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives 1855 and names the Philadelphia firm of Bernard & Co. as its maker. The Service's 1934 Bulletin also says the bell was cast in 1855.[^1][^9] The sources also differ on how often the striker had to be wound. The National Register form says the clock weights were "wound by hand every 90 minutes until 1922," while LighthouseFriends says the striking mechanism had to be wound every forty-five minutes.[^2][^1]

In 1916 the Service changed the light. The Bulletin lists "Browns Point, Commencement Bay, Wash., oil to acetylene" among the lights whose illuminant changed that year.[^6] The National Register form says that electric power was installed in 1922.[^2]

The wooden tower came down in 1933. The National Register form says "the wooden structure was razed and burned," and that the present concrete tower replaced it. The tower "rises 34 feet high."[^2] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive record gives the tower's year as 1933 and its height from base to lantern as 34 feet (10.4 m).[^10]

## The keepers

Oscar V. Brown was the keeper from 1903 to 1939.[^10] LighthouseFriends says he joined the Lighthouse Service in 1890, and that he was in charge of Smith Island Light from 1898 to 1903 before he came to Browns Point.[^1] He arrived by tender in 1903 with his wife, Annie.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the family's animals were lowered to the water by a sling and swam ashore. It describes Brown giving piano and cornet lessons, planting an orchard and keeping a flower garden.[^1]

The bell needed a keeper's hand. LighthouseFriends says that when fog settled around the point, the striking mechanism for the 1,200-pound bell had to be wound every forty-five minutes. When the machinery failed, Annie Brown timed the strokes at twenty-second intervals and signaled her husband to strike the bell.[^1] In 1934 the Bulletin reports that "O. V. Brown, keeper at Browns Point since 1903, was also present" at the college's founders' day ceremony, and that he gave the history of the bell.[^9]

LighthouseFriends says Brown retired in 1939 after thirty-six years at the point.[^1] The roster then lists Arthur J. Woods (1939 to 1944), Cyril D. Beaulieu (1944 to 1956), William Covington (1956 to 1957), Harold G. Nelms (1957 to 1960) and Robert V. Deason (1960 to 1963) as principal keepers.[^10] The National Register form says numerous keepers served eight-hour watches at the station until 1963, when the Coast Guard "replaced the three-man crew with automated equipment."[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Bulletin for 1914 records a storm on the Pacific coast. "During the storm of November 29 on the Pacific coast," the Bulletin says, the New Dungeness and Browns Point stations had their "revetments damaged." The damage across the district "amounted to about $11,000."[^5]

On 8 September 1917, the Bulletin reports, Brown rescued three Japanese from drowning near the light station and furnished them clothing.[^7] LighthouseFriends says he gave them dry clothing.[^1] In 1922, according to LighthouseFriends, he towed a disabled fishing boat to a nearby wharf and then repaired its machinery so it could get under way again.[^1]

Fog was a regular problem at the point. In 1931 the keeper sent the Bulletin an account of a dense fog that "travels at high speed, often against the wind." He had often seen the passenger steamer Tacoma come out of Tacoma harbor "traveling 18 miles an hour," and in one such case the fog reached Browns Point five minutes before the steamer did.[^8] LighthouseFriends says Browns Point is said to have the longest periods of dense fog on Puget Sound.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over the light when Brown retired in 1939, LighthouseFriends says.[^1] After automation in 1963, the diaphragm foghorn was activated by a telephone call from Foss Launch and Tug Company.[^1]

The point became a public park a year after automation, LighthouseFriends says.[^1] The National Register form says that since 1964 Pierce County Parks and the Metropolitan Park District of Tacoma have alternately taken responsibility for the grounds "under an agreement with the U.S. Coast Guard."[^2] The keeper's dwelling was rented as a private home from 1964 to 2000.[^1]

The bell went through a few hands. The Bulletin says it had been "transferred to the college for preservation."[^9] LighthouseFriends says the bell hung in a church on Fox Island from 1984 and returned to the point in July 2000, where it is now housed in the old pump house.[^1]

The National Register form, prepared in August 1988, describes the tower and the generator house as "boarded up today."[^2] LighthouseFriends says restoration began in July 2021 and cost $150,000. It ended with a rededication on Sunday, October 17, after the Coast Guard placed an LED lamp inside the lantern.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by the Coast Guard, and the grounds are a park. LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds are open, the keeper's dwelling is open during tours, and the tower is closed.[^1] The restored keeper's cottage holds a small history museum, and the dwelling can be rented by the week.[^1] Points Northeast Historical Society restored the dwelling in 2000 and furnished it with period pieces.[^1] The account here comes from LighthouseFriends, which describes the 2021 work, so check the park's current hours before visiting. Do not climb the tower, which is closed.
