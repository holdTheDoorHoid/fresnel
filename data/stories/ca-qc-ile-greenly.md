Île Greenly Lighthouse stands on a low island in the Strait of Belle Isle, about three kilometres southwest of Blanc-Sablon in Quebec.[^1][^3] The first light there was lit on 13 August 1878.[^1] Fire destroyed that tower in 1947.[^2][^1] The light has been automated since 1983 and still operates, but the tower is closed to visitors, and the island lies inside a migratory bird sanctuary.[^2][^1][^3]

## The place

The island is low and flat.[^1][^3] Environment and Climate Change Canada says of the sanctuary's two islands, "Both of these islands are mostly flat, only 15 to 19 metres in elevation."[^3] In 1860 John Page, chief engineer of the Department of Public Works, described the island as lying "on the Labrador side of the entrance of the Gulf through the strait", about 13 miles west-south-west of Point Amour. LighthouseFriends quotes his report.[^1]

The Department's Quebec lighthouse division in 1900 covered "strait of Belle isle, north and west coasts of Newfoundland and Labrador".[^6] In the year ended 30 June 1901 the Department's inspector spent part of his time "particularly on the great routes of navigation, including the Belle Isle and Cape Race routes to Montreal".[^5]

## Building the light

Parliament appropriated money for a lighthouse on Greenly Island in 1875. Two years later a contract went to James Mowat of Chatham, New Brunswick, for $5,290.[^1] The first tower was wood, octagonal, and built into one end of the keeper's dwelling.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives its height as seventy feet (about 21 m); the Quebec register gives 20 m (66 ft).[^1][^2]

Louis Couillard de Beaumont was appointed first keeper and lit the light on 13 August 1878.[^1] Its pattern was half a minute of white, half a minute of red, half a minute of white and then a one-and-a-half-minute eclipse, with nine lamps turning once every three minutes.[^1]

Before the steam foghorn of 1888, the fog signal was a gun. In 1882 a thirty-two-pound carronade replaced the nine-pound one used as the fog gun.[^1] A steam foghorn went into service at the opening of navigation in 1888. In 1896 it was moved 900 feet to the island's southern end, and a 900-foot handrail ran from the light to it, so keepers could reach it in high winds and when the ground was covered in ice.[^1]

The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1905 said the former whistle was being "replaced by a modern diaphone plant".[^8] The new fog-alarm building went into operation on 15 August 1906. It is a rectangular wooden building, painted white with a red roof, which stands 212 m (695 ft) from the lighthouse and 18 m (60 ft) back from the water's edge.[^10]

The lens changed in 1906 as well. The report for the year ended 30 June 1906 says "On July 9, 1906, a second order dioptric single quick-flashing light was put in operation at Greenly island", replacing a catoptric group revolving light.[^9] LighthouseFriends gives 6 July 1906 and names the lens's makers, Chance Brothers of Birmingham.[^1] The two dates disagree, and this story gives both. In the year ended 30 June 1908 the tower and keeper's dwelling were reshingled and repainted.[^11] The keeper's dwelling was extended in 1923.[^12][^1]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends lists seven head keepers from 1878 to 1983. Two of them also appear by name in the Department's keeper lists.[^1][^5][^7][^9]

The first keeper was paid $400 a year and given $200 to hire an assistant for the fog gun, which was fired every half hour in fog and snowstorms.[^1] Beaumont kept the light until 1890.[^1]

George Fagot followed him.[^1] The Department's keeper list for the year ended 30 June 1901 prints "Fagot, George Greenly Island Lighthouse and Fog Alarm".[^5] The foghorn was moved in 1896, during his time.[^1]

Octave Dubois was keeper from 1903 to 1908 by LighthouseFriends' dates.[^1] The Department's keeper lists for the years ended 30 June 1904 and 30 June 1906 both name "Dubois, Octave" at Greenly Island, Light and Fog Alarm.[^7][^9] The new lens and the fog-alarm building came in the summer of 1906, while he was keeper.[^1][^9][^10]

LighthouseFriends names Napoléon Coté as head keeper from 1908 to 1920, and Jacques Letemplier from 1920 to 1939.[^1] Letemplier was keeper when the Bremen came down on the island in 1928.[^13][^14][^15]

LighthouseFriends lists Chesley G. Thomas as head keeper from 1939 to 1968 and John Thomas from 1968 to 1983, when the station was automated and its last keeper left.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1900 printed a Greenly Island note on the winter's ice. The first ice formed on 15 January 1900, and from that date "heavy open ice filled the Strait until about the end of May, when it all disappeared."[^6]

In April 1928 the Bremen, a Junkers monoplane, was flying the first east-to-west crossing of the Atlantic when it came down near the lighthouse.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the flight lasted about thirty-six and a half hours, and that the plane came down on a small pond near the lighthouse.[^1] The monument's inscription, as LighthouseFriends transcribes it, names the crew as Baron Ehrenfreid Guenther Von Huenefeld, Captain Hermann Koehl and Major James C. Fitzmaurice, and dates the landing to 13 April 1928.[^1]

The Indianapolis Times of 14 April printed the landing under the headline "BREMEN CROSSES OCEAN SAFELY, LANDS ON TINY ICE-BOUND COASTAL ISLE". It reported that the Canadian government steamer Montcalm had been dispatched to Greenly Island to bring the crew back to civilization.[^13]

A first-person instalment in the Washington Times, dated from Greenly Island on 23 April, is by Baron Günther von Hünefeld. He writes as the Bremen's passenger, and says the flyers' wheels "touched the ice of the lake here at Greenly". He says that "Letemplier conducted us" to his house, brought supplies from his larder, and that there was "no supply ship to Greenly since last autumn".[^14] The Quebec heritage register, in French, says the keeper and his family welcomed and cared for the flyers until they could be rescued.[^2]

The Indianapolis Times of 25 April reported that residents of nearby hamlets had "dared the dangerous ice blockades" to come and see the airplane. The keeper, Jacques Letemplier, said: "The surprise of the Bremen's arrival almost paralyzed us."[^15][^1] The instalment says the crew waited on the island for Fitzmaurice to return from outside "with our spare parts and benzol".[^14] LighthouseFriends, citing a 1928 newspaper, says Fitzmaurice left in a relief plane.[^1]

Letemplier and his brother built a cairn of granite boulders gathered from the island's pond, and a monument was dedicated on 14 August 1928.[^1]

Fire destroyed the first light in 1947.[^2][^1] None of the sources gives a cause or reports injuries.

## After the keepers

The Quebec register says that two years after the fire the Department of Transport built a steel skeleton tower on the island to replace the light.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes the replacement as an electric revolving optic on a steel tower, with a dwelling and a powerhouse nearby.[^1]

In 1983 the light was automated and a second metal tower replaced the 1949 one. The last keeper left.[^1][^2] In 1998 the site and buildings were transferred to the municipality of Blanc-Sablon.[^2]

## Today

The light still operates. The Quebec register says "Ce phare est toujours opérationnel", meaning the light is still operational.[^2] The Canadian Coast Guard's list of lights still carries it, on the southwest part of the island.[^4] The island is part of the Baie de Brador Migratory Bird Sanctuary, created in 1925 to protect a major seabird nesting site. Quebec's largest colony of Atlantic puffins is in the sanctuary.[^3]

The tower is closed to visitors, while LighthouseFriends records the grounds as open.[^1] The sanctuary's page asks visitors to read its restrictions.[^3] LighthouseFriends also says a distant view can be had from Blanc-Sablon, and that the better view is from the water.[^1] A replica of the Bremen can be seen at the airport in Blanc-Sablon, according to a LighthouseFriends note.[^1]
