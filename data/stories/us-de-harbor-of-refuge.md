A white-and-black cast-iron tower stands 76 feet (23 m) above the seaward end of a stone breakwater off Lewes, Sussex County, Delaware, where it has marked the way into Delaware Bay since 1926.[^1] It is the second permanent lighthouse on the breakwater, and for most of its working life it was also a home: the keepers' quarters were inside the tower itself.[^2]

## The place

The breakwater exists because an older one was not deep enough. The Delaware Breakwater, begun in 1829, gave an anchorage of only about sixteen feet at low tide, too shallow for several new naval and commercial vessels. A commission of three Army engineers, appointed in 1890, picked a deep-water pocket west of the main ship channel for a national harbor of refuge.[^3] Work began on 4 May 1897 and finished on 11 December 1901, at a cost of roughly $2.2 million; the stone wall is 7,950 feet (2,423 m) long, with a set of ice piers across the upper end of the harbor.[^3] The Light-House Board described it while it was still going up as a stone wall that would "provide a secure anchorage area of over 700 acres."[^4]

## Building the light

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 said that, with the harbor nearing completion, the interests of commerce and navigation required a light, a fog signal and a keeper's dwelling at the southern entrance to it. It put the cost at no more than $30,000.[^5] Congress appropriated that sum in an act approved 6 June 1900.[^4]

The first lights were temporary. A 30-foot (9 m) frame tower with a fixed red light went up at the southeast end of the breakwater, and a post light of the same kind at the other end; both were shown for the first time on 1 January 1902.[^3][^1] (The US Lighthouse Society's digital archive dates the station to 1901,[^6] a year before the date the other two sources give.) A 700-pound (318 kg) fog bell worked by a striking machine, and a temporary dwelling, followed in May 1903.[^3]

The permanent lighthouse cost more than Congress had first allowed. By the time work was ready to start, labor and materials had risen so much that another $20,000 was appropriated in 1906, and to save money the brick superstructure first proposed was replaced by a wooden one.[^3] The metalwork for the concrete foundation reached Lewes in June 1907. Seventeen months later, on 20 November 1908, the light was exhibited from a white, hexagonal, three-story frame tower on a brown cylindrical iron foundation: a fourth-order lens showing a white flash every 12 seconds, and a compressed-air siren for fog. The work cost $49,941.[^3][^7]

The tower that stands today replaced it in 1926. The Lighthouse Service reported the work as a reconstruction that raised the light, improved the light and fog signal, lifted the deck above the reach of the waves and strengthened the structure; the Bureau said it was done as part of a general scheme to improve the aids around the bay, because erosion at Cape Henlopen had made it necessary to give up the old lighthouse there.[^2] The new tower is a cast-iron shell: a cylindrical pier lined with reinforced concrete, with a brick-lined tower of three stories above it holding the keepers' quarters, and the fog signal on the third floor. Its fourth-order lens turned on ball bearings, driven by a clockwork run by weights inside the hollow central column.[^2] The light was put back in commission on 15 November 1926, and the fog signal re-established on 2 November 1927.[^2] The National Register nomination and the Delaware state marker describe the 1926 building as a replacement for the 1908 tower, while the Lighthouse Service called it a rebuilding of the station; the sources read do not say how much of the old foundation was reused.[^8][^1][^2]

## The keepers

The archive's roster begins with Charles E. Marshall in 1903, who is named as the keeper in charge of the temporary lights.[^6][^3] After him come Thomas Hasting (1903-1906), Thomas T. Moore (1906-1910), Joseph M. Harrison (1910-1914) and William H. Schellenger, who served as an assistant from 1909 and as head keeper from 1914 to 1919.[^6] The roster lists a head keeper and a first and a second assistant.[^6]

The longest stay was Robert C. Taylor's. The archive lists him as second assistant in 1916-1917, first assistant in 1917-1919 and head keeper from 1919 to 1950.[^6] Under him served George H. Hickman (first assistant 1920-1926), Virgil L. Dennis (first assistant 1926-1942) and Theodore W. Simpler, who was second assistant from 1924 to 1944.[^6]

Some of the work was mechanical. In 1916 the Bulletin reported that the station's oil engines ran hot after seven hours of service with the fresh-water cooling system they had used. A temporary salt-water cooling system installed in November 1915 had given satisfaction for a year, and one engine ran 36 hours without overheating.[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A storm struck before the permanent lighthouse was built. On 16 September 1903 a southern gale that had been working up the coast struck the Delaware capes. A New London schooner, the Hattie A. Marsh, loaded with stone from Maine for Philadelphia, was caught outside the new breakwater; her captain tried for the harbor of refuge but had to anchor and ride it out, and, as the Daily Kennebec Journal reported, "Her anchor, however, did not hold." She was dashed on the rocks of the harbor of refuge. The steam pilot boat Philadelphia saved only Mate Norman Campbell and one other man, and Captain J. B. Mehaffy and four of the crew were drowned, in the paper's account.[^10] Lighthousefriends tells it differently: the schooner slipped her anchors and was dashed against the stone breakwater, and the captain is named Joseph Mehaffey.[^3] The breakwater keeper, Marshall, later reported that the windows of his dwelling there were smashed and the whole house flooded, and that the storm had swept away the fog bell, striking machine and frame tower.[^3]

The exposed wooden tower of 1908 took repeated punishment. The Bulletin of 1918 reported that in a storm the sea broke into the basements of the towers at Harbor of Refuge and Brandywine Shoal, flooded them, moved the heavy riprap about and caused leaks in the structures.[^11] Lighthousefriends prints entries from the station logbook by Taylor recording that two storms, in April 1918 and February 1920, each shifted the big light about 2 inches on its foundation.[^3]

The February 1920 storm was the worst. The Bulletin reported damage of $14,160 at the station: about 40 feet (12 m) of gallery decking ripped up, all the outbuildings on the platform behind the tower destroyed, including the fog bell and its equipment and the boat davits, and the wharf badly damaged.[^12] The Department commended Taylor, then keeper, and assistant Hickman, and Lighthousefriends ties the commendation to this storm.[^13][^3] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1920 also names the February storm as having seriously damaged the station and puts the emergency repairs at an estimated $14,000.[^14] The next year's report said that the station was "urgently in need of additional riprap protection and other repairs and remodelling," after severe storms of the past winter, and that its fog-signal plant was old and needed renewing.[^15]

The Lighthouse Service had been arguing since at least 1915 that the breakwater light should take over from Cape Henlopen. "Every purpose now served by Cape Henlopen Light," it wrote, "would be better served by the rebuilding of Harbor of Refuge Light to a height of about 140 feet."[^16] Congress did not provide money until 1922, and the tower that was finished stands 76 feet above the breakwater, against the roughly 140 feet the Service had proposed.[^3][^8] Cape Henlopen Light was discontinued on 1 October 1924 and the old tower fell into the sea in April 1926.[^3]

The station's keepers also answered boats in trouble. On 2 July 1916 Schellenger and second assistant Clinton H. Hickman rescued a disabled motor boat with six men aboard that was being blown offshore from the station.[^17] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1921 Taylor and second assistant Floyd Schmierer helped an injured man whose power boat was drifting to sea in a northeaster.[^18] On 12 July (the Bulletin's 1930 volume reports it) Taylor and Simpler helped a fishing party of eight men whose boat was disabled by a line tangled in the propeller; the boat was towed to the station and raised so the line could be removed.[^19]

On 14 to 16 April 1929 Taylor logged wind of about 78 miles an hour and a hard time keeping the light working. "The lighthouse shook bad," he wrote, and he asked for stone on the northeast side to break the sea before it reached the house.[^3]

## After the keepers

The National Register nomination says the two lighthouses in the district continued to be manned until the practice was discontinued in the 1950s,[^8] but the archive lists Coast Guard crews at Harbor of Refuge from 1959 to 1973, and the Delaware state marker and Lighthousefriends say the Coast Guard automated the light in December 1973.[^6][^1][^3] One of the last crew, Angelo Rigazio, later became president of the Delaware Bay Lighthouse Keepers and Friends Association and gave commentary on its boat tours.[^3]

A DCB-36 aerobeacon replaced the Fresnel lens in 1947, and a Vega VRB-25 replaced the aerobeacon in 1996.[^3] The tower had a major overhaul of its metalwork and concrete foundation in 1998-1999.[^3] The non-profit Delaware River and Bay Lighthouse Foundation took a 20-year lease from the Coast Guard on 1 April 2002, ran its first tour on 21 June 2003, and received the lighthouse under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act in 2004; the foundation gives the date as 30 November 2004.[^3][^20]

## Today

The tower is owned by the Delaware River and Bay Lighthouse Foundation,[^3][^20] and the state marker describes it as showing a flashing white light 72 feet (22 m) above mean high water.[^1] It stands on a breakwater that is itself in poor repair. Lighthousefriends reports that Congress paid in 2007 for an Army Corps of Engineers study, and that a contract of nearly $3 million repaired the wall under and near the tower in 2011. It also records that winter storms in 2010 damaged the docks, that a new dock was installed in 2016, and that the tower reopened to tours in 2018.[^3] The foundation's own website now says differently: the Corps of Engineers is still studying the breakwater, has asked that no public tours be run until further notice, and the foundation is "NOT permitted to offer lighthouse tours to the public at this time."[^20]

The tower is an offshore lighthouse, reached only by boat, and it is closed to visitors for the time being. A distant view of it can be had from the northeastern end of Cape Henlopen State Park, north of Lewes.[^3]
