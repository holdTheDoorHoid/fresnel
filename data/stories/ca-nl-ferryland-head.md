Ferryland Head Light stands at the end of a long peninsula southeast of the town of Ferryland, on the Avalon Peninsula of Newfoundland and Labrador. It was first lit in 1871, in a round tower of stone and red brick, later encased in cast iron.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Ferryland Head lies roughly halfway between Cape Spear and Cape Race, and the head itself is an obstacle to ships on that run.[^1] The town was older than the light. Sir George Calvert bought land at Ferryland in 1620, and Calvert's first settlers arrived the following year.[^1]

The light first needed a law. In the spring of 1859 the Legislative Council and the Assembly passed "An Act to provide for the erection of a Lighthouse on or near Ferryland Head," which was to be paid for out of light dues. The work did not begin for another decade.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1869 William Campbell and Thomas Burridge won the contract to build the tower and a keeper's dwelling. A road was cut from Ferryland Beach to the site, and arrangements were made with the Edinburgh engineers David and Thomas Stevenson to supply the lantern room and the lens.[^1] The 2006 municipal heritage designation calls Campbell the contractor and Burridge the mason, both of St. John's, and dates the dwelling to 1870 and 1871.[^5]

The tower was built in 1870 and 1871. It is round and was built of stone and red brick, and its lantern room has two rows of triangular glass panes.[^1] Beside it stood a two-storey dwelling with a pitched roof, built for the keeper and his assistant.[^1]

The third-order Fresnel lens first showed a fixed white light on 1 October 1871. Austin Oke, the keeper at Harbour Grace Island, was sent over to help install the apparatus.[^1]

The brickwork gave trouble almost from the start. Inspector John Nevill recorded new cracks in the brick year after year, and an experimental coat of silicate paint was put on in 1878.[^1] By 1887 he judged the damage serious enough to need either an iron casing or a new cement facing. The iron went on in 1892.[^1][^3] The Lighthouse Directory gives the tower's height as 14 m (46 ft).[^3]

The keepers' dwelling got a concrete cellar in 1886, after rats had got into the old one.[^1]

## The keepers

Michael Kearney, a master boat-builder, was hired as the first keeper. William Costello was his assistant.[^1]

In 1875 Inspector Nevill investigated reports that the light had gone out during the assistant's watch. Costello was reassigned to Cape Pine, Kearney was reprimanded, and Philip Keough was appointed assistant.[^1] In April 1882 Keough filed a formal complaint of serious neglect against Kearney. After an inquiry by the Board of Works, Kearney was retired, and William Costello came back from Cape Pine as head keeper.[^1]

In October 1878 diphtheria struck the station. LighthouseFriends, citing Nevill, blames a shawl that had been used to wrap one of Keough's children. The first two children died a day apart, and the twins died a week later. Only two of the nine Keough children lived to adulthood.[^1]

After Keough died in 1893, only Costellos were employed as keepers. William Costello kept the light until 1905, when his son John William took over. John William died in 1927, aged 51, and his brother Augustan followed him. Augustan kept the light until 1939, when William "Billy" Costello took charge. Billy retired in 1970, and the keepers' work at Ferryland ended then.[^1]

A fire damaged the lens in 1919. Measurements had been taken for a new one, but it had not arrived when a kerosene vapour light went into service on 1 August 1921, with a different flash pattern. The light was electrified in 1931.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 4 August 1883 the Scandinavian ship Octavia, on her way from Sydney, Nova Scotia, to St. John's, ran aground just offshore in dense fog. LighthouseFriends says the ship was within hailing distance of the lighthouse, and that Keough shouted for the crew to throw a line ashore. When the ninth and last man was coming in, the rope snapped. Keough, with a rope round his waist, went into the surf and rescued him. The Royal Humane Society gave Keough a silver medal the following spring.[^1] The 2006 municipal designation gives the date as 14 August 1883.[^5] The two sources disagree on the day.

In September 1914 the collier Cacouna went ashore near the light and was a total loss.[^1] Her captain and crew wrote a note of thanks to the Costello keepers and their wives for their hospitality during a short stay after the rescue. The Evening Telegram printed it a few days later, and LighthouseFriends reproduces it.[^1]

The 2006 municipal designation also says the Danish ship Sigrid lost her entire crew near the light in 1903, and that local men saved the crew of the Evelyn from the icy cliffs in a January 1913 blizzard.[^5] No other source here gives either event, so both stay unconfirmed.

## After the keepers

When William Costello retired in 1970, the Memorial University Extension Service placed the artist Gerry Squires in the dwelling under its artist-in-residence program. Squires began art classes at the light in 1971 and left in 1983. The dwelling then stood empty for about twenty years.[^1]

In 2003 two Newfoundland women began selling picnic lunches on the headland under the name Lighthouse Picnics. The food was prepared in Ferryland and carried out to the site. In 2004 they restored the dwelling and began using it for the business.[^1]

The Coast Guard refurbished the tower in the mid-1990s.[^3] The lighthouse was declared a Recognized Federal Heritage Building in 1991, and the keeper's dwelling was designated a municipal heritage site by the Town of Ferryland in 2006.[^1][^5]

In August 2012 ownership of the station passed to the Town of Ferryland.[^3] The Lighthouse Directory says the keeper's house is owned by a local development association, which leases it as a café. LighthouseFriends says the town already owned the dwelling when the lighthouse was transferred in 2012. The two accounts disagree about who owns the dwelling.[^1][^3]

## Today

The Coast Guard's list gives the light as a flash of white every ten seconds, with a focal height of 57.9 m and a range of 15 nautical miles. The tower is red and white.[^4] The Lighthouse Directory gives a white flash every six seconds.[^3] The original lighting apparatus is on display at the Ferryland Museum in town.[^3]

The light station is reached on foot, at the end of an easily walked trail of 2.5 km (1.5 mi).[^3] The café in the dwelling is open daily from mid-June through September, and lunch reservations are essential.[^3] LighthouseFriends gives the picnic business's hours as open daily except Mondays, from late May through September.[^1] The tower is closed to visitors.[^3]
