Buffalo Harbor Light was first lit in 1872 at the north end of a detached breakwater that guards the entrance to Buffalo's harbor on Lake Erie.[^1][^3][^4][^6] The Lighthouse Board called it "Buffalo Breakwater, north end."[^6] Its tower was rebuilt between 1912 and 1914.[^4] In 1958 a lake freighter struck it and tilted it 15 degrees.[^5] A 71-foot (21.5 m) automated tower replaced it in 1961.[^5][^3][^18]

## The place

Buffalo's harbor lies at the eastern end of Lake Erie, at the head of the Niagara River, 16 miles (25 km) south of Niagara Falls.[^18] The harbor had lights before the breakwater.[^3] The first tower, of 1818, was quickly obscured by the smoke of the village.[^3] Its replacement, of 1833, was built on a pier near the harbor's entrance.[^3]

In 1838 Lieutenant Charles T. Platt inspected the harbor and noted the "insufficiency of the present entrance to the harbor," as LighthouseFriends quotes his report.[^3] Work on a detached breakwater began in 1868. It was 4,000 feet (1,220 m) long and lay 2,500 feet (760 m) out in the lake, to give ships more room to dock and turn.[^3][^4]

In 1870 the Treasury Department contracted for two lights on the breakwater, with $30,000 appropriated for them.[^3][^4] LighthouseFriends says the north-end light was to have keepers' quarters, because the breakwater could be hard to reach in some seasons. The appropriation "would not admit of a very elaborate structure," and the building would have to be timber.[^3]

## Building the light

On 18 May 1871 a forty-foot-square (12 m) crib was sunk at the north end of the breakwater. LighthouseFriends gives its position as twenty feet (6 m) behind the breakwater's end and twenty-three feet (7 m) from it.[^3] Work stopped on 9 September 1871 because of what LighthouseFriends quotes as "continued and irregular settling of the pier," and did not resume until the next spring.[^3]

The superstructure was framed in oak timbers and held by iron rods. It had a square keeper's house with a short octagonal tower rising from the roof.[^3] The U.S. Lighthouse Society archive's record gives the tower's material as iron, not timber.[^1] The two sources disagree, and this story does not settle the question.

The light was a fixed fourth-order Fresnel lens, 37 feet (11.3 m) above the lake, according to LighthouseFriends. The Buffalo history site describes it as a fixed red beacon at the same height.[^3][^4] The archive's record gives a fourth-order lens installed in 1872, but a height of 53.5 feet (16.3 m) above water.[^1] The story uses the 37-foot (11.3 m) figure that the two secondary sources give. The fog bell was moved from the old 1833 tower to the new station.[^3][^4]

The bell did not work well. The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 says that "A bell is used as a fog signal at this light-station. It is inefficient," and recommends "that it be replaced by a steam fog signal."[^6] LighthouseFriends says funding for a steam signal came through in August 1892. A ten-inch steam whistle was ready for operation on 30 September 1893.[^3]

The keeper's dwelling and light tower, which had stood in the center of the crib, were removed to the west corner "to provide space for the erection of a fog-signal house."[^19] The report for the year ended 30 June 1894 says the whistle ran for 352 hours that year and burned nearly 27 tons of coal.[^7]

In June 1895 the Board changed the signal after complaints. The report for the year ended 30 June 1895 says the signal was "an annoyance to the people of Buffalo," and it went from two blasts of three seconds each minute to one. A reflector was put on the signal house.[^8] LighthouseFriends describes the earlier pattern as a three-second blast every thirty seconds, which matches the two-blasts-a-minute pattern in the Board's report.[^3]

In 1899 the Board raised the lantern 12 feet (3.7 m). The report says "The light will now be less obscured in the direction of Buffalo Harbor and should be seen from a greater distance on the lake."[^9]

The steam signal was stopped on 20 June 1900, and a fog bell was rung in its place until 20 July.[^10] The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1901 records new water-tube boilers, and says the steam signals had been "temporarily discontinued on June 20, 1900."[^11]

LighthouseFriends says the Board reported in 1908 that the fog-signal house was settling and the lighthouse was "leaking and too much decayed for economical repairs." It also says "Congress allocated $60,000 on March 4, 1911" for rebuilding the station.[^3][^4]

In 1912 the old light and fog signal were removed, and a temporary light and fog signal was set up, according to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin.[^12] The new house was built of "vitrified cream colored brick, with trim of Westerly granite" on a tall concrete pier.[^3] The work ran from 1912 to 1914.[^4] By the 1914 Bulletin the rebuilding was 90 per cent complete.[^13]

LighthouseFriends says the contractor finished on 25 April 1914, and the fog signal came on 15 July. The third-order Fresnel lens was moved from the 1833 tower.[^3][^4] The Bulletin reported that a diaphone fog signal had gone into commission at the station that year.[^14] LighthouseFriends calls it the first diaphone installed in the United States. This story has not confirmed that claim.[^3]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists ten principal keepers from 1872 to 1914.[^1] The first, Howard Winship, served two years, from 1872 to 1874. His successor, Myron H. Woolsey, served one year. James S. Stygall was principal keeper from 1877 to 1893, which was the longest stint in that first run of keepers.[^1]

Several men moved up from assistant to principal. Edward Ahart was first assistant from 1893 to 1903, and principal from 1903 to 1905.[^1] Michael A. Fitzpatrick was first assistant from 1903 to 1905, and principal from 1905 to 1906.[^1] William L. Gordon was first assistant in 1910 and principal from 1910 to 1914. LighthouseFriends calls him "Captain William L. Gordon."[^1][^3]

The archive's list for the rebuilt station starts in 1914 with Thomas H. Holtan as principal, until 1928.[^2] James Rawson appears as a fourth assistant from at least 1919, and as principal from 1929 to 1943.[^2] Bert E. Egelston moved through three assistant ranks between 1917 and 1929.[^2] Edward F. Miller was third assistant from 1929 and principal from 1943 to 1947.[^2] The archive lists Henry S. Smith as a fourth assistant from 1939 to 1941, and under the Coast Guard heading from 1941 to 1946.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The local history site lists four collisions that the breakwater light suffered: a tug in 1899, a barge in 1900, a freighter in 1909 and a steamer in 1910.[^4] LighthouseFriends gives the 1910 steamer's name as the Norseman.[^3]

On 26 July 1958 the freighter Frontenac came out of the Buffalo River and struck the breakwater light.[^5][^3] The local history site says the crash tilted the lighthouse 15 degrees and moved it nearly 20 feet backward.[^5] LighthouseFriends says it was left with a list of fifteen degrees. A temporary light tower had to be put up to replace the damaged lighthouse.[^3]

The two accounts name different keepers. The local history site says one of the two Coast Guard keepers on duty was fishing on the adjoining breakwater, saw the ship's course swing wider than usual, and yelled a warning to his watchmate.[^5] LighthouseFriends names the keeper tending the light, George Harrington, who went down three flights of stairs and across to the breakwater when he realized the ship would strike the station.[^3]

## After the keepers

In 1921 a submarine cable was laid in a trench between the breakwater and the south pier. LighthouseFriends says the next year it allowed the light and fog-signal compressors to be electrified.[^3]

The local history site says the breakwater light got one of the first radio beacon installations on the Great Lakes in 1925.[^5] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that the service contemplated six more radio fog signals, and named the Buffalo Breakwater among the places.[^16] LighthouseFriends gives the radio beacon's start as 15 April 1927.[^3] The sources do not agree on the year. The index to the 1924 to 1929 Bulletins lists a radio beacon at the Buffalo light station.[^17]

LighthouseFriends says the exterior walls were covered with steel in 1929.[^3]

The local history site says that in 1962 the three Coast Guardsmen who were the last of the port's lighthouse keepers were relieved from duty, the same year the 1903 south entrance light was automated. It does not say which light they served.[^5]

## Today

The 1961 tower is a white, automated tower of 71 feet (21.5 m). Rowlett's directory lists it as active, with a green flash every 2.5 seconds.[^18] The Pittsburgh firm American Demolition Company took down the 1914 lighthouse in 1961.[^5][^3] The only remnant of the old station is a metal oil house a short distance from the modern beacon.[^3]

The three sources agree on the place: the outer end of the breakwater. The local history site says the tower stands at the end of the harbor's farthest breakwater.[^5] LighthouseFriends places it at the southern end of the outermost breakwater.[^3] Rowlett puts it at the south end of the outer (detached) breakwater, off the mouth of the Buffalo River.[^18]

The site and tower are closed, and the light can be reached only by boat. Rowlett lists harbor tours and gives the owner as the U.S. Coast Guard.[^18] LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard owns the lighthouse.[^3]
