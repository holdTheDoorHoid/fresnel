A round, shingled brick tower stands alone on a red-clay point where the Northumberland Strait meets the approaches to Charlottetown. Lit on 4 December 1845, it was the first lighthouse built on Prince Edward Island.[^1][^2]

## The place

Point Prim is a headland that runs out into the Northumberland Strait and is surrounded by water on three sides.[^3] Provincial heritage researchers describe it as a major hazard on the sailing route to the entrance of Charlottetown Harbour, and the heritage lighthouse statement speaks of extensive reefs there.[^1][^3] Shipowners and others with business in Charlottetown petitioned the colony's House of Assembly for a light in 1841.[^2] The Lieutenant Governor asked Royal Navy captains where it should go. Three of them said that a light meant only for entering Charlottetown Harbour belonged at Blockhouse Point or Sea Trout Point, while one meant for general navigation in Hillsborough Bay should stand on Point Prim.[^2] Lighthouse Friends adds that in 1844 the directors of the Prince Edward Island Steam Navigation Company complained of the "total absence of any lights", and that merchants and shipowners pointed out that they had paid light duty for years without a single lighthouse on the Island.[^2]

## Building the light

In November 1844 a committee reported in favour of a tower sixty feet high.[^2] Isaac Smith, the Charlottetown architect who had designed Government House, drew the plan.[^2] The Province of Prince Edward Island's account says he first proposed a wooden tower, but the Legislative Assembly thought brick would stand up better to the weather, so he revised it.[^1] The land was given by the sixth Lord Selkirk.[^1] On 31 March 1845 a committee travelled sixteen miles from Charlottetown to the point in ten sleighs to choose the site.[^2] The lighthouse's museum page says 22 men went, led by William Douse, a member of the Assembly and agent for Lord Selkirk, with Smith and the surveyor L. W. Gall among them.[^5]

Tenders were called on 2 May 1845 and Richard Walsh won the contract.[^1] Lighthouse Friends reports that Walsh defaulted and that James Peake was paid to finish and fit out the tower in 1846.[^2] The provincial account says the work was done by 4 December 1845.[^1] The first keeper, John Ings, lived on the tower's second floor until the keeper's cottage was ready.[^2]

The soft brick weathered quickly. The provincial account says the tower had to be enclosed in wooden shingles within two years.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates a payment for shingling to 1847.[^2] The Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society's page gives "1947", which is evidently a slip for 1847.[^4] The brickwork can still be seen from inside.[^1] The Federal Heritage Buildings review describes walls a foot thick, faced with shingle, and a central vertical shaft tied to the working of the flashing light.[^9]

The light began with four lamps set in reflectors. Because the tower's arc of coverage had dim spots, three more lamps were added in 1850.[^2] That February a naval officer, Commander Beecher, wrote that no keeper could be sure of staying awake through "the thirteen or fourteen consecutive hours of a November or December night" and recommended an assistant. The Lighthouse Society notes that the help was never granted.[^4] Lighthouse Friends gives the date of the present lantern as 1882.[^2] A fourth-order Chance Brothers lens replaced the old reflectors in 1909, and the Department of Marine report for 1909-10 says: "The catoptric light has been superseded by a 4th order 360° Chance lens."[^2][^8]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society counts twelve full-time and interim keepers from 1845 to 1969. All but the first, John Ings of Charlottetown, came from the Point Prim area.[^4] Ings served until mid-August 1854 and was followed by Ewen McLeod, who fell ill and died in the summer of 1857.[^2] The Point Prim lighthouse's own keepers' list names Finlay MacDonald, Simon Murchison and Alex John MacRae among those who followed; Michael MacLeod held the post from June 1873 to 1897.[^6] Lighthouse Friends' list differs from the museum's on some dates, notably Ings's start (1841 against 1846) and the end of Donald Gillis's term (1909 against 1910).[^2][^6] A Department of Marine table of appointments gives 10 December 1897 as a Point Prim date, which fits the changeover to Gillis.[^7]

Angus Murchison kept the light for 35 years, from 1920 to 1955, the longest-serving keeper by the museum's account.[^6] Norman Gillis took over in September 1955, and Manson Murchison returned from 1956 until the light was made automatic.[^6] The Lighthouse Society records a local story that in a spring before the light was lit for the season, Angus Murchison saw a burning ship in the Strait and went to open the light, and the ship vanished.[^4] The cottage was small enough that the keeper usually lived there alone, with his family often joining him in summer.[^2] The Lighthouse Society's general account of the keeper's duties says a daily log was kept, noting weather, shipping, chores such as painting and notable events; it does not say whose logs survive.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

During the Second World War several Prince Edward Island lights, Point Prim among them, stayed lit through the winter to help pilots flying from the training schools at Mount Pleasant and Summerside.[^2] On a stormy October day a pilot on a first solo flight crashed in the water off the point. Divers searched without finding him or the aircraft before winter, and in the spring the keeper's daughter found his remains on the shore.[^2]

In 1964, the year of the Charlottetown centennial celebrations, Manson Murchison tried to make the light extra bright for the Royal Yacht's arrival with the Queen and Prince Philip. The Lighthouse Society says that too much air pressure in the lamp's tank made it leak, so the light went out at the key moment.[^4]

## After the keepers

The light was converted from kerosene vapour to electricity on 10 March 1969.[^1][^2] In 1958 a new lens with flash panels had already replaced the fixed light, wound by hand every four hours with weights.[^2] The keeper's cottage and oil shed were sold and removed soon after automation.[^2] The Point Prim and Belfast Women's Institute has run a museum in the tower in summer since 1980.[^2] When erosion threatened the point, the Coast Guard considered moving the tower but built a retaining wall instead.[^2] The lighthouse was recognised as a provincial heritage place on 3 October 2012.[^2] A new building on the site, completed in 2017 with nearly $400,000 in federal funds, holds a gift shop and interpretation centre.[^2] In 2010 a modern optic replaced the Fresnel lens, which was moved to the first floor, where it is on display.[^2]

## Today

The tower is 18.5 m (61 ft) tall, one of only two round brick lighthouses in Canada.[^2][^3] The light still flashes white.[^10] The Lighthouse Directory lists the Belfast Community Development Corporation as owner, while Lighthouse Friends says the Canadian Coast Guard leases the lighthouse to that corporation.[^10][^2] Grounds are free to enter; admission is charged for the tower, which in 2026 is open from 17 May to 31 October.[^11] Its stairs are very steep.[^10]
