The Port Borden range lights are two square wooden towers, painted white, built in 1917 and officially established in 1918 to guide ships into the ferry harbour at Borden-Carleton, on Prince Edward Island's south shore.[^1][^5][^6][^7] The rear tower now stands in a historic park in Borden-Carleton.[^1][^7] The front tower stays at its last official site by the shore.[^2][^7] Both have been out of service since the Confederation Bridge opened in 1997.[^1][^6][^7]

## The place

Before 1917 the winter crossing to New Brunswick was made by ice-boats. LighthouseFriends describes the route from Cape Traverse, on the island, to Cape Tormentine as the shortest distance between the island and the mainland, at just over 13.7 km (8.5 miles).[^1] Ice-boat service there began in 1827 and ended in 1917.[^1] The boats were metal-sheathed and pulled across the ice by men in harnesses, and passengers could pay to help push.[^1]

In 1917 the icebreaking railcar ferry *Prince Edward Island* began service between Cape Tormentine and a new port at Carleton Point, a few miles west of Cape Traverse.[^1] The terminal was named Borden for Prime Minister Robert Borden, who commissioned it.[^8] LighthouseFriends dates the town's incorporation as Borden to 1919.[^1] Rowlett's directory names the New Brunswick end of the ferry as Cape Jourimane.[^7] LighthouseFriends and the Society name Cape Tormentine, so the sources disagree here.[^1][^5]

## Building the light

The government acquired the site in 1917. The Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society dates the expropriation of the site to 12 February 1917, and says the range light was built later that year.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the government purchased sites that year for $225.[^1]

The Department of Marine and Fisheries report for the fiscal year 1917-18 lists, under new aids to navigation for Prince Edward Island, "Port Borden": the erection of range lights, with 20-inch reflectors and duplex burners, at a cost of $1,204.97.[^3] LighthouseFriends gives the same 20-inch reflectors and duplex burners.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the range lights were officially established in 1918, the year after the land was bought.[^1] Rowlett's directory gives 1918 for both towers, where the Society gives 1917 for the build.[^7][^5]

Heights differ between sources. Rowlett gives the front tower as 6.5 m (22 ft) and the rear as 12 m (35 ft).[^7] LighthouseFriends gives the front as 6.2 m (22 ft) and the rear as 12.8 m (42 ft) from base to vane.[^1] The Society's technical data give 6.5 m (21 ft 3 in) for the front and 11.6 m (38 ft) for the rear, though its description of the rear gives 12.8 m (42 ft).[^5][^6]

The lights did not stay put. Rowlett's directory says they were moved in 1923 and again in 1954-55, as the approach to the ferry dock was modified.[^7] The Society gives the same two dates.[^5][^6] A Marine report for the fiscal year 1920-21 also lists a change in the position of range lights at a station printed "Port Bordon". That entry sits in a list that mixes provinces, and the printed name is hard to read in the scan, so the match with these lights rests on the name alone.[^4]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends and the Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society both name William Carruthers as the first keeper of the range lights. The Society says he was the first keeper of both lights.[^1][^5][^6]

LighthouseFriends dates Carruthers's service from 1917 to 1938, and gives Joseph George McDonald as keeper from 1938 to at least 1957.[^1]

## Today

The Town of Borden-Carleton owns both lights, according to Rowlett and the Society.[^7][^5][^6] LighthouseFriends says the Borden-Carleton Marine/Rail Historical Park manages them.[^1][^2]

The ferry service ended on 1 June 1997, when the Confederation Bridge opened.[^1] The Society dates the decommissioning of the lights to June 1997.[^5][^6] The rear tower has been relocated to a marine park in Borden-Carleton, near the foot of the bridge.[^1] Rowlett says it has been restored as part of a community development project on the former rail yard just east of the bridge.[^7]

The sources disagree on the front tower. The Society says it "had deteriorated badly but has since been restored."[^5] LighthouseFriends describes the front tower as abandoned and neglected at its last official site.[^2] Rowlett says the front light was repainted in 2012, but that the shoreline erosion endangers it.[^7]

The grounds are open and the towers are closed.[^1][^2][^7] LighthouseFriends says the front tower can be seen from the park.[^2]
