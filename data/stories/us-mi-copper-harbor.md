Copper Harbor Light stands on the eastern edge of the harbor entrance, near the tip of the Keweenaw Peninsula in Keweenaw County, Michigan, on Lake Superior. Work on a stone tower began there in August 1848, and the light first shone in the spring of 1849. The brick tower that stands now dates from 1866, and a steel skeletal light beside it, put up in 1933, is still the active aid.[^1][^2]

## The place

Copper Harbor had a copper land rush before it had a light. LighthouseFriends traces it from Douglass Houghton's 1840 survey, through the Treaty of La Pointe in 1842, to a Mineral Land Agency at the harbor in 1843 and Fort Wilkins, built in 1844 "to maintain law and order."[^2] The harbor entrance had its own hazard. The Life-Saving Service's 1884 report contains a list of places on the coasts of the United States where vessels had stranded, and it includes "Copper Harbor (reef at entrance), Lake Superior, Mich."[^7]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $5,000 for a lighthouse at Copper Harbor on March 3, 1847. The contract went to Charles Rude, and work began in August 1848 on a stone tower 44 feet (13.4 m) tall, with a one-and-a-half-story dwelling beside it.[^2] The light commenced operation in the spring of 1849. LighthouseFriends calls it the second lighthouse to be activated on Lake Superior.[^2] The first keeper, Henry Clow, was "tasked with minding thirteen lamps" set in reflectors, which showed a fixed white light.[^2]

The lighting changed in 1856, when a sixth-order Fresnel lens "replaced the antiquated array of lamps and reflectors," according to LighthouseFriends. A fourth-order lens followed in 1859.[^2] The Lighthouse Society's record gives the original optic as a fifth-order Fresnel lens installed in 1856.[^1] The sources do not agree on which order it was.

By 1866 the stone tower had fallen into disrepair, and Congress allocated $13,700 for a replacement on April 7, 1866.[^2] The new tower was a square brick tower built against the gable end of the keeper's house, and the Lighthouse Society gives its height to the lantern as 35 feet (10.7 m).[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the whole tower as 42 feet (12.8 m).[^2] Archaeologists in 1975 found the stone tower's foundation walls just west of the brick one.[^2]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society's roster lists eight head keepers, from Henry Clow in 1849 to Henry Corgan, who served until 1919.[^1]

Napoleon Beedon was appointed in 1855 and stayed fourteen years, until he resigned on September 9, 1869. LighthouseFriends says he had lost an arm as a teenager while working as an assistant blacksmith, and that he later became keeper at Au Sable Point, where his wife served as his first assistant from 1876 to 1879.[^2]

Charles Corgan kept the light from 1873 to 1881, and also looked after the abandoned Fort Wilkins.[^1][^2] His son Henry began his service at Manitou Island as his father's assistant, then came to Copper Harbor in 1888.[^2] Henry was then at Peninsula Point, and when he heard the light was to reopen he asked for the transfer. LighthouseFriends quotes his letter: "My light is so situated that I am unable to send my children to school."[^2] The light reopened on June 1, 1888, under his care.[^2]

In 1892 the keeper built a small boathouse, connected to the lighthouse by 200 feet (61 m) of sidewalk, and the Lighthouse Board's report records that work.[^2][^5] A boat landing followed in 1895, and the Board's report says a barbed-wire fence was stretched across the point of the lighthouse reservation.[^6]

Henry Corgan voluntarily retired on September 20, 1924. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin says he first entered the service in 1866 at Manitou Island, where he served until 1867. He reentered in 1882 at Gull Rock, and from 1885 served continuously at Gull Rock, Point Peninsula, Copper Harbor and St. Clair Flats Canal Upper Light, with "more than 50 years' service" in all.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he began his career at sixteen and retired at seventy, which would put his start about 1870 rather than 1866, so the two accounts do not agree. LighthouseFriends also says he moved to St. Clair Flats after thirty-one years at Copper Harbor, in 1919, and calls him reportedly the oldest lighthouse keeper in Michigan when he retired.[^2] The sources name no woman who kept the Copper Harbor light in her own right.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources give few dated accounts of wrecks at the station itself. A letter dated December 31, 1926, from the district commander of the United States Coast Guard at Green Bay, printed in the Lighthouse Service Bulletin, says the crew of the Eagle Harbor Coast Guard station was rendering assistance to two steamers.[^4] The same letter thanks the keepers at Copper Harbor and at Mendota, who provided food and shelter for the shipwrecked sailors and the Coast Guard crew.[^4]

## After the keepers

The light was made automatic in 1919. A sun valve turned the acetylene lamp on and off, and LighthouseFriends says a resident keeper "was no longer required."[^2] On June 23, 1919, the light's characteristic changed to a white flash every three seconds.[^2]

The lighthouse was leased in 1927 to Roger T. Vaughan, a Chicago-area physician, who used it as a summer residence for roughly two decades.[^2] In 1933 a 62-foot (19 m) skeletal tower was put up near the lighthouse, and the light was converted to electricity in 1937.[^2] The Lighthouse Society's record gives 1933 as the year the old tower was discontinued.[^1]

In 1957 the Coast Guard sold the lighthouse to the State of Michigan for $5,000, and it was incorporated into Fort Wilkins State Park.[^2] Restoration began in the early 1970s. The restored lighthouse was dedicated on August 3, 1975, and boat trips to it began the following June.[^2] The complex was placed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2012, and LighthouseFriends says the 1933 skeletal tower "remains an active aid to navigation."[^2]

## Today

The road to the lighthouse is not open to the public, so visitors reach it by boat from the Copper Harbor marina. Boat service runs from the Saturday before Memorial Day to the first week of October.[^2][^8] The grounds are open, the dwelling is open during tours, and the tower is closed.[^2] The lighthouse belongs to the Michigan Historical Museum System and sits inside Fort Wilkins Historic State Park.[^2][^8] In summer, costumed interpreters demonstrate frontier army life at the fort, according to the same visitor guide.[^8]
