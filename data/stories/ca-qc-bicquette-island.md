The Bicquette Island light stands on a small island in the St. Lawrence River, off Le Bic near Rimouski, Quebec. Its round masonry tower, 22.5 m (74 ft) high, was erected in 1843 according to the Quebec heritage record. LighthouseFriends gives 1844 as the year the light began operation, Radio-Canada gives 1844 as the year it was built, and a 2006 article gives 1841.[^3][^1][^5][^4] It has run without keepers since 1987,[^4] and the island is a national wildlife reserve that visitors are not allowed to enter.[^5]

## The place

Upstream of Pointe-au-Père the river is dotted with islands that are tricky obstacles for marine pilots, and Île Bicquette lies farther out in the river than its larger neighbour, Île du Bic.[^1] Ships running up the river at night gave the island a wide margin and often ran aground on the north shore, at Portneuf or Mille Vaches.[^1] Shipowners and captains had asked for a light there since 1828, according to the Quebec heritage record.[^3]

That record says the site was chosen by Trinity House of Quebec, which had operated lighthouses in Lower Canada before Confederation.[^3][^2] The light was meant to remedy the many wrecks in this stretch of river. With the lights at Île Verte and Île Rouge it would make "un nouvel alignement de trois phares" (a new alignment of three lights; translation).[^3]

## Building the light

The sources do not agree on who designed the tower. The Quebec record says it was built to the plans and specifications of Charles Atherton, a civil engineer of Montreal.[^3] The federal register names Atherton as the engineer under contract to Trinity House.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the work was carried out under Joseph Archer, and that the tower was modelled on the one at Île Verte.[^1]

The Quebec record describes outer walls of limestone brought from Europe, lined inside with brick, and a circular lantern, though traces suggest the first lantern was polygonal.[^3] LighthouseFriends describes the first light as a revolving catoptric white light, with twenty-one flat-wick lamps before 53 cm (21-inch) reflectors and a flash every two minutes. Its focal plane was 34 m (112 ft).[^1]

The wooden cladding was added to shield the stone. LighthouseFriends dates it to 1866, while the federal statement puts it around 1900 and the Quebec record probably about 1902.[^1][^2][^3]

The station had a stone dwelling, a stone powder magazine, a wooden gun house and a wooden shed at the landing by 1876.[^1] During fog and snowstorms a cannon was fired every half hour.[^1] The dwellings are dated differently. LighthouseFriends has a new dwelling in 1883 and a modern one in 1962; the Quebec record says the two keepers' houses on the site were built in 1955 and 1961, replacing the nineteenth-century house.[^1][^3]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends names John E. Hammond as the first keeper, serving from 1844 to 1857.[^1] He was the keeper when the barque *Berbice* went on the rocks in 1853, described below.[^1]

LighthouseFriends also reports that on 2 March 1859 Keeper Hammond and his assistant drowned while travelling out to the island to get it ready for the opening of navigation, when ice floes punctured their boat.[^1] Its own keeper list ends Hammond in 1857, so the dates in the article do not agree. A Mr. Fortier was then named temporary custodian, and LighthouseFriends tells how he took a small dog to the island, heard thumps and squeaks in the upper part of the tower at night, and left for good once the river froze. No other source consulted confirms the drowning or the Fortier episode.[^1]

Thomas Lebel was keeper in 1875, when all the lamps in the tower caught fire, for a reason LighthouseFriends does not give.[^1] The later keepers on LighthouseFriends's list include Elzear Heppel, Louis Pinault, E. Laprise and Louis Rioux, before Ernest Thibault arrived in 1938.[^1] Ernest Thibault stayed 24 years, until 1962.[^4]

The Thibault family kept the light for more than 75 years, and the federal statement is less exact, giving "a combined total of seventy-five years" for the members of the family who served there.[^2][^3] Maurice Thibault, Ernest's son, became keeper on 4 August 1944, at the age of 15, after learning the trade beside his father.[^4] He had to live on the island nine months a year, and went home to Le Bic for three winter months.[^4] The 2006 article says three keepers usually shared the day on the island, each on two four-hour shifts, one by day and one by night. After the keepers unionised in 1967, their shifts changed from 1970 to eight hours at a stretch.[^4] LighthouseFriends prints his memory of the weight that drove the light: "At Bicquette, it took 810 turns and it took ten minutes to raise the weight."[^1] He retired, and the article says he saw the keepers' trade end when the light was automated on 15 September 1987.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the night of 7 June 1853 the Scottish barque *Berbice* ran onto the rocks near the island, in dense fog, just at midnight.[^1] The letter her passengers later wrote to the Quebec Morning Chronicle, as LighthouseFriends prints it, says the whole party, "upwards of one hundred in number," was landed from the ship's boats.[^1] The writers thanked "the extraordinary kindness of Mr. Hannon and his wife," the keeper and his wife, and said that their home was thrown open to the passengers.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls the keeper "John Hammond" in its own text, while the letter prints "Hannon".[^1]

In 1875 the lamps in the tower caught fire. The heat broke all forty-eight glass panes in the lantern room, and a steamer was sent with new lamps. The light was out for only eight hours.[^1]

On 10 June 1902 the steamer *Sahara*, loaded with rice at Rangoon for Montreal, was in fog off Bicquette, looking for a pilot. While she stopped to take the pilot from a boat, she drifted onto the south-west reef of the island and her Nos. 1, 2 and 3 holds began to fill. The report says the crew all went ashore as the ship took a heavy list to port, and it was thought she would capsize. She got off the reef at 11.40 p.m. on 17 June.[^6]

The preliminary inquiry into the Sahara blamed the captain, not the lights. It found that the casualty was caused by "the imprudence of the captain in too hastily leaving Father Point, and lack of experience in not knowing the set of the tides in the vicinity of Bic Island," and it recommended a formal inquiry.[^6] The same finding says the Bic Islands and Bicquette lighthouses "were plainly in view."[^6]

On 7 November 1919 a vessel stranded on Bicquette Island. The entry does not name the vessel. The court found the master, John Clive, "incapable to administrate or exercise vigilance, having indulged in intoxicating liquors." It cancelled his master's certificate, but recommended that a mate's certificate be granted him.[^7]

The 2006 article, based on Maurice Thibault's recollections, describes German military men landing on the island. The keepers, who were unarmed, pretended to ignore them while the Germans looked into the houses.[^4] The article gives no date for the landing. It does mention Maurice Thibault's extra pay for working in a war zone during the Second World War.[^4] A 2016 Radio-Canada teaser refers to the "Invasion du phare par les Allemands lors de la bataille du Saint-Laurent" (the German invasion of the lighthouse during the Battle of the St. Lawrence; translation).[^5] No other source consulted confirms the landing.

## After the keepers

The steam foghorn that replaced the cannon in 1889 blasted for ten seconds every minute, from a building 30 m (100 ft) east of the tower.[^1] In 1908 a 76 mm (3-inch) diaphone plant replaced it. The Department's report for the fiscal year 1908–09 says a new wooden fog-alarm building was erected, and "a 3-inch duplicate diaphone plant installed, which supersedes the steam horn formerly in operation."[^8][^1]

LighthouseFriends says a third-order Fresnel lens was installed in 1909, making the light a white flash every three seconds.[^1] The Quebec record describes the present lantern as holding a first-order catoptric rotating light.[^3] The two accounts do not match.

The light was electrified in 1970, when an electric bulb replaced the oil lamp.[^4][^1] Automation came on 15 September 1987.[^4] Keeping the light was then a job that no longer needed a person on the island.[^4]

The lighthouse received federal heritage recognition in 1988, and the fog-alarm building was declared a Recognized Federal Heritage Building in 1996.[^1] The site was also covered by the Quebec lighthouse inventory of 2009–2010.[^3]

## Today

The light is automatic and still operates, according to LighthouseFriends and the Les amis des phares du Saint-Laurent page.[^1][^9] The island is part of the Réserve nationale de faune des îles de l'estuaire and has been closed to visitors since the 1980s, according to Radio-Canada.[^5] Do not land on the island. LighthouseFriends says it is best seen by boat, and a very distant view is possible from Pic Champlain, a lookout in the Parc national du Bic.[^1]

The accounts of access disagree. LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] The Quebec record calls the island uninhabited and says it "n'est pas entièrement accessible" (is not entirely accessible; translation), and that many tourists visit each year.[^3] The 2016 Radio-Canada article carries a notice that its information may be out of date.[^5]

Ownership is also given in two ways: Radio-Canada says Fisheries and Oceans Canada still owns the light, while LighthouseFriends names the Canadian Coast Guard as the owner.[^5][^1] The Quebec record credits the Thibault family with an international recognition for its work protecting the eider ducks on the island.[^3]
