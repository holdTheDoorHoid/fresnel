Falkner Island is a long, narrow strip of land in Long Island Sound, a few miles south of Guilford, Connecticut, and its octagonal brownstone lighthouse has marked the spot since 1802.[^1] Over two centuries it has been a keeper's family home, a testing ground for fog signals, a Coast Guard duty post, a burned-out ruin and a tern colony.

## The place

The National Register nomination describes the island as five miles south of Guilford; LighthouseFriends puts it about three and a half miles offshore, a difference the sources do not explain.[^1][^2] The island's name appears in several spellings, including Faulkner's, and the National Register file uses both.[^1] LighthouseFriends writes that ship traffic in the Sound grew through the eighteenth century and that many vessels ran aground on the reefs around the island.[^2]

The government bought the island from Medad Stone on 12 May 1801 for $325, and Connecticut's governor ceded the land to the United States on 23 July 1801.[^1] Congress had allotted $6,000 for the lighthouse, paid from duties on imported goods.[^1]

## Building the light

The nomination says the builder's name was unknown to its author, though the tower follows the plan the Treasury Department advertised for early lighthouses.[^1] LighthouseFriends names Abisha Woodward as the contractor, who agreed with John McComb Jr. to build it for $5,500 and won the work by offering to face the tower with hewn stone; the Lighthouse Society archive also lists Woodward as a contractor here.[^2][^3] The result was a tapering octagonal tower of hammered brownstone, with walls four feet six inches thick at the bottom and eighteen inches at the top.[^1] The nomination gives its height as 36 feet 8 inches (11.2 m); LighthouseFriends calls it forty feet (12 m).[^1][^2]

At first the light came from twelve lamps with reflectors on two circular tables. The lantern was replaced in 1840, and the work was contracted at $2,842 for nine lamps with sixteen-inch reflectors.[^1] LighthouseFriends and New England Lighthouses date the fourth-order Fresnel lens to 1856, while the nomination can only say that an 1873 inspection recorded one, a Sautter lens made in Paris.[^2][^4][^1] That lens was taken to New Haven for safekeeping after the 1976 fire and vandalism.[^1]

The keeper's house is dated differently by different sources. The nomination describes a six-room frame dwelling put up in 1801, enlarged in 1837, then replaced in 1871 by a larger frame house that lasted until 1976.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the first house decayed and was replaced in 1858, and that a story was added in 1871.[^2]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society archive lists Joseph Griffing as first keeper, from 1802 to 1813; New England Lighthouses ends his term in 1812.[^3][^4] That site says that he once found the bodies of seven sailors from a nearby wreck and buried them on neighbouring Goose Island.[^4]

Eli Kimberly, a Guilford native, held the post from 1818 to 1851.[^2][^3] He arrived with his wife Polly and two children and, by LighthouseFriends' count, raised nine more there; New England Lighthouses says twelve children in all and that a tutor boarded with the family.[^2][^4] Summer visitors came in numbers, and the site reports that Kimberly built a bowling alley and bar. On 4 July 1829, it says, twenty young men from New Haven drank there, trampled the vegetable garden and wrecked the keeper's boat.[^2]

Oliver N. Brooks followed, from 1851 to 1882.[^3] The two modern accounts agree that he played the violin and mounted birds killed by striking the lantern, until the house was a small natural-history museum.[^2][^4] On the morning of 16 May 1878 he picked up 216 dead birds at the foot of the tower, with more injured on the ground.[^2] In 1888 a newspaper said that the Brooks family "made a paradise out of that little island", as New England Lighthouses quotes it.[^4] Mary M. Brooks served as assistant keeper from 1869, first assistant until 1879 and then second assistant until 1882; the roster does not say how she was related to Oliver.[^3]

In 1911 Arthur Jensen became keeper. LighthouseFriends reprints a local newspaper account of a night when he was laid up with a back ailment, his assistant was ashore and fog came down. His wife, Ingrid, turned over the five-foot flywheel of the oil engine that drove the siren and ran it for four hours, then lit the lamp in the tower and "watched it all night long".[^2]

George Zuius was the last Lighthouse Service keeper, leaving in 1941 when the Coast Guard took over by New England Lighthouses' account, and was on the island for the 1938 hurricane, which destroyed the boathouse while the light kept burning, according to New England Lighthouses.[^4] The Lighthouse Society archive gives him 1935 to 1943, a disagreement the sources do not resolve.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends reports that more than a hundred wrecks were recorded in the station's logs between 1851 and 1882.[^2] In November 1858, during a winter storm, Brooks rescued five people from the schooner *Moses F. Webb*, according to LighthouseFriends, and the same source says he received a gold medal.[^2] That site also says the steamer *E. A. Woodward* grounded on the reef north of the island on 7 February 1875, and it blames the lack of an adequate fog signal.[^2]

The fog signal grew with the traffic. Joseph Henry, head of the Smithsonian Institution and chairman of the Lighthouse Board, used the island in 1865 to set up bells, steam whistles and other devices and judge how far each could be heard on the Sound, the nomination says.[^1] A first-class steam whistle replaced the bell in 1879, and a compressed-air siren in a new masonry engine house followed in 1902, with trials against a whistle and a diaphone that September.[^1] The nomination elsewhere dates the masonry fog-signal building to 1903.[^1] The Light-House Board reported that the ten-inch steam whistle, in duplicate, ran about 509 hours in the year to mid-1899 and about 423 hours in the year to mid-1900.[^5][^6] The nomination dates the typhon horns to 1932 in one place and 1934 in another.[^1]

Later keepers appear in the service's own reports for helping boaters. Leonard Fuller towed a disabled boat with two men and seven women aboard to Stony Creek, according to the Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended June 1921.[^7] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1926 records Samuel L. Fuller going out during a heavy thunderstorm to a drifting motor boat on 18 July and towing its occupants to Guilford.[^8] In its 1937 volume it records Zuius rescuing four men whose boat had capsized in a thunderstorm about a mile from the station, on 8 August.[^9]

Coast Guard crews lived with the same sea. LighthouseFriends tells of Officer in Charge Robert Simpson, who in September 1954 jumped overboard with two others when their boat's engine caught fire; a man watching the Sound with a telescope called the police.[^2] The same page says James Marshall, on duty at Christmas 1958, spent nine hours coaxing the diesel generators that charged the light's batteries to start.[^2]

## After the keepers

A fire on 15 March 1976 destroyed the keeper's dwelling while two Coast Guardsmen were on duty, and the personnel were removed from the island.[^1][^2] The tower was sandblasted, repointed and painted that year, its lower windows were bricked in and a steel door fitted.[^1] The old lens was replaced by an automatic electric light, which solar panels powered by 1989.[^1] By 1990 the Coast Guard had given up the manned station, vandals had broken windows and the doorway, and erosion on the east bluff was threatening the tower's foundation.[^10]

The Coast Guard transferred the island to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in July 1985, while keeping responsibility for the light and navigational facilities.[^1] The tower was listed on the National Register on 29 May 1990.[^10] A rabbit colony that grew from a pair left behind on the island was captured by humane society officers in October 1980, and the rabbits were put up for adoption.[^2]

The Faulkner's Light Brigade, begun in 1990 under the Guilford Preservation Alliance, raised money for repairs and for erosion control, and Congress appropriated the balance of the $4.5 million erosion project in 1998.[^10] Construction began in September 2000, storms twice threw the work barges against the shore, and the work was finished in 2001.[^10] LighthouseFriends dates the stone revetment 2000 to 2002.[^2] Guilford won $250,000 in transportation enhancement funds for the lighthouse's restoration.[^10]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the lighthouse and the island is part of the Stewart B. McKinney National Wildlife Refuge.[^2] It still flashes white every ten seconds, though the fog signal has been discontinued.[^2] The island is a nesting site for common and roseate terns and, by LighthouseFriends' account, is best seen from a boat; the same page marks the grounds and tower closed, with an open house usually held in September.[^1][^2]
