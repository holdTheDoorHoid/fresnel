Ontonagon Light stands on the western side of the mouth of the Ontonagon River, on Michigan's Lake Superior shore.[^1] The first light was shown in 1852 or 1853, and the records disagree.[^1][^2] The brick house and tower that stand today date from 1866, and the light was discontinued in 1964.[^2] The Ontonagon County Historical Society owns it now.[^1][^2]

## The place

The light stands on a 22.63-acre (9.2 ha) parcel on the west side of the river entrance, which had been acquired in April 1847.[^1] The sources on this station do not say what hazard the light was built to mark.

The river mouth was becoming a settlement. James Paul, the first settler of Ontonagon, opened the Deadfall Saloon at the river mouth in 1843.[^1] In 1847 prospectors found a copper mass a few miles inland, and the Minesota Mine that followed was the most productive copper mine of its time in the United States.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $5,000 for a lighthouse at Ontonagon on 28 September 1850, and Detroit contractor W. F. Chittenden was hired to build it.[^1] The first building was a one-and-a-half-story stone dwelling, 38 by 20 feet (12 by 6 m), topped by a short tower. Six lamps in fourteen-inch reflectors gave a fixed white light 35 feet (11 m) above the lake until a fifth-order Fresnel lens was installed in 1857.[^1]

Congress appropriated $14,000 on 7 April 1866 for repairs and renovations at the station.[^1] What went up that year was a new brick dwelling with a nine-foot-square (2.7 m) tower in its lake-facing gable, and the old lens was moved into a new decagonal lantern room.[^1] An eighteen-foot-square (5.5 m) kitchen was attached to the rear in 1890, and a brick oil house, with room for 360 gallons, was built in 1902.[^1] The Board's report for fiscal 1901 records that the walk from the dwelling to the pier had been rebuilt and that material for an oil house had been bought.[^3]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists nine principal keepers, from Samuel Peck (1853–1859) to Arnold A. Huuki (1945–1964).[^2] LighthouseFriends gives 26 August 1853 as the date Peck was appointed the light's first keeper.[^1] The archive's place record gives 1852 as the year the station was established. The sources do not settle whether the light first shone in 1852 or 1853.[^1][^2]

Thomas Stripe kept the light from 1864 to 1883.[^2] His successor, James Corgan, came from Gull Rock Light, where he had been head keeper from 1877 until 1883. He had begun in 1866 on Manitou Island, where he served as an assistant under his father and then replaced him as head keeper in 1873.[^1] His log for his arrival on 27 October 1883 says he came "with my wife six children and Dan Corgan my brother."[^1] Corgan kept the light for thirty-seven years, longer than any other keeper, and was seventy-one when he retired in 1919. LighthouseFriends gives thirty-seven years; the roster's dates, 1883 to 1919, come to about thirty-six.[^1][^2] A 1916 newspaper article, as LighthouseFriends summarises it, counted five offices he held at once: lighthouse keeper, harbour master, deputy collector of customs, government appraiser and coroner.[^1]

Fred Warner kept the light from 1919 to 1939, James Gagnon from 1939 to 1944, and Alva "Carp" Carpenter from 1944 to 1945.[^1][^2] Arnold A. Huuki, the last of the nine, kept the light from 1945 until it was discontinued. He had been presented a diamond pin for forty years of efficient and faithful government service, and he retired on 1 January 1964. He and his wife Lillian went on living in the lighthouse.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources on this station record no wreck or rescue tied to the light. The most detailed account they hold is of a fire on land.

On 25 August 1896 a fire began in the swamp south of the station and reached the dwellings of the Diamond Match Company's employees. Corgan's log records that by two in the afternoon "the town was ablaze."[^1] The Ontonagon Herald's account of 5 September 1896, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, said the town was "afire in 100 places at once."[^1] Corgan's log also says the heat at times was so intense that the family could not get water from the river, and that his wife, son, a hired girl and he had to carry water and keep the roofs and buildings wet.[^1]

A storm did damage at the harbour. On 12 December 1899 "the open framework tower at the outer end of the west pier was carried away during a severe storm." That tower belonged to the separate Ontonagon Pierhead light, not to this one.[^4]

## After the keepers

The property was eventually turned over to the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, which was dredging the river. For some years the lighthouse served as a clubhouse for the local Coast Guard auxiliary.[^1] It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1975.[^1]

In 1994 the Corps offered to lease the lighthouse to the town. The town's Downtown Development Authority said that, if the lighthouse could not be moved, it did not want it in its present place, surrounded by coal piles, where it would be a massive liability. Because the lighthouse had been placed on the National Register in 1975, moving it was out of the question. A limited use permit was eventually granted to the Ontonagon County Historical Society.[^1] An Act of Congress in 2000 authorised the transfer, and a patent conveying the lighthouse to the society was issued on 6 August 2003.[^1]

In 2008 and 2009 over $100,000 was spent restoring the lighthouse, with new hardwood floors, subfloor heating and wooden shutters, in an effort to return it to its circa 1915 appearance.[^1] In 2009 the paper mill that surrounded the lighthouse closed and laid off 200 employees. The mill had been supplying the lighthouse with power, so a diesel generator was installed until a permanent solution was found. The mill itself was sold for scrap.[^1]

## Today

Visits are by escorted tour only. Tours start at the historical society's museum, which is at 422 River Street, and the fifth-order lens formerly used in the lighthouse can be seen there. A distant view of the lighthouse is possible from the end of River Street.[^1] Tours can also be arranged by appointment in the off-season, so check with the historical society before you go.[^1]
