Pass a l'Outre Light stands in the marshes of the Mississippi River delta, in Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana, about 12 miles (19 km) east of Head of Passes.[^4] It was first lit in 1855.[^1][^2] Its light was put out of service in 1930 or in 1934, depending on the source, and the cast-iron tower now stands as a stump in the marsh.[^1][^2][^4]

## The place

The Mississippi divides into three main channels at Head of Passes, and Pass a l'Outre is the eastern one.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the name was once spelled Pass a la Loutre, "otter pass."[^1]

Until 1855 a light on Frank's Island, at the mouth of Northeast Pass, served ships in the delta.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates that tower's first light to March 1823 and says it served through most of 1855.[^1] Putnam's 1917 history says the Frank's Island tower was "not lighted since the building of Pass-a-Loutre" in 1855.[^3] As Northeast Pass began to shoal, Pass a l'Outre to the north deepened and gave large vessels better access to the river.[^1] A tall iron tower at Head of Passes was no longer needed there, and it was moved to Pass a l'Outre.[^4]

## Building the light

Rowlett's directory dates the tower to 1852, when it was built at Head of Passes as the nation's tallest cast-iron lighthouse, 85 ft (26 m) round.[^4] The Lighthouse Friends history says Congress appropriated $6,000 in March 1853 to move it and to build a small beacon at Head of Passes.[^1] The tower was moved to Pass a l'Outre and lit in December 1855. John Lory, the last keeper of Frank's Island, was its first keeper.[^1][^2] The USLHS record gives a third-order Fresnel lens, installed in 1855, on a cast-iron tower with a brick lining.[^2]

The USLHS record gives the height of the light above mean high water as 70 ft (21 m), and the height from the base of the tower to the center of the lantern as 63 ft (19 m).[^2] Rowlett gives the original height as 85 ft, and says the tower now stands about 40 ft (12 m) high, having sunk into the mud.[^4]

By 1868 the keeper's dwelling, which rested on nine brick piers, had settled three feet into the soft ground, and the tower had begun to sink.[^1] By 1876 high tides were flooding the tower floor, and the floor was raised five feet.[^1] Congress provided $10,000 in 1871 for fog signals at Pass a l'Outre and Southwest Pass. A steam whistle began work at Pass a l'Outre that year and was discontinued in 1880.[^1]

## The keepers

The USLHS roster lists head keepers and assistants from 1855 to 1930, one line per stint.[^2] John Lory was first, from 1855 to 1857. Prosper Falgout was head keeper for the longest stretch, from 1895 to 1908.[^2] Albert Matulich served as head keeper from 1915 to 1918, and John McNamara from 1918 to 1926.[^2]

Women also served at the station as assistants.[^2] The roster lists Emma Saulsbury as first assistant from 1867 to 1869, Louisa Ripps from 1869 to 1871, Ellen E. Segersten in 1871, and Henrietta E. Marshall from 1871 to 1872. Margaret Donovan was second assistant from 1872 to 1876.[^2] It also lists a first assistant "Mrs. J. Ervine" in 1871, without a first name.[^2]

The bureau recommended John McNamara's retirement for 26 April 1926, when he would reach the age of 70.[^5] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin says he had served as a fireman, oiler and deckhand on vessels in the eighth lighthouse district from 1 July 1876 to 1 September 1893, and again from 1 August 1896 to 1910.[^5] Lighthouse Friends says he retired on that birthday, after serving as a lighthouse keeper from 1911 to 1926.[^1] The roster lists him as head keeper only from 1918, so the sources do not agree on when his keeping began.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In June 1861 Confederate forces removed the lamps from the station and destroyed the oil on hand, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] Union forces later took down the Fresnel lens.[^1] On 1 January 1862 Confederate soldiers burned the keeper's dwelling.[^1] The light was back in service on 20 April 1863, after a new dwelling was built and the iron tower was given a new brick lining.[^1] Putnam, quoting Raphael Semmes's account of June 1861 from Head of the Passes, describes a similar removal from the delta's lighthouses: an officer was sent "to the different lighthouses" to stave the oil casks and bring away the lighting apparatus. Putnam does not name Pass a l'Outre in that passage.[^10]

Lighthouse Friends dates a powerful hurricane at the station to 28 September 1917.[^1] It blew away the tool house and badly damaged the dwelling, walks and wharf.[^1] The Bulletin's November 1917 storm report gives 28 September as the day the hurricane struck the Gulf coast, and says the Pass a l'Outre station was badly damaged, with the tool house blown away and the dwelling, walks and wharf also hit.[^9] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1912 to 1917 lists the station as damaged by a hurricane, and records a keeper's commendation on the same page as the storm damage.[^6] Steven Coludrovich, the assistant in charge while the head keeper was away, was commended by the Secretary of Commerce for his work during the storm.[^1][^6]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' reports describe the repairs that followed. At the end of the fiscal year 1920, work in progress included lumber bought for raising the dwelling and outbuildings, and for rebuilding the boathouse and walks.[^7] The 1921 report says the dwelling was raised and placed on concrete piers, the boathouse and sidewalk were rebuilt, and a new door was cut in the tower. The report lists this work under an appropriation dated 28 March 1918 for $100,000.[^8]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the tower was given a spiral black and white daymark in 1919.[^1] The USLHS record, whose source is dated 1908, gives its daymark as black.[^2]

On 31 October 1929 Coast Guard personnel climbed the tower with binoculars to watch a schooner anchored nearby.[^1] Both keepers said they had not noticed the vessel.[^1] The British schooner O.C.L. was seized on 1 November 1929 with no liquor found aboard, and immigration authorities ordered her captain deported.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the district superintendent judged the keepers negligent.[^1]

The light was decommissioned on 11 January 1930, when the tender Magnolia removed the Fresnel lens, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] Rowlett's directory says the light was inactive from 1930.[^4] The USLHS record gives 1934 as the year of discontinuance.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the Coast Guard used the abandoned tower as a lookout for rumrunners until the end of Prohibition in 1933.[^1]

Hurricane Katrina loosened the lantern. By 2007 it had fallen into the water, leaving only the stump of the tower standing.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says the tower's spiral bands had begun to fade by the 1950s, and that less than half of its original height now shows above ground.[^1]

## Today

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service owns the site, and the Delta National Wildlife Refuge manages it.[^1][^4] The site is reached only by boat.[^4] Rowlett's directory lists the site as open and the tower as closed, and Lighthouse Friends also gives the tower as closed.[^1][^4] The USLHS record lists the station as not open to the public, with its optic removed.[^2]
