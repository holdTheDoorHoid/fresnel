Cranberry Island Light stands on the south part of Cranberry Island, off Cape Canso, at the eastern entrance to the Strait of Canso in Guysborough County, Nova Scotia.[^1][^2][^3] Lighthouse Friends says it was first shown on 1 November 1818; the NSLPS gives 1815, so the first date is not settled. The light is active today, showing a white flash every 15 seconds that can be seen 21 nautical miles out.[^3]

## The place

Cranberry Island is a low group of rocky ledges. At low tide the ledges are joined, and at high tide the island divides into a northern and a southern part. Lighthouse Friends says the light marks the entrance to Canso Harbour and to the Strait of Canso, the waterway between Cape Breton and the mainland.[^1]

In 1807 the House of Assembly of Nova Scotia appointed a committee to look into a light near Canso. Its report of 1808 said a light on Cranberry Island would be "of the utmost consequence" to the province's trade and to the safety of shipping, and put the cost at four hundred pounds.[^1]

A duty to be collected at the harbours near the Gut of Canso was set up in 1822 to support the Cranberry Island light. An act of 1827 continued it, as 8 George IV, chapter 12.[^4]

## Building the light

The Nova Scotia Archives holds three pages of documents on the building of the Cranberry Island lighthouse, dated 1817.[^5]

Lighthouse Friends says that on 20 May 1817 James Bowers, a carpenter, and others hired to help build the lighthouse left Halifax aboard the schooner Union for a four-day sail to Canso. The men worked six days a week from 1 June until 19 October, and the work went on the next summer. The light was put into operation on 1 November 1818.[^1]

NSLPS gives a different start. Its entry for the earlier tower says it began in 1815, and names the "Engineer: Patrick Lannegan."[^2] Lighthouse Friends names Patrick Lanigan as keeper, at least from 1824.[^1] The 1817 papers and the building work Lighthouse Friends describes do not fit well with an 1815 start, so this story uses 1818 and records the difference.

In 1858 the tower was described as a large octagonal tower with a dwelling, painted white with red horizontal stripes. It showed two fixed lights, one in the lantern room and one in a bow window lower down.[^1]

Fog signals came early. Lighthouse Friends says Daboll fog trumpets were put into operation on 20 September 1865, with a caloric engine in a building about 100 metres from the light to supply the air.[^1] NSLPS gives a fog signal in 1864, which it calls the province's first.[^2] The engines broke down often. The trumpet was disabled on 30 August 1870 and was not replaced until a steam whistle was installed. John Cormack took charge of the whistle in 1871 at $450 a year. He left after just over a year, and James Hanlon, the keeper of the light, took responsibility for the whistle as well.[^1]

A fire changed the station. Lighthouse Friends says that on 13 October 1881, as a new tower with dwelling was nearly finished, a fire broke out in the tower. It destroyed every building on the island except a new oil store and a storehouse, along with the new lighting apparatus, the lantern room, and the keepers' furniture and provisions.[^1] A Wilmington, Delaware, newspaper of 15 October 1881 reported that the light-house and "all but one of the buildings therewith, on Cranberry Island, Nova Scotia, were burned yesterday morning."[^6] The paper's "yesterday morning" points to 14 October, a day after the date Lighthouse Friends gives. The sources do not settle the day, and NSLPS dates the fire to 1883.[^2] A Halifax dispatch of 18 October, printed in the New-York Tribune, said "a lighthouse will be erected at once at Cranberry Island" to replace the one recently burned.[^7]

Lighthouse Friends says two temporary lights were shown on 8 November 1881, from a 15 m (50 ft) skeleton tower.[^1] The fog alarm, whose machinery had been salvaged from the fire and taken to Halifax to be overhauled, was running again on 1 July 1882.[^1]

The octagonal tower of 1883 was built under a contract of $3,690 with John G. Sinclair of Country Harbour, who was to finish it with an attached dwelling by the end of June 1883. Lighthouse Friends says the tower was 23.8 m (78 ft) tall, carried a third-order Fresnel lens by Chance Brothers in the lantern to show a fixed white light, and had a sixth-order light in a bay window 10.7 m (35 ft) lower.[^1] NSLPS gives the tower as 18.3 m (60 ft) high, and also dates the octagonal tower to 1883.[^2]

A Neptune fog trumpet replaced the steam whistle in 1886, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1]

In the year to June 1906 the Department of Marine built a new fog alarm on the summit of the southern part of the island. Its report says it "was put in operation on July 1, 1906." It is "a square wooden building," and it houses a diaphone, operated by air compressed by oil engines, which gives "one blast of seven seconds' duration every minute" in thick or foggy weather.[^8] NSLPS dates its diaphone fog signal to 1911.[^2]

In the year to June 1908 the Department built a new house for the fog-alarm engineer, "by contract for $2,200." It was framed, with concrete foundation walls and piers.[^9]

In the year to June 1910 the upper light was changed from a fixed white light to an occulting white light: visible three seconds, eclipsed five seconds, visible seventeen seconds, eclipsed five seconds, every thirty seconds. The lower light was discontinued.[^10][^11]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends gives the head keepers in order: Patrick Lanigan (at least 1824), James P. Hanlon (1854 to 1883), James Lepper (1883 to 1887), James Sullivan (1887 to 1903), Havelock Cohoon (1903 to 1905), James P. Hanlon (1905 to 1914), Walter T. Jamieson (1914 to 1918), C. P. Richards (1918 to 1928), C. Richards (1928 to 1934), D. Richards (1934) and F. J. Manuel (1934 to at least 1937).[^1]

The Department's own lists confirm some of these names. The list of lightkeepers for the year to June 1902 places a James Sullivan at "Cape Canso, Cranberry Island, L. H. & F. W."[^12] The list for the year to June 1906 has "Hanlon, James P" at "Cranberry Island Light and Fog Alarm," which fits Lighthouse Friends' second stint from 1905.[^13]

Lighthouse Friends says James Hanlon served on the island from 1854 until he retired in 1883.[^1] It also gives a James P. Hanlon for 1905 to 1914. The sources do not say whether these were one man.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 24 August 1873 the Great Nova Scotia Cyclone struck the Atlantic coast of the province. Lighthouse Friends quotes the Department's report of the damage at Cranberry Island: "the sea was within a few feet of the lighthouse and dwelling" at ten at night, and it ran over the island until midnight. It carried away two boats, about ten tons of coal, and the shingles off the lighthouse porch. The report also says, "The upper Light was out from 10 p.m. until daylight."[^1]

The fire of 13 October 1881 is described above.

On 15 June 1923 the steamer Imperoyal, bound from Montreal to Halifax, "stranded on Grimes rock near Cranberry island" at 3.25 in the morning. The government ship Arras, then at Canso, was asked whether it could help. The Imperoyal's master asked for it to be sent out, but the Imperoyal got off the rock before the Arras reached her.[^14] The Arras escorted her to Halifax, where she was placed in dry dock. The Department's report for the year to June 1924 records the event.[^14]

## After the keepers

In 1929 a new tower was attached to the fog-alarm building on the southern part of the island, and the light in the old tower was discontinued, Lighthouse Friends says.[^1] NSLPS dates the present square concrete tower, white with a red lantern, to 1929.[^2] Lighthouse Friends dates a square concrete-block tower to 1978, after the steel skeleton tower of 1971, so the two accounts do not match.[^1]

In 1963 a two-storey double dwelling was added, and the island had a head keeper and two assistants.[^1] In 1971 a steel skeleton tower with an enclosed central stair was built on the island, and an electronic horn replaced the diaphone. One assistant's post was removed.[^1] In 1978, Lighthouse Friends says, a square concrete-block tower was built and the skeleton tower was moved to Jeddore Rock.[^1] A submarine cable brought electricity to the station in 1981. The island's only dwelling was torn down in 1985, the station was de-staffed in 1986, and the boathouse was taken down in 1989 after it was declared surplus.[^1] NSLPS says the station was automated by 1986.[^2]

Lighthouse Friends also says a light now stands on a square skeleton tower beside the lighthouse, and the Coast Guard list describes its light as on a "square skeleton tower, red and white rectangular daymark."[^1][^3] The sources do not say which structure carries the light today.

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light, and Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] NSLPS gives site access as by boat only, and the light sits 2.7 kilometres offshore.[^2][^1] This is a working Coast Guard light reached by water. This history does not suggest landing there.
