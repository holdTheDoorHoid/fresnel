Cape Poge Light stands at the northeast point of Chappaquiddick Island, off the eastern end of Martha's Vineyard.[^1][^11] Congress approved $2,000 for a light there on 30 January 1801.[^11] The wooden tower standing today was built in 1893, and it has been moved inland several times as the cliff eroded.[^2][^1][^11] The light has been unattended since either 1943 or 1946; the sources differ.[^11][^13]

## The place

The National Register nomination calls the point "one of the easternmost landfalls in New England and on the route to Edgartown Harbor."[^1] Edgartown had a substantial whaling fleet at the beginning of the nineteenth century, and LighthouseFriends says the government saw that some kind of navigational aid was needed to guide ships around Cape Poge and into the harbor.[^11] The Trustees of Reservations describe the same job: helping ships through the shallows of Muskeget Channel and into Edgartown Harbor.[^12]

Erosion was a constant issue at the northern tip of Chappaquiddick.[^11] In 1825 keeper Matthew Mayhew reported that two acres, half of the original station, had been lost to the sea.[^11] He wrote that it was "very unpleasant, particularly to females to be thus situated in storms", with spray flying against and over the house and no other dwelling within five miles for refuge.[^11] Four more acres were bought, and the keeper's brother was paid $250 to move the house farther inland.[^11] By 1836 the tower was only forty feet (12 m) from the edge of the cliff, and a stone wall built under the cliff made little difference.[^11]

## Building the light

Four acres were bought for $36 a couple of months after Congress approved the money, according to LighthouseFriends.[^11] The nomination's chronology gives $30.00 for four acres bought in 1801 from three named sellers, so the two sources disagree on the price.[^1] A local contractor, Duncan McBean, built a 35-foot (11 m) octagonal wooden tower and a small two-room keeper's house, and LighthouseFriends says Keeper Mayhew lit the light in December 1801.[^11] The nomination dates the first tower to 1802 and describes it as 35 feet (11 m) high, shingled, with an iron lantern on a stone foundation.[^1] The sources give different dates for the first tower and do not explain the gap.

LighthouseFriends says the first tower held a spider lamp. In 1812, it says, Winslow Lewis supplied an array of oil lamps and reflectors, and in 1857 a new lantern room was installed and a fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps and reflectors.[^11][^13] The nomination dates a change from reflectors to "revolving red and white prisms" to 1853–59.[^1] The sources do not agree on what changed when.

The nomination records that in 1838 the old tower was lost with the eroding shoreline and a new one was set back from it.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes that period differently: an 1838 inspection found the 1801 tower being moved a few yards back from the cliff edge.[^11] In July 1844 Winslow Lewis finished a new tower, joined to the keeper's dwelling by an enclosed walkway, according to LighthouseFriends, and the Trustees also give 1844 as a rebuilding year.[^11][^12]

The keeper's dwelling was a two-story house built in 1851, and the nomination gives its cost as $12,000.[^1]

The next tower is the one that stands today. The Light-House Board's report for fiscal 1893 lists Cape Poge among the lights moved that year: it was moved 40 feet (12 m) "to a new tower, June 26, 1893."[^2] The nomination gives a different date and distance, saying the conical wooden lighthouse was set back 35 feet (11 m) in 1892.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a 35-foot (11 m) wooden tower was built 40 feet (12 m) inland, and that the lantern room was inched across the staging to the new tower in a single day, in time to show the light that evening.[^11] The nomination says the existing tower conforms to 1893 plans for a "Temporary" light.[^1]

Later reports record smaller work. The Board's report for 1894 says an iron railing was put around the deck of the lantern.[^3] The report for 1899 says the boathouse was rebuilt and enlarged, an oil house was built, and a set of quarters in the dwelling was rearranged to make it easier to keep warm.[^4] In 1898, according to LighthouseFriends, the light was changed from fixed white to flashing white and red after complaints that its beacon was confused with the light of the Cross Rip Lightship.[^11]

## The keepers

Matthew Mayhew kept the light from 1801 until he died in December 1834. LighthouseFriends says he and his wife Magdalen had eight children, several of them born while the family lived at the light.[^11][^13]

His successor, Lot Norton, reported in 1842 that the dwelling had only three small rooms and no chambers, which left him very much troubled to find sleeping room for his family.[^11] He also reported that storms had "racked and twisted" the lantern so as to break its glass.[^11]

Edward Worth was head keeper twice, from 1850 to 1853 and again from 1866 to 1882.[^13] His son Jethro was assistant keeper from 1867, then head keeper in 1882 and 1883, when he moved to Edgartown Harbor.[^13][^11][^16] LighthouseFriends says the two men were father and son.[^11]

Joseph E. Barrus was assistant keeper here from 1904 to 1908 and head keeper from 1908 to 1919, and he then went to Edgartown Harbor.[^13][^16]

Henry L. Thomas was head keeper from 1919 to 1931, with P. S. King as his assistant in 1919 and 1920 and Albert S. Smith from 1920 to 1922.[^13] In 1923 a newspaper item printed a radio letter from Thomas, which LighthouseFriends quotes. He wrote: "I am a light keeper and am classed among the shut-ins." He added that the station was three miles from a small town, that the household was almost always alone, and that the radio had been a blessing.[^11] Thomas moved to Edgartown Harbor in 1931.[^11][^13][^16]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the War of 1812 the lights were ordered "forthwith extinguished." Keeper Mayhew removed the lighting apparatus from the tower and hauled it four miles to the residence of Samuel Huxford on Chappaquiddick, where it was stored in the basement. LighthouseFriends says the light was relit in early 1815.[^11]

Before Mayhew's successor, Lot Norton, could reach the station after Mayhew's death in December 1834, the schooner Hudson was lost near the point. LighthouseFriends says one of her passengers died at the lighthouse and that it is not known whether the light was operating at the time.[^11]

In its 1880 report the Life-Saving Service recommended a station at Cape Poge, among a list of places on the coast.[^14]

In fiscal 1915 the Commissioner's table of lives and property saved credits Barrus and E. H. Hopkins, his assistant, with helping a power yacht off a bar and assisting a three-masted schooner that had struck 2½ miles south of the station.[^5]

On 12 July 1919 Thomas and King towed a disabled motor boat, carrying a man, a woman and a child, to Edgartown. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin printed the rescue.[^7][^15]

In 1921 two yachts ran aground near the station. The Commissioner's report says Thomas and Albert S. Smith "assisted in floating the large sloop-yacht Ladrone, of New York, that had run aground off the reservation." They also helped float the yacht Dolly, of Boston, which had run aground on a ledge, and took five passengers from her to Edgartown in the station motor boat.[^6] The Bulletin says the department commended the two men for help given on 22 July to the Dolly.[^8] LighthouseFriends names the same two yachts.[^11]

On the night of 28 February 1925 the keepers of two second-district lights reported slight earthquake shocks. At Cape Poge the shock lasted about five minutes, the ground around the lighthouse trembled, and the lighthouse swayed so that "the rotation of the light was stopped."[^9]

During the gale of 16 and 17 November 1925, a disabled motor boat drifted off its course. Thomas gave it assistance on the shoals near the station.[^10]

## After the keepers

Markus Peiffer was keeper from 1931 to 1938, and Joseph H. Dubois from 1938 to 1943.[^13] The US Lighthouse Society's record places the light's automation in 1943, and LighthouseFriends says Dubois was keeper when it was automated.[^13][^11] The roster also lists Henry S. Brown as keeper in 1945 and 1946, and LighthouseFriends calls Brown the last keeper and says the light was made unattended in 1946.[^13][^11] The sources disagree on the last years, so this story does not choose between them.

LighthouseFriends says the keeper's house was used by a Coast Guard shore patrol during the Second World War. It was sold to a local resident in 1954 and torn down for lumber.[^11]

In 1981 the National Register nomination described the light as unmanned and active, on four acres of Coast Guard land. It also said the Coast Guard planned to move the tower 500 feet (152 m) southwest in the fall of 1986, to land owned by the Trustees of Reservations, because erosion of the sand cliff was continuing.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the move happened in 1987, when the tower was carried 500 feet (152 m) inland by helicopter. It also says the Trustees have maintained the tower since 1994.[^11] On 6 October 1997 the lantern room was lifted off by helicopter and trucked to New Bedford to be sandblasted, painted and fitted with new glass. It returned to the tower on 16 December.[^11]

The Fresnel lens has been replaced by a solar-powered 300 mm optic, which shows a flashing white light every six seconds.[^11][^13]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the lighthouse. The sources differ on who maintains it. The Trustees' page says the Coast Guard maintains the lighthouse, while LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard still owns it and cares for the optic, and that the Trustees have maintained the tower since 1994.[^12][^11]

The lighthouse is reached through the Cape Poge Wildlife Refuge, which the Trustees manage. From the Edgartown–Chappaquiddick ferry, Chappaquiddick Road runs 2.5 miles, and then Dike Road, a dirt road, runs half a mile to the gatehouse over Dike Bridge. Pedestrians enter free. Vehicles need an oversand permit.[^12] LighthouseFriends warns that walking to the lighthouse means "over three miles one way through loose sand."[^11]

LighthouseFriends says the refuge offers a lighthouse tour, with a free shuttle from the Chappy Ferry, and that the tower is open during tours. The Trustees' page lists guided tours among the refuge's activities but does not say whether the tower is open to visitors, so check before going. LighthouseFriends notes that the tour gives visitors the chance to climb the tower, so do not climb it outside a guided tour.[^11][^12]

The Trustees say the light flashes white 63 feet (19 m) above the sand dunes and can be seen by sailors nine miles out to sea.[^12]
