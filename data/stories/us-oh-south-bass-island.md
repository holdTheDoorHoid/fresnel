South Bass Island Light stands on the island's southwest point, at Put-in-Bay, Ohio, beside the South Passage into Lake Erie's western basin.[^7][^2] It was first lit in 1897 and discontinued in 1962.[^1][^2] Ohio State University has held the property since 1967, as guardian and then as permanent owner from 1997, and runs summer tours of the tower.[^2][^8]

## The place

LighthouseFriends says the 1890s were the island's peak years, when the Hotel Victory opened in 1892.[^2] The South Passage ran with the Marblehead peninsula on one side and the southern ends of South Bass and Kelleys islands on the other.[^2] In its report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1891, the Light-House Board said the passage was much used by vessels bound to and from Sandusky and Marblehead, and that "there are several dangerous shoals in it, which are only marked for daylight passage."[^3] The Board also recommended a light on the southerly end of the island, to range with the Green Island and Marblehead lights, at a cost "not to exceed $8,600."[^3]

The idea was older still. In 1838 Lieutenant Charles T. Platt found an appropriation for a light on the island's northwest tip. He preferred the southwest cape, and LighthouseFriends says he carved the letters "LH" into a tree there to mark the spot.[^2] The government bought about two acres at the southwest point from the Parker family in 1895.[^4][^2]

## Building the light

Congress made the appropriation on 18 August 1894.[^4] The Board blamed a delay in the land transfer on the time the grantor needed to prepare an abstract of title, and reported that "it was impracticable to commence the erection of the station buildings in this fiscal year."[^4] In its report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1896, the Board wrote that a contract "is being made for building the structures, consisting of a brick dwelling, tower, and woodshed," and that the metal work for a fireproof oil house had been furnished under contract.[^5]

The first winning bidder never signed the required bond, and the second round of bids came in above the money available, so the work was done by hired labor buying materials on the open market.[^2] The design departed from the usual large lighthouse of the day, which put a small keepers' house apart from the tower.[^2] This one had a "two-and-a-half-story, redbrick dwelling" with an attached tower twelve feet (3.7 m) square and forty-five feet (13.7 m) tall, crowned by a fourth-order L. Sautter & Cie. Fresnel lens.[^2][^9] The Digital Archive gives the tower as 40 feet (12.2 m) from base to lantern center, so the two sources disagree.[^1] The lamp's ruby chimney made the light red.[^2][^9] The National Register record names the architect as Lieutenant Colonel Jared A. Smith of the Corps of Engineers.[^7] An iron oil storage house went up in 1897, and a wood-frame barn in 1899.[^1]

## The keepers

The Digital Archive's roster lists eleven principal keepers from 1897 to 1962.[^1] The first was Harry H. Riley, who LighthouseFriends says lit the light for the first time on 10 July 1897.[^2] On 1 September 1898 Sandusky police took him into custody. LighthouseFriends says this was for "problems associated with his mental health," and that he "passed away the following March at a state hospital."[^2] His wife, Mary Riley, took charge of the light in the meantime.[^2] The roster lists her as principal keeper for 1898–1899, and LighthouseFriends says "Mrs. Riley took charge of the lighthouse" during her husband's illness.[^1][^2]

Enoch W. Scribner (1899–1900) and Colonel Orlo J. Mason (1900–1908) followed.[^1][^2] Charles B. Duggan arrived in 1908 from five years at West Sister Island. He cultivated twenty acres, including a vineyard of eight acres, and he kept the light until 1925.[^2][^9] Duggan "fell to his death from a forty-five-foot cliff" on the island's west end in April 1925.[^2][^9] His son Lyle, aged twenty, looked after the light in his father's place until the next year, when William Gordon was moved over from Green Island. The roster gives him as William L. Gordon, and LighthouseFriends as William F. Gordon. He kept the light from 1926 to 1937.[^2][^1]

Frank LaRose kept the light from 1937 to 1941, after serving as principal keeper at Fairport Harbor West Breakwater from 1927 to 1937. The Digital Archive's record of him names both posts.[^10] Robert E. Jones (1941–1944) and Kenneth Nester (1944–1947) followed. Paul F. Prochnow, the last keeper, served from 1947 until 1962. LighthouseFriends counts fifteen years at the station for him.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The burning of the steam barge *Isabel Joyce* in 1915 is the one rescue LighthouseFriends ties to the station. The steam barge burned, and Duggan brought eight of her crew ashore at the station and looked after them, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year 1915 also names Keeper C. Duggan of South Bass Island in its table headed "Life and Property by Vessels or Employees of the Lighthouse Service."[^6]

## After the keepers

The light was automated on 26 September 1962, and the Coast Guard put an automatic light on a steel tower in its place. The Fresnel lens went to the "Lake Erie Island Historical Museum."[^2] A tenant rented the dwelling for five years after the keepers left.[^2]

In 1967 the Coast Guard turned the property over to the federal surplus property division. The director of Ohio State University's Stone Laboratory proposed that the university apply for it, and in October 1967 the university became guardian under a thirty-year quitclaim deed.[^2] In 1983 the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration chose the site for a meteorological station, and one was built there.[^2] The lighthouse was listed on the National Register in 1990.[^7] When the quitclaim expired in 1997 the university gained permanent ownership. LighthouseFriends notes that "The Coast Guard, however, still maintains ownership of the skeletal light tower."[^2]

The light had changed in 1926 to alternating red and white flashes, LighthouseFriends says. In 1929 electric lighting made the light far brighter.[^2] Tours began in 2007.[^2][^9]

## Today

Ohio State's research and academic staff use the lighthouse today.[^2][^9] Ohio Sea Grant's current listing gives the summer tours as Fridays and Saturdays, from 12 June to 15 August, 11 a.m. to 4 p.m., free. It says "Visitors can climb the stairs" to the top of the tower.[^8] The season is over. Outside the tours, LighthouseFriends describes access as "normally restricted," since the university owns the lighthouse and its grounds.[^2] Ferry passengers bound for South Bass Harbor can see the lighthouse.[^2]
