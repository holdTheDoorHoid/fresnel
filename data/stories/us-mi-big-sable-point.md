Big Sable Point Light stands on a sandy point of Lake Michigan's eastern shore in Mason County, Michigan, about nine miles north of Ludington. Its 112-foot (34 m) tower was first lit in 1867 and has been encased in metal since about 1900.[^1][^8]

## The place

French explorers and traders called the point Grande Pointe au Sable, and the Lighthouse Board's reports of the 1890s list the station under that name.[^1][^7] The state's historical marker says that twelve ships were wrecked along this stretch of shore in 1855, and that the lumber trade made the case for a light. According to the marker, State Senator Charles Mears pressed the legislature to ask the federal government for one.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives an earlier try: Congress appropriated $6,000 in 1856 and Michigan ceded the land the next year, but only $888 was spent before the rest of the money went back to the treasury.[^1]

The question came back after the Civil War. In 1865 the Lighthouse Board reported, in words Terry Pepper quotes, that "the interests of commerce demand that Grand Point Au Sable be suitably lighted."[^3]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $35,000 in July 1866, and Michigan deeded 933 acres to the United States for the station.[^3] Work began in early 1867 with Lighthouse Board and Army Corps of Engineers workers, who first built a dock for unloading supplies and a cofferdam to keep water out of the foundation.[^3] The tower is Cream City brick from Milwaukee, with walls five feet thick at the base and two feet thick below the gallery, and it stood 112 feet (34 m) from the ground to the top of the ventilator ball.[^3] The keepers' dwelling was built of the same brick and joined to the tower by a covered passageway; it was a duplex, with the head keeper on the ground floor and the assistant above.[^1][^3] Accounts of the foundation differ: Lighthouse Friends describes stone laid 6.5 feet below ground, Pepper describes cut stone starting six feet below grade and rising three feet above it, and the Lighthouse Society archive lists wood pilings.[^1][^3][^4] The lantern held a fixed white third-order Fresnel lens made by L. Sautter & Cie.[^1][^4] Lighthouse Friends says the first head keeper, Alonzo Hyde, lit the lamp for the first time on 1 November 1867; the archive gives only the year, 1867.[^1][^4]

The tower soon needed help. A steam fog signal for the point, at a cost not to exceed $5,500, was authorised by the act of 15 February 1893, and the Lighthouse Board's reports for 1893 and 1895 both say no money had yet been appropriated for it.[^5][^6] The report for the year ended 30 June 1895 also records that "a brick oil house with metal roof, door, and shelving was built."[^6] By the report for the year ended 30 June 1899 the station had a signal house, a hoisting engine, track and a turntable, and its 10-inch steam whistle had run about 164 hours that year.[^7] The sources do not agree on dates for the fog signal: the Lighthouse Society archive lists a fog signal building constructed in 1897, while Lighthouse Friends describes a brick fog signal building of 1908 with duplicate oil engines, first sounding on 20 May 1909.[^4][^1] The sources do not settle which building was which.

The brick was failing. Pepper says the district inspector reported flaking in 1898.[^3] In the year ended 30 June 1899 the Board prepared "a plan and estimate of cost for encasing the tower with metal."[^7] A contract was made on 14 December 1899, and the work was finished in June 1900.[^8] The Board described it as 18 metal cylinders, each stepping back from the one below, filled with concrete as the work went on, and called it a "novel construction, which was adopted to avoid building a new tower, or more extensive repairs."[^8] The Board's list of changes to aids to navigation records that the colour of the tower was changed on 26 June 1900.[^8] Pepper puts the cost at $4,925 and names the J. G. Wagner Company of Milwaukee as the contractor; the Michigan historical marker dates the encasing to 1902 instead of 1900.[^3][^2] Lighthouse Friends says the watchroom section stayed bare until 1905, and that the watchroom and lantern were painted black in October 1916, giving the tower its present look.[^1]

Later work changed the dwelling and the light. Pepper says a second assistant was authorised on 12 July 1909 once the fog signal was running, and that the dwelling was remodelled rather than a second house built.[^3] Lighthouse Friends adds that an incandescent oil-vapour lamp raised the light's intensity on 25 June 1910.[^1] In its 1921 report the Commissioner of Lighthouses recorded $5,999 spent at Big Sable on general repairs, a water-supply pump, a sewage-disposal plant and concrete walks.[^10]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the keeper registers, lists twenty head keepers from Alonzo Hyde in 1867 to Bernard Smith in 1971.[^4] Hyde's entry gives his dates as 1804 to 1878, a starting salary of $600, and says he resigned in 1869.[^11] The roster then shows an Alonzo W. Hyde as head keeper from 1869 to 1871 and a Mrs. Elsa J. Hyde as first assistant from 1869 to 1871; the sources do not say how the Hydes were related.[^4][^1] The marker names the first keeper as Alonzo A. Hyde and says he kept the light with his wife, Laura.[^2] The first assistant's post was held by a woman again from 1874 to 1882, when Sarah M. Caswell served under Burr Caswell, the head keeper; the sources do not state the relationship.[^4]

Samuel Gagnon's seventeen years as head keeper, 1905 to 1922, is the longest stint of any head keeper on the roster; Lewellyn Vannatter (1923 to 1936) and George Rogan (1936 to 1947) followed him.[^4] Lewellyn A. Vannatter came to Big Sable as second assistant in 1912, was transferred to South Fox Island in 1918 as first assistant, and returned to take charge in 1923, according to Lighthouse Friends. It also says he had earlier served at Rawley Point and Milwaukee Pierhead, and that he retired on disability in 1936.[^1] On 15 June 1934 he reported that several lights with a normal range of about 20 miles were visible from Big Sable: Twin River Point at 53 miles, Sturgeon Bay Canal at 65, Point Betsie at 46 and Frankfort at 42, along with the airway beacon on a Chicago building 160 miles away.[^12] Lighthouse Friends says that in 1931 a winch handle spun and struck him on the head, and that the Coast Guard took him by boat to Ludington for treatment of a slight skull fracture.[^1]

Before a road reached the point, Lighthouse Friends says, keepers sometimes walked the nine miles to Ludington and back in a day for supplies and mail.[^1] It dates the land deed to the state for a park and the road to 1926, while Pepper says the road was built in 1933.[^1][^3] Homer R. Meverden, the last civilian keeper, had served at Skillagalee, Grays Reef, Beaver Head and Seul Choix before coming to Big Sable as first assistant in 1949, and he was head keeper from 1964 until 1968; Lighthouse Friends calls him the station's last civilian keeper.[^13][^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Board's reports record trouble with the fog signal after the encasing. It was damaged and discontinued on 15 November 1900, and was re-established when navigation opened in 1901.[^9] The report for that year also records materials delivered for repairing the fog signal's water-supply crib, the tramway and the keeper's dwelling roof.[^9]

Erosion came later. By 1941, Lighthouse Friends says, the water had undermined the fog signal building, so a skeletal tower was put up in 1941 to house the equipment and the old building was torn down; steel pilings were driven in front of the station in 1943.[^1] Pepper puts a seawall at the tower in 1943 and says it was breached in 1977, with waves coming close to undermining the tower before it was replaced.[^3]

## After the keepers

Big Sable was the last Great Lakes light to be electrified, in 1949, according to Pepper, who calls electrification a double-edged sword because it opened the way to automation.[^3] Lighthouse Friends adds that a diesel generator, indoor plumbing and heating came in 1949 and power lines in 1953.[^1] The last civilian keeper left in 1968, and Coast Guard personnel looked after the station until April 1971.[^1] Left unattended, Pepper says, the buildings deteriorated through vandalism and neglect.[^3] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 4 August 1983.[^3]

In 1985 the Coast Guard built a backup tower farther inland, expecting the old one might fall, and the Fresnel lens was removed and replaced by a modern beacon outside the lantern room; the backup was never needed.[^1] The Foundation for Behavioral Research took a 25-year lease in 1986, and the Big Sable Point Lighthouse Keepers Association was formed in 1987.[^3][^1] The state's Department of Natural Resources took the property from the federal government in 2002 and leased it to the association.[^1] The third-order lens, restored in 2016, is displayed at the Port of Ludington Maritime Museum.[^1]

## Today

The tower and dwelling are in Ludington State Park, and Lakeshore Keepers operates the lighthouse for visitors.[^14] It is reached only on foot or by bike.[^14] Lighthouse Friends gives a 1.6-mile walk over a sandy road from the park, and the operator's 2026 announcement describes a 1.8-mile out-and-back walk or bike ride from the park.[^1][^15] The operator opens its lights to the public from May to October, charges a fee to climb the tower, and requires shoes with heel straps for the climb.[^14][^15] Lighthouse Friends says volunteer keepers live in the dwelling's upper floor in summer and give tours, and that the keepers' association has recreated the station as it appeared in 1948, including a 2009 replica of the 1941 fog signal tower.[^1]
