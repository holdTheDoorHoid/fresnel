Burnt Coat Harbor Light stands on Hockamock Head, at the southern tip of Swan's Island, Maine, where it marks the entrance to Burnt Coat Harbor.[^1][^3] It was first lit on 15 August 1872, and it is a square, tapered brick tower beside an L-shaped keeper's house.[^3][^1] The tower is still an active aid to navigation, the Town of Swan's Island owns the station, and the restored keeper's house is open to visitors in summer.[^2][^3]

## The place

The Light-House Board's report for fiscal 1893 calls Burnt Coat Harbor "an excellent, capacious harbor of refuge."[^5] The Board's 1871 report, as the National Register nomination quotes it, calls the harbor "commodious and safe" and says it is "a distant 36 miles from the nearest place of safe anchorage on that coast."[^1]

The Board described the station as "situated at the extreme end of the peninsula," separated from the town by the harbor.[^4][^6] In winter, it reported, the harbor was sometimes filled with ice, so that communication with the town by water "can not be relied upon."[^4][^6] In its reports for fiscal 1894 and fiscal 1896 the Board recommended an appropriation of $500 for the legal expenses of obtaining title to the land and for the cost of a road from the public road to the light. The owners of the adjacent land offered to convey the right of way to the Government at a nominal price.[^4][^6]

## Building the light

The station was built in 1872 as a pair of range lights, which were meant to guide ships to the harbor entrance.[^1] The rear tower carried a fourth-order Fresnel lens, and LighthouseFriends says the front tower had a fifth-order lens.[^2][^3] In 1881 an 80-foot (24 m) covered walkway was built between the two towers.[^3][^1]

The range lights did not work as intended.[^3][^5] The Board's report for fiscal 1893 says that one of them had been discontinued, and that a vessel was wrecked "last year" while trying to make the harbor by their aid. It proposed a light on Green Island, about a mile south of the entrance, in place of the discontinued light.[^5] LighthouseFriends dates the front tower's fall to 1 August 1883, when it was jacked up and tipped into the sea.[^3] The nomination says the front light was discontinued in 1884 and later removed.[^1] The two accounts differ, and this story does not settle which is right.

A 3,500-gallon rainwater cistern was added in 1883, and a boathouse in 1885.[^3] The brick oil house dates from 1895.[^1][^2] The bell house was erected in 1911, and automatic striking machinery for the fog bell was added to the station that year.[^1][^3]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists eighteen keepers, from 1872 into the Coast Guard years.[^2] The first, Frederick Alexander Allen, was appointed by President Ulysses S. Grant in January 1872, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] The archive lists him from 1872 to 1875.[^2] LighthouseFriends says his logbook is now in the National Archives, and that many of its notes concern the weather, repairs, and whitewashing or painting the station.[^3] On 10 February 1875 Allen recorded that the area was frozen solid, and that "it was possible to walk five miles to the town of Brooklin."[^3] He had nine children. He died on 11 December 1875.[^3]

William N. Wasgatt was appointed keeper after Allen's death.[^2][^3] In July 1876, LighthouseFriends reports, the station's dory capsized during an outing, drowning a visiting woman from Boston, Mrs. Cutler, and nearly costing Wasgatt his wife and two daughters.[^3] Thomas E. Dodge followed from 1883 to 1886.[^2]

James H. Orcutt served from November 1886 until late 1897, when he died at the age of sixty-four.[^3][^2] Orrin L. Milan was promoted to keeper on 23 October 1897 at a salary of $540 a year, after five years as an assistant at Mount Desert Rock.[^3] The archive lists him until 1932.[^2] LighthouseFriends says his salary rose to $552 in 1912 and to $780 in 1918.[^3] His wife, Nettie, was the Swan's Island newspaper correspondent and president of the Ladies Aid for thirty-five years.[^3] LighthouseFriends reports that she was asked in a newspaper interview whether she had ever been lonely at the station. She said she had never been lonely in her life: "Too much going on!"[^3] Their son Frank was born at the station, seven months after his parents arrived.[^3]

The archive prints Roscoe M. Chandler's stint as 1560 to 1943. That first date cannot be right.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates his keeping from 1932 until the early years of the Second World War. It says that, because of the war, the Coast Guard asked his wife, Mary, to leave the station, making way for four or five young coastguardsmen who were to help the keeper and serve as lookouts.[^2][^3] The roster then runs on through the Coast Guard years, with keepers listed until 1975.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The only wreck the Board's fiscal 1893 report ties to the station is a vessel wrecked "last year" while trying to make the harbor by the range lights. The report does not name it.[^5]

LighthouseFriends reports that on New Year's Day 1902 the British schooner *Prohibition* ran aground near the station. Her anchor parted during a gale off Harbor Island. Orin Milan and two seamen reached six of her crew on Scrag Island and brought them to the light.[^3]

## After the keepers

The station was electrified in 1935, and a siren whistle foghorn was installed after the electrification.[^3] The Coast Guard automated the light in 1975.[^1][^3] In 1977 the fourth-order lens was removed and an automatic light was set on a nearby skeleton tower. The next year an automatic 250 mm optic went back into the lighthouse.[^3] In 1982 the Coast Guard stripped the peeling paint from the brick tower and sealed it, to save money on repainting. Complaints that the bare brick blended into the pines led the Coast Guard to paint it white again.[^3]

The Maine Historic Preservation Commission prepared the station's National Register nomination in 1987.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the station was transferred to the Town of Swan's Island, under legislation that Congress passed, and gives the year as 1993 in one place and 1994 in another.[^3] The town replaced the shingles on the bell tower in 2001, and used a 2002 grant for an architectural study of preservation options. The non-profit Friends of the Swan's Island Lighthouse took on the restoration, which LighthouseFriends says has steadily moved forward since 2006.[^3] The tower was restored from 2016 to 2019, funded in part by an $85,000 National Maritime Heritage grant. An additional grant in 2020 supported restoration of the oil house, and LighthouseFriends says the work was completed in 2021.[^3] A solar-powered LED optic was installed in 2017.[^2]

## Today

The Town of Swan's Island owns the station.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends says the keeper's house and tower are open to the public in summer, and gives the hours as Tuesday through Saturday, 11 a.m. to 3 p.m. The upstairs apartment of the keeper's house has been available for weekly rental since 2017.[^3] The ferry from Bass Harbor serves the island. LighthouseFriends gives the directions as a half mile from the ferry landing to a stop sign, then 3.8 miles along the main road to the light.[^3] The archive records the station as open to the public.[^2] The hours come from LighthouseFriends, so check them before you go.
