The Sturgeon Bay Canal Light stands on the north side of the entrance to the ship canal at Sturgeon Bay, in Door County, Wisconsin. It is a steel tower about 98 feet (30 m) tall, built in 1898 and 1899 and largely rebuilt in 1903, after the first design failed in the wind.[^1][^2] The canal let ships cut across to the southern end of Green Bay, and the keepers at the canal looked after two lighthouses, a fog signal and some minor canal beacons.[^5]

## The place

A pierhead light went up on a pier at the canal's eastern end in late 1881, with only a sixth-order lens.[^5] The United States bought the canal in 1893 for $103,000 and handed it to the Corps of Engineers.[^5] In 1896 the Lighthouse Board took over responsibility for three small lights along the canal.[^5]

The Life-Saving Service planned a station here too. Its annual report for the year ended June 30, 1884, said that titles to sites at several Lake Michigan places, Sturgeon Bay Canal among them, "have been secured," and that plans for the stations were being prepared.[^10]

## Building the light

In 1896 test borings went down 49 feet, and they showed the ground could carry a cylindrical steel tower, LighthouseFriends reports.[^5] The lighthouse steamer *Amaranth* brought materials to Sturgeon Bay on 13 May 1898.[^5] The foundation was a concrete slab, 29 feet square and 8 feet deep.[^5] The nomination names the contractor as John P. McGuire of Cleveland. It describes the first design as an iron tower, round in section, 78 feet (24 m) tall to the base of the watchroom, braced by eight lattice buttresses.[^1]

The Lighthouse Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1899, says the tower "was all completed on October 3, 1898."[^2] The light itself was not lit until 17 March 1899, when a third-order light began to show.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the light flashed alternating red and white.[^5]

The tower did not hold up. LighthouseFriends says that Keeper Charles Chapman tested the light later in October 1898, and that it soon became apparent the tower had serious design problems.[^5] The nomination says the design "failed because it did not take into account the heavy winds of this area," and that the structure had to be modified considerably in 1903.[^1] The 1903 work turned the cylindrical tower into a stair tower and set a new skeletal steel frame of six posts around the watchroom and lantern. The buttresses were enlarged, so the foundation grew from 25 to 36 feet across. The watchroom, lantern and lens from the original tower were retained.[^1]

The Board's report also describes a third-order lens at the canal light that was revolved on ball bearings. The writer did not think the arrangement had been in use long enough to get beyond the experimental stage.[^4]

The sources disagree on the lens. The Board's 1899 report and LighthouseFriends call it third order.[^3][^4][^5] The nomination describes the lens as a "Second Order Fresnel, with eight panels," made in Paris, and the archive's place record lists the original optic as second order and the current one as third order.[^1][^9] This story follows the Board's 1899 report, which was written when the light was new.

In August 1900 the tower was painted white so that it would serve better as a daymark. Mariners had complained that the reddish-brown paint blended with the trees, so that the white keeper's dwelling was often easier to spot by day than the tower.[^5] A new dwelling for an assistant keeper went up that year.[^5]

## The keepers

The archive's roster begins in 1899, but LighthouseFriends gives a longer history for the station. It lists a head keeper named Rufus Wright from 1882 to 1886, spelled "Rufus J." in its keeper list and "Rufus M." in its text.[^6][^5]

Charles O. Chapman came to the station as an assistant in 1884 and was promoted to head keeper in 1886, according to LighthouseFriends.[^5][^6] The archive's roster gives his years as head keeper as 1899 to 1910.[^9] He died in office in 1910.[^5] His son Claude was the station's third assistant when his father died. The roster lists Claude as third assistant from 1906 to 1910, second assistant from 1910 to 1919, and first assistant from 1919 to 1925.[^9][^5]

Claude was head keeper at Chambers Island Light from 1925 until 1933, when he came back to the canal as head keeper.[^5] LighthouseFriends says he died in an accident five months after returning, on 4 November 1933.[^6] It describes the accident: Chapman had gone aboard the government dredge *Kewaunee*, which was tied up at the station, to talk with its engineer, and a shotgun that the tug's captain was unloading nearby went off and killed him.[^6]

Charles Bavry was the first assistant from 1899 to 1903, then head keeper from 1910 to 1920 and again in 1926 and 1927.[^9] LighthouseFriends gives his start as first assistant as 1896.[^6] Carl Witzmann was first assistant from 1903 to 1910.[^9] Nels Engberg was head keeper from 1920 to 1926, and Conrad A. Stram was head keeper from 1927 to 1933.[^9]

John T. Trucker was third assistant from 1912 to 1916 and head keeper from 1934 to 1942.[^9] LighthouseFriends calls him a quiet man, five feet six inches tall. It says the Bible was often his companion on watch, and that he read the whole book seven times over. In winter he applied his woodcarving to snow, making a giant horse and a dog.[^6] After the Coast Guard took over the lights in 1939, Trucker stayed on as a civilian keeper for a year and then joined the Coast Guard.[^7] John J. Hahn was first assistant from 1925 to 1932 and head keeper from 1942 to 1947.[^9]

The archive lists 44 keeper stints for the station. Many of the assistants moved between the first, second, third and fourth assistant posts, as the roster shows.[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Life-Saving Service had its own station at the canal, which its reports call the "Sturgeon Bay Canal Station" in the Eleventh District. It had its own keeper and surfmen, and it was not the lighthouse.[^12]

On 27 October 1887 the schooner *Dreadnaught*, of Milwaukee, was trying to make the canal entrance in a fresh south wind and heavy sea. She became unmanageable and struck the end of the north pier. The life-saving crew launched the surfboat, found they had no suitable line to reach the pier, went back to the station for one, and then got the schooner safely into the canal. Her captain thanked the crew for the service.[^11]

On 19 September 1888 the wife of the life-saving keeper, William Nequette, and a 17-year-old friend fell into the canal from the dock. The keeper was close by, "and at once ran to the place," the report says. He jumped into the water and held the two women up until the surfmen arrived and helped get them out.[^12]

The sources read for this story give no other wreck or rescue tied to the lighthouse itself.

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over the country's lighthouses in 1939.[^7] The archive's place record gives 1972 as the year the light was automated.[^9] LighthouseFriends, however, says the Canal Station is still staffed by the Coast Guard.[^7] The two sources do not agree, and this story does not settle it.

In March 2016 the Coast Guard asked for public comment on a plan to demolish the empty 1896 keeper's dwelling, which was enlarged in 1904. The Coast Guard judged the house unfit to live in, citing hazards from lead paint and asbestos in the plaster.[^7]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light, which stands within a Coast Guard station.[^1][^8] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower and dwelling are closed. Visitors keep to the station road, and from there a good photograph of the lighthouse is hard to get.[^8] The archive's place record marks the site as "Open to Public: Yes."[^9] This story follows LighthouseFriends on what a visitor can reach, because it describes the site on the ground.
