Point Hueneme Light marks the eastern entrance to the Santa Barbara Channel, on the Ventura County coast of California. The first lighthouse on the point was a two-story wooden keeper's house with a three-story light tower, first lit in December 1874.[^1][^2] Harbor works moved that building in 1940 and it was later demolished. Its successor, a concrete tower at the entrance to the port, still shows a light, and the light has been automated since 1972.[^1][^3]

## The place

The channel's eastern entrance is about eleven miles (18 km) wide, between Point Hueneme and Anacapa Island.[^2] Steamers had used the channel since 1849, when it saved ten to twelve hours each way on the voyage.[^2]

On 2 December 1853 the Pacific Mail steamer *Winfield Scott*, carrying at least 200 passengers, ran into fog and struck rocks near Anacapa Island.[^2] The passengers were put ashore in lifeboats on a rocky shelf off Anacapa, and then taken to an accessible spot on the island. The Museum's account says the fog was "so dense you could cut it with a knife."[^2] The following morning the steamer *California* sighted the stranded passengers and took the women, children and infirm aboard.[^2] The wreck was abandoned on 12 December 1853.[^2] The Museum places it twelve miles (19 km) from Point Hueneme.[^2]

Thomas M. Ward dates official recognition of the need for a light at the point to 1857.[^1] In 1873 Congress appropriated $22,000 for a light station at Point Hueneme.[^1] The county's landmark record calls the reservation 60 acres; the Museum and Ward give 30 acres.[^3][^2][^1]

Getting to the light was a separate problem. Congress had appropriated $250 in 1890 for a right of way from the light station to the county road.[^14] The owners asked an "exorbitant" price, so the Board's report for 1893 says it was decided to obtain title by condemnation.[^6] The 1894 report repeated the plan.[^7] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1901 says a roadway was then built from the town to the light, over land bought the year before.[^9]

## Building the light

The contract went to Salisbury and Company, and work began on 25 April 1874.[^1][^11] The design was by Pelz, the Museum says, and the Point Fermin light, a near twin, still shows what the original looked like.[^2]

The house had ten rooms and four fireplaces.[^2] The keeper's family lived on the ground floor and the assistant's family on the second story, and a tower about 65 feet (20 m) tall rose above the dwelling.[^1][^2][^11] Ward says the lantern room was reached by four flights of stairs, the last nearly vertical.[^1]

The light was first exhibited on 15 December 1874.[^1] LighthouseFriends notes that the same day a nearly identical light began at Point Fermin.[^11] The first lamp burned lard oil in a Funk float; the Museum says it was later changed to a kerosene lamp.[^1][^2]

Sources disagree about the optic. Ward describes a fourth-order Fresnel lens from the first lighting, and a second Fresnel lens in 1897.[^1] The Museum and the county date the installation of the Fresnel lens to 1897.[^1][^2][^3]

The signal changed several times. The light flashed white until 1889, then showed fixed red, which LighthouseFriends dates to 1 April 1889.[^2][^11] The Board's 1892 report records the change from fixed red to occulting white on 15 April 1892, the date Ward also gives.[^13][^1] The same report lists Point Hueneme among the new occulting lights in use.[^5] In 1899 the Board recorded a change of characteristic on 10 January 1899. Its report for that year says the occulting apparatus "was taken out and a lens with flashing white characteristics substituted."[^8][^15] LighthouseFriends describes a revolving fourth-order Fresnel lens showing a white flash every five seconds.[^11]

Water and fog were the next problems. The Board's 1882 report, as Ward quotes it, called a steam fog signal "important" because passing steamers ran close inside Anacapa and the low land gave poor sight in fog.[^1][^2] The report recommended $7,000 for the signal.[^1] The Museum says Congress approved the money quickly and a signal followed.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the $7,000 was requested but never provided.[^11] Ward says the station was outfitted with a fog signal, without giving a date.[^1]

The same report said the artesian well water was unfit for domestic use and had caused sickness in the keepers' families, so a 10,000-gallon tank was put up to catch rain from the roofs.[^1] The Museum gives a different cause: by 1882 saltwater had invaded the well, and the tank replaced it.[^2]

## The keepers

Samuel Ensign wrote his first log entry on 9 November 1874, having been promoted from first assistant at Pigeon Point to principal keeper here.[^1][^4] The first assistant, Melvin P. Giles, reached the station from San Francisco with his wife and two sons on the steamer *Constantine*.[^1][^4]

Ensign's log for 11 February 1875 mentions fog, and says he went to the watch room to relieve Giles, who had gone to San Buenaventura as a witness in a criminal case.[^1] He found Giles asleep and the light very low.[^1] Ward found no report of any punishment for Giles.[^1] The Museum says Giles was caught asleep on watch twice and was promoted rather than dismissed.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he became principal keeper at Pigeon Point in August 1875.[^11] The archive roster lists him only as first assistant, 1874 to 1875.[^4]

The Museum says both the principal keeper and the assistant were dismissed in 1878 for failing to keep the grounds in order.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives a different reason for Ensign's dismissal that August: physical incapacity to do the keeper's work.[^11] The roster shows John A. F. McFarland as principal keeper from 1878.[^4]

Heap's 1889 account of the service describes how vacancies were filled: the Collector of Customs of the district nominated a replacement, who first served as an acting assistant keeper.[^12] This is general context on the service, not a record of this station.[^12]

Charles F. Allen appears on the roster twice: as acting keeper in 1894 and as principal from 1911 to 1914.[^4] The Museum gives his tenure as 1894 to 1915 and credits him with winning a $2,000 appropriation for the road to the light.[^4][^2] The roster also lists Anna H. Allen as acting principal keeper for 1911.[^4] Only the roster names her.[^4]

On 1 May 1908, during the Allens' time, two thousand people swarmed to the beach as the Great White Fleet passed the Ventura coast, and as many as possible were allowed onto the porches of the lighthouse.[^2] In May 1914 the Allens' fourteen-year-old daughter Melba invited the seventh and eighth grades of the school to a birthday party at her home in the lighthouse.[^2]

Walter White kept the light from 1927 to 1948.[^4] The Museum says the lighthouse usually welcomed the public in his time, and that his greatest contribution was managing the transition from the old lighthouse to the new harbor facilities.[^2] In 1934 an electric motor replaced the clockwork that the keeper had hand-wound every six hours.[^2][^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the evening after the United States declared war in December 1941, reports that unidentified airplanes had been heard in the area led to the light being blacked out for the rest of the night.[^2] It was off for the first time since 1874.[^2] The next day Keeper White complained that he had been ordered to shut it off. He saw no logic in it, while the surrounding community stayed lit and was an easy target for enemy aircraft.[^2]

## After the keepers

The 1912 Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists a first-class siren fog signal established at Point Hueneme.[^10] The Museum says the old steam system was replaced in January 1912 at a cost of $18,000, with pneumatic equipment, twin air compressors and a fog horn.[^2]

The light was electrified in 1925, and Ward dates the shift from the oil lantern to an electric lamp to that year.[^1][^2]

During the harbor's construction the harbor commission bought the old building at auction for $51, with plans to make it the center of a yacht club on the new harbor's west jetty.[^1] On 15 February 1940 a local house mover, John R. Brakey, hauled the building onto a barge for the move across the channel.[^1] The Museum adds that a broken cable made the structure lurch toward the spectators, and that the barge jammed onto a mud flat, so the move waited for the next high tide.[^2] A temporary tower carried the lantern and lens while the new concrete tower was built.[^2][^1]

The new Art Moderne tower is 48 feet (15 m) high and received the old fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^2][^4] The county dates its dedication to 1941.[^3] Ward says the Navy took over the harbor in 1942 and the old building was demolished; the county says the demolition came in 1943.[^1][^3]

The light was fully automated in 1972.[^3] The county designated the lightworks Ventura County Landmark No. 57; its text gives 1 October 1979, and its footer gives 1980.[^3]

## Today

The present light is a Coast Guard aid.[^3] The county's record places it at a Coast Guard station at the Navy's civilian engineering laboratory, at the end of the public beach promenade.[^3] It carries a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^4] The Museum's 2010 article says the lighthouse was open to tours on some days and advises checking the city's website for hours; the archive's place record says it is not open to the public.[^2][^4] Check before you go, and do not climb the tower or walk onto Coast Guard ground without arranged access.
