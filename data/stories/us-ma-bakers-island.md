Bakers Island Light stands on the northern end of a 60-acre summer-colony island in Salem Sound, Massachusetts, where two stone towers, nicknamed "Ma and Pa", stand beside the approach to Salem Harbor.[^1][^2][^14]

## The place

The island, about three miles east of the entrance to Salem's harbor, was first granted to Salem in 1660.[^1] By the late 1700s Salem was a busy trading port, yet the islands and rocks outside it had no major aid to navigation.[^1] On 25 May 1791 the Salem Marine Society voted a committee of three to put up a beacon on the island, and a public subscription paper was circulated that July. It said the beacon was wanted because of accidents and "frequent Mistakes made by Vessels coming into this Port in the Night & in thick weather."[^1] Seventy-one people signed and gave an extra £89.[^1] The Reverend Dr. William Bentley recorded in his diary that the intended beacon "was raised by a large & Jovial party" on 28 July; it was a red conical tower 57 feet (17 m) high, topped with a two-foot black ball, and Bentley thought its foundation stones "very miserably laid."[^1] It served as a daymark only.[^1][^2]

## Building the light

The Society kept pressing for a proper light. According to New England Lighthouses, President Washington signed an appropriation of $6,000 for a lighthouse on 8 April 1796.[^1] The Massachusetts lighthouse superintendent, General Benjamin Lincoln, worried that two lights could be mistaken for the twin lights already at Thacher Island and Plymouth, and proposed a building with three lights; the idea was dropped.[^1][^2] Solomon Blake, a Boston contractor, built the station on ten acres for $3,674.57, and the lights went into service on 3 January 1798.[^1][^2] They were two towers about 40 feet (12 m) apart, rising from the roof of a two-story wooden keeper's house.[^1][^2] The two accounts disagree on which was the taller: New England Lighthouses puts the south light 95 feet (29 m) above mean high water and the north light at 78 feet (24 m), while LighthouseFriends reverses them.[^1][^2]

The twin lights did not last in that form. LighthouseFriends says a severe storm in August 1815 shattered glass in the lanterns and closed the station for some months, and that it reopened with a single octagonal stone tower.[^2] New England Lighthouses instead dates the change to a June 1816 notice to mariners, which announced that the lights would go dark for two months while the station was altered, leaving one fixed light.[^1] Whichever account is right, the single light was blamed for trouble: on 24 February 1817, in a pre-dawn snowstorm, the ship *Union*, carrying pepper and tin, ran onto the island's rocks, and mariners said one light was hard to tell from Boston Light.[^1]

In 1820 the Salem Marine Society and citizens of Salem, Marblehead and Beverly asked for a second light to be restored. A new conical stone tower and the refurbished shorter octagonal tower were lit together in October 1820, according to New England Lighthouses.[^1] The National Register nomination and the U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive give 1821 for the present tower, and the sources differ on its height: the archive and New England Lighthouses give 59 feet (18 m), LighthouseFriends 47 feet.[^3][^4][^1][^2] The pair picked up the nicknames "Mr. and Mrs." and "Ma and Pa".[^1][^2]

Engineer I. W. P. Lewis surveyed the station in 1842 and found fifteen lamps and 14-inch reflectors in the taller tower and ten lamps and 13-inch reflectors in the shorter, though LighthouseFriends counts eleven in the shorter.[^1][^2] As New England Lighthouses quotes his report, he wrote that the double light should be maintained "in the most perfect state of efficiency" but that this was "far from being the case", and called the dwelling "old and crazy".[^1] Fourth-order Fresnel lenses replaced the old lamps and reflectors: 1857 in both LighthouseFriends and New England Lighthouses, 1855 in the Lighthouse Society archive.[^1][^2][^3] The National Register nomination says the dwellings were originally built in 1878 and improved in 1958.[^4] New England Lighthouses instead dates a keeper's house to about 1857 and an assistant keeper's house to 1873.[^1] It also says a fog bell tower stood before 1868, was replaced in 1877, was destroyed by lightning in July 1879 and was rebuilt that October with new striking machinery.[^1] The same source says the lamps went to kerosene in 1878 and to electricity in 1938.[^1] In June 1906 Congress voted $10,000 for a new fog signal to replace the bell, and a siren in a new brick building went into service in July 1907 (the 12th in New England Lighthouses, the 8th in LighthouseFriends).[^1][^2] Island residents complained loudly, and the signal was eventually aimed out to sea through a megaphone, so that it was barely audible on the island.[^1]

## The keepers

The first keeper, Captain George Chapman of the Salem Marine Society, stayed until 1815, when he was about 75; he later went blind, which people blamed on the brightness of the lamps.[^1][^3] His successor, Joseph Perkins, had received a federal pilot's commission in 1791. A story told of him is that during the War of 1812 he rowed out to the frigate *Constitution*, pursued by two British ships, and piloted her to Marblehead.[^1] In August 1817 Bentley visited him and wrote that Perkins had "a garden enclosed, three cows, six pigs", and was cutting hay for the winter.[^1]

Nathaniel Ward, keeper from 1821, died in 1825 when he and his assistant, a Mr. Marshall, were caught by a storm crossing in a flat-bottomed boat loaded with supplies. Ward's body was found on the island's shore and Marshall's in the boat; Ward was 49 and left a large family.[^1][^2] New England Lighthouses dates the drowning to the end of March 1825, LighthouseFriends to late October.[^1][^2]

Ambrose Martin kept the light from 1825, at $300 a year, raised to $400 in 1829.[^1][^2] The archive lists him until 1850, while New England Lighthouses' own list ends his term in 1843 and names a Joseph G. Martin from that year.[^3][^1] His daughter Jane learned to help tend the station and later became keeper of Marblehead Light.[^1][^2] His son Elbridge Gerry Martin, then 13, made the newspapers in November 1827 for shooting an eagle on the island with a wingspan of nearly seven feet, which went to the East India Museum in Salem.[^1] In 1833, two of Martin's sons saw what looked like a sea serpent six miles east of the island, and it turned out to be a line of pilot whales.[^1] Lewis, in 1842, praised the keeper "for the extreme neatness and order of the whole establishment" and said seventeen winters' residence "in a house little better than a barn" entitled him to a comfortable dwelling in his old age.[^1]

Walter S. Rogers came as first assistant in 1872, in poor health at 101 pounds, and was head keeper from 1874 to 1881, when he left for West Chop on Martha's Vineyard; he returned in 1892 and stayed until 1911.[^1][^3] In February 1875, he reported, Salem and Beverly harbors froze solid out to the island, and the ice piled up to 15 feet when it broke.[^1] During a bad stretch of fog in October 1876 the bell was sounded for 72 hours straight, and by his own count in 1894 as many as 500 visitors came to the station in one day.[^1] When he retired in October 1911, a newspaper credited him with helping in 21 wrecks, saving six men's lives and furnishing over 400 meals to shipwrecked sailors, according to New England Lighthouses.[^1] Eugene Terpeny, assistant keeper from 1888 to 1897 in the archive's roster, received a Massachusetts Humane Society medal for helping save six men from the sinking schooner *Hero* in October 1894.[^1][^3]

Elliott Hadley, earlier the first keeper of Graves Light in Boston Harbor, kept the light from 1911 to 1918. His children were the last to go to school on the island.[^1] Arthur L. Payne was assistant from 1917, then head keeper from 1918 to 1943, the longest term at the station, and he was the last civilian head keeper.[^1][^3] Ernest A. Sampson was his assistant from 1929 to 1942 in the archive's roster.[^3]

The smaller tower's light was discontinued on 30 June 1926; New England Lighthouses adds that Payne found an 1847 penny hidden under the lens pedestal when it was removed.[^1][^11] LighthouseFriends says an addition to the taller tower housed the weights that turned its new lens.[^2] The Lighthouse Service later wrote that twin lights had been built to tell lighthouses apart, a job done better by revolving lenses.[^11] Heap's 1889 handbook on lighthouses, a general work rather than a record of this station, says the double lights at Baker's Island "are of use mainly as 'ranges.'"[^13]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the Light-House Board's fog-signal tests of 22 September 1893, the *Myrtle*, with observers aboard, ran east-northeast from the Baker Island lights and heard the fog bell, struck by the Stevens machine, at two and a fraction nautical miles (the fraction is unreadable in the scan text) in a light south-southwest wind on a bright, pleasant day.[^6] The Board's 1894 report records a hard-pine boat slip 122 feet (37 m) long, a brick oil house, a cistern and a drain built at the north part of the island.[^5] A wreck that obstructed the boat slip was broken up and removed in the 1897 report, and the 1901 report lists a new fog-bell striker and 1,100 feet of fence posts and wire.[^7][^8]

In Payne's years the reports are full of small rescues. At 6 a.m. on 29 March 1921 he sighted an open motor boat in distress about two miles northeast of the station, with a very strong northwest wind and cold, clear weather. He and his assistant, Elno C. Mott, found two men nearly exhausted from cold and hunger, gave them a hot breakfast and towed their boat to Salem.[^9][^10] On 17 October 1921 Payne and assistant Charles A. Lyman towed the gasoline launch *Brother Bill* of Boston, which had a broken propeller shaft, into Bakers and then to Marblehead.[^10] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1931 says that on 10 April Payne rescued two men left stranded on the Dry Breakers when their boat drifted away, and on 20 April he helped put out a grass fire at a nearby cottage, with Sampson assisting.[^12]

New England Lighthouses says that in the First World War a Naval Reserve unit watched for German submarines from the island, and that in the Second World War the Coast Guard built a lookout tower east of the lighthouse for the same purpose.[^1]

## After the keepers

Coast Guard crews followed Payne's retirement in 1943.[^1][^3] New England Lighthouses says the siren was replaced by an air horn in 1959.[^1] The light was automated in 1972, and the National Register nomination calls it unattended.[^1][^3][^4] When the Coast Guard tried to remove the fourth-order Fresnel lens, which floated on a bed of mercury, the crew were overcome by fumes and flown out by helicopter, LighthouseFriends reports; the lens is now at the Maine Lighthouse Museum in Rockland.[^2] The station's National Register reference is 76000289, from a thematic group nomination for the lighthouses of Massachusetts.[^4]

The Baker's Island Association was granted a license to use and preserve the two keeper's houses in 1988.[^1] In 2002 the station was offered under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act; a committee of the National Park Service chose the Essex National Heritage Commission, and the Baker's Island Lighthouse Preservation Society, made up of island residents, challenged the choice until a federal judge upheld it in October 2006.[^1][^2] The government transferred the deed to Essex Heritage on 27 August 2014.[^14]

## Today

Essex Heritage owns and operates the 10-acre light station; the rest of the island is private, including its wharf.[^14] The public can get there only on Essex Heritage's landing craft *Naumkeag*, which leaves from the Salem Ferry Dock.[^14][^16] Essex Heritage offers summer boat tours, volunteer days, camping and two- and four-night stays in the Assistant Keeper's House (for members, with a fee).[^15][^16] Essex Heritage says the island itself is private with no public access, and the tours are the way to see the station.[^14]
