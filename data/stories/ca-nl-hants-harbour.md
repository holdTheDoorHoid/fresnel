Hant's Harbour Light stands on Custard's Head, at the eastern side of a horseshoe-shaped harbour in Trinity Bay, Newfoundland and Labrador. A light has shown there since 1881, when a wooden tower was completed. The square tower standing now dates from 1957.[^1][^2]

## The place

Hant's Harbour lies on the western side of the Bay de Verde peninsula. The harbour is bounded by Grassy Island on the west and Custard's Head on the east.[^1] In 1871, eighty-one of the town's 104 households were listed as fishermen.[^1] Eight vessels carrying 294 men had sailed from the harbour in 1853. Two decades later, two vessels with sixty-four men took part in the seal hunt.[^1]

In 1880 inhabitants of Trinity Bay sent a petition to the House of Assembly "in relation to a Light-house at Hants Harbor."[^3] The Journal entry names only the subject of the petition.

## Building the light

In 1880 the House sent a message to the Legislative Council, asking it to concur in addresses on the erection of a light-house at Hants Harbor and one at Bay Roberts Point.[^4] The inspector's report for 1880 says that sites were surveyed and reported on for a proposed light at Bay Roberts Point and for a beacon light at Hant's Harbour.[^5]

The next year's report, for 1881, says that "A harbor light has been completed at Hants Harbor," in Trinity Bay.[^2] LHF describes the first tower as a wooden octagon with a one-storey store attached, built on Custard's Head. Its lamp was an Argand lamp in an eighth-order lens, which gave a fixed white light from a focal plane of 19.8 m (65 ft).[^1]

## The keepers

LHF names W. Soper as the first keeper, serving from 1881 until at least 1912. It also names Frank Soper and Leonard Soper, without dates.[^1] The inspector's report of 1 January 1886 does not name the keeper. It refers to him only as "the keeper."[^6] LHF identifies that keeper as W. Soper.[^1]

In the report dated 1 January 1886, the inspector recorded complaints that the light made "a very indifferent appearance during the earlier hours of the night, but improving as time passed."[^6] The keeper was contacted about it. The inspector judged his reply, "though defensive of himself," an admission of the correctness of the charge, and the keeper was warned to be more careful.[^6]

The report printed in 1890 found the station "in good order and well cared for." A leaky fountain had caused trouble with the lamp for a time, but after repair no further difficulty arose.[^7]

The report printed in 1894 says that a sixth-order dioptric light had been substituted for the eighth-order light first supplied, and that the change was much appreciated in the locality. LHF dates the change to 1893.[^8][^1]

## After the keepers

From 1914 the Light List described the tower as square, not octagonal, though its focal height stayed at 19.8 m (65 ft).[^1] In 1931 the fuel changed from kerosene to acetylene gas, and the light changed from fixed white to flashing white.[^1] The present square, pyramidal tower was built in 1957, according to the Canadian Coast Guard.[^1] In 1968 commercial electricity was strung to the lighthouse, and B. Ash was given a contract to service the light.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard lists the light as an isophase white light with a six-second period, shown from a red and white structure. Its focal height is 19.8 m (65 ft) and its range is 13 nautical miles. The list gives the structure as 8.0 m (26 ft) high. LHF says the present tower stands nine metres (30 ft) tall.[^9][^1]

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light.[^1] LHF reports that the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] It describes a picnic site near the light, created by the Lower Trinity South Development Association, and says there does not appear to be any formal parking. The short trail goes up and over the headland at the end of the road.[^1]
