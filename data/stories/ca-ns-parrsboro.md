Parrsboro lighthouse stands at the end of a breakwater on the west side of the entrance to Parrsboro Harbour, in Cumberland County, Nova Scotia.[^1][^2][^7] Its green light flashes every six seconds from a square concrete tower built in 1980.[^1][^2][^8] The wooden lighthouse that stood there from 1852 is said by Lighthouse Friends to have been lost to erosion after a hurricane in 1945. The Preservation Society's list has a skeletal tower from 1941.[^1][^2]

## The place

Acadians settled west of the mouth of Parrsboro Harbour in 1670. After the expulsion of 1755, New England planters moved to the banks of the Parrsboro River, where mills and a harbour were built, and the settlement was renamed Parrsboro in 1784 to honour Governor John Parr.[^1]

In the mid-nineteenth century the town was the hub of a string of shipbuilding communities known as the "Parrsboro Shore". In 1850 it was the port of registry for 115 locally built schooners and square riggers.[^1] Lighthouse Friends places the first tower on a sand spit at the western side of the harbour entrance, where its exposed position meant repeated storm damage.[^1] The Preservation Society's list places the first light on Partridge Island, on the west side of the harbour mouth.[^2] The two accounts disagree on the site, and neither source explains the difference.

## Building the light

The first lighthouse was an octagonal wooden tower with an attached dwelling, built in 1852. A temporary light was shown from it that year until the lantern room was placed on top the following spring.[^1][^2]

The keeper, James R. Howard, was paid sixty pounds a year with a ten-pound fuel allowance. He did not live at the lighthouse. He paid his teenage younger brother to look after the light and a sister to keep house.[^1] A separate building, about 12 by 5 m (forty by sixteen feet), held the oil and the stable.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends dates the lens change to 1909.[^1] The Department's report for the fiscal year 1909 to 1910 says "a 4th order dioptric illuminating apparatus" replaced the catoptric apparatus at Parrsboro.[^4] The Preservation Society's list gives 1922 for a fourth-order lens, which is later than the Department's report.[^2][^4]

## The keepers

Howard was replaced in 1857 by Captain John Pettis. Shortly after Pettis took charge, the inspector William Condon found the light house, lamps and lantern "in a filthy and disgraceful state". Condon had new lamps put in and the repairs made, and he warned Pettis to give his duties careful attention.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends lists the later head keepers as William Armstrong (at least 1867 to 1874), John H. Newcomb (1874 to 1888) and William Pettis (1888 to 1911).[^1] The Department's keepers' table for 1911 has "Parrsboro' - William Pettis" on the list, which places him at the station that year.[^6] The Preservation Society's list gives Howard's term as ending in 1855, not 1857.[^2]

W. J. Simpson kept the light from 1911 to 1917, and Captain J. McNamara from 1917 to 1921.[^1] Russell C. Spencer was head keeper from 1921 to 1960, and Ronald A. Wood from 1963 until the station was de-staffed in 1987.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 4 October 1869 a gale destroyed a building at the station and damaged the breastwork built to protect it. Lighthouse Friends says the remains of the building were carried off by the sea.[^1] A breakwater of 218 m (715 ft), paid for from a $2,000 appropriation, was built in 1873 to shield the light.[^1] On 23 and 24 November 1874 a violent storm washed away roughly 30 m (100 ft) of the new breakwater.[^1]

In 1945 a hurricane hit the area. Lighthouse Friends says it made the original tower lean, and that Keeper Russell Spencer "raced up the tower to extinguish the kerosene light" as it leaned.[^1] A neighbour on the mainland offered to fetch him by boat, but he chose to remain.[^1] The Nova Scotia Vacation guide says the hurricane made the tower list dangerously, and that Spencer stayed behind to put out the light before the tower was removed.[^7] The leaning tower was taken down, and for a few years a light was shown from a mast.[^1] The Preservation Society's list dates the skeletal tower that followed from 1941 to 1960, which conflicts with the 1945 date.[^2]

## After the keepers

A fog bell replaced the hand horn in 1905.[^3] The Department's report for the year ending 30 November 1905 says the bell is supported by a framework on the south side of the tower and "will give one stroke every 6 seconds".[^3] Lighthouse Friends says the fog signal was upgraded that year.[^1]

Oil engines and a diaphone foghorn came next. The Department's report for the fiscal year 1919 to 1920 says "a new wooden building was erected at Parrsboro, and a small diaphone plant installed".[^5] Lighthouse Friends dates the oil engines and diaphone to 1919, while the Preservation Society's list gives 1917 for the diaphone.[^1][^2]

The present tower, a square concrete one rising from the corner of a one-storey building, was built in 1980. The light was automated by 1987.[^1][^2]

The Parrsboro Lighthouse Society, set up in 2014, works to preserve the lighthouse.[^1] The Coast Guard took the lantern room down in late December 2018 for refurbishment. A new roof went on in the spring of 2019, and a helicopter set the restored lantern room back in place in June.[^1][^7]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light.[^1] Its List of Lights gives a green light with a flash of two seconds and an eclipse of four, a focal height of 8.4 m and a range of 7 nautical miles. The fog horn gives a three-second blast followed by 27 seconds of silence.[^8]

Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower closed. Visitors can reach the breakwater from the end of Lighthouse Road, but may not park there.[^1] The Preservation Society lists the site's access as "By Car".[^2] The Parrsboro Lighthouse Society maintains the lighthouse.[^7]
