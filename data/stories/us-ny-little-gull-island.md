Little Gull Island Light stands on a small island at the eastern end of Long Island's North Fork, in Suffolk County, New York.[^1][^2] The granite tower on the island was finished in 1869 and is 81 feet (24.7 m) tall. It replaced the original freestone tower, which the sources date to 1805 or 1806, and it is still an active aid to navigation.[^1][^2][^14]

## The place

LighthouseFriends puts the island about seven miles northeast of Orient Point. Newsday, in 2012, put it about eight miles east.[^1][^14] The island sits in the Race, the tidal passage where Long Island Sound opens to the sea. Great Gull Island lies just west of it, and four miles of open water separate it from Fishers Island to the east.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes currents in the Race that sometimes exceed five knots, and rips when a strong wind meets an ebbing tide.[^1] Putnam's 1917 history says the Race Rock and Little Gull Island lights "mark the entrance proper at the Race."[^3] When surveyors visited in 1803 the island had about an acre of land above high-tide mark. Almost all building material had to come by ship.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress provided $8,000 in 1802 for lighthouses and buoys on the Sound, and the government bought the island from Benjamin Jerome for $800 in 1803. New London builder Abisha Woodward took the tower contract.[^1] The freestone tower rose 53 feet (16 m) above sea level, with a wooden spiral stair to the lantern. A wooden dwelling of one and a half stories stood apart from it.[^1] The sources disagree on the first year of the light. LighthouseFriends says the station went into operation in 1805, and the archive gives 1806 as the year established.[^1][^2]

In 1816 Congress appropriated $30,000 for works to preserve the island, and a circular stone wall about 100 feet (30 m) across was built around the station in 1817.[^1] The first light burned whale oil in a multi-lamp apparatus. A third-order Fresnel lens went into the old tower in 1858, two years after a fog bell was set up.[^1]

The granite tower that stands now was begun in 1867. It was finished in 1869 using some stone from the old tower, and a second-order Fresnel lens was first exhibited there on 15 December 1869.[^1][^2] The keeper's house beside it was a three-story granite and wood building in the Second Empire style, with a mansard roof.[^1][^2] A steam fog signal was set up at the same time, and a duplicate followed in 1872.[^1] The light's fuel changed from whale oil to lard oil, then kerosene in 1883, incandescent oil vapor in 1907, and electricity in 1937.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive's roster for the station has 222 lines: 39 head keepers, 60 first assistants, 75 second assistants, and 48 entries for Coast Guard and other staff.[^2] In the early years a keeper's family shared a small house with the assistant's family, and the station was sometimes cut off for up to two months at a time. LighthouseFriends describes that as typical of lighthouse families of the period.[^1]

Israel Rogers was the first keeper, from 1805 to 1809.[^2] John Rogers II, whom LighthouseFriends describes as a grandson of the first keeper, kept the light from 1816 for about ten years. LighthouseFriends says he was dismissed for repeatedly letting the light go out, and attributes that neglect to boredom and alcoholism.[^1][^2]

Henry P. Field was head keeper from 1875 to 1901.[^2] Under William J. Murray, head keeper from 1909 to 1918, the station's keepers went to the aid of others on the water. In April 1913 Murray and two assistants brought ashore the captain and crew of a schooner that had foundered on Little Gull Island Reef.[^10] On 8 February 1918 Murray and his assistants rescued a man adrift on an ice cake near the station.[^11]

Eugene H. Merry succeeded Murray as head keeper in 1918 and kept the light until 1922.[^2] On 15 December 1919 he and assistant Charles H. Douglas rescued six people from two barges that had broken adrift and drifted onto the north side of the island.[^12] Arthur M. Bettencourt is listed as head keeper in 1944, the year of the fire described below.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A small British force landed on the island on 28 July 1813 and removed the lamps and reflectors, leaving the station out of service for the war. LighthouseFriends gives this account.[^1]

LighthouseFriends also describes the great hurricane of 23 September 1815. The lantern was heavily damaged, the windows were broken, and everyone at the station took refuge inside the tower.[^1]

On a calm, foggy night in May 1881 the propeller steamer *Galatea* grounded on Little Gull Island, about an eighth of a mile from and behind the fog signal. After it was alleged that the signal had not been sounding, the Light-House Board immediately ordered an investigation. The assistant inspector found that it had been, but that it was heard faintly, if at all, from the ship.[^9] An 1893 Board report gives the grounding as 1880, a year earlier than Johnson's date. The two accounts are not reconciled here.[^5][^9]

The annual reports recorded the fog signal's hours in several years. The steam siren ran about 450 hours in the Board's 1891–92 report, about 617 hours and 35 tons of coal in the fiscal year ended 30 June 1895, and about 565 hours and 37 tons of coal in the fiscal year 1896–97.[^4][^6][^7] In January 1900 an automatic siren, powered by oil engines and compressed air, replaced the steam siren. The steam siren had run about 210 hours that year before the change. The automatic siren, in use from then on, ran about 372 hours in the report year and used about 785 gallons of oil.[^8]

LighthouseFriends reports that on a day in July 1920 Keeper Douglass's rowboat sank about 100 yards from the light, drowning his wife. The three others in the boat reached the lighthouse.[^1] This account rests on LighthouseFriends alone.

The LighthouseFriends history also describes the 1938 hurricane. It blew outbuildings into the Sound, and waves reached the second floor of the keeper's house.[^1]

## After the keepers

A radiobeacon was installed at the island in 1928. The Bulletin reported that it would be placed in commission on 15 May 1928.[^13]

On 20 April 1944 a fire destroyed much of the granite keeper's house and spread into the tower.[^1][^14] The lens survived, though the tower had to be refurbished.[^1][^14] The house was replaced by a one-storey building, which the Coast Guard removed in 2002. A concrete slab marks where the old house stood.[^14]

The station was automated in 1978. The Coast Guard has kept the beacon and foghorn in service.[^14] In 1995 the Fresnel lens was taken from the tower and put on display at the East End Seaport Maritime Museum.[^1]

The General Services Administration offered the island for sale. It was first offered at no cost to eligible public and non-profit bodies, and when none came forward it went to auction in 2012 with a starting bid of $50,000. LighthouseFriends counts eight bidders, while Newsday names six others besides the winner. The high bid of $381,000 was accepted in October 2012.[^1][^14]

## Today

The granite tower stands. The Coast Guard has kept the beacon and foghorn going, and in 2012 Newsday reported that it would continue to do so.[^14] The island was sold at the federal auction to a private buyer, and LighthouseFriends reports that the buyer wants the tower restored and made accessible to the public. The archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^1][^2] The island lies in the Race, and LighthouseFriends says it is best seen by boat. It should not be landed on. The lens is on display at the East End Seaport Maritime Museum.[^1]
