The Grand Marais Harbor of Refuge Outer Light is the front range light on the stone pier that shelters the harbor at Grand Marais, on Michigan's Lake Superior shore. The Chamber of Commerce describes the front light as "a room perched on a steel skeleton."[^1] The light was first shown on 10 December 1895. A rear light completes the range, and the sources give its first showing as 1898 or 1899.[^2][^3][^4]

## The place

Entering the harbor at Grand Marais was difficult because of a bar with just nine feet (2.7 m) of water over it. A federal project adopted on 5 August 1881 built piers and a dredged channel to create a harbor of refuge.[^3][^2] In 1892 the Lighthouse Board asked for a light and bell there, saying that the federal work had "advanced to a point where it is desirable to light it." It estimated the cost of a light and bell at no more than $15,000.[^5] Congress authorized that money in 1893 but made no appropriation, and the act of 2 March 1895 provided the $15,000.[^6][^7]

## Building the light

The front light's tower was not new. The Board took the bell tower from the Point Iroquois station and sent it to Grand Marais.[^8] LighthouseFriends says it was bolted to the western pier and capped with a decagonal iron lantern room, and that the fixed white light and fog bell went into operation on 10 December 1895 under Keeper Samuel F. Rogers.[^3] The National Park Service brochure gives the same first-light date and says no keeper's dwelling was built with the station.[^2] The Board's list of lights established in its 1896–1897 report gives the new light as sixth-order, first shown 10 December 1895.[^13]

Congress allowed a second light in 1897, and LighthouseFriends says a rear range tower of iron, nearly 62 feet (19 m) tall with a fifth-order Fresnel lens, was erected on the inner end of the west pier.[^3] The Board's annual report printed for 1899 says the rear beacon "was completed and the light was exhibited on July 18, 1899."[^4] LighthouseFriends dates the first showing to 18 July 1898.[^3] The two sources disagree by a year, and this story does not settle it.

The keeper's house came last. LighthouseFriends says Rogers had been forced to build a small shack near the pier out of driftwood, and that Congress provided $6,500 for a new dwelling on 26 February 1907, too late for him.[^3] The National Park Service brochure says he built a temporary shanty on Corps of Engineers property at the south end of the pier.[^2] Work on the keeper's dwelling began on 10 June 1908.[^3] LighthouseFriends also says the characteristic of both range lights was changed from fixed white to fixed red in 1905, when the front tower was moved near the end of the newly extended pier.[^3]

## The keepers

The sources do not agree on when Samuel F. Rogers began. The National Park Service brochure says the first harbor of refuge keeper was assigned in 1895, and LighthouseFriends has him lighting the tower that December.[^2][^3] The USLHS roster begins him in 1898 and ends him in 1904.[^12] LighthouseFriends says his ill health forced him to resign in 1904, and that he had spent most of his life at sea or at offshore lights.[^3]

George A. Barkley, keeper from 1904 to 1916, moved his family into the new dwelling by the end of September 1908, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] Otto M. Bufe, keeper from 1916 to 1922, was the keeper during the 1919 rescue described below; the Bulletin spells his name "Buffe."[^10][^12] Herbert P. Crittenden (1922 to 1927) and Roger W. Campbell (1927 to 1935) followed.[^12] LighthouseFriends says Campbell had twelve children with his wife, Viola, who died in the late 1920s.[^3] Thomas E. Radcliff's roster stint runs from 1935 to 1947, although the LighthouseFriends roster stops him at "at least 1941."[^12][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A storm on 6 January 1906 carried away roughly 250 feet (76 m) of the new walkway, and LighthouseFriends says it was replaced the next May.[^3]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1913 lists the Grand Marais Harbor of Refuge light station among the stations damaged, with the damage for the group put at about $4,000.[^9]

On 14 November 1919 the steamer *H. E. Runnels* went ashore near the west pier in a gale.[^10][^3] The Bulletin says keeper Otto Buffe "rendered assistance to the survivors of the steamer Runnels," and that a Coast Guard surfman and volunteers also assisted in the rescue work.[^10] The National Park Service brochure gives the wind as a northwest gale and the temperature as 18 degrees, and says no lives were lost.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the ship missed the harbor entrance in blinding snow, lost her steering gear and drove ashore, and that ice on the line made a breeches buoy impossible, so the surfboat took the crew off in four trips.[^3]

The sources disagree on the awards. The National Park Service brochure names gold congressional medals for the head surfman, seven of his crew, one Coast Guardsman and four Grand Marais civilians. LighthouseFriends counts nine Coast Guardsmen and four local men. LighthouseFriends says Bufe did not receive a medal, but the Lighthouse Service acknowledged his effort.[^2][^3]

## After the keepers

The front light's fog signal changed from a bell to an electric siren in 1922, according to the Bulletin, and LighthouseFriends says it became an air diaphone in June 1923.[^11][^3] LighthouseFriends also says the front light changed to a red flash every two seconds that year.[^3]

## Today

A National Park Service brochure says the Coast Guard "operates and maintains the fog signal and rear range light on the pier."[^2] The Chamber of Commerce describes the front light as a room perched on a steel skeleton on the pier, and places the rear light on shore behind it, with an octagonal lantern.[^1] The sources disagree about where the rear light stands. LighthouseFriends' visitor notes say the grounds are open, the dwelling is open in season, and the towers are closed.[^3] Do not climb the closed towers.

The Grand Marais Historical Society runs a maritime museum at Coast Guard Point. The Chamber says the museum is in the keeper's house across the road.[^1] The National Park Service brochure says the building is owned by the National Park Service and "once served as the keepers quarters" for the light.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the Historical Society received ownership of the 1908 keeper's dwelling in 1984 and made it a museum, yet its visitor notes give the dwelling's owner as the Park Service.[^3] The sources agree that the keeper's house holds the museum, but they do not agree on who owns it.

The Chamber describes the stone pier as a fishing spot, reached by a parking area by the low dunes.[^1] Check current conditions before going out on the pier.
