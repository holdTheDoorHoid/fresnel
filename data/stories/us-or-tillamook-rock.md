Tillamook Rock Light stands on a small basalt islet about 1.2 miles (1.9 km) off Tillamook Head in Clatsop County, Oregon, some 20 miles (32 km) south of the mouth of the Columbia River.[^1] The National Register nomination calls it Oregon's only offshore light; it was nicknamed "Terrible Tilly."[^1][^2] It was lit in January 1881 and went dark in September 1957.[^1] The federal government offered it for sale in 1959, and it has been privately owned since.[^1][^2]

## The place

The rock is less than an acre of basalt and rises nearly 100 feet (30 m) from the sea, with the heaviest surf running up its sloping eastern face.[^2] A Lighthouse Service account of 1913 described it as a precipitous rock about 90 feet high, open "to the sweep of the Pacific Ocean," and the light as "one of the most exposed in the world."[^3] Sources do not agree on how far it lies from the Columbia River entrance: 17 miles in the 1913 account, 18 in the Light-House Board's report for 1895, and 20 in the National Register nomination.[^3][^4][^1] The Oregon Encyclopedia notes that nineteenth-century reports describe hundreds of sea lions and countless seabirds on it.[^2]

The light was meant for the coast outside the Columbia River Bar. In 1878 Congress appropriated $50,000 for a first-class lighthouse there. It was first slated for Tillamook Head itself, but officials judged that the south-facing headland would not give mariners heading for the Bar a good view of it, and the plan moved to the rock.[^2]

## Building the light

The survey was deadly work. The nomination records that in June 1879 the district's construction superintendent, John R. Wheeler, made the first successful landing on the rock, but could not get his instruments ashore and had to survey with only a tape measure.[^1] John R. Trewaves (Trewavas in the Oregon Encyclopedia), a master mason who was to survey the rock before taking charge of construction, was killed in the attempt.[^1][^2] A Lighthouse Service article of 1934 puts it simply: in 1879 "an engineer lost his life in landing on the rOCk."[^5]

Work began in October 1879 under Charles A. Ballantyne (Ballentyne in the Oregon Encyclopedia), who rigged a heavy rope from the top of the rock to a ship's mast and ran a traveller pulley along it to carry men and supplies.[^1] According to the Oregon Encyclopedia he hired eight quarrymen from outside the district, who first reached the rock on 12 October 1879 and then built wooden shelters on its wet slope; one gale swept away the supply house and left the workers stranded for sixteen days.[^2] The 1934 article says a storm in the first months confined the crew to "a s~all shack for 10 days."[^5] The top thirty feet of the rock were blasted away to make a level site.[^1] The cornerstone was laid in June 1880, on the 24th by the nomination and on the 22nd by the Oregon Encyclopedia and the Oregon Historical Society's finding aid.[^1][^2][^6] The walls are 14-inch-thick basalt blocks lined with brick; the nomination says the stone came from Mount Tabor in Portland.[^1] The designer-engineer was Major G. L. Gillespie of the Army Corps of Engineers.[^1]

The nomination says the British bark *Lupatia* ran aground on Tillamook Head on the foggy night of 3 January 1881, with the loss of her whole crew, while the builders were still at work.[^1] Eighteen days later, on 21 January 1881, the first-order Fresnel light was exhibited for the first time.[^1] The nomination gives the cost as $123,493; the Oregon Encyclopedia says Congress had to add $50,000 in 1880 and $25,000 in 1881 to the first $50,000.[^1][^2]

The inspection report written in July 1881 describes what the builders had made. Landing was by a derrick with a long boom and a suspended cage, worked by hand or steam. The first-order apparatus was lit by a Funk's hydraulic float lamp, and the fog signal was a steam siren giving a five-second blast every 90 seconds. All the station's water was rain caught from the roof, held in iron tanks and a brick cistern cut in the rock. The staff was one keeper and three assistants.[^7] Heights given for the light differ from source to source: 136 feet in 1881, 132 in 1913, 133 in 1934, and "nearly 145 feet" above mean sea level in the nomination.[^7][^3][^5][^1]

Storms soon showed the roof was too weak. The nomination says a new roof of steel beams and two feet of concrete was added in 1898 and the attic over the quarters made into a second storey, with the windows facing south and west cemented up and replaced by portholes.[^1] The same document elsewhere says 1889, and notes a blueprint for a roof alteration dated 1889 found in the lighthouse in 1980.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for 1895 said only that plans for "a new and much stronger roof" were being prepared, which fits 1898 better.[^4]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the keeper registers, lists twenty terms as principal keeper between 1881 and 1957, and many more assistants.[^8] The first, Albert Roeder, stayed only about four months, according to the Oregon Encyclopedia.[^2][^8] Some men stayed for decades. William Dahlgren appears in the register as an assistant from 1901 and as head keeper from 1910 to 1919; Robert Gerlof as an assistant from 1903 and head keeper from 1919 to 1928.[^8] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's notice of Gerlof's death on 7 August 1930 said he "had lived and served for 25 years" on the rock, and that before he came he had been aboard Columbia River lightship No. 50 when it broke its moorings in the November 1899 storm and was stranded on Cape Disappointment.[^9]

Duty on the rock meant isolation, the Oregon Encyclopedia says.[^2] The shore telegraph line was still unfinished in 1895, when the Board reported that no attempt had yet been made to lay the cable;[^4] the tender *Manzanita* laid it in the year ended June 1896.[^10] The siren worked hard: 134 hours in the Board's 1899 report, 181 hours in 1900, and once, in July 1898, it was shut down for a month "owing to scarcity of water."[^11][^12][^13] A Lighthouse Service review in 1922 said that at Tillamook Rock there had been stretches of seven weeks when the tender could not reach the rock, and that "Landing is made on this rock only by means of a crate swung ou t over the boat from a long boom."[^14]

The keepers also rescued others. The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for 1922 credits Howard L. Hansen, first assistant, and Walter T. Lawrence, second assistant, with picking up a man drifting in a helpless condition from exposure, saving his launch and giving him food and lodging at the station.[^15] In 1939 the Lighthouse Service was folded into the Coast Guard; afterwards Coast Guard personnel manned the rock, but the principal keepers stayed civilians.[^1] Oswald Allik, an assistant from the late 1930s, was head keeper from 1952 to the end by the register; the nomination calls him a head keeper of twenty years, which does not fit those dates.[^8][^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

**December 1894.** A severe storm on 9 December 1894 broke over the whole station, destroyed 13 panes of lantern glass and damaged the lens and revolving gear. The seas broke in the roof of the dwelling and flooded the premises, and stones torn from the rock crushed the roof of the dwelling and fog-signal building in many places. A landing for temporary repairs was possible only on 18 December.[^4]

**October 1912.** On the night of 18 October 1912, "one of the heaviest storms ever known on the Oregon coast" reached the rock. Keeper William Dahlgren wrote on 20 October: "we lost a portion of the wes.t end of the Rock, water and rocks coming over with so much noise we could not tell." At 12.35 a.m. on the 19th a sea broke a pane of the lantern, put the light out and flooded the watch-room; by 12.50 it was burning again behind a storm pane. The siren was found part full of rocks. Everyone, Dahlgren wrote, "worked hard and faithfully, regardless of water and glass," and was "drenched to the skin." The tender *Manzanita* could not reach the rock until 9 December.[^3] Dahlgren, F. C. Hammond, Charles Miller and Olaf Dahlin were commended for restoring the light so promptly.[^16]

**October 1934.** A storm the Bureau of Lighthouses called "of unusual severity" struck the Oregon coast on 21 October 1934. At the rock, it reported, "Repeatedly the entire station was completely submerged." A section of the rock itself was torn away and hurled over the station; 16 lantern panels were shattered, and the keepers struggled to fix storm shutters in the flooded watch-room. The shore cable broke at the start. No light showed that first night, and the keepers got a fixed light going from a lens lantern until the main light was repaired on 27 October. The storm raged four days; the men could not leave the tower and quarters until 25 October. The four men were William Hill, keeper, and assistants Henry Jenkins, Hugo Hanson and Werner Storm. Jenkins, a qualified radio operator, built a short-wave set from scrap and, near midnight on 23 October, reached the superintendent through amateur operators ashore with the first report. The derrick, the only way to move people and supplies, was carried away.[^5]

Repairs dragged on through the winter. In late November another storm kept the rock under heavy seas, and three men, the foreman of the repair party and two keepers, fell ill from exposure and influenza; on 1 December the tender *Rose* took them off by breeches buoy, a rescue the Secretary of Commerce called notable. A new derrick replaced the lost one, and the emergency repairs were completed at the end of January 1935.[^17] The storm guard of steel mesh around the lantern dates from 1935, the nomination says.[^1] Much of the surviving correspondence at the Oregon Historical Society concerns this storm, chiefly between Hill and the district superintendent.[^6]

## After the keepers

The nomination says the last log entry, dated 1 September 1957, was commissioned by head keeper Oswald Allik to Jim Gibbs, a former Coast Guard attendant at the station. It reads: "Farewell, Tillamook Rock Light Station. An era has ended with this final entry, and not without sentiment I return thee to the elements."[^1] The light was replaced by a whistle buoy and the station decommissioned; the Oregon Encyclopedia adds that radar and modern communications had made it obsolete and that it had become the costliest lighthouse in the country to run.[^1][^2]

The General Services Administration offered it for sealed-bid sale in 1959 after the State of Oregon declined it. A Las Vegas group paid $5,600 and did nothing with it (the nomination speaks of seventeen years of ownership before the 1973 sale, which does not fit a 1959 purchase); it was sold in 1973 for $11,000 to George Hupman, who failed in his plan for a summer retreat, and in 1978 to Max M. Shillock of Portland for $27,000.[^1] In 1980 it was bought by Mimi Morissette (Morrissette in the Oregon Encyclopedia) and turned into the Eternity at Sea Columbarium, a place for urns. The interior was stripped and sealed except for one door.[^1][^2] The columbarium lost its licence in 1999, the Oregon Encyclopedia says.[^2]

## Today

The rock is privately owned and part of the Oregon Islands National Wildlife Refuge, and the lighthouse is on the National Register of Historic Places.[^2] As private property it is not open to visitors. The nomination, written in 1980, said the only access was by helicopter and that the lamp remained in the lantern, though the deed forbids lighting it.[^1] It can be seen from public beaches and from Ecola State Park on Tillamook Head.[^1] The rock is private and protected as a wildlife refuge, so the shore is the place to see it from. Current ownership and access have not been rechecked since the 1980 nomination and the Oregon Encyclopedia article.[^1][^2]
