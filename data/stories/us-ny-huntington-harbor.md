Huntington Harbor Light stands on a reef at the mouth of Lloyd Harbor, on the north shore of Long Island, where it marks the entrance to both Lloyd Harbor and Huntington Bay.[^1][^3][^7] Its concrete tower dates from 1912. The light it replaced, an 1857 station with a brick tower on the tip of Lloyd's Neck, was destroyed by fire in 1947, when the fire took its wooden dwelling.[^2][^3]

## The place

Huntington Bay was a popular anchorage on Long Island Sound, but when the wind blew onshore, vessels were pushed into Lloyd Harbor, which opens off the bay.[^3] The entrance was hard to use. Ships had to steer clear of a sand spit that jutted into the channel on one side and of rocks on the other, and getting in was close to impossible at night or in poor visibility.[^3] In 1838 Lieutenant George M. Bache reported more than seventy vessels lying in Lloyd Harbor and advised a light there.[^3] In 1853 Captain Hiram Paulding of the Navy pressed the same case with the Treasury, arguing that Lloyd Harbor was the only refuge for many miles in stormy weather.[^3] Congress approved $4,000 in August 1854. In July 1855 the Denton family sold the government about two and a half acres of the sandy spit, with a right of way, for $250.[^3]

## Building the light

The first light was finished in 1857 as a two-story frame dwelling set on a brick foundation, with a square brick tower joined to one corner.[^2][^3] The original optic was a fifth-order Henry-Lepaute Fresnel, giving a fixed white light from a focal plane forty-eight feet (14.6 m) up.[^3] The light was first lit on 15 November 1857.[^3]

The work after 1857 was mostly repair against the water.[^3][^4][^5] A gale on 22 November 1870 damaged the lighthouse. The Light-House Board's 1871 report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says repairs were made and a granite wall was raised to shield the station.[^3] Ice damaged the sea wall in the winter of 1872, and in 1874 Congress gave $2,000 for heavy stone set around the station.[^3] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1892, records a contract "for the protection of the foundations of the dwelling and the light-house tower," which took down part of the east breakwater and built a new one.[^4] In its report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1893, the Board said the underpinning of the dwelling's southwest corner had been rebuilt.[^5]

In the early 1900s residents and their politicians pressed for a new offshore light that could serve both harbors. Congress appropriated $40,000 for it. LighthouseFriends gives the date as 4 March 1907, and the Society's own history gives 7 March 1907.[^3][^7] The contract for building it was let in fiscal 1910.[^3]

The new light was reinforced concrete. Its foundation was a caisson about twenty-six by thirty feet (7.9 by 9.1 m) and sixteen feet (4.9 m) high, made ashore and towed out to the site. Water let into its interior compartments sank it into place; stone was laid around its base, and the compartments were drained and filled with concrete. A concrete pier on top carried a cellar that held the station's oil and coal stores and a cistern.[^3] The National Register record names the architects as "Mead,Chas. & Co."[^6]

The tower was finished in 1912, and on 16 June the light passed from the old tower to the new one.[^1][^3] The sources give different heights. The Society's 2026 announcement gives the tower as 42 feet (12.8 m), while the Digital Archive gives 34 feet (10.4 m) from base to lantern and 37 feet (11.3 m) of height above high water.[^1][^9] They also disagree about the lens. LighthouseFriends says the fourth-order lens moved from the old tower to the new one. The Digital Archive gives a fifth-order Fresnel as the original 1857 optic and a 300 mm optic in use today.[^1][^3] The sources do not reconcile these.

## The keepers

The old light at Lloyd Harbor has eight lines on the Digital Archive's roster for 1857 to 1912, seven men in all. John S. Wood is listed first, but LighthouseFriends says Abiathar Johnson replaced him on 11 August 1857.[^2][^3] Johnson kept the light twice, in 1857–1861 and again in 1861–1869.[^2] Alanson Pearsall served briefly in 1861, and Lester C. Darling from 1869 to 1874. George R. Johnson kept it from 1874 to 1884, and Neil Ward from 1884 to 1885.[^2]

Robert McGlone kept the old light from 1885 to 1912, the longest tenure on that roster.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he was acting keeper from September 1885 and permanent keeper from 9 July 1886. It calls his time at Lloyd Harbor twenty-four years, which does not match the roster's span.[^3][^2] After his wife died of pneumonia in March 1900, he hired Augusta Harrigan, a local woman, to help look after the five children and the house.[^3] Harrigan does not appear on the keepers' roster.[^2][^3]

From 1912 McGlone kept watch in the new tower, while Harrigan lived on in the old dwelling, which was kept as a shore station.[^3] Robert and Isabelle, the two children still at home, did much of the work at the new light for their father, who by then had arthritis.[^3] LighthouseFriends quotes Isabelle's recollection of the work: "Bleakness and drudgery is what I remember."[^3] Isabelle's work at the light ended when her father died in 1919. Harrigan kept living in the old house until 1934.[^3]

The Digital Archive lists the keepers of the new light from 1912 to 1948. After McGlone came John Grime (1919), then Marvin E. Burnham (1919–1926), James J. Gallen (1926–1928), Andrew Zuius, Jr. (1928–1929), Emil J. Brunner (1929–1930), Joseph H. Dubois (1930–1933), Robert L. Howard (1933–1935), Arthur Bouder (1935–1938) and Richard J. White (1938–1943). Bernard A. Bentley kept it for one year in 1943, and White returned for 1943–1948.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The storm damage the sources record here starts with the gale of 22 November 1870 and the ice of 1872 (see Building the light).[^3]

LighthouseFriends says Marvin Burnham took a man and a woman off a leaking schooner in Huntington Bay in 1920.[^3] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports that Keeper E. J. Brunner picked up two men who had gone overboard from a motorboat on 21 September 1929.[^10] LighthouseFriends also credits Brunner with rescuing the people in a speedboat that had lost its wheel near the light.[^3] On 27 July 1930 Keeper Joseph Dubois pulled a woman and a child from an overturned boat, brought them into the light and gave them dry clothes.[^11]

## After the keepers

The state received the old light in 1924, and the Town of Huntington took it over in 1928.[^3] On 12 November 1947 the dwelling burned: a fire that hunters had lit in its fireplace got out of control.[^3] The Digital Archive records the old station as destroyed by fire that day.[^2] That year a power cable was laid out to the new light, which LighthouseFriends says made automation possible the next year.[^3] The Digital Archive's last keeper on the new light, White, served to 1948.[^1]

The lens came out of the tower in 1967. The Coast Guard planned to discontinue the light, but local protests reversed the plan.[^3] By the mid-1980s, LighthouseFriends says, demolition was again a threat. A local group formed to save it in 1985, the year it received a thirty-year lease from the Coast Guard.[^3][^7] The National Register asset record gives the listing date as 31 May 1989, while LighthouseFriends gives 1988.[^6][^3] The group changed its name to the Huntington Lighthouse Preservation Society in 2003.[^3]

In 2011 the Interior Department said the lighthouse would pass to the Society.[^3] Foundation work began in 2016, and the lighthouse reopened to visitors in July 2018 after roughly $1.1 million had been spent.[^3] In 2025 the Society installed twelve custom-fabricated windows made to replicate the early 1900s design, and carried out roof and structural work. The Society says it has raised about $3.5 million since its founding in 1985.[^9]

## Today

The light is still an active aid to navigation.[^7][^1] The Society runs tours. They leave by boat from the Town of Huntington's Goldstar Battalion Beach dock and take about an hour and fifteen minutes. Visitors go inside the lighthouse, and those who want to can climb the tower.[^8] The 2026 tours were scheduled on Sundays through the summer, including 28 June and 27 September.[^9] The season is over, and the Society's tours page says to watch for 2027 tour dates.[^8][^9]

The lighthouse is reached by boat, and the tower is climbed on the Society's tours.[^8] The Society says the tours are not handicap accessible, and children under five may not go.[^8]
