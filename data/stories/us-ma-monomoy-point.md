Monomoy Point Light stood at the southern end of Monomoy Island, the sandy arm that runs south from Chatham at the elbow of Cape Cod.[^1] Work on a light began there in 1823, and a cast-iron tower replaced the first tower in 1849.[^2][^3] The light was discontinued in February 1923, after a new channel through the shoals reduced its usefulness.[^3][^4] Today the tower stands inside the Monomoy National Wildlife Refuge and can be reached only by boat.[^1]

## The place

Monomoy Island is a coastal barrier beach that extends about ten miles (16 km) south from the elbow of Cape Cod.[^1] Erosion and storms have split what was once a peninsula into North and South Monomoy, and the light stands on South Monomoy.[^3] The thematic group nomination says that the island's shifting topography formed the shoals that led to the building of the light, and that it may also have led to the light's decommissioning in 1923.[^5]

Nantucket Sound lies to the west of the point and the Atlantic to the east. LighthouseFriends says that dangerous rips, where the two waters come together over shallow shoals and bars, have led to a number of shipwrecks.[^3] Fishermen at Whitewash Village worked the island in the early nineteenth century, catching cod, mackerel and lobsters.[^3] LighthouseFriends says some of the village's residents were wreckers, who stripped the ships the shoals wrecked, and that a hurricane and the sudden departure of fish helped end the village by the 1860s.[^3]

The light was first meant to guide ships into Old Stage Harbor. After sand filled that harbor, the Lighthouse Board wanted the light upgraded to serve the coast.[^3] A Life-Saving Service station stood nearby, and a telephone line built in 1898 linked the light with it.[^3][^6]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says the U.S. government approved $3,000 for the light on 3 March 1823.[^3] The National Register nomination says that James B. Gill began construction that year, the same year the federal government took jurisdiction of the four-acre site.[^2]

The sources disagree about when the light first shone. LighthouseFriends gives 1 November 1823, with David Bearse as the first keeper.[^3] The nomination says the first flame, rated at 23,000 candlepower, appeared in 1828.[^2]

The first tower was wooden, with an iron lantern room on top of a brick keeper's dwelling. Its lantern held eight lamps with thirteen-inch reflectors.[^3]

The station's early troubles were recorded in detail. In 1842 the keeper, Solomon Doane, reported to I.W.P. Lewis that "the foundations of the light-house have settled, and the walls are cracked in several places."[^3] He also wrote that the beach, once sixty yards (55 m) from the light, was by then 261 yards (239 m) away.[^3] As the nomination quotes Edward Rowe Snow, Lewis recorded the station as being in "ruined condition."[^2]

The cast-iron tower dates from 1849. The nomination says Cyrus Alger built a cast-iron lighthouse that year.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes the new tower as cylindrical and about forty feet (12 m) tall. It says a contract shows that Alger put the tower up, while Pelham Bonney built the two-story, wood-frame keeper's house.[^3]

An inspector in 1850 found the lighthouse not "stiff enough." Wooden braces were added to stiffen the tower, and six iron trusses went in in 1892 to reduce vibration.[^3]

The sources disagree about the lens and the rebuilding. The nomination quotes the 1857 *List of Lighthouses* as saying the light was refitted in 1849. It quotes Edward Rowe Snow as saying the tower was rebuilt in 1855 and again in the 1870s, and Malcolm F. Willoughby as putting a fourth-order Fresnel lens in place in 1855. It also quotes a National Park Service historian as describing Monomoy Light as dating to 1855.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse received a brick lining and a Henry-Lepaute fourth-order lens in 1857.[^3]

Between 1871 and 1874 the Board asked Congress for a second-order light with red flashes. Congress did not provide the money, and Monomoy stayed a fourth-order light.[^3]

On 26 May 1892 the light's characteristic changed from fixed white to fixed white with a fixed red sector.[^7]

## The keepers

The USLHS roster lists seventeen principal keepers from 1823 to 1923, and a custodian line for 1924.[^8]

LighthouseFriends says the records show one woman among Monomoy's keepers, and that the isolated post kept keepers for about five and a half years on average.[^3]

The roster has David Bearse as the first keeper, from 1823 to 1841.[^8] Solomon Doane took the post in 1841 at a salary of $400 a year.[^3] Through the 1840s and 1850s the roster alternates Doane and Asa Nye.[^8]

John B. Tuttle, a Harwich man who had been wounded in the Civil War, kept the light from 1865 to 1875 at $700 a year. He then passed it to his stepson, Asa L. Jones, who had also been wounded in the war.[^8][^3]

James P. Smith, a native of Copenhagen and a former assistant at Boston Light, took over in 1899 and kept the light until his death in 1910.[^8][^3] LighthouseFriends reports that in May 1900 Smith drew a pistol on a surfman from the nearby lifesaving station. A deputy sheriff arrested him, and he was fined $25 and allowed to return to the light.[^3] After his wife died in October 1900, his daughters helped with the work. Smith died of heart trouble in March 1910, coming down the tower stairs after putting out the light.[^3]

The roster lists Anna M. Smith as principal keeper for 1910. LighthouseFriends says that the eldest daughter, Annie, took charge of the station for a month after her father's death, and the sources do not say whether the two are the same person.[^8][^3] In a 1904 interview quoted by LighthouseFriends, Annie was asked whether it was lonely at the light. She answered: "Oh, no! We don't have time to be lonesome."[^3]

The archive lists Edward E. Brewer and Edward Eveleth Brewer for the same span, 1910 to 1911, under two spellings.[^8]

Charles H. Jennings of Provincetown kept the light from 1911 to 1916. LighthouseFriends says he earned $660 a year, and that his son Harold later wrote about the family's life there in *A Lighthouse Family*.[^8][^3] The family kept a cow, a horse and chickens. Crossing the mudflats to Chatham with the horse and buggy, the keeper sometimes had to get out and lead the horse around places where it could become mired.[^3] LighthouseFriends says that in 1914 the keeper lost his wedding ring in the mud on one crossing, and that he found it on the day before his transfer to Boston Light.[^3]

Douglas H. Shepherd was the last keeper, from 1917 to 1923, and the roster lists him as custodian in 1924.[^8] The Bulletin of 1923 calls him a former keeper who had reconstructed an automobile for sand and marsh roads. It reports that a trip over soft beach took 20 minutes by car, where a horse and cart needed hours on the same route. The wheels had treads 10 inches wide.[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In November 1883 the Monomoy Life-Saving Station received a message by signals from the light that a shipwrecked crew was there. The report dates this to 20 November, but a list of the year's wrecks in the same report gives 26 November for the schooner Effort at the Chatham station.[^6][^13] Keeper Tuttle of the station went to the light and found four men who had landed an hour earlier in their boat from the schooner Effort, of Bangor, Maine.[^6] The roster has Asa L. Jones as the light's keeper in those years, so Tuttle was the lifesaving keeper, not the light keeper.[^8]

The 1899 Light-House Board report describes the keeper's dwelling in some detail. The dwelling "is old," and all except the frame and sheathing boards "are in bad condition." The house, "standing on a narrow peninsula, is exposed to the cold winds blowing off the fields of ice." A useless hall separated the rooms, "so that they can not be kept warm."[^10]

The Board estimated that the house could be remodelled and renovated for "not exceeding $2,000," and recommended an appropriation of that amount.[^10] LighthouseFriends gives a different figure, saying $2,600 was requested to rebuild the keeper's house.[^3] In the year that followed, the Board reported, the interior of the lower story was entirely renewed and rearranged.[^11]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says that after the Cape Cod Canal opened in 1914, and the light at Chatham was made stronger, the need for Monomoy Point Light diminished.[^3]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1 March 1923 reports that a recently dredged channel, the new Pollock Rip channel around Cape Cod, was opened on 17 February. The opening made it possible to discontinue one light vessel and one lighthouse. The Bulletin does not name the lighthouse.[^4]

LighthouseFriends dates the discontinuance of the light and of the Pollock Rip light vessel to 16 February 1923, a saving of $26,269 a year.[^3] The Bulletin gives the opening of the channel as 17 February.[^4] The tower's fourth-order lens was removed, and the station was sold to George Dunbar of Chatham for $500.[^3]

Later the property passed to George Smith Bearse, a Chatham car dealer whose great-grandfather David Bearse had been the first keeper. LighthouseFriends says that Navy planes had been using the property for machine-gun target practice.[^3]

The Massachusetts Audubon Society restored the lighthouse and keeper's house in 1964. It sold the property to the federal government in 1977, and a federal grant in 1988 helped pay for a major overhaul.[^3]

In late October 2009 the Department of the Interior announced $1.5 million of American Recovery and Reinvestment Act money to shore up the tower and dwelling. The bid for the work was $2.1 million, more than the money available, so the work was scaled back.[^3] The tower was restored and received new glass panes in the lantern room, and the keeper's dwelling got a new roof, siding, windows and a well. Interior restoration of the keeper's house was completed in 2016.[^3]

The register nomination said that active protection of the property was "next to impossible at the present time."[^12]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and sits inside Monomoy National Wildlife Refuge, on one of two parcels excluded from the wilderness designation.[^1][^3] The island can be reached only by boat.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open, and the dwelling and tower are closed. Overnight stays in the keeper's quarters were discontinued around 2009. A ferry company offers naturalist-led walks on South Monomoy that include a visit to the lighthouse.[^3] Check current tours with the refuge and the ferry before going. The tower and dwelling are closed, and visitors should not try to enter them.
