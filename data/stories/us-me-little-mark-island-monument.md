Little Mark Island Monument is a 50-foot (15 m) stone pyramid on an uninhabited, treeless one-acre island in northern Casco Bay, off Harpswell, Maine.[^1] The federal government paid for it in 1827 as a daymark, and it was built with an interior room where shipwreck victims could find refuge.[^2][^3] A lamp was added to its top in 1927.[^4][^5] The Lighthouse Directory says the light was deactivated in 2019.[^6]

## The place

Little Mark Island lies in northern Casco Bay, at the mouth of Merriconeag Sound (also called Harpswell Sound) and the eastern end of Broad Sound.[^1][^2] The nomination describes the island as uninhabited, with no trees and a seasonal colony of double-crested cormorants. It rises to about 40 feet (12 m) above mean low water, and the monument stands on its highest ground.[^1]

The island was a marker for ships using the sounds. The 1847 edition of *Blunt's American Coast Pilot*, as the nomination quotes it, called the monument "a land-mark for vessels running into, or passing either Harpswell or Broad Sound."[^2] The nomination says it "appears that only three daybeacons of monumental height were constructed," all in Maine: at Cape Elizabeth, on Stage Island, and here.[^2] The Cape Elizabeth column was demolished in 1828, and the Stage Island daybeacon still stands.[^2] The nomination counts this monument as one of just two Maine structures with a shipwreck refuge room that remain intact.[^3]

## Building the light

Maine and Massachusetts both passed resolves in 1825 supporting a transfer of the island to the United States.[^2] The nomination says the reason for their action "is unclear," and suggests a link to Maine's separation from Massachusetts in 1820.[^2]

On 2 March 1827 Congress authorized a column on the island and appropriated $1,200 to pay for it.[^2] A deed dated 9 May 1827, signed by land agents for Maine and Massachusetts, transferred the island to the United States for twenty dollars.[^2] The design resembled that of the Cape Elizabeth monument, which Congress had funded at $1,800 in 1811.[^2]

The tower is square in plan and steeply pyramidal, 50 feet (15 m) tall, with sides 18 feet (5.5 m) wide at the base.[^1] The rocks "are fitted together tightly and bound with lime mortar," and the nomination says the stone "resembles the native rock of Little Mark Island and appears to have been quarried there."[^1] The Lighthouse Directory says the tower was built of finished granite blocks, so the two sources disagree about the stone.[^6] Inside, the room is about 12 feet (3.7 m) on each side, and its roof is open to the top like a chimney flue.[^1]

The monument was completed late in 1827 at a final cost of $1,065.54. The $134.46 left over went back to the Treasury's surplus fund.[^2] In February 1828 the Maine legislature passed an act to cede to the United States the jurisdiction of several tracts of land. The act describes Little Mark Island as "containing about one acre," and provided that Maine would share jurisdiction with the United States for civil and criminal processes.[^2]

## After the keepers

The monument never had a keeper, according to the Lighthouse Directory, and the 1932 light list describes the light as "(unattended)."[^6][^4]

In 1927 the Lighthouse Service decided to install a beacon on top.[^4] The work that year included a concrete slab on the tower and a 200-millimeter optic, which burned compressed acetylene gas.[^5][^4] The Lighthouse Directory says the date when the monument became a lighted aid is unknown, though the nomination dates the beacon to 1927.[^6][^4]

A 1939 reorganization abolished the Bureau of Lighthouses and assigned the Lighthouse Service to the Coast Guard.[^4] The nomination says a cabinet inside the tower indicates that the acetylene lamp was replaced in the 1940s with an electric lamp.[^4] A 1960 list described the light as 150 candlepower with eight miles of visibility.[^4] The 2015 Coast Guard list gives "Flashing white, 4 seconds," at a height of 74 feet (23 m).[^4]

A 1936 Lighthouse Service Bulletin describes the tower as "a square tower, about 60 feet high" (18 m), which does not match the 50 feet (15 m) in the nomination.[^7][^1] In the fiscal year ended June 30, 1900, the Lighthouse Board's annual report noted that "The beacon was whitewashed."[^8]

The nomination describes the lamp as "powered by batteries recharged using a solar array," maintained by the Coast Guard's Aids to Navigation Team at Station South Portland.[^4] In its 2016 nomination, the monument was "continuing to function as a Federal aid to navigation."[^3] The Lighthouse Directory says the light was deactivated in 2019, and a 2023 Harpswell newspaper said it was no longer operating.[^6][^9] Only the Directory gives the 2019 date.

## Today

The property is still federal. In 2023 the Harpswell Anchor reported that the federal government no longer had a use for the island and its historic tower, and was offering to give the island to an eligible municipality or nonprofit.[^9] The Town of Harpswell planned to apply jointly with The Presumpscot Foundation, which owns Halfway Rock Light Station.[^9] In August 2025 the foundation's founder wrote to the Harpswell Anchor that federal cutbacks had slowed the process, and that the effort was still underway.[^10]

Visitors need a boat. The nomination says the island is accessible by boat, and the 2023 newspaper says it is hard to reach.[^1][^9] The sources describe no public landing. While the island remains federal property, do not land on it without permission.
