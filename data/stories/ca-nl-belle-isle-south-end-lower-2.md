Belle Isle South End Lower Light is a small light on a cliff at the southern tip of Belle Isle, a barren island in the Strait of Belle Isle on the Newfoundland and Labrador coast. The lower light was first lit in 1878 or 1879, rebuilt in 1908 on a concrete base, and is still shown today.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Lighthouse Friends quotes Jacques Cartier, who met the island's bleak landscape in the spring of 1534, as writing "I believe this is the land God gave to Cain."[^1] The island sits at the eastern entrance to the Strait of Belle Isle, the northern gateway from the Atlantic to the Gulf of St. Lawrence. It is about 19 km (12 miles) long and about 3 km (2 miles) across at its widest.[^1]

With steamships using the strait more often, the Canadian Register says lights were needed to speed passage through it. Commercial vessels and local fishermen used the strait regularly.[^2] The light sits low on the south end of the island, on the ledge of a cliff about 61 m (200 ft) high.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1853 François Baby, who ran a tug line below Quebec, was contracted to build major lights at four points in the Gulf of St. Lawrence. Belle Isle was one of them. John Page, Chief Engineer of Public Works for the Province of Canada, had marked the site the year before. Lighthouse Friends says the workmen sent in 1854 could not find the spot Page had marked, so no work was done that season.[^1]

In 1855 twenty-two men were kept on the island over the winter, so that building could start earlier in 1856. The contractor had to build a road nearly a mile and a half long, cut through granite outcrops, to reach the site. By the end of 1856 the double dwelling for the keepers was finished, and only the upper storey of the tower remained.[^1] The lantern and first-order Fresnel lens were made in Paris, and French mechanics were sent to fit up the tower and train the keepers. The first light on the upper south end of the island shone for the first time on 15 March 1858.[^1][^2]

The steamer *Doris*, carrying fodder for the horses at work on the island, was wrecked in 1856.[^1]

The lower light came later. In 1879 a second light was set up 91 m (300 ft) lower than the old one, where fog would be less likely to hide it. It was a wooden pyramidal building with a fixed catoptric light at an elevation of 49 m (160 ft).[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives 1879 for the start of the lower light. The Parks Canada heritage statement, as reproduced by the Canadian Register, says the wooden lower light dated from 1878.[^1][^2]

The wooden tower was taken down in 1908. A new circular metal lantern was placed on the old foundation, which had been raised three feet, and it was fitted with a second-order Fresnel lens.[^1] The register describes the present structure as a massive concrete base with a masonry wall, with a red metal lantern topped by a dome. It is 5.7 m high, and the register calls it "a rare example of a Canadian lighthouse which has a lantern, but no tower."[^2] The Coast Guard's list describes instead a "square skeleton tower, red and white rectangular daymark." The sources disagree about what stands on the site, and this story reports both.[^3]

The new light showed an occulting white light, visible five seconds and dark five seconds. Its illuminant was petroleum vapour burning under an incandescent mantle.[^1] The Coast Guard list now gives one white flash every 20 seconds, with a one-second flash and a 19-second eclipse.[^3]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists the keepers of the station in order: D. Vaughan from 1856; Martin Colton, at least 1867 to 1882; Michael Colton, 1882 to 1900; P. J. Colton, 1901 to 1906; Jean L. Thibaudeau, 1907 to 1910; Octave Dubois, 1910 to 1915; Paul Thomas, 1915 to 1918; Michael Thomas, 1918 to 1944; Fred A. Davis, 1944 to 1958; Fred Roberts, 1958 to at least 1964; and Clifford Flynn, at least from 1969.[^1] Some entries begin before 1879, when the lower light was added, so they may include keepers of the upper light; the list does not say which light each keeper tended.

Keepers used to fire a fog gun as a salute to ocean mail steamers passing through the strait. Lighthouse Friends says the practice was stopped when captains reported that they could not tell whether the firing was "a greeting or an indication that the keepers desired to communicate with them."[^1]

A single dwelling was added to the light station in the 1960s, to house the family of a third keeper.[^1]

In 1966 an assistant keeper asked for a switch at the upper light so the lower light could be lit from there. He wrote that in October, November and December the going was very rough, that "it is very dangerous for any man to travel down this steep incline," and that "there is not a secure life-line."[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

At half-past nine on the night of 9 August 1876 lightning struck the tower and the dwelling. It tore clapboards off the tower, smashed the door and frame, passed through the dwelling's upper and lower floors, and seriously injured Keeper Martin Coltin and a member of his family. Lighthouse Friends says lightning rods were added that autumn.[^1]

The station's fog signal grew over the years. Its fog gun was a nine-pounder, fired every hour in fog or snowstorms, and it used about 2,000 pounds of powder each season. A thirty-two-pounder replaced it in 1882. Rockets were put into service in 1891, and a first-order double siren came into operation on 9 September 1899.[^1]

In March 1969 a fire broke out in the fog alarm building while the keepers were scraping an old floor, and three keepers were taken to hospital.[^1]

## After the keepers

The fog alarm was moved to the upper light in 1912.[^1] A radio direction-finding station was built near the lighthouse in 1924. By 1958 the light station was using electricity generated by the radio station, and the lighthouse itself was electrified in 1960.[^1]

The lower, upper and keepers' duplex were declared Recognized Federal Heritage Buildings in 1990. The station was de-staffed in 2001, but the upper and lower lights remain active.[^1] The register calls the lower light an unusual lighthouse, and says that the island's isolation makes its lighthouses a valued landmark for the marine community in the strait.[^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse. Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1] The Coast Guard list gives a nominal range of 18 nautical miles, a focal height of 49.7 m, and the light is marked seasonal, operating 24 hours.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says the light is best seen by boat or from the air.[^1]

Visitors should not try to reach the light. It stands on a cliff, an iron staircase clings to the slope between the two lights, and Lighthouse Friends says there is no secure life-line.[^1][^2]
