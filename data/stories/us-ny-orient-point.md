Orient Point Light is a brown cast-iron tower standing on a reef off Orient Point, on the western side of Plum Gut. Local mariners nicknamed it the "Coffee Pot". It replaced a beacon that the ice carried away, and for almost sixty years it was manned by keepers who lived inside a tower only 45 feet (14 m) high.[^1][^2]

## The place

Plum Gut is the narrow channel between Orient Point and Plum Island. LighthouseFriends describes ebb-tide currents there above five knots, and Oyster Pond Reef, a hazard just under the surface, running out from Orient Point about a third of the way across the passage.[^2] Before there was a lighthouse, the Lighthouse Board kept a beacon on the reef. The Board's report for 1894 describes it as a cast-iron cylinder filled with concrete, topped by an iron shaft and a square cage, with riprap placed around its base "to protect it against the ice."[^3] (LighthouseFriends instead describes a pyramidal stone beacon placed in 1874.[^2]) The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 1897 says the beacon "was carried away by the ice" and proposed a lighted beacon and fog signal at an estimated $5,000.[^4]

## Building the light

The cheaper plan was dropped. By an act approved 4 June 1897, Congress appropriated $30,000 for a light and fog signal on the site of the old beacon.[^4] LighthouseFriends says work began in October 1898, in a stormy autumn, when a gale broke twenty of the forty-eight plates already sunk for the foundation cylinder and damaged nineteen more, so that work stopped for the year. It gives the foundation as finished by August 1899 and most of the superstructure in place by the end of September.[^2] The Lighthouse Board's report for 1900 records that the tower was completed and 803 tons of riprap placed under contract, and that a fixed red fifth-order light was first shown on 10 November 1899.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the fifth-order lens proved too weak and was replaced by a fourth-order lens on 1 May 1900; the Board's report lists the "order of light increased" on that date.[^2][^5] A blower siren fog signal began on 1 June 1900.[^5] The USLHS archive record gives the original optic as fourth order, which does not match the Board's fifth-order light of 1899.[^1][^5]

The tower itself is a cast-iron plate shell lined with brick, with six interior decks.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends reports that more than 9,000 further tons of riprap were added over the next three years as ice and storms attacked the base, and that repairs to cracks and rust on one side of the foundation added weight that left the tower leaning about five degrees, though some accounts blame currents undermining the foundation instead.[^2] In 1905, it adds, the fog signal was changed to a second-class Daboll trumpet and sounded about 400 hours a year.[^2]

## The keepers

Because the tower stood in open water, LighthouseFriends writes, it was thought too dangerous for a keeper's family, and the first head keeper's wife lived ashore in the town of Orient.[^2] That keeper, a Norwegian-born man named Ole N. A. Anderson, is the station's best-documented early story. In November 1900 his assistant, Terence Riordan, took the station boat ashore and did not come back. The *New-York Tribune* of 13 November 1900 reported that after "a hard ten days work" Anderson was almost worn out, getting only a few hours' sleep each day, and that he had flown the flag upside down and tried to signal by "manipulating the light." It says a farmer strolling along the beach saw the flag reversed and rowed out, and relief followed. Riordan's belongings were still at the lighthouse and, said the paper, "What became of Riordan cannot be learned."[^6] LighthouseFriends cautions that the newspaper version made good reading but overstated the danger: the keeper had plenty of canned food, and the man on the beach came out because the station boat had been ashore a long time.[^2]

The USLHS archive, drawing on the keepers' registers, lists a succession of head keepers between 1899 and 1958, among them Silas E. Stanton (1901-1906), Charles E. Whitford (1911-1916) and Manuel J. Freitas (1924-1928), with assistants who often stayed only weeks or months.[^1] The archive's roster ends with John R. Kettering as the last head keeper, to 1958.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends gives an incident on 30 August 1912, when the steamer *Halyoake*, its steering failed, was carried toward the tower in Plum Gut; Keeper Whitford wrote that "the bow ran up on the rocks surrounding the station," but the steamer backed off with no damage to ship or station.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that in May 1924 Assistant Keeper John Wallwork, returning from the mainland with a repairman named Walter Gill, was in a rowboat that became unmanageable and overturned, and that Keeper Manuel Freitas, watching through glasses from the tower, could not reach them; the two men were lost.[^2]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1936 records two rescues. On 18 July, Keeper Francis B. Britland and Second Assistant John Duff launched the station boat and rescued a man exhausted by an outgoing tide.[^7] On 6 September, in heavy seas, Marvin Andrews, keeper of the station, went out in the station motorboat and rescued three people aboard a dismantled sloop, and was commended by the Secretary of Commerce.[^8] LighthouseFriends also tells of a February 1936 episode in which Duff's motorboat failed and drifted into the Sound in ice; the Coast Guard's icebreaker reached him after about five and a half hours.[^2]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends writes that on 5 April 1958 the Coast Guard cutter *Hawthorne* took off the furniture and the Coast Guard keepers, who were replaced by automatic equipment.[^1][^2] In 1970 the Coast Guard proposed demolishing the tower as unsafe to service and too costly to repair; a citizens' campaign led it to back down, and the base was filled with concrete and the tower sandblasted and repainted.[^2] The Fresnel lens was replaced by a modern optic in 1988, and work in 2000 removed the generator and replaced the submarine power cable with solar panels and batteries.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard declared the light excess in 2011. Two online auctions followed, the first winning bidder failing to close; the second ended on 18 September 2013 with a high bid of $252,000.[^2] The lighthouse is privately owned and the tower is closed. It cannot be reached by land, and the reef around it is shallow, so private boats should keep clear. It can be seen from Orient Point and from the Cross Sound Ferry between Orient Point and New London.[^2]
