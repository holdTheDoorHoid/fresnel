Round Island Light stood on the southwest end of a long, low island in the Mississippi Sound, about three miles from the mainland at Pascagoula.[^1] Its first tower went up in 1833. The brick tower that replaced it in 1859 was toppled by Hurricane George in 1998, and in 2010 the city barged its base and remnants to a park on the mainland, where a restored tower opened to the public in 2015.[^14][^15]

## The place

The National Register nomination calls the island "inaptly named as it is actually a long, narrow island." It is low enough to be "subject to overwash during high-intensity storms," and it sits between the outlets of the East and West Pascagoula rivers.[^1] The light was built, the nomination says, to warn shipping of "the dangerous shoals which extend southerly from the island," and to guide vessels bound for Pascagoula Harbor and the water between Round Island and Horn Island.[^1]

In 1831 Congress authorized $7,000 for "a lighthouse on St. Joseph's Island, or some other suitable place off Pascagoula Bay."[^1] The nomination does not say which place the money went to. Round Island was part of land that the Spanish governor Grimarest granted to Francisco Krebs on 13 December 1783.[^1] The federal lighthouse reservation was not set aside until an executive order of 3 September 1900, long after the first tower stood.[^1]

## Building the light

The nomination says that in 1833 "the Lighthouse Board contracted with a Marshall Lincoln of Hingham, Massachusetts" for the first tower.[^1] The earliest Board report the nomination quotes is from 1854, and the nomination does not explain who let the 1833 contract, so this story leaves that question open. The state marker gives the cost as $5,895 and names "Marshall Lincoln and David Henshaw" as the builders. It says the work used "methods similar to those used on the East coast" rather than methods suited to the Gulf.[^14]

The nomination quotes the historian David L. Cipra, who wrote that the first lights on the northern Gulf coast "were apparently copies of the proven New England brick towers."[^1] Cipra also reported that, of the 40 or more brick lighthouses constructed, at least 25 sank into the bottom or blew down.[^1] The first tower here had trouble early. The nomination quotes the Board's 1854 report: "The tower at this place was badly built originally and is now liable to be undermined in a heavy storm."[^1] In 1855 the Board, as the nomination quotes it, said the tower was "exposed to destruction from the sea in any easterly storm." It recommended rebuilding "of bricks, in a position withdrawn from the present site," with "the tower and dwelling to be combined."[^1] The nomination says the Board's recommendations "were not strictly followed."[^1]

Congress authorized $8,000 for the rebuild in 1856, and the brick tower went up in 1859.[^1] The marker gives its cost as $7,130.97.[^14] The nomination says the tower housed a fourth-order Fresnel lens whose focal plane stood 44 feet (13 m) above the sea, visible "at a distance of 12 to 14 miles."[^1] The nomination gives the tower's height as 50 feet (15 m), from a 1955 federal property report, and notes that "various sources" give 60 feet (18 m).[^1]

A separate light stood on the spit to the south. The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 lists a "Round Island Spit beacon light" among the new lights of that year,[^20] and says its "fixed red 5-day lens-lantern light was first shown on the night of February 15, 1900."[^5] Later reports call the spit light the Round Island South Spit Light (1916) and the South Spit Beacon (1926).[^7][^12]

## The keepers

The Archive's roster for the station runs from 1834 to 1944.[^2] It names Curtis Lewis as the first principal keeper, for "1834-at least 1836."[^2] Women held the principal's post here. Margaret Anderson was principal from 1872 to 1881, and Mary A. Anderson is listed as principal for 1881.[^2] Mary Hansen is listed as principal for 1907.[^2] Caroline Bailey was first assistant from 1868 to 1871.[^2] The roster also lists Augusta Dimitry as acting principal keeper in 1904, the year Robert A. Dimitry's time as principal ends.[^2] It lists Charles Anderson as principal from 1870 to 1872 as well. The archive does not say how the three Andersons were related.[^2]

The keeper the sources show most clearly in the 1890s is John Murray, principal from 1881 to 1896.[^2] After the gale of 2 October 1893 he was in Pascagoula, and on Wednesday, 4 October, he told the local paper that the lighthouse "sustained no damage," although the dwelling and some outbuildings were "more or less damaged."[^6]

The nomination also lists some of the men who kept the light, without dates: "Andrew Steiner, Harry Brewer, a man named Bailey of Pascagoula and Sam Maddox."[^1] The roster has Andrew E. Steiner as principal from 1925 to 1933, and Harry Brouwer from 1920 to 1925. The Commissioner's reports and the Bulletin spell the second name Brouwer, so "Brewer" is most likely the same man.[^2][^8][^10] Sam Maddox does not appear on the roster.[^2] The nomination also mentions a grave: a topographical plan made in 1892 or 1893 "indicated a grave location of a former keeper," and the Coast Guard's New Orleans records "shed no light on the identity of the keeper."[^1]

The roster gives Frederick A. Schrieber as principal from 1917 to 1920. The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1917 still calls him assistant keeper, so the roster and that report disagree about his rank in those years.[^2][^21]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A gale struck the coast on Monday, 2 October 1893.[^6] The Pascagoula Democrat-Star of 6 October said the wind "began to blow during the night," and that the coast had "for years and years enjoyed a perfect immunity" from destructive gales.[^6] At Round Island, the paper reported, "the only damage reported is the loss of the quarantine sloop and the wharf."[^6] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 gives the storm's date as 1 October 1893, in its entries for two beacons at the mouth of the Pascagoula River.[^4]

The Board's reports for these years list repairs at the station.[^3][^4][^5] In its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891, "An oil house was erected and minor repairs were made."[^3] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 the Board reports a new iron fence around the buildings and a wharf 300 feet long.[^4] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900, "The dwelling was repaired. The tower was put in good order."[^5]

Later events come from the Commissioner of Lighthouses and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin. On 29 March 1916 the South Spit Light "was collided with and destroyed by the tug Catherine." The Commissioner's report says the cost of repairs, "$1,184.49," was assumed by the owners of the tug.[^7]

In March 1917 the keeper, John H. Portman, and assistant Frederick A. Schrieber brought seven people from a disabled launch to the station and gave them food and shelter.[^17] On 5 May 1917 Schrieber rendered assistance to a small sailing boat that he found leaking near the station, towing it to harbor.[^18]

On the Saturday night before the Pascagoula Chronicle's issue of 5 March 1920, a fishing launch owned by W. V. LeJune sank "in fifteen feet of water on the north side of Horn Island during a strong norther." The Pascagoula Chronicle reported on 5 March that LeJune and a companion spent Sunday and Monday on the island and that on Tuesday they rowed to Round Island, where they were taken in "by the keeper of the Round Island light."[^13]

On 16 February 1921 keeper Harry Brouwer "rendered assistance to the disabled auxiliary boat A. H. Daughdrill," which had run aground near the station.[^10] The Commissioner's report for that year says the boat "had grounded during fog," and that Brouwer and assistant D. C. Powell helped float it.[^8] The next day Powell helped tow a disabled skiff with one occupant to the station.[^19]

In the fiscal year 1922 Brouwer and Powell "went to the assistance of a disabled launch, with 10 persons aboard," which had drifted near the station. They also made temporary repairs to its engine.[^9] On 25 January 1925 Brouwer towed the disabled fishing launch Raich to Pascagoula.[^11] On 10 August 1926 keeper A. E. Steiner and assistant Tony Ryan helped a fishing party whose boat "became disabled near the South Spit Beacon."[^12]

## After the keepers

The marker says the light was "automated around 1940."[^14] The roster, though, lists William E. Ewing as an assistant keeper at the station from 1940 to 1944, so the station still had keepers in 1940 to 1944.[^2] The light was decommissioned as an operational light on 20 March 1944, and its lens was removed with the rest of the equipment.[^1][^14]

The Coast Guard then kept the tower as an unlit day beacon.[^1][^14] The nomination dates the end of that service to 19 September 1954, while the marker gives 1945.[^1][^14] This story does not choose between them.[^1][^14]

Fire took the keeper's dwelling. It was "Gutted by fire on February 24, 1951," and a second fire destroyed what was left in August 1954.[^1] In 1955 the General Services Administration declared the property excess to the Coast Guard's needs. The excess-property report described the tower as "reported to be in sound condition in June, 1955."[^1]

The nomination, written in 1986, says that "the only remaining evidence of auxiliary structures are two brick cisterns and four of the five steel posts that once supported the keeper's dwelling." It also says the "biggest threat to the lighthouse reservation was reported to be vandals."[^1] The marker says the lighthouse was deeded to the City of Pascagoula and added to the National Register in 1986.[^14]

Hurricane George toppled the tower in 1998, and the marker says the fall prompted the founding of the Round Island Lighthouse Preservation Society.[^14] The marker says Hurricane Katrina in 2005 ended any hope of restoring the tower on the island.[^14] In 2010 the city, using grant money, barged the base and remnants to the mainland. A December 2010 magazine report said the tower would be rebuilt "using as much of the original lighthouse as possible" at a new location.[^16]

## Today

The tower stands in Lighthouse Park at 815 Cypress Avenue in Pascagoula.[^15] The city says it was restored and opened to the public in 2015, and it lists Friday and Saturday hours, 10 a.m. to 5 p.m.[^15] A ticket building stands beside the tower.[^14] No source read for this story describes the island site after the 2010 removal, so nothing is said here about what remains there.[^1][^14]
