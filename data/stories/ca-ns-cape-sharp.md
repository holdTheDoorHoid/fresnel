Cape Sharp light stands at the south end of Cape Sharp, on the north side of the entrance to Minas Basin, by the mouth of Parrsboro Harbour in Cumberland County, Nova Scotia. A light has shown there since 1886. The Coast Guard's list gives a white square tower with a red upper portion, an occulting white light and a fog horn.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The cape takes its name from a sharp rise about 91 m (300 ft) high, according to the 1885 Sailing Directions as Lighthouse Friends quotes them.[^1] Across the water, Cape Split lies about 4.3 km (2 2/3 miles) away. Between the two capes is the narrowest part of the entrance to Minas Basin.[^1]

In 1838 New Brunswick was preparing to build a light at Cape Enrage. In return for that light, and one at Quaco Head, its officials suggested that Nova Scotia put a white light on Cape Split or on Cape Sharpe.[^1] Nova Scotia chose Cape Sharp. The light was not in service until 1 November 1886.[^1]

## Building the light

The first tower was a square white wooden tower about 10.4 m (34 ft) high, attached to a two-storey dwelling. Its sixth-order lens showed a fixed red light, which Lighthouse Friends says could be seen for 16 km (10 miles).[^1] Robert J. Ward was its first keeper.[^1]

A hand fog horn went to keeper Elisha Phinney in 1899.[^1] The fog alarm came next. The Marine Department's report labelled 1904 records a fog alarm at Cape Sharp "put in operation on March 1, 1904", in a rectangular wooden building.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says a Parrsboro contractor built it for $1,307, and a Toronto firm supplied the machinery for $2,400.[^1]

The report labelled 1908 records that a 3-inch diaphone was installed to increase the power of the fog alarm, in place of the earlier one.[^5]

The report labelled 1911 records that the fixed red light had been changed to an occulting white light, visible seven seconds and eclipsed three seconds in turn, with fourth-order dioptric apparatus.[^6] The Society instead dates a fourth-order dioptric lens to 1922 to 1941.[^2] The sources disagree, and the 1911 report is the earlier of the two. In the report labelled 1922 an oil storage tank was installed at the light.[^7]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists Robert J. Ward (1886 to 1893), Elisha Phinney (1893 to 1902), Freeman Yorke (1902 to 1913) and J. E. George (1913 to 1926).[^1] The 1911 report's keeper table pairs Freeman Yorke with Cape Sharpe.[^6] The Marine report labelled 1923 credits a "Mr. J. E. George" with making fifty new spar buoys at Cape Sharpe, to replace old condemned ones. It does not say whether he was the keeper.[^8]

Four keepers named George held the post in turn from 1913 to 1936, according to Lighthouse Friends. The sources do not say whether they were related.[^1] Norman Morton Durrant kept the light from 1936 to 1954. Albert Stanley Green is listed from 1963, and Victor Elliott from 1965 to 1985. Lighthouse Friends says Elliott's wife Betty served with him for about twenty years.[^1] Kevin Harvey took charge in 1985 and stayed until the station was destaffed in 1988.[^1]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the light was destaffed in 1988. The Society says it was automated by 1989.[^1][^2] By 1992 the Society records an electric fog horn and an electric light with the fourth-order lens.[^2]

Lighthouse Friends says that the keeper's dwelling was sold to a Parrsboro owner, cut in half and hauled over Cape Split Road. It now stands on West Bay Road, about two kilometres west of the Ottawa House.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends dates the present tower to 1973, a pepper-shaker-style white tower with a red lantern room, built with a keeper's dwelling.[^1] The Society's record describes a tapered square wooden tower, with its dwelling attached, as still standing, and a tourism guide says the tower built in 1886 still stands.[^2][^9] The Coast Guard's list gives the present tower as square and 11.7 m high.[^3] The sources do not settle which tower stands today.

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the station.[^1] Its list gives an occulting white light, seven seconds on and three off, every ten seconds, with a focal height of 17.4 m and a nominal range of 11 nautical miles. The list gives the light as operating year round and 24 hours a day, and it also lists a fog horn.[^3]

The Society's index marks the light as not operating, while the Coast Guard's list does not.[^2][^3] Lighthouse Friends reports that the road is too rutted and overgrown to drive. Visitors park and walk the 1.3 km to the lighthouse, where the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1] The Society's record gives the access as requiring a hike.[^2]
