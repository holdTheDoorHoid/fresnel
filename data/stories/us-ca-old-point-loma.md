Old Point Loma Lighthouse stands on the summit of Point Loma, at the mouth of San Diego Bay, inside Cabrillo National Monument.[^4][^5][^8] Its light was first shown on November 15, 1855, and it guided mariners into San Diego Bay for 36 years, until the light was moved to a new tower lower on the point in 1891.[^4][^5] The old tower is now a restored museum run by the National Park Service.[^4][^5]

## The place

The tower stands about 422 feet (129 m) above the entrance to San Diego Bay, and its lens was 462 feet (141 m) above the water, at the top of the tower, according to the National Park Service.[^4] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1931 gives the same 462 feet as the old light's height above the sea.[^6]

The National Register nomination says the lighthouse "guided ships along the coast of California" from 1855 to 1891. It also says the building was one of the first eight lighthouses built on the Pacific coast of the United States.[^1]

Water was the first problem. The station had one small cistern in its basement, so when it ran dry the keepers hauled barrels by wagon on a 7- to 10-mile trip, up a steep climb to the summit.[^3] "Even with the addition in 1883 of a huge concrete catch basin in front of the lighthouse," the NPS history says, the supply was not always enough.[^3] LighthouseFriends dates the catch basin to 1882.[^8]

The building was made of brick and sandstone. Bricks were used in the tower, and tiles for the basement floor came from the ruins of the old Spanish fort Guijarros, the nomination says.[^1]

## Building the light

The nomination says the lighthouse was one of the first eight on the Pacific coast, and that "since all eight were built by one firm under one contract," the building stands as a symbol of the nation's first successful effort to get navigational aids for the newly acquired west coast.[^1]

The light was first planned as a first-order light. The NPS history says the plan changed to a third-order lens after the staff had been hired, and "when the light was changed to one of the third order there was a concomitant reduction in personnel allowance."[^3] LighthouseFriends says the tower had first been designed for an Argand lamp and was reworked to take the Fresnel lens.[^8]

The lens was installed in November 1855, and the light was lit on November 15 that year.[^1][^4] LighthouseFriends says the contract grew from a sum of $15,000 to almost $30,000 by the end.[^8]

Personnel were hard to keep. George Tolman, appointed first assistant on January 29, 1855, learned late that year that his pay would start only on the day the light was lit. He wrote to the secretary of the Lighthouse Board, and he resigned "on January 29, the first anniversary of his appointment."[^3]

The sandstone walls were left bare at first. By 1879 the soft stone was crumbling, and the west and south walls were covered in cement and painted. The tower was not painted the "traditional" white until 1887.[^3]

For its first 34 years the light was a fixed white. On April 1, 1889, its characteristic was "changed to fixed white, varied by flashes, alternately red and white, interval between flashes one minute." The lens did not turn, and the NPS history thinks "a rotating red shield outside the lens was used" to make the flashes.[^3] The lamp burned sperm whale oil.[^4]

## The keepers

The NPS history says there were "11 keepers and 22 assistants" in the 36 years the light was in service.[^3] The Lighthouse Society's roster lists the principal keepers and assistants by role.[^9]

The first principal keeper was James P. Keating, who heads the roster from 1854 to 1859.[^9] Four months after the light was lit, Keating complained: "I have been unfortunate in respect of assistants. There comes a strange one every month."[^3] The NPS history says the first keeper of the Point Loma light operated San Diego's first shipyard, which in 1857 launched the first vessel built in the city.[^3] That passage does not name him. The same history names Keating as the keeper in 1855 and calls him "Capt. Keating" in its account of an 1858 gale, so Keating was probably that keeper, but no source connects the two directly.[^3]

Robert D. Israel came to the station as an assistant in 1871 and was made keeper in 1873.[^3][^9] The NPS history describes him as "by trade a chairmaker," who had joined the army in the Mexican War, and says that "Captain Israel served as keeper of the Point Loma lighthouse for nearly 20 years."[^3] The roster gives his years as head keeper as 1873 to 1891.[^9]

His wife, Mary A. Israel, served as first assistant from 1873 to 1876, and the NPS photograph captions call her "wife of Captain Israel."[^2][^9] The NPS history quotes the San Diego Union's 1874 visit, when the reporter was "conducted through the entire establishment by the gentlemanly keeper, Mr. Israel, and his wife, who is his assistant in the care of the light."[^3] The history says she "served as assistant keeper for 3 years." It adds that "some say" she knitted through her night watches, sitting on the first floor as near the stairwell as possible, where a circular hole in the tower's deck let the lamp's light reach her work.[^3]

John D. Jenkins was head keeper from 1867 to 1871. The roster also lists an Eliza Jenkins as first assistant over the same years, and the sources used here do not say how the two were related.[^9] LighthouseFriends quotes a January 1869 visit by W. Jeff Gatewood, the first editor of the San Diego Union. Gatewood found Mr. Jenkins meeting visitors at the gate, his wife receiving them at the entrance, flower beds ringed with abalone shells, and tomatoes ripening against the house. LighthouseFriends quotes the account as it appeared in the paper.[^8]

When the light moved in 1891, Israel went with it. The NPS history says he was there "for nearly a year" before "a disagreement, the facts of which have been obscured by time," made him resign. He returned to Point Loma in January 1908, died that month, and was buried at Fort Rosecrans.[^3]

David R. Splaine was an assistant at Point Loma from 1886 to 1889.[^9] The NPS history says that after his time there he "was appointed first keeper of that light station," meaning Ballast Point.[^3] The Ballast Point roster lists him as head keeper there from 1895 to 1916, and names John M. Nilsson as the first keeper, from 1890 to 1892.[^10]

For context beyond this station: the NPS history says a principal keeper earned $1,000 a year across the service and a first assistant $650. "Everyone, from the keepers up to the Lighthouse Board itself, considered the salaries inadequate."[^3] This is general context about the service of the time, not a record of Point Loma.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On October 2, 1858, a severe gale struck San Diego for six hours, and several ships in the harbor dragged their anchors.[^3] At Point Loma the NPS history quotes a contemporary account: "So fearful was the gale at Point Loma the Lighthouse keeper, Capt. Keating, was obliged to leave at 12 o'clock M., fearing the tower would fall."[^3] The lighthouse was not harmed.[^3]

Four years after that gale, "an earthquake rocked San Diego, and this time the lighthouse was damaged." The NPS history says the extent of the damage is not known. The Board's secretary felt he could order immediate repairs without first consulting the Board.[^3]

A visitor's account from 1874 describes the wind. The NPS history quotes the reporter: "The roar of the wind about the tower is almost deafening, and necessitates the voice being raised to the highest pitch whilst conversing within."[^3]

## After the keepers

The light moved on March 23, 1891, to a new iron skeleton tower on the tip of the point, with the light 88 feet (27 m) above the sea.[^6] The Board's annual report for the year ended June 30, 1891 says: "On March 23, the light was exhibited for the first time."[^7] The old tower, 462 feet (141 m) up, had often been obscured by fog.[^6]

"After it was abandoned in 1891, it was vandalized," the nomination says. The NPS history adds that windows were broken and pieces of the building were carted away. By 1913 it was in a dilapidated condition, and the commanding officer at Fort Rosecrans had recommended that it be torn down.[^1][^3]

In 1913 the Order of Panama, a group promoting California's Spanish heritage, proposed a statue of Juan Rodriguez Cabrillo, 150 feet (46 m) tall, on Point Loma.[^3] The order held dedication ceremonies at the lighthouse on September 26, 1913. A Presidential Proclamation dated October 10, 1913 "was signed setting aside one-half acre" around the old lighthouse as Cabrillo National Monument. The statue was never built, and the order dissolved.[^3]

The Army, which controlled the point, kept the old building.[^3] The NPS history says "the Army encouraged soldiers and their families to live in it," spent $360 on repairs in 1915, and used the building as a radio station around the mid-1920s.[^3]

In 1931 the Ninth Army Corps found funds to renovate the old lighthouse.[^3] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin that year reported that the old structure "is being preserved as a memorial and has just been repaired and painted."[^6][^3] In 1933 the monument passed to the National Park Service, and "the restoration work was completed in 1935," with a concessioner who lived in the building and ran a tearoom.[^3]

In 1941 visits to the monument were stopped for wartime security. The Navy used the lighthouse as a signal tower: "Ships coming to San Diego were signaled from the lighthouse," and a correct reply let the submarine nets open for them.[^3] The public was welcomed back on "November 11, 1946."[^3] A National Park Service team that inspected the building in 1947 found the outside of the lantern needed to be sandblasted to remove the Army's camouflage paint, and the lighthouse was reopened with "a fresh coat of white paint."[^3]

On November 15, 1955, the centenary of the light, the tower was lit by an electric bulb "for the first time in over 60 years," for show only.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1974.[^8]

The name "Old Spanish Lighthouse" became popular while the building was a ruin. The NPS history says "Evidence indicates that the term 'Old Spanish Lighthouse' is of 20th Century origin."[^3]

## Today

The old lighthouse is a unit of Cabrillo National Monument. According to the NPS it is "Open daily from 9:00 AM to 5:00 PM."[^5] The grounds and first floor are accessible. The tower is not wheelchair accessible because of its narrow staircase, and it is open to visitors only on special Open Tower Days.[^5]

The National Park Service "has refurbished the interior to its historic 1880s appearance." The reconstructed assistant keeper's quarters beside the tower house an exhibit on the lives of the lightkeepers.[^4][^5]

The lantern now holds a third-order Fresnel lens. LighthouseFriends says it came from Mile Rocks Light, near San Francisco.[^8] The sources used here do not say what became of the lens first installed in 1855.
