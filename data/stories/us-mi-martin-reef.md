Martin Reef Light Station is a square steel-and-concrete tower standing on a pier in northern Lake Huron, east of the Straits of Mackinac, built in the 1920s to mark a shoal that had a few feet of water over it in places.[^1] It replaced a lightship, and its keepers lived inside it, miles from the nearest shore.[^1][^2][^6]

## The place

Martin Reef is a rocky shoal that lies close to the track of vessels sailing between the Detour Passage and Mackinac Island.[^1] In some patches it is covered by as little as three feet of water.[^1] The steamer *Muskegon*, loaded with lumber, ran aground on it in November 1908, one of many ships caught there before it was properly marked.[^1]

By the mid-1890s the Light-House Board was asking for a light-vessel here. A report in the volume labelled 1896–1897, already repeating an earlier recommendation, argued that vessels bound to and from the Michigan ports, the largest of the lake class, "pass close to the dangerous Martins Reef, hence a light-vessel there is much needed," and estimated that one could be built and placed for $15,000.[^3] The Board repeated the request in later reports; the 1899 volume says it was "renewed" again, describing the traffic as running between the mouth of the St. Marys River and the Straits of Mackinac.[^4] In the volume labelled 1901 the Board's estimate for a steam light-vessel had risen to $35,000.[^5] A gas buoy, Martin Reef No. 5, was established on the reef with a lens-lantern light on 20 May 1901.[^5] Lightship 89, a steel vessel built at Muskegon in 1908, was anchored five-eighths of a mile south of the reef at the opening of navigation in 1909. She showed a fixed white light from a cluster of three oil lens lanterns and sounded a steam whistle in poor visibility; a submarine bell was installed on 22 April 1915.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1923 the Lighthouse Service asked for $100,000 for a permanent station, arguing that a fixed light and fog signal would cost about $4,450 a year less to maintain than the lightship, and would free Light Vessel 89 for other duty.[^1] Allotments of $16,000 (31 December 1924) and $34,000 (19 March 1925) let work begin in 1925.[^1] The builders worked from a site on Government Island, off Cedarville, where they made the foundation, a timber crib of twelve-by-twelve-inch timbers wrapped in steel ice plating. Late in the summer of 1925 it was hauled out to the reef, sunk, and its pockets filled with rock and cement. During 1926 a concrete pier was built on top, and the work camp moved from the island to the pier.[^1]

The Lighthouse Service's own account says the work was started in spring 1925 and completed in August 1927, at a cost of about $112,000, entirely by the district's own force and plant, with the reservation on Government Island used as the base until the pier was ready;[^6] LighthouseFriends, drawing on other material, says work at the station wrapped up in July 1927.[^1] The pier is 60 feet square and stands in about six feet of water on a timber crib resting on hardpan and boulders.[^6] The tower above it is steel plate backed with hollow tile, with quarters "for four keepers," and two compressed-air cranes at opposite corners of the pier for hoisting boats.[^6] The sources differ on the optics. A 1927 Lake Carriers' Association description quoted by LighthouseFriends calls the lens fourth-order, with an electric light of about 8,000 candlepower.[^1] The Lighthouse Service also calls the lantern fourth-order, puts its focal plane 70 feet above the water, and describes a 300-watt lamp in a fixed lens showing an occulting light.[^6] The US Lighthouse Society's archive gives the height of the light as 65 feet and the original lens as third order.[^2]

The station was placed in commission at the opening of navigation in 1927.[^6] Lightship 89 was released on 7 June 1927 and transferred to the twelfth district as a relief vessel.[^1]

## The keepers

The first head keeper was Herbert P. Crittenden, who had earlier been head keeper at Gull Rock, Manitou Island, Crisp Point and Grand Marais, and who held the post from 1927 to 1933; John Franklin "Frank" Davis followed in 1934 and is recorded through at least 1941.[^1][^2] The first assistants were Richard E. Dissett (1927–1934) and, after a short run of changes, William J. Miller (1939–1951), the longest listed stay.[^2] Stanley William Clarke was second assistant from 1927 to 1930 and returned as first assistant in 1936–1939.[^2] From about 1950 the archive lists Coast Guard crew, among them Wallis G. Cogswell, and through the 1960s a rotating roster of Coast Guardsmen served under a succession of head keepers, none listed for more than about three years, the last of them in 1970.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In early April 1954 Wallis G. Cogswell, then serving at the light, was injured in a fall down a flight of stairs. A helicopter from the icebreaker *Mackinaw* came out and, hovering about 25 feet above the lantern deck, lowered a web sling to take him off. He was treated at Cheboygan.[^1]

## After the keepers

The archive's roster runs to 1970; no automation date is given in the sources cited here.[^2] On 1 May 2013 the General Services Administration declared the light excess to the Coast Guard's needs and offered it free to public bodies and non-profits under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act. No qualified steward came forward, and an online auction began on 2 July 2018 at $25,000. Six bidders took part, and the high bid on 1 October 2018 was $52,777.[^1] The National Park Service's report for 2018 records the light as conveyed by public sale for that amount.[^7]

## Today

The light remains an active aid to navigation.[^2] It stands 4.3 miles from the nearest land and is best seen from a boat; the tower is closed to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends notes a very distant view from Highway 134 east of Port Dolomite and a tour boat cruise out of Les Cheneaux, and that the station's fourth-order Fresnel lens is on display at Point Iroquois Lighthouse.[^1] Neither cited source names the 2018 buyer, and LighthouseFriends still describes the Coast Guard as owner, so current ownership is unconfirmed; the tower should be treated as closed and not to be approached or boarded.[^1][^7]
