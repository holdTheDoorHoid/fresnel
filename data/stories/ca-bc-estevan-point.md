Estevan Point lighthouse is a tall, white, eight-sided concrete tower on the exposed outer coast of Vancouver Island, British Columbia, held up by flying buttresses.[^8] It went into service in the first years of the twentieth century, and in June 1942 it became the only light station in Canada to come under enemy gunfire.[^1][^3]

## The place

Estevan Point is part of the Hesquiat Peninsula, about halfway up the west coast of Vancouver Island. Lighthouse Friends records that Juan Pérez named it Punta San Esteban on 8 August 1774, and that charts have used Estevan Point since 1848.[^3] The light stands on the south-west extremity of the point, at a spot the Canadian Coast Guard's List of Lights calls Hole-in-the-Wall.[^2]

The coast was poorly lit. Lighthouse Friends says only two lighthouses served the west coast of Vancouver Island in the nineteenth century despite heavy shipping, that the wreck of the *Valencia* near Pachena Point in 1906 pushed the government to add more, and that approval for a light station at Estevan Point came in 1907.[^3]

## Building the light

The Department of Marine and Fisheries' report for the year ended 31 March 1907 describes "a light, fog alarm and wireless telegraph station" under construction at Estevan Point, by day labour under Mr. Thomas Tubman.[^4] There was no safe landing at the point. According to Lighthouse Friends, supplies came ashore about five miles away at the First Nation village of Hesquiat, and the work gang cut a trail through the forest and laid a horse-drawn tramway.[^3] In November 1908 a high tide and a winter gale carried off twenty-five yards of the track; Alfred Nelson, who had taken over from A. L. Gray as officer in charge, wrote a few months later that the weather had been "simply awful". One workman wrote that after ten days of fresh meat from a wild bull shot by the gang came "a period of salt meat, day after day, morning, noon, and evening."[^3]

The report for the year ended 31 March 1908 lists a fog-alarm building for a diaphone plant, and a double dwelling for the engineers, as built that year.[^5] The following year's report records a 5-inch, 12-horse-power diaphone plant, bought from the Canadian Fog Signal Company of Toronto for $12,500, installed in the new fog-alarm building.[^6] In the report for the year ended 31 March 1910, the Chief Engineer's section records that he "arranged for the completion of the 100-foot concrete tower at Estevan point".[^7]

The tower, about 30 m (100 ft) high and stiffened by eight flying buttresses, was designed by Colonel William P. Anderson of the department.[^3][^8][^7] The federal heritage statement calls it among the early reinforced-concrete buildings in Canada and the finest example of Anderson's experimental buttressed design.[^8]

The date of first lighting is not settled. Lighthouse Friends says the tower was finished late in 1909 and the light, with a first-order Fresnel lens by Chance Brothers, began service in February 1910.[^3] The department's list of new lights in its report for the year ended 31 March 1910 records at Estevan Point a lighthouse "established", flashing white in a group of three every 10 seconds, which fits a 1910 start.[^7] The next year's report (to 31 March 1911) still lists, among the works of the Chief Engineer's branch, the "completion of the erection" of the 100-foot tower, stiffened with eight flying buttresses, with the note that the work had started in the 1909-10 season.[^16] Our record gives 1909. Today's light is two white flashes every 15 seconds, 37.5 m (123 ft) above the sea, listed as visible 17 nautical miles.[^2]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends names Otto Buckholz as the first keeper, a veteran sealer who had helped build the station.[^3] It quotes a *Daily Colonist* item, undated in that quotation, in which the wife of the Hesquiat storekeeper asked the provincial police to act after a telegram reported that a cow had been shot; she suspected the keeper, who she said had threatened it. The paper did not report the outcome.[^3]

Lighthouse Friends gives the head keepers who followed: Jack Reddy in 1910, Jens Peter Jensen from 1911 to 1919, Donald B. McPhee to 1922, Percival C. Pike to 1926, and Robert M. Lally from 1926 to 1942.[^3] The station was also a wireless station, and a busy one. Lighthouse Friends says it became the busiest on Canada's west coast, and on 4 January 1925 it was in contact with the *Sa Mukura* as that ship arrived in New Zealand.[^3] A Department of Marine report describes the station answering distress calls: at 4.55 a.m. on 15 February 1923 it received a call from the steamer *Tuscan Prince*, "on the rocks, breaking up fast", and at once broadcast the S.O.S.[^10] The same report says the daily weather broadcast was relayed from Estevan to ships within about 3,000 miles.[^10] Another report notes a fire risk from red-hot carbon thrown out by the exhaust stack of the station's semi-diesel engine.[^11]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Just after midnight on Friday 6 December 1918, according to Lighthouse Friends, an earthquake struck off the coast while Jensen was on duty. Mercury sloshed out of the lens table, parts of the lens were smashed and the tower cracked along its length in several places. Jensen wrote to headquarters asking to be relieved, for a trip or for good.[^3] The Marine department's report for the year ended 31 March 1919 also mentions that during the "Vancouver quake of December 6" the light was put out of commission when mercury was shaken from the lens table; it gives no year for the date, but the report period places it in 1918.[^9]

On the evening of 20 June 1942 the Japanese submarine I-26 surfaced about two miles off the point and fired on the station. The accounts differ on the details. Edward T. Redford, the chief wireless operator, reported that shelling began about 9.40 p.m. and lasted about forty minutes, with about 25 shells.[^3] Another account puts the surfacing shortly after ten o'clock, and quotes the submarine's commander as remembering about 17 shots; a third gives 25 to 30 rounds.[^12][^1] Redford's radio message reached Pacific Command at Esquimalt at 10.14 p.m.[^12]

Lighthouse Friends says Lally put out the light at once; another account has him just finished lighting the lamp, climbing down to warn his wife, then going back up to douse it.[^3][^12] Redford reported that the first shells landed on the beach about 100 yards in front of the lighthouse, then went overhead as the gunners raised their sights, and that buildings were hit only by fragments.[^3] Women and children were helped away from the settlement. In Redford's words, everyone "took the whole incident in their stride", and the next day went hunting for souvenirs.[^3] Warships and fishermen's reserve vessels were sent, and an RCAF flying boat flew over the area but found no submarine.[^12]

The village of the Hesquiaht First Nation lay close enough that people there heard the shells.[^13] CHEK News, reporting family accounts passed down in the community, says people ran into the bush or took to canoes and fishing boats; Chief Councillor Mariah Charleson, retelling what her grandmother told her father, said the frightening part for her was "the whistling sound".[^13]

For shipping the cost was high. The lights of the outer coast stations were turned off to keep them from submarines, which pinetreeline says all but paralysed the shipping still on the coast, and keepers were issued rifles.[^1] The federal heritage statement says the tower was "reputedly shelled by a Japanese submarine" while Parliament debated conscription, and a 1995 CBC *Fifth Estate* programme questioned the official account, with eyewitnesses suggesting a government conspiracy to build support for conscription. Two Department of National Defence historians, in a 2004 newspaper article, called any suggestion that the attacks were staged "unfounded", saying Japanese doctrine was to shell shore facilities on leaving a patrol area.[^8][^13] Shell fragments turned up in the area later: one found on 3 July 1973 was destroyed by a bomb-disposal team from CFB Comox, and a fragment is on display at the Maritime Museum of British Columbia.[^1] Lighthouse Friends adds that the wife of a member of the station staff found a stray unexploded shell while walking in the woods in 1973.[^3]

## Today

Estevan Point was a staffed Canadian Coast Guard light station when, in a 2022-23 assessment of a proposed solar installation, the federal government the federal government described it as staffed by two lightkeepers around the clock, with three residences, diesel generators and a fog-alarm building that would house the batteries; the station lies within Hesquiat Peninsula Provincial Park.[^14]

The radio station closed in 1979, and about then the first-order lens was replaced by an AGA PRB-21 beacon.[^3] The lens went to the Sooke Region Museum, which re-assembled it under the original lantern dome from Triangle Island, set on a custom-built concrete tower, and dedicated the exhibit on 26 June 2005; Lighthouse Friends dates the museum's acquisition to 2004.[^15][^3]

The station is the keepers' home and is remote. Lighthouse Friends says it is most easily seen from the air, that the lighthouse is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard with the tower closed, and that there are no developed trails in the park.[^3]
