Burlington Breakwater North Light stands at the north end of the breakwater that shelters Burlington harbor on Lake Champlain, in Chittenden County, Vermont.[^1][^2] The tower there is a replica of a light of about 1890. The breakwater has carried lights since the 1840s or the 1850s, depending on the account: the nomination dates the first lantern to 1842, and LighthouseFriends dates the first wooden towers on its ends to 1857.[^2][^1]

## The place

Before the breakwater, the harbor was open to the weather. LighthouseFriends says it gave ships little shelter until federal work on a breakwater began in 1837.[^1] The National Register nomination lists three federal appropriations for the first section, beginning on 4 July 1836 with $10,000.[^2]

The breakwater served the lake trade that grew up with the canals. By 1873, LighthouseFriends says, Burlington had become the third-largest lumber port in the country.[^1] The first section was a V-shaped structure of about 1,000 feet (300 m), completed by 1854 at a cost of $28,727.[^1][^2]

The breakwater grew in stages. Its northernmost part is a separate section, built in 1889 and 1890, and a gap separates it from the main structure.[^2]

## Building the light

Accounts of the first lights do not agree. The nomination says the first light on the breakwater was a lantern kept by a steamboat company, placed in 1842. It also says lighthouses were built at each end in 1886, when signal lanterns gave way to beacons with a Fresnel lens around a kerosene wick.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives a different start: Congress provided $2,000 in 1854, and wooden towers stood on the ends from 1857, moving as the breakwater grew.[^1]

The keepers' dwelling came later. In 1870 the Lighthouse Board asked for one on the pier, arguing that in spring and fall the keeper reached the beacons "with great difficulty".[^1] Congress provided $7,500 in 1871. LighthouseFriends says the dwelling was built between the two beacons in 1875, and the nomination dates a prefabricated keeper's house to 1874, on the breakwater north of center.[^1][^2] The two accounts agree on the rest. Neither says a keeper lived in it. It was sold at auction in 1884, and it survives as a private house in Burlington.[^2][^1]

The north end's light changed with the detached section. The Board's report for the fiscal year that ended in 1891 lists a sixth-order light at the "North Beacon" in Burlington harbor as discontinued on 22 October 1890.[^4] The same report says the beacon on the old north end was moved to the north end of the new section.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the northernmost of the two new lights became the Burlington Breakwater North Light.[^1] The Board's report for the fiscal year 1893 says a new beacon was built at the north extension.[^6]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster for the north light lists eleven principal keepers from 1856 to 1939, plus a first assistant.[^8] The roster's first name, George W. Beckwith, begins in 1856, a year before the 1857 date LighthouseFriends gives for the first lights. The sources do not explain the gap.[^8][^1]

Beckwith served until 1862. Henry C. Johnson (1862 to 1863), Amaziah G. Lucas (1863 to 1867) and F. O'Donahan (1867) followed in quick succession. Rufus P. Tibbetts kept it until 1870, and Leonard C. Bliss from 1870 to 1873.[^8]

The longest stint of the nineteenth century belonged to James Wakefield, who kept the light from 1873 to 1890. The roster calls him James Wakefield, Jr. LighthouseFriends calls him Sr., and says he preferred to live in the city with his family and row out to the breakwater to tend the lights.[^8][^1] John W. Wakefield served as first assistant from 1877 to 1882.[^8]

Horatio M. Price kept the light from 1890 to 1893. Henry Chiott followed from 1893 to 1907, and Edgar Chiott from 1907 to 1933, a twenty-six-year stint. Henry and Edgar share a surname, and neither source says how they were related.[^8]

The last keeper on the roster, Rolla W. Hill, is listed for 1937 to 1939.[^8] LighthouseFriends says the lights were lit by hand for the last time in 1938. That evening Hill was taken back to shore by a small flotilla of twelve boats carrying seventy-five people.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends says the northern tower burned in 1870.[^1]

The schooner *General Butler*, which the nomination calls a dramatic breakwater story, was driven onto the southern end of the breakwater in December 1876. Its crew and passengers reached the wall and were taken off by a Burlington man in a rowboat. The full account belongs to the south light's story, because the schooner struck near the south end.[^2][^3]

The Board's report for 1896 and 1897 records a beacon blown from the north extension pier one winter, which was repaired by contract and replaced.[^7]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the wooden towers were replaced in 1925 with skeletal towers that had tank houses at their bases.[^1] The nomination describes the modern metal light towers as dating from about 1960. The two accounts disagree on the date.[^2]

The nomination says automatic lights, eliminating the need for a light tender, did not come until 1938.[^2]

Federal money later paid for repairs to the breakwater. In 1999 Senator Patrick Leahy secured $1.3 million to stabilize it, and a second act of Congress added $2.5 million in 2001. In the same year Leahy obtained $250,000 for replicas of the original lights.[^1]

The replicas were built on the Burlington waterfront so the public could watch. The south light was activated on 12 September 2004 before a crowd, and seconds later the north light began to flash too, LighthouseFriends says.[^1]

In 2011 high water on Lake Champlain stripped the lower skirting from the north tower. LighthouseFriends says it had not been replaced as of 2014.[^1]

## Today

The City of Burlington owns the light, according to the Society's record and LighthouseFriends.[^8][^1] The tower is closed to visitors.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes a public fishing pier, reached by Lake Street, from which the light can be seen well.[^1]

The breakwater itself was nominated to the National Register of Historic Places.[^2] The keeper's house survives as a private house in Burlington.[^2]
