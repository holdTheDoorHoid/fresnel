Pointe aux Barques Light stands on the Lake Huron shore at the tip of Michigan's thumb, in Huron County.[^1] The brick tower standing today dates from 1857. A light has marked the point since 1847 or 1848, and a Life-Saving Service station stood nearby.[^4][^1] The light is still an active aid to navigation, and the Pointe aux Barques Maritime Museum offers tower climbs in season.[^3][^2]

## The place

The name is French for "Point of the Little Ships."[^1] Huron County's blog translates it as "Point of Small Boats," because the water there is very shallow.[^3] In 1838 Lieutenant James T. Homans of the Navy inspected the lakes for new lights and chose the more westerly of two points, "because it is sooner seen by vessels approaching from the northward and westward," as LighthouseFriends quotes him, and because it lay near a shoal.[^1] Congress voted $5,000 for a light there in 1838, but LighthouseFriends says that money "seems to have been used to construct a beacon at the mouth of Saginaw River." LighthouseFriends dates the next $5,000 to 3 March 1847.[^1] The county blog dates the $5,000 allocation to 3 July 1847, so the two sources disagree.[^3]

## Building the light

A 65-foot (20 m) tower went up in 1847 and was fitted with nine lamps in fourteen-inch reflectors, according to LighthouseFriends. Peter L. Shook was appointed the first keeper on 3 March 1848.[^1] The county blog says that first tower was completed nearly 4.5 miles east-southeast of the point itself, and that problems of design and materials led to a rebuild ten years later. LighthouseFriends does not say that, and no second source settles it.[^3]

The present tower was built in 1857, and the sources describe it differently. The county blog calls it a cylindrical brick tower, 89 feet (27 m) tall, connected by an enclosed corridor to a two-level house.[^3] LighthouseFriends describes yellow brick, 79 feet (24 m) tall, with a focal plane 88 feet (27 m) above the water, and a one-and-a-half-story dwelling linked to the tower by a covered passageway.[^1] The USLHS record gives the tower's shape as conical, 79 feet from base to lantern, and 89 feet as the light's height above the water, so the 89-foot figure is probably the height of the light, not the tower.[^4] The light first showed in the spring of 1858 with a third-order lens, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1]

The Board reported oil houses for the station in 1893 and fog-signal apparatus contracted for in 1899.[^5][^6] LighthouseFriends says a second keeper's dwelling was authorized by an act approved 26 February 1907, built in 1908, and that the illuminant was changed from oil to incandescent oil vapor on 28 June 1914.[^1] The county blog says electricity came to the lighthouse in 1932.[^3]

## The keepers

Peter Shook drowned on 31 March 1849 when his boat capsized on the way to Port Huron, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] His widow, Catherine, was appointed in his place. LighthouseFriends calls her "the first female lighthouse keeper in Michigan," and it is the only source read for that claim.[^1] The roster lists her as "Catharine Shook (1849-1851)," and LighthouseFriends says she resigned on 19 March 1851.[^4][^1] After Shook's death a fire broke out in the keeper's dwelling. The inspector of lights blamed it on a faulty chimney, LighthouseFriends reports.[^1]

Between 1851 and 1863 five men held the post. An assistant was added in 1859, and the roster shows Godolphin Dodge as first assistant from 1859 to 1862.[^4][^1] Andrew B. Shaw kept the light from 1863 to 1895, a longer tenure than any keeper before Richards.[^4] In the Life-Saving Service's report for 1878, an inspector recording a wreck-response says two pairs of horses were obtained, one pair "from Mr. Andrew Shaw, of the light-house near by."[^7]

Peter Lemieux Richards was appointed first assistant in 1895 and became head keeper in 1905. He retired in June 1939, and LighthouseFriends says he served longer than any other keeper here.[^4][^1] His son Leland recalled that Sundays were kept strictly, with no ball or checkers allowed, and that Richards spent a good portion of Sundays giving visitors tours of the tower.[^1]

A 1925 Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports that Charles Kimball, keeper of the Marquette light, retired that April at 71, after entering the service "at Point Aux Barques Lighthouse, Mich." on 27 September 1872.[^8] The roster lists a Charles A. Kimball as first assistant here from 1872 to 1878.[^4] The Bulletin gives no middle initial, so the link rests on the name and the date.

Daniel McDonald, who reportedly retired in 1955 as caretaker, later described the watch from the tower, "keeping a constant lookout for changing weather conditions and ships."[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Life-Saving Service's annual report for 1884 records "heavy fog" in the vicinity of the Point aux Barques station on 12 June. In the afternoon the station's patrol discerned a schooner "very near the shore and steering south."[^9]

The scow schooner *Mona*, of Detroit, was bound home from Thompson's Harbor with lumber when she sprang a leak ten miles off shore in a northerly gale on 10 September 1887. She drifted and stranded about two miles east-by-south of the station.[^11] The Life-Saving Service's 1888 report, which lists the people aided by the Women's National Relief Association's stores that year, includes the schooner Mona at the Pointe aux Barques Station on 10 September 1887.[^10] She broke up on the 23rd and became a total loss. Station men hauled the remainder of her lumber out of the water.[^14]

The Great Lakes storm of November 1913 reached the station as wind-driven snow. A member's essay on the Pointe aux Barques Maritime Museum's site says that by midday Sunday, 9 November, "the snow was so thick and so heavily driven by the wind that vessels out on the lake could not see the rays of the light."[^12] LighthouseFriends, citing a son's recollection, says Richards climbed the tower repeatedly during the storm, and when the light began to dim he "tied the wick up with a piece of string."[^1] The Bulletin's report of storm damage that year says the Pointe aux Barques gas and whistling buoy "has not been found since the storm."[^13]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took control of all lights in 1939, according to the county blog.[^3] The sources give different dates for the end of resident keepers. LighthouseFriends cites Light Lists that show the station unwatched in 1941, and the USLHS place record gives 1958 for automation.[^1][^4] The county blog says the light "has been remotely controlled by the United States Coast Guard since 2003."[^3]

The lens also has conflicting dates. LighthouseFriends says the third-order lens was removed in 1969, and that it was returned to the lighthouse in 2013 after a federal court settlement. The county blog says the original lens was replaced with rotating DCB-224 aero beacons in 1950, and that the original beacon's lens is on display at the Huron City Museum. The sources do not agree.[^1][^3]

The property passed out of federal hands in stages. LighthouseFriends says the grounds were sold to Huron County in 1958 for a park, and that the light itself was transferred from the Bureau of Land Management to Huron County in June 2003.[^1] The county blog says the keeper's house and the land were made part of the county park system in 2003.[^3] A volunteer clean-up in 2002 led to the formation of the Pointe aux Barques Lighthouse Society, and a new cedar roof and brick chimneys were added to the keeper's dwelling in 2005.[^1]

## Today

The Maritime Museum's 2026 season page says the museum and gift shop are open Wednesday through Sunday, 10 a.m. to 5 p.m., in season. From May 2026 the tower is open for self-guided climbs, and the life-saving station is open on certain dates. Combined admission for the museum and tower is $8 for adults.[^2] A 2014 county blog post says the light station "is not open to the general public."[^3] LighthouseFriends' visitor notes say the tower is open only on select dates.[^1] Check the current season page before going.
