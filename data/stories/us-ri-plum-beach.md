Plum Beach Light stands in the water off the eastern edge of Plum Beach Shoal, in the West Passage of Narragansett Bay off North Kingstown, Rhode Island, about 250 yards (230 m) north of the Jamestown Bridge.[^1][^12] It is a cast-iron tower on a caisson foundation, built to warn steamers away from a shoal that ships had grounded on in fog.[^1][^2][^3] The first permanent light was shown on 1 July 1899.[^9] The Coast Guard put it out in 1941, when the bridge made it unnecessary.[^2][^3] In 2003 a nonprofit that owns the tower relit it.[^3][^14]

## The place

According to LighthouseFriends, captains trying to avoid Dutch Island in heavy fog steered too far west and ran onto Plum Beach Shoal or other obstructions.[^3] The nomination says the tower was built "in response to a number of steamers which grounded on Plum Beach Shoal in trying to avoid Dutch Island while travelling up the Bay."[^2]

LighthouseFriends names one of those steamers, the *Pequot*, which ran aground on Bonnet Rock in June 1892, in a thick fog, while steering around Dutch Island.[^3] The Lighthouse Board cited the same grounding in its report: "there is extreme danger of grounding on Plum Beach, as is shown by the recent grounding of the steamer Pequot."[^4]

LighthouseFriends says the masters of the large steamers that ran between Providence and New York asked for a fog signal near the shoal for many years.[^3] The Board recommended a light and fog signal there and put the cost at no more than $60,000.[^5]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated money for the light in 1895.[^6] The Board's report quotes the act: "By the act approved March 2, 1895, an appropriation of $20,000 was made for establishing a light and fog-signal station at this point."[^6] The nomination gives the 1895 sum as $10,000.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives $20,000 for 1895 and $40,000 for 1896.[^3] Where the sources differ, the Board's figure is given here.[^6]

The foundation went down by a pneumatic method.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the wooden caisson was set on the bottom and its airtight working chamber was pumped dry.[^3] Workers inside cleared out debris while the caisson settled, which let more sections of the iron foundation cylinder be added.[^3] The Board's report says the caisson was "completed and launched August 19, 1896."[^7] LighthouseFriends names the contractor as the I.H. Hathaway Company of Philadelphia, and says a layer of quicksand lay just below the planned depth, so the iron had to go ten feet (3 m) deeper than planned.[^3] The Board reported that "Unexpected difficulties occurred in laying the foundation." The station could not be completed for the original appropriation, and the Board estimated that the work could be finished for not exceeding $9,000 and recommended that amount.[^7] Congress allocated $9,000 in 1898.[^3][^15]

Meanwhile the station showed a temporary light.[^7] The Board reported that "A temporary red lantern light was on February 1, 1897, established on the completed foundation cylinder," and that on June 1, 1897, a fog-bell signal, a 1,028-pound bell "struck by machinery," was also established.[^7]

The weather was the other problem. In a letter of 3 December 1898, the contractor asked the Board for a temporary halt to construction and wrote: "Plum beach, Narragansett Bay is the stormiest place we ever worked."[^3] Work ceased shortly after and resumed in the spring.[^3] The tower was finished in May 1899,[^8][^3] and on 1 July 1899 a fourth-order light was first shown.[^9][^3] The Board's report of 1899 says the light flashed white every five seconds, and that the cylinder was painted black and the superstructure white below and brown above, "surmounted by a black lantern."[^8]

The lens was a stopgap too. The Board's 1899 report says: "A temporary lens apparatus has been installed, which was on hand at the depot, and should be replaced." It estimated that a replacement apparatus would cost $857.[^8] A local history site says a fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed and first lit on 1 July 1899.[^15] The USLHS record gives a fourth-order Fresnel lens as the original optic, installed in 1897.[^12] The sources do not say when the permanent lens arrived. The nomination describes the interior: "a kitchen and living area were in the first floor; a bedroom above; and a tool room at the fourth level."[^1]

## The keepers

Joseph L. Eaton was appointed the first keeper on 5 June 1897, LighthouseFriends says, and served until the light was commissioned on 1 July 1899.[^3][^12] Judson G. Allen then came over from Whale Rock Light to take charge.[^3] The USLHS roster has Allen as principal keeper from 1899 to 1904, after which George Ehrhardt kept the light to 1911 and George A. Troy to 1913.[^12]

Charles L. Ormsby kept the light from 1913 to 1935.[^12] LighthouseFriends says he served more than twenty years there, longer than any other keeper.[^3]

The assistants changed far more often.[^12] The roster prints twenty-three first-assistant entries, one name twice for 1910, and sixteen of the entries last a year or less by the roster's years.[^12]

In October 1916 Ormsby came back from a shore furlough and found his assistant, John H. Boldt, dead on the kitchen floor.[^3] LighthouseFriends says Boldt was fifty-three, had been appointed that January, and apparently died of natural causes. Mariners had reported that the light burned less brightly than usual the night before.[^3] The Rhode Island history site says it was believed Boldt died of natural causes on 23 October.[^15]

Reuben W. Phillips was principal keeper from 1935 to 1939, and John O. Ganze was first assistant from 1935 to 1941.[^12]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The winter of 1918 froze the bay.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the shifting ice caused a one-inch crack that circled the entire lighthouse, two feet (0.6 m) above the low-tide level.[^3] The nomination dates the ice damage to the spring of 1919, when the ice nearly toppled the tower, and says the pile of rocks around the base was laid after it.[^2] The Board reported that placing 1,500 tons of riprap "for the protection of the site" was finished on 22 February 1897.[^7] Bids for riprap work at Plum Beach were rejected as excessive in 1920,[^10] and by 1922 the Service reported riprap at Plum Beach completed.[^11] LighthouseFriends puts the crack repairs and an added 9,000 tons of riprap in 1922.[^3]

The nomination says the 1938 hurricane "nearly destroyed the tower, which was manned during the storm."[^2] LighthouseFriends names the two men as Substitute Keeper Edwin Babcock and Assistant Keeper John Ganze, and says they could not get away from the station before the storm and had to ride it out in the tower.[^3] The Friends of Plum Beach Lighthouse tell it differently. They say Babcock, a substitute keeper, had been visiting Ganze, tried to row back to shore as the storm came on, and returned to the lighthouse.[^13] They call Ganze "the keeper at the time."[^13] Both accounts agree that the two men rode out the storm in the tower. The Friends say they "waited two days to be rescued."[^13]

LighthouseFriends says waves as high as thirty-five feet (11 m) tore open doors and flooded the building, and that the two men retreated to the fourth level and tied themselves to the central pipe that held the weights turning the lens.[^3] The Friends say they lashed themselves to the clockwork of the lantern.[^13] The Rhode Island history site gives a thirty-foot (9 m) wave that tore open the kitchen door, so the sources disagree on the height.[^15]

Five miles away, the storm took Whale Rock Light, another tower of the same design.[^2] The Lighthouse Service's bulletin reports that the assistant keeper there, Walter B. Eberle, was the person at the station at the time and lost his life.[^16]

## After the keepers

After the hurricane the light was "unmanned and automated."[^2] The Coast Guard put the light out on 1 May 1941.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends dates the opening of the Jamestown Bridge to 1940; the nomination gives 1941.[^3][^2] The Coast Guard then asked for bids, "preferably from wreckers," to take the tower down within ninety days. None came in.[^3]

The tower was left to decay. LighthouseFriends describes windows and doors gone, rust on the cast iron, and bird droppings piling up on the decks.[^3] In 1971 and 1972, according to LighthouseFriends, a professor and a graduate student from the University of Rhode Island visited the tower to research pigeons. The floor was covered with up to six inches (15 cm) of guano.[^3] The Rhode Island history site dates a pigeon study to 1974.[^15]

Ownership was the real problem. LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard and the State of Rhode Island "bickered" over the tower for years, with neither wanting responsibility for it.[^3] A man who had been paid to paint the tower in 1973 sued the state in 1984 for $500,000. He said he had contracted histoplasmosis, a rare disease, from the dried guano in the tower.[^3] The case went back and forth between Rhode Island's Superior and Supreme Courts for years. In June 1998 the courts determined that the state owned the tower, and three months later the state paid the painter $42,000.[^3][^15]

In 1988 a private company tried to buy the tower and move it to a condominium complex in Quincy, Massachusetts, where it would become a lighthouse history museum.[^3] When that plan became public, the Friends of Plum Beach Lighthouse formed to keep the tower in place and restore it. The Friends' own history says Shirley Silvia of Portsmouth, Rhode Island, formed the group.[^13] In 1999 the state gave the Friends the deed.[^3][^13]

The restoration began in 2003.[^3] Crews removed more than 50 tons of droppings, fully cleaned the interior, and painted and repaired the outside.[^3] In December 2003 the station was lit again for the first time in sixty-two years, with a solar-powered beacon flashing white every five seconds.[^3] LighthouseFriends calls that the tower's original characteristic.[^3] The Friends sell license plates with the lighthouse on them, and the proceeds go to its upkeep. The license plate money paid for a fresh coat of paint in 2010.[^3] A new, brighter LED beacon went in in 2015, the tower was repainted again in 2022, and in 2025 a grant from a local charitable trust paid for a rotating LED beacon.[^3]

## Today

The Friends of Plum Beach Lighthouse, Inc., a nonprofit, owns the tower.[^14] The tower is closed to visitors.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the best views are from the water, from the Jamestown Bridge, or from the shore at either end of the bridge.[^3] The tower stands in 15 feet (4.6 m) of water on a cement foundation, so reaching it means going by water.[^1] The nomination describes a small landing pier on the east side, but the tower is closed, so do not land on the pier or climb the tower. The nomination says the lamp and lens have been removed; the sources read here do not say where they went.[^1] The Rhode Island history site lists the light's status as a private aid to navigation.[^15]
