Jones Point Light stood on the Virginia bank of the Potomac, at the southern corner of the District of Columbia, where Alexandria meets the river. It was lit in 1856, and it guided ships to Alexandria, Georgetown and Washington until 1926. Beside it stands the District's south cornerstone, set in 1794 to replace the temporary stone placed in 1791 to mark the start of the boundary survey.[^1][^2]

## The place

The light was built against a hazard. The National Park Service says it was "designed as a navigational aid to help ships avoid shifting underwater shoals on the Potomac River."[^2] The National Register nomination says the light "protected Potomac shipping to and from the ports of Alexandria, Georgetown, and Washington from 1856 to 1926."[^1]

The point has a longer history than the light. The surveyor Andrew Ellicott began "surveying the ten mile square on February 12, 1791," and he hired Benjamin Banneker, "a free black astronomer and mathematician from Maryland," to make the astronomical calculations that fixed the square's southern corner at Jones Point.[^1] The Alexandria Masonic Lodge placed a stone there on 15 April 1791, at a ceremony attended by Ellicott and the federal commissioners.[^1]

The lighthouse was one of many built in the 1850s along the navigable rivers of the country. The nomination says that its construction was "doubtless influenced by the presence of the Washington Navy Yard."[^1]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says that in August 1852 Congress appropriated $5,000 to buy land and put up a beacon at Jones Point.[^3] The land came three years later, a tract 30 by 100 feet (9 by 30 m) bought from the Manassas Gap Railroad Company for $501, and it included one of the original District boundary stones.[^3] The Lighthouse Service's 1926 Bulletin dates the purchase to April 1855.[^6]

The sources do not agree on the first year of the light. The nomination dates the building to 1855, placing it beside the south cornerstone, but LighthouseFriends says the light was first lit on 3 May 1856 by its keeper, George L. Deeton.[^1][^3] The National Park Service dates its operation from 1856.[^2]

The lantern held "a fixed fifth order Fresnel lens illuminated by an oil lamp," the nomination says.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the lens a focal plane of thirty-five feet (10.7 m).[^3] The National Park Service says that the light "produced a light beam which could be seen nine miles away."[^2]

The boundary stone stayed in the wall. The nomination says the stone "ceremonially installed in 1791 was temporary and was replaced in 1794 with a permanent stone," and that the permanent stone was covered when the retaining wall was built in 1861 and "was not reexposed until 1912."[^1]

The lamp was first fuelled by whale oil.[^3] LighthouseFriends says that in 1858 the Lighthouse Board let an Alexandria gas company extend its lines to the station.[^3] The nomination says that "By 1866 the lamp had been converted to gas."[^1]

## The keepers

The archive's roster names Benjamin Franklin Greenwood as principal keeper from 1866 to 1906.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the family lived at the lighthouse for forty years.[^3] The National Park Service calls him "the longest serving keeper," and says he "lived with his second wife and 11 of his 14 children in the building at one point."[^2] Greenwood's first wife, Julia Savage, died in 1873, and he then married Margaret Grover.[^3]

Greenwood died in 1906. The roster lists "Margaret Greenwood" as acting principal keeper for that year alone, and Francis E. Wilkins as principal keeper from 1906 to 1918.[^4] LighthouseFriends says Wilkins took charge after Greenwood's death.[^3] The sources do not show how long Margaret Greenwood acted, or whether she held the post at all.

The keepers lived in the house. "Keepers did get to live in the lighthouse for free," the National Park Service says.[^2] The nomination says the keeper lived in the lighthouse building until 1919.[^1] A Park Service photograph caption gives the name "Frank Wilkins" and calls him "the last Jones Point Lighthouse keeper." The caption's Frank is most likely the Francis E. Wilkins on the roster, but no source says so outright.[^2]

## After the keepers

The light changed color in 1900. The Lighthouse Board's 1901 report lists "Color of light changed November 20, 1900" for the Jones Point light-station on the Potomac.[^5] The nomination says the lamp was "reconverted to oil in 1900 and fitted with a red chimney."[^1]

In 1915 the Commissioner of Lighthouses, in its report for fiscal 1915, said that "Jones Point Light Station is of little use on account of changes in shore line at this point."[^7] The report proposed new range lights, after which "Jones Point Light Station and the 5 minor lights above mentioned may then be discontinued."[^7]

The shore was not the only change. The National Park Service says that "In 1918 a massive shipyard was constructed at Jones Point to build ships for WWI," and that the shipyard "obscured the lighthouse's beacon light."[^2] In 1919 the keeper's light gave way to an automatic lamp. The nomination says that "In 1919 an automatic flashing white light of 590 candlepower fueled by acetylene gas replaced the manually tended light."[^1]

The nomination says that "On April 15, 1921, a program marking the 130th anniversary of the District of Columbia was held at the site."[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1921 dates the Commissioner's visit to 13 April 1921, for the same celebration, and says the Service would be glad to cooperate in preserving the boundary stone.[^8] The two dates differ by two days, and both are given here.

The lighthouse went out in 1926. The nomination says that "The beacon was relocated to a tower on the extended east corner of Jones Point in 1926, rendering the lighthouse obsolete."[^1] The Lighthouse Service said the new light was needed because filled land had been built out beyond the original high-water mark.[^6]

The three accounts of the transfer do not agree. The nomination says "an act of Congress transferred it to the Mount Vernon Chapter of the D.A.R."[^1] The Lighthouse Service's 1926 Bulletin says the Department of Commerce "granted a revocable license to the Mount Vernon Chapter," pending "possible legislation" for a permanent transfer.[^6] The National Park Service says the lighthouse "was deeded to the Mount Vernon Chapter of the Daughters of the American Revolution."[^2]

The site had more uses after that. The Army Signal Corps "built a classified communication facility" on the former shipyard in 1936, and the nomination says "The replacement light was removed in the 1940s."[^2][^1] The National Park Service says soldiers used the building for target practice during the Second World War, and that "Vandals repeatedly defaced the building" after the Army reopened the site in 1953.[^2]

The Park Service acquired the area in 1964. The nomination says "The National Park Service acquired the area from the D.A.R. in 1964 as a part of Jones Point Park."[^1] The park opened that year, and the Service says it has been "an area of recreational usage for thousands of people."[^2]

## Today

Jones Point Park is run by the National Park Service. The archive's record gives the owner as the Service and says the site is "Open to Public: Yes."[^4] The National Park Service calls the light "one of the last riverine lighthouses in the country."[^2]

The boundary stone is still there. The 1980 nomination says it "stands today in its apparent original location," in a recess in the retaining wall. The river reaches the edge of the recess at high tide, and occasionally rises into it, and at those times the stone can be seen from land only through a small hole in the concrete cover above it.[^1]

The building's own condition has suffered from weather, tides and vandalism, according to the Park Service.[^2]

The sources do not confirm whether the building's interior is open to visitors; the grounds and the stone are part of the public park.[^2][^4]
