Devils Island Light stands at the south-east end of Devils Island, the outermost island in Halifax Harbour, just offshore from Eastern Passage, Nova Scotia.[^1][^4] A light has shown from the island since 1852.[^1][^2] The present light is a red flash on a small square skeletal tower.[^4] The sources disagree on whether the 1877 wooden tower beside it still shows a light.[^4][^3][^2]

## The place

Devils Island covers about 15 ha (37 acres).[^1] It was granted to Captain John Rous in 1752 and was known for a time as Rous Island. The British called it Wood Island for its trees, and the French called it Île Verte, "Green Island".[^1] LighthouseFriends says the name Devils Island may be a corruption of a Frenchman's name, recorded as Deval, Devol or DeVille, but when the change happened is not clear.[^1]

A day beacon stood on the island before 1849. That year the Nova Scotia lighthouse commissioners recommended a light, and LighthouseFriends quotes their argument that "several valuable vessels have been lost on, and in the vicinity of the Island."[^1]

In 1850 the brig Southampton, carrying flour, ran aground on the south-west point of the island, and all but one of her crew got ashore on lines.[^1] In 1851 merchants, traders and mariners from Halifax petitioned the provincial government for a lighthouse on the island.[^1]

## Building the light

In early 1852 the government voted up to £1,500 for new lights in the province. That year a lighthouse went up on Devils Island at a cost of £283.[^1] The plan was to burn gas made from asphaltum on the island, but the asphaltum could not be obtained, so the light went into service on oil.[^1]

The first keeper, Edward Bowen, lit it in 1852. He had no room for anyone else at the lighthouse and lived on the island alone. In bad weather he could go for weeks without reaching his family at Eastern Passage.[^1] A dwelling for him was built in 1859.[^1]

A report of 1857 describes the lighthouse as painted dark brown, with a fixed red light, and mariners complained about the dimness of the light.[^1] Albertine oil replaced the earlier oil in 1863.[^1]

In 1877 a second tower went up to the east. The old tower was moved 40 m (132 ft) to the north-west, at a cost of $679, and the new wooden octagonal tower was set 160 m (524 ft) east of it.[^1] Jacob Bowser built the new tower under contract for $2,143, and E. Chanteloup of Montreal supplied the iron lantern and lamps for $1,652.[^1] On 15 October 1877 two fixed white lights were shown from the island. Mariners could line up the two towers to avoid Thrum Cap Shoals.[^1]

After the second light was added, the keeper's salary rose from $380 to $500 a year. In 1880 a new lantern and lighting apparatus went on the western tower to make the two lights equal.[^1] In 1892 a wire fence went round the keeper's dwelling and the western tower, to keep cattle from the garden.[^1]

The report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1913 records that the east light's apparatus was improved by a fourth-order dioptric lens.[^8] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society (NSLPS) gives 1922 for the fourth-order lens.[^2]

In 1949 a flashing light went into the eastern tower, and the western light was discontinued.[^1] NSLPS says the original tower was discontinued that year and the light changed to group flashing red, and that in 1959 it changed again to group flashing white.[^2] Russ Rowlett's Lighthouse Directory (Rowlett) gives the end of the western light as 1950 in one list and 1958 in its own entry.[^3]

## The keepers

Edward Bowen kept the light from 1852 to 1861, and Edward Horne was named for 1861. Benjamin Fulker kept it from 1861 to 1886, although NSLPS starts his term in 1863.[^1][^2]

William G. Fulker kept the light from 1886 to 1918. William J. Faulkner followed from 1918 to 1936.[^1][^2] Kenneth A. Faulkner served 1936 to 1942 and again 1944 to 1949, with E. S. Faulkner in between, 1942 to 1944.[^1][^2] Benjamin A. Henneberry (1949 to 1953), Lawrence V. Henneberry (1953 to 1954) and B. C. Messenger (1954) followed. Charles C. McDonald was the last keeper, from 1954 to 1967; NSLPS spells his name MacDonald.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 15 January 1874 the schooner Union was wrecked near Devils Island. Keeper Benjamin Fulker and five other men went out in a wild storm to rescue her crew of two men and a boy. The Minister of Marine and Fisheries sent each of the six a letter of thanks and a cheque for $20.[^1]

In the summer of 1874 the Department gave Fulker a wooden 8.5 m (28-foot) lifeboat. It noted that the island's residents were fishermen who could be called to man it.[^1]

The Department's life-saving station on the island was established in 1885, with a coxswain and a crew of seven. It is listed among the Department's life-saving stations, separate from its lights.[^5] NSLPS dates the lifeboat's stationing on the island to 1885–1940.[^2] In May 1905 Coxswain G. E. DeYoung resigned, and Benjamin H. Hennebery was appointed to succeed him.[^6]

The life-saving section of the report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1910 gives the date only as 26 April. That report covers April 1909 to March 1910, so the fishing schooner Montana went ashore in a gale at Devil's Island on 26 April 1909 and got off again with the lifeboat's help.[^7]

## After the keepers

In 1967 the light was automated, the island became unwatched, and Keeper Charles McDonald left.[^1][^2] All of the station's buildings except the east tower were declared surplus and sold.[^1]

In 1978 the lantern room came out of the 1877 tower. The headless tower stayed in service for several more years. Rowlett says the light was then shown from a short mast on top of it.[^1][^3]

In the 1990s the Coast Guard made extensive repairs to the wooden tower.[^1]

The sources disagree on when the present light was moved. Rowlett says the light was removed from the tower in early 2009 and placed on a 6.5 m (21 ft) square skeletal tower at the island's southern tip. LighthouseFriends dates a square steel tower, erected near where the western tower had stood, to 2011.[^3][^1]

In 2012 a storm flattened the keeper's dwelling, which had been built in the 1930s.[^1] Rowlett describes the house as in ruins.[^3]

## Today

The island and station belong to the Canadian Coast Guard.[^3][^1] It is reached only by boat. The grounds are open, but the tower is closed.[^2][^3][^1]

The present light shows a red flash for 2 seconds in every 6, from a square skeletal tower 6.6 m (22 ft) high at the south-east end of the island.[^4]

The sources also disagree about the 1877 tower. NSLPS says it still stands and operates, Rowlett calls it inactive, and LighthouseFriends says it was decommissioned and left to the elements.[^2][^3][^1]

LighthouseFriends reports drone footage from 2016 that shows some restoration work on the tower. Footage from 2017 shows the enclosure around the external stairs had collapsed and that the tower had lost a good part of its shingles.[^1]
