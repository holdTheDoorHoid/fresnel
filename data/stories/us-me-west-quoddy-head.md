West Quoddy Head Light stands in Lubec, Maine, on the easternmost light station in the United States, a brick tower banded in red and white above the cliffs at the mouth of Quoddy Roads.[^1] Its lamp has been lit since 1808, but for much of its history its fog signal mattered as much as its light.[^2]

## The place

The station sits at the southern entrance to Quoddy Roads, between the mainland and Campobello Island.[^3] Offshore lies Sail Rock, about 500 yards (460 m) out, where Captain Joseph Smith proposed a light and bell in 1837.[^2] In 1806 Lewis Delesdemier of the Port of Passamaquoddy and five other leading citizens petitioned for a lighthouse.[^4] They proposed a site on the mainland, on a bank forty feet above high water, and asked that the tower rise "not less than 75 feet above the ground exclusive of the lantern."[^2]

Fog was the other danger. A later account says that fog shrouded this coast for roughly half the time in summer, and that a reliable fog signal was judged more essential here than a light.[^2]

## Building the light

Sources disagree on when Congress acted. The Keeper's Log of the U.S. Lighthouse Society gives an Act of 10 February 1807 that authorised $5,000 for a lighthouse "at West Passamaquoddy Head."[^4] New England Lighthouses gives 21 April 1806 for the $5,000 appropriation.[^3] Massachusetts ceded 100 acres on the headland, and Benjamin Beal and Duncan W. Thaxter were contracted to build a wooden octagonal tower and a dwelling; the specification called for a pyramid of oak or white pine, covered in shingles and painted white.[^2] The light was in service in 1808, and Thomas Dexter was appointed keeper at $250 a year. The soil would not grow crops, so he had to travel far for supplies, and his pay went up to $300 in 1810.[^2][^4]

The wooden tower did not last. The Keeper's Log says Congress authorised $8,000 on 18 March 1830 to rebuild it, while Lighthouse Friends says Congress approved funds in March 1831; both agree on the result.[^4][^2] The contractor Joseph Berry built a rubblestone tower for $2,350, and it was lit on 1 August 1831.[^2][^3]

The third tower is the one that stands. Lighthouse Friends reports an appropriation of $15,000 on 18 August 1856 for rebuilding the light and fitting it with "proper illuminating apparatus," meaning a fixed third-order Fresnel lens made by L. Sautter of Paris.[^2] That account and New England Lighthouses say the brick tower and its dwelling were finished in 1857; the National Register nomination, the U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive and the Keeper's Log put the tower and the lens in 1858.[^2][^3][^1][^5][^4] The nomination describes a circular brick tower 49 feet (15 m) tall, with the light 83 feet (25 m) above the water, and a 1½-story frame keeper's house.[^1] The red and white bands appear to have been added soon after the tower was built.[^3]

The fog signal has its own history here. A fog gun, a carronade 5 feet long fired with four pounds of powder, was used when the Boston steamer coming from St. John approached; firing began when the steamer's whistle was heard and continued until she answered.[^6] A Congressional Act of 15 May 1820 provided up to $1,000 for a bell, and an Act of 1826 gave the keeper $60 a year extra for ringing it.[^4] In 1837 a Captain Smith counted four different bells at the station, none of them satisfactory.[^2] The Collector of Lubec had written in 1817 that "Some years since a fog bell was put up on West Quoddy head," so the 1820 act may not mark the first bell.[^4]

Steam came later. The nomination says a steam-operated horn replaced the old bell in 1869, and that West Quoddy was one of the first two stations in the country so equipped.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates a steam whistle to 1868, after a Daboll trumpet in 1866, and adds a duplicate whistle in 1877; New England Lighthouses says a Daboll trumpet came in 1869.[^2][^3] A brick fog signal building followed in 1887 and an oil house in 1892.[^5] The Light-House Board's annual reports counted the whistle's work: some 1,266 hours in the year ended June 1895, and some 1,232 hours in the year ended June 1901.[^7][^8]

## The keepers

Peter Godfrey kept the light from 1813 until 1839, twenty-six years.[^5][^2] The War of 1812 complicated the job: according to New England Lighthouses, British troops claimed control of the station, an officer promised that the British would pay him, and the pay did not come.[^3] His son Alfred followed him. Questioned in the early 1840s for I. W. P. Lewis's examination of the lighthouse establishment, Alfred Godfrey reported that "My leisure time is occupied in boat building. I sometimes pilot vessels into Eastport, when no other pilot is at hand." He described a six-room dwelling that leaked, and a water supply from "a spring about 200 yards from the house."[^3][^2]

An assistant keeper was added in the 1850s.[^3] In 1899 the Light-House Board reported that the dwelling had been rearranged for two families, with a four-room addition and the barn moved farther off.[^9]

Ephraim N. Johnson, a native of Roque Bluffs, arrived as assistant in 1900 or 1901 and became head keeper in 1905, his pay rising from $480 to $660 a year.[^3][^5] The USLHS roster lists him as head keeper until 1928; Lighthouse Friends says he retired in 1930; New England Lighthouses captions him as in charge from 1901 to 1931.[^5][^2][^3] His granddaughter Gwen Wasson remembered that the family kept chickens and sometimes pigs, and that "we never lacked for food."[^3] A girl who visited in the early 1900s recalled a fog horn that sounded for 56 straight hours.[^3]

Eugene Larrabee served at Petit Manan, rose to head keeper there by 1911, left the service in 1914 and returned as assistant at West Quoddy Head; he became head keeper in 1928 and stayed until 1940.[^2][^5] Howard "Bob" Gray, assistant from 1934 and head keeper from 1940 on the USLHS roster, was the last civilian keeper and then served under the Coast Guard, which took over the lighthouses in 1939. He was painted for the cover of the Saturday Evening Post of 22 September 1945.[^5][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Alfred Godfrey wrote that "Wrecks occur upon the Sail rock as often as once a year," and that on one occasion two lives were lost.[^2]

On 19 August 1883 the steam yacht Ideal, bound from New York to Eastport on a pleasure cruise, struck Sail Rock in thick fog. The five passengers, including her owner, and the crew of ten all got ashore in their own boats; the Quoddy Head life-saving crew, who were off duty for the summer, were told about an hour later and worked at landing the yacht's movable property until after midnight and again from daybreak; on the 22nd the vessel was abandoned as a complete wreck.[^10]

On 6 July 1887 the schooner Ontario of Calais, running for Quoddy Bay in a strong south wind and dense fog, lost her jibs. The report of the Life-Saving Service says that "the bearing of the fog-whistle at West Quoddy Head was lost," and the schooner struck the rocks about 400 yards south of the life-saving station. Her crew of four got ashore.[^11] On 28 December 1887, in a gale and snow, the captain of the schooner M. A. Baston mistook the light on West Quoddy Head for East Quoddy Head Light on Campobello and ran into Quoddy Bay; the life-saving keeper took his surf-boat out to her.[^12]

Another story, from a grandson's recollection published in Down East and retold by New England Lighthouses, places a two-masted ship aground in thick fog about a mile south of the lighthouse in 1929; Larrabee ran down the shore with rope and a line was got aboard, and the crew climbed to the rocks one at a time before the ship slid off and sank.[^3] That account calls Larrabee the assistant keeper, where the USLHS roster lists him as head keeper from 1928.[^5]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service planned a radiobeacon at West Quoddy Head in 1932, to be synchronized with Mount Desert Rock and the Portland lightship.[^13] The light was electrified in 1934, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^2] The Coast Guard's crews ran the station until 1988, when it was automated; its last keeper, Malcolm Rouse, was transferred to Owls Head.[^5][^2] Rouse remembered that in his first summer "we had 31 straight days of fog."[^2]

One account says the property passed to Maine's state park system in 1986; Lighthouse Friends says ownership went to the Bureau of Parks and Land under the Maine Lights Program in 1998.[^4][^2] Volunteers of the West Quoddy Head Light Keepers Association have run a visitor center in the keeper's dwelling since 2002.[^2]

## Today

The light is still active and maintained by the Coast Guard; a Sealite LED beacon shines through the 1858 Fresnel lens, flashing 2 seconds on, 2 off, 2 on and 9 off.[^14] The tower carries eight red and seven white stripes.[^14] The station is in a state park on the head, and the keeper's dwelling is a visitor center.[^2] The tower is closed except for scheduled tours, normally on Saturdays in July and August, during the July celebration and on Maine Open Lighthouse Day; the association lists no tower tours for 2026 and the lighthouse closed for the season.[^14][^2]
