North Head Light stands on a rocky bluff some 200 feet (61 m) high at the north side of the mouth of the Columbia River, in Cape Disappointment State Park near Ilwaco, Washington.[^1][^11] The brick tower was completed in 1898 because the older light on the cape could not be seen well from the north.[^1]

## The place

By 1853, the nomination says, many ships had been lost near Cape Disappointment.[^1] The Cape Disappointment light was begun that year "to guard the treacherous entrance to the Columbia River," and the nomination records that the bark Oriole was lost at the river mouth in September of 1853.[^1] The nomination names three major elevations in the vicinity: Cape Disappointment at the south, North Head at the north, and McKenzie Head, an isolated outcrop positioned between them.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress authorized a first-order light at North Head in February 1893, at a cost not exceeding $50,000, but made no appropriation that year.[^2] In August 1894 an act appropriated $25,000 toward it.[^3] The Board's annual report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1897 records that proposals for the metal work and the erection were opened on 3 August 1896, and that contracts were made on 8 and 10 September 1896. The metal work was due at Fort Canby by 23 February 1897. The Board reported that the contractors for the metal work "have made poor progress," and on 10 May 1897 the erection contractor, held up by that delay, was granted an extension of time.[^4]

The keepers' history gives a later start. It says construction began in 1897, that the station was completed on 10 April 1898, and that the light went into service on 16 May 1898. It names George Langford, a Portland contractor, as the builder.[^10] The same history quotes the Board's 1898 report, which said the metal work was delivered on 15 August 1897, 173 days after the time for completion had expired, with a penalty for delay of $4,325. That report is not in the local copy of the Board's reports, so the penalty figure comes second-hand.[^10] A 2014 visitor brochure credits the design to the German-born engineer C. W. Leick.[^12]

The nomination gives the tower as 65 feet (19.8 m) high, built of brickwork covered with stucco.[^1] The keepers' history and the 2014 brochure also give 65 feet.[^10][^12] The Society's record gives 50 feet from the base to the center of the lantern, and describes the tower as dressed stone with a brick lining.[^9] The sources do not reconcile these figures.

The keepers' history says the tower was "designed large enough to hold the First-order Fresnel lens transferred from Cape Disappointment lighthouse." That lens had arrived at Cape Disappointment in 1856, and the nomination says it was moved to North Head in 1898.[^1][^10] The old Cape light then received a fourth-order light and became a harbor beacon.[^1] In its report for the fiscal year 1899 the Board records that four wrought-iron braces were put on the framework of the lens, and that a call-bell system was put in between the dwellings and the tower.[^5]

## The keepers

Alexander K. Pesonen was the first head keeper, from 1898 to 1924.[^9] The keepers' history calls him "Alex K. Pesonen," says he was placed in charge on the day the light went into service, and says he lived at North Head for 26 years.[^10] Andros G. Siniluoto served as second assistant from 1907 to 1922, first assistant until 1924, and then head keeper until 1936.[^9] John G. Granberg was first assistant from 1903 to 1922.[^9] Leonard W. Gabriel served as second assistant (1924 to 1933), first assistant (1933 to 1937) and head keeper (1937 to 1944).[^9]

Mabel E. Bretherton was second assistant from 1905 to 1907. The keepers' history says she was the only woman assigned as a keeper at North Head, and that she had served at Cape Blanco in Oregon before coming to North Head.[^9][^10] The station's roster runs from 1898 to at least 1961, and its last head keeper listed is John C. Webb, from at least 1961.[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The nomination calls North Head "the windiest station on the Pacific Coast," and says gales of up to 160 miles an hour have been recorded there.[^1] The weather station was closed in 1899 and reopened at North Head in August 1902.[^1]

On 29 November 1899 Columbia River light-vessel No. 50, stationed off the river bar, broke away from her station and went ashore near McKenzie Head, between North Head and Cape Disappointment.[^6][^1] The Board's report for the fiscal year 1900 says efforts were being made to get her off the beach.[^6]

On 22 February 1904 the three-masted schooner Frank W. Howe, loaded with railroad ties and bound from Ballard to San Pedro, was wrecked at Seaview, six miles north of the Cape Disappointment station.[^7] The life-saving report says the weather observer at the North Head signal station saw the schooner standing toward the bar, and "called the attention of the keeper by telephone" at the Cape Disappointment station.[^7] The report says all seven people aboard were taken off by the Ilwaco Beach crew. It also records the captain's statement that two of his crew had already been lost before she stranded: one hurled overboard about three miles from land, and one thrown from the rigging when she first touched bottom.[^7]

In April 1932 a wild duck struck one of the storm panes at North Head, breaking the glass and causing slight damage to the lens.[^8]

## After the keepers

A historical marker gives 1961 as the year of automation.[^13] The keepers' history says the original buildings are still on the site, including the keeper's residence and a duplex built for the two assistants.[^10] A 2014 brochure says a fourth-order Fresnel lens, rotated by electricity, replaced the first-order lens in 1937, and that a revolving searchlight replaced the Fresnel lens in 1950.[^12]

The Coast Guard handed the station to Washington State Parks in 2012. The 2014 Washington State Parks brochure, hosted by the National Park Service, gives the transfer date as 22 October 2012.[^12]

## Today

The Society's record lists the Washington State Parks and Recreation Commission as owner and manager, and the lighthouse sits within Cape Disappointment State Park.[^9][^11] The park describes the lighthouse as one of only a few on the state's coast open to the public, and the keepers' residence nearby is available for overnight stays.[^11] The Coast Guard continues to maintain the light, which the brochure calls still functional.[^12] The sources do not say whether the tower may be climbed, so check with the park before going.[^11][^12]
