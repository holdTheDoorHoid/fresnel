Henry Island Lighttower is a white and red octagonal wooden tower on an island about six kilometres off Port Hood, on Cape Breton's west coast.[^1][^3] It has been lit since December 1902, marking the entrance to Port Hood.[^6][^7]

## The place

Henry Island is a triangular island, 1.6 km (a mile) south of Port Hood Island.[^1] Its seaward side is high and rocky.[^5] The best landing area on the island is French Cove, a sandy beach on the landward side, according to a Port Hood local history.[^5] The same history says the island was granted in 1832 to a John Campbell, and that a general store on the island served the fishermen who lived there during the fishing season.[^5] A post office called "Outer Island of Port Hood" opened in August 1877.[^5]

The local history says the island is "believed" to be named after Henry Hood, son of Admiral Samuel Hood.[^5] The Department's 1902 report calls the site "the highest part of Henry island, at the entrance to Port Hood."[^6] The register ties the tower to campaigns to provide aids to navigation in coastal locations, and to a period of growth for Port Hood at the start of an expansion in the early 1900s.[^2]

## Building the light

The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1902 announced the new light.[^6] It described an octagonal wooden tower with sloping sides and a red polygonal iron lantern, 16.2 m (53 ft) high from base to vane.[^6] The light was to be a group-revolving white, elevated 73 m (240 ft) above high water, and it "should be visible 22 miles from all points of approach by water." The report does not say whether those miles are nautical.[^6]

The contract went to Jos. McDonald for $3,489.[^6] The NSLPS list gives the same cost and names the engineer as Joseph MacDonald. The report does not say whether the contractor and the engineer were the same man.[^4][^6] The register's heritage statement says the design came from the Engineering Branch of the Department of Marine and Fisheries.[^3]

The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1903 says the light "was put in operation on December 1, 1902."[^7]

The walls are of plank on plank construction: planks were stacked one on another and interlocked at the corners, without support beams at each corner.[^3][^1] The register's statement calls this unusual, and says it should be maintained.[^3]

One Port Hood local history calls the 1902 tower "a second lighthouse" and gives its builder as Joseph MacDonald.[^5] The Department's index to the 1903 report lists a "Henry Island, new light."[^10] No source read names an earlier light on the island.[^5][^10]

## The keepers

John A. McLennan was appointed the first keeper on 21 July 1903, at $400 a year.[^1] The Department's keepers' list for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1904 prints "McLennan, John Angus" against Henry Island, with the same appointment date and salary.[^8]

Daniel A. McLennan replaced him in 1907 and kept the light until 1944.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says he was suspended that year for handing his lightkeeping duties to someone else.[^1] Charles G. McLennan was appointed in 1944 and kept the light until he retired in 1961.[^1]

Stanley Morrison served from 1961 to 1962, and Lighthouse Friends calls him the last keeper.[^1] The Port Hood local history names a "Charlie MacLennan" as the last keeper instead.[^5]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the illuminant was changed to acetylene in Stanley Morrison's time, so that the light could be automated.[^1] The local history gives 13 June 1962 as the date the light was automated.[^5] The NSLPS list gives only "Automated by 1993."[^4] A non-resident caretaker looked after the light from 1963 to 1967, and after that the Coast Guard made regular inspections.[^1]

The NSLPS list gives a fourth-order dioptric lens installed in 1966, and says the light was electrified in 1985.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says that in 1985 a solar panel was fixed to the gallery railing to charge batteries that fed a new beacon in the lantern room.[^1]

The register's heritage statement, numbered 90-290, recognizes the lighttower for its architecture and its historical associations.[^3] Lighthouse Friends dates the recognition to 1991.[^1]

The island has been privately owned since 1992.[^4][^5] In 2015 the National Trust for Canada and the Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society launched a crowdfunding competition for Nova Scotia lighthouses, and this light placed second in its Ebb Tide category.[^1] The $20,000 prize went to the Henry Island Lighthouse Preservation Society, which re-shingled and stained the tower.[^1]

## Today

The island is privately owned.[^4][^5] The Canadian Coast Guard still maintains the light, which is fully automatic.[^4][^3] The Coast Guard's list gives a focal height of 61.0 m and a nominal range of nine nautical miles, with a white flash once every four seconds.[^9] The 1902 report gave the light's height as 73 m (240 ft) above high water.[^6] The present focal height is 61.0 m (about 200 ft), the figure NSLPS also gives.[^9][^4]

The tower's height is also given differently. The 1902 report gives 16.2 m (53 ft) to the vane, while the Coast Guard list gives the structure as 11.6 m (38 ft), and NSLPS gives 38 ft too.[^6][^9][^4]

The island is reached by boat only.[^4] The island is maintained by the Henry Island Preservation Society, an affiliate of the Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society. Trips can be made by appointment, weather permitting.[^4] The keeper's house, which had been vandalized, has been restored, and the ground around it cleared and grassed.[^4]
