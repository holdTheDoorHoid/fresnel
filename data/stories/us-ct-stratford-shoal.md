Stratford Shoal Light rises from a gravel shoal in open water, seven miles south of Bridgeport, Connecticut: a grey granite house with a lantern tower on its south face, set on a round granite foundation.[^1] It is also called the Middle Ground Light, and for most of its life the government could not quite decide which state it belonged to.[^2]

## The place

The shoal is about three-quarters of a mile long and was covered by less than two feet of water, a danger in the middle of the Sound's shipping lane.[^2] Land for the lighthouse was ceded to the federal government by the State of New York on 11 May 1874, yet official maps today put the station about 1,000 feet (300 m) on the Connecticut side. Lighthouse Service records mostly listed it as a New York station and sometimes as a Connecticut one.[^2]

Marking the shoal took several tries. Around 1820 a couple of spar buoys were placed on its north and south sides, and in 1831 a contract went to a man named Hicks for an iron spindle, though it is not known whether the spindle was ever set.[^2] The National Register nomination records that Congress appropriated $1,000 in 1831 for a beacon on "the middle ground between Stratford and Crane Neck"[^1] A lightship followed. The nomination says one was commissioned in 1837 for $8,500; LighthouseFriends says the Lighthouse Board placed one in 1838.[^1][^2] It had a crew of six and a single anchor, and drifted off position eight days after going into service.[^2] The nomination notes that it had to be removed in 1866 and was replaced in 1868 with a new vessel.[^1] In the winter of 1874-75, a later Light-House Board report recalled, the light-vessels moored at Bartlett Reef, Cornfield Point and Stratford Shoal were all carried from their moorings by ice and set adrift on the Sound.[^4]

## Building the light

In 1872 the Lighthouse Board recommended a lighthouse to replace the lightship and asked for $125,000; $50,000 was provided to begin.[^2] The Third District engineer's 1872 plan for a granite, riprap and concrete foundation was adopted, even though the Board had in March 1873 been considering the new tubular foundations for sites like this one.[^1] The nomination calls Stratford Shoal a late example of the masonry method.[^1]

A ring of riprap went up first, then the foundation, which the nomination says D. V. Howell completed "with great difficulty" in 1874-1876. M. K. Chase supplied the cut stone and Atlantic Steam Engine Works the metalwork.[^1] The finished foundation is 55 feet (17 m) across at the base, 46 feet (14 m) at the top, and rises 18 feet 6 inches (5.6 m) above high water.[^1] The house on it is a square 28 feet (8.5 m) on a side, with the three-storey tower on its south face.[^1] LighthouseFriends counts a living room, kitchen, five bedrooms, a sitting room and a supply room inside.[^2] On the tower's first floor the original red-and-yellow checkered floor tile survives.[^1]

The house went up in 1877 and 1878, and the work nearly came apart in a storm when the construction schooner *Mignonette* broke from her moorings and sank. The nomination says the men and supplies were saved and the men lived in the nearly finished building until work resumed.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the loss to 8 November 1877, with no lives lost.[^2]

The sources do not agree on when the light first shone. LighthouseFriends gives 15 December 1877. The nomination says the light was exhibited for the first time on 15 December 1878, while also reading the stone plaque over the door as dated "AD 1877."[^1][^2] The two accounts are left side by side here. They also differ on the early flash rate. The nomination has the first light flashing every 30 seconds, quickened to 15 seconds in 1879 and to 10 seconds the year after. LighthouseFriends puts the 15-second flash in the winter of 1878-79 and the 10-second one on 15 December 1879.[^1][^2]

Fog signals came in stages. The nomination dates the second-class Daboll trumpet and its caloric engines to 1880, LighthouseFriends to 1881, and both record oil engines in 1897.[^1][^2] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 the trumpet ran about 383 hours.[^6] In 1915 the Bureau of Lighthouses reported duplicate first-class air sirens in place of the old signal, put in service on 15 February 1915 in machinery installed in the dwelling's basement.[^8] A new lens was supplied in the fiscal year ended June 1894, and the nomination adds that a fourth-order flashing lens replaced it in 1905.[^1][^5]

## The keepers

The first head keeper, William McGloin, was an Irish immigrant who had previously been captain of the lightship.[^2][^3] Three men kept the light at a time, and the posts turned over quickly; the archive's roster lists many second assistants for a single year each.[^2][^3] Visitors were urged to bring bundles of recent newspapers to keep the keepers in touch with the mainland.[^2]

A crisis that reached the newspapers came in August 1905. The New Haven *Union* reported that the first assistant, whom it called Merrill Halse (the archive lists Morrell Edson Hulse), had to "defend his own life" against the second assistant, Julius Koster, whom the paper called a "raving maniac." According to the paper Koster attacked Halse with a razor lashed to a long pole, was found trying to take bricks from the tower with a hammer and chisel, and tried to destroy the light with an axe. The paper said Halse fought his way into the room and saved the light, then watched the lamp day and night for seven days, until the head keeper arrived. Koster was bound and sent to Port Jefferson and dismissed from the service.[^7][^3] LighthouseFriends counts five days instead of seven, and adds that later keepers blamed odd happenings at the station on Koster's ghost.[^2]

The archive's roster lists Gilbert L. Rulon as head keeper from 1901 to 1909 and Lewis J. Allen from 1928 to 1940. Harry C. Buede appears first as an assistant from 1938, then as head keeper from 1940 to 1962; LighthouseFriends says he retired in 1962 as officer-in-charge after twenty-four years at the station.[^3][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The station's rescue notices come mostly from the Lighthouse Service Bulletin. On 18 October 1921 keeper Henry R. McCarthy and J. A. Burk, whom the Bulletin calls an additional keeper, lowered a boat to the power boat *Gladys*, adrift with four men aboard. After two hours they brought her under the lee of the station and gave the crew medicine, food, dry clothing and beds; the men, the Bulletin said, had been adrift 18 hours.[^9] On 27 July 1930 first assistant John A. Tatay rescued a swimmer carried off by a strong ebb tide when a fishing party anchored near the station.[^10]

The rescue that earned a commendation from the Secretary of Commerce came on the morning of 10 February 1933. Keeper Lewis J. Allen and assistant Alfred Auger first thought the vessel *Saugatuck* had engine trouble; further watching showed something worse. Chopping away heavy ice from their power boat and its launching gear, they got clear of the rocks and brought the occupants to the lighthouse. The Bulletin says the ten people, some severely frozen, had been without food or heat for 62 hours. A radio electrician who was working at the station altered the radiobeacon transmitter so an emergency call could go out, and an oyster boat came that evening and took the party ashore.[^11] The station had a radiobeacon by the late 1920s.[^13]

## After the keepers

Automation came in stages. LighthouseFriends records a sun relay in 1965 and a 24-inch beacon that fall in place of the Fresnel lens, whose clockwork had to be wound, with trouble over the foghorn and radiobeacon, before the last crew of four Coast Guardsmen left on 1 July 1970.[^2] The nomination says the station has been unoccupied since 1970, that the light went to battery power in 1978, and that much interior detailing has been lost. A new copper roof was laid in 1985.[^1] The lighthouse was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 29 May 1990.[^1]

The Coast Guard declared it excess, and it was offered to public bodies in May 2014. A plan to make it a museum with overnight stays was accepted in 2016 but fell through over bottomlands and uncertainty about which state held it.[^2] The General Services Administration opened a public auction on 20 June 2023 with bidding at $10,000; LighthouseFriends reports seven bidders and a high bid of $261,000 when it ended on 13 September.[^2][^12]

## Today

The lighthouse is reachable only by boat, and the 2023 auction notice said it would remain an active aid to navigation.[^12] Who owns it after the auction is not stated in the sources cited here, and the archive's record lists it as not open to the public.[^3] LighthouseFriends notes it can be seen from a distance from the Bridgeport-Port Jefferson ferry.[^2]
