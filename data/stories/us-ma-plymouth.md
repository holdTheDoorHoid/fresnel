Gurnet Light stands at the tip of Gurnet Point, a sandy peninsula on the north side of the entrance to Plymouth Bay in Plymouth County, Massachusetts. The Massachusetts legislature established a light here in 1768, and for most of the next 156 years it was a pair of lights, not one.[^1] The white wooden tower that remains is described as the oldest freestanding wooden lighthouse in the United States.[^2][^16]

## The place

The Gurnet is a 27-acre peninsula about 3.8 nautical miles north-east of Plymouth Rock.[^2] A 1910 description in the National Register nomination places the station on the north side of the entrance to the harbors of Plymouth, Kingston and Duxbury.[^1] The legislature set up the light in 1768 to guide shipping into those three towns' harbors.[^1] By the 1770s, LighthouseFriends reports, about seventy-five fishing vessels were based in the area.[^2]

The land belonged to John and Hannah Thomas, who rented the site to the colony for five shillings a year.[^2] Out in the approach, the red sector of the modern light covers Mary Ann Rocks, which the 1976 nomination calls "the scene of many wrecks."[^1]

## Building the light

The first lighthouse was a wooden keeper's dwelling about 15 by 30 feet (4.6 by 9 m) with a lantern at each end of its roof. LighthouseFriends dates its completion to September 1768 and its cost to 660 pounds, and says the two lights set Plymouth apart from the single light at Boston.[^2] The 1910 record in the nomination gives 1769 as the year the lights were first built or established, and the keepers' roster starts in 1769, so the exact year the lamps were first shown is not settled.[^1][^3]

That building burned on 2 July 1801. Merchants of Plymouth and Duxbury put up a temporary beacon, and in April 1802 Congress appropriated $2,500 for a new light.[^1] LighthouseFriends adds that Congress also repaid the merchants $270 and paid the Thomas family $120 for the land, and that twin towers 22 feet (6.7 m) tall and 30 feet apart were built in 1803.[^2] The Plymouth County Registry of Deeds dates the Thomases' sale of the lighthouse to the United States to 1802.[^4]

Complaints followed that the two lights ran together into one. Lt. Edward W. Carpender's 1838 report, as LighthouseFriends summarises it, blamed the small distance between them, and the 1842 rebuilding widened the gap by only a foot.[^2] The nomination dates the present tower to 1843: octagonal, pyramidal, of wood, 39 feet (12 m) high, its light 102 feet (31 m) above the water.[^1] LighthouseFriends puts the start of work in 1842.[^2]

By the nomination's 1910 description the station had a first-class Daboll trumpet as its fog signal, and a double dwelling with six rooms for each keeper.[^1] Drinking water came from a 60-foot well, hoisted in two buckets on a roller pulley, and the nearest public road was 4.5 miles away.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates a fog bell to 1907, the trumpet to 1909, and the addition of an assistant keeper to 1909.[^2] The Light-House Board's reports record materials for an oil house landed at the station in fiscal 1893, and repairs to the towers and dwellings, with 140 feet of drain laid, in fiscal 1894.[^12][^13] In 1924 the north-east tower was taken down and the remaining one was given a revolving light.[^1][^2] The Lighthouse Service listed Plymouth (Gurnet) among the Atlantic twin lights whose duplicate light was discontinued that year.[^9]

## The keepers

John Thomas was a surgeon and the station's first keeper. He left for the Continental Army and died of smallpox on 2 June 1776 while serving in Canada.[^2][^18] His widow Hannah kept the light afterwards, and LighthouseFriends and the U.S. Lighthouse Society archive both call her the first woman to keep a lighthouse in America.[^2][^5] The keepers' roster gives her years as 1776 to 1786.[^3] The county's land-records leaflet says 1776 to 1790, and Alexander Hamilton's June 1790 report to President Washington still named "the widow of the late General Thomas" as the officer at Plymouth.[^4][^8] LighthouseFriends says Hannah hired Nathaniel Burgess in 1786 and that John Thomas took over when the United States assumed the light in 1790, at $200 a year, less than at harder stations because the Gurnet offered fishing and good garden soil.[^2] The Project's website names the 1790 appointee as John Thomas, Jr.[^14] The sources do not agree on the handover date, and this story does not settle it.

Joseph "Uncle Joe" Burgess, Nathaniel's son, took charge on 16 October 1812 and stayed about four decades; the roster ends his term in 1852, LighthouseFriends in 1851.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends repeats a story from an 1896 article that Burgess's sixteen-year-old daughter Eunice, forbidden to marry a soldier from the fort, died by a leap from a rock later called Lover's Rock.[^2] The story is unverified: no report is cited for it.

Willis Higgins kept the light from 1903 to 1915.[^3] A visitor in 1904, as LighthouseFriends tells it, found him cleaning a lantern and fishing. When the visitor asked to photograph the keeper in his work clothes, Higgins put on his full uniform first, saying he was an officer of the Lighthouse Establishment.[^2] LighthouseFriends adds that visitors were allowed at the station for two hours a day, except Sundays and holidays.[^2]

Milton Reamy kept the light twice, in 1881-87 and 1915-18 by the roster.[^3] The Lighthouse Service recorded at his death in 1925 that he had joined as an assistant at Duxbury Pier in December 1878 and later served at Plymouth and Minots Ledge until January 1919.[^11]

Frank A. Davis came from Tarpaulin Cove in 1925 with his wife Olive and three children and stayed until 1946.[^2][^3] According to LighthouseFriends, Olive drove the children to school in a Model T, carrying a wrench to tighten belts that slipped in the sand, and raised the storm-warning flags for $11 a month. Their son Frank Arthur held a lobster licence by age nine.[^2]

The roster lists Coast Guard crew under the heading "USCG," mostly from 1959 or 1960 onward.[^3] LighthouseFriends says a new ranch house for the Coast Guard replaced the old keeper's house in 1963, while the 1976 nomination dates the one-storey dwelling, 55 by 35 feet (17 by 11 m), to 1960.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends reports that it was the only place on the peninsula with electricity around the clock, and that neighbours came by for laundry, television and the telephone.[^2] That is why, LighthouseFriends reports, they objected in the 1980s to plans to automate the station.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The nomination reports that during the Revolution a fort stood on the Gurnet, and in an engagement between the fort and the British frigate Niger a wild shot pierced the lighthouse.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls the event reported rather than proved and notes that one local historian disputes it.[^2] The Project Gurnet & Bug Lights website says only that the lighthouse may have been hit.[^14]

In the last week of 1778 the armed brigantine General Arnold, caught in a blizzard, anchored rather than enter the inner harbor without a pilot, dragged, and struck White Flats. Seventy-two of her crew froze to death, and ice kept the keeper from reaching them; a causeway built over the ice rescued the few survivors, according to the nomination.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the toll came from a crew of just over a hundred and that the men died within sight of the light, before a causeway could be built.[^2] The two accounts differ on whether the survivors were reached.

In 1786, LighthouseFriends writes, two seamen from a sloop that struck a sandbar walked seven miles through a snowstorm to the light, and Keeper Burgess warmed them and sent his assistant for the rest of the crew.[^2]

On 27 November 1920 the keeper William L. Anderson Jr. and assistant Gerald M. Reed gave assistance to the officers and crew of the disabled mine sweeper Swan No. 34, which had been trying to float an oil barge that had gone ashore.[^6] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report says they assisted the Coast Guard in taking 62 men off the vessel; LighthouseFriends gives the date as 28 November and the number as 56.[^7][^2] The report adds that the two men helped put out a fire in a nearby cottage.[^7]

In August 1929 Keeper Davis rescued two men stranded on an overturned sailboat in Duxbury Bay, and a month later helped a motor boat that had grounded on Browns Bank with several people aboard.[^10]

## After the keepers

The station was automated on 1 October 1986, when a modern beacon replaced the fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^2] The Massachusetts chapter of the U.S. Lighthouse Society leased the station in 1989, with volunteers living on site.[^2] Project Bug Light, which had cared for Duxbury Pier Light since 1983, was granted a Coast Guard lease in 1999 (the Project and the Duxbury Clipper call it a licence) and became Project Gurnet & Bug Lights in 2000.[^2][^14][^17] The Project had formed in 1983 to save Duxbury Pier Light.[^15] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 8 March 1977.[^14]

Erosion forced a move in 1998. LighthouseFriends and the Project's website say the tower was moved about 140 feet (43 m) back from the eroding bluff; the Plymouth Independent says it was moved back 48 feet (15 m).[^2][^14][^15] The old fog-signal building had been abandoned earlier to erosion.[^2]

In 2023 the General Services Administration declared the station surplus, and the Project applied for it.[^17] On 12 May 2026 the Secretary of the Interior accepted the National Park Service's recommendation that the Project receive it under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, and a deed-transfer ceremony was held on 19 September 2026.[^16]

## Today

Project Gurnet & Bug Lights now owns the lighthouse, keeper's cottage and surrounding property, and the Coast Guard continues to maintain the working light, fog signals and solar equipment.[^15][^16] The tower is closed. Access to the Gurnet is restricted to property owners, with an occasional open house, and the keeper's cottage can be rented; the cottage sleeps up to 14.[^2][^15] The Duxbury Beach Reservation manages access to Gurnet Point, and under federal ownership the Project was generally unable to visit between mid-May and early September because of rules protecting nesting piping plovers.[^15] The fourth-order Fresnel lens is on display at the Hull Lifesaving Museum.[^2]
