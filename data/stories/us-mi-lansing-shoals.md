Lansing Shoals Light Station is a concrete crib 74 feet (23 m) square, standing alone in northern Lake Michigan about 6.3 miles (10 km) off Michigan's Upper Peninsula. It was finished in 1928 to replace a lightship that had marked the same rocks since 1900, and it was one of the last light stations built on the Great Lakes.[^1]

## The place

The shoals are a scatter of rocky reefs at the northern end of a narrow passage between the Straits of Mackinac and the harbours of northern Lake Michigan and Green Bay, with water less than 17 feet (5 m) deep over them.[^1] The Lighthouse Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1900 records that the rock itself "was discovered and marked by a spar buoy September 19, 1899"; the gas buoy was moved on 4 October 1899, replacing the spar buoy.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses later put the traffic past the shoal at "not less than 25 to 30 million tons" a year.[^3]

The first permanent mark was a lightship. By joint resolution of 3 May 1900, Congress allowed the Board to move Light Vessel No. 55 to the shoal, and the report for the following year says she was "substituted for and moored in about the same position as the former Lansing Shoal gas buoy July 10, 1900."[^2][^4] Lighthouse Friends dates the lightship to 1901; the Board's own reports say 1900, and the United States Lighthouse Society's archive also gives 1900 as the year the station was established.[^5][^2][^4][^13] The station's owners place her arrival after the Simmons Reef gas buoy was established on 19 July 1900, nine days later than the Board's 10 July.[^6][^4] Lighthouse Friends quotes the Board's 1908 request for a permanent station, which describes the ship showing two fixed red lantern lights and a steam fog signal while moored in 33 feet (10 m) of water. The Board wrote that she could not be kept on station until the Straits of Mackinac closed with ice, and that in spring she could not be back before the rush of vessels into Lake Michigan began.[^5]

No. 55 was replaced in 1920 by Light Vessel No. 98, which took up her anchorage on 29 September 1920.[^6] The Commissioner's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1922 lists No. 55 among four old light vessels sold for a total of $1,261, "indicating their extremely poor and worn-out condition."[^3]

## Building the light

The Board asked Congress for $250,000 for a permanent station on the shoal in 1908, and the request went unfunded for years.[^5][^6] In 1919 the Bureau of Lighthouses estimated the cost at $304,000, noting that maritime interests were urging "a better light and a more adequate fog-signal, located on a fixed crib"; its 1920 report called the shoal "the most important point on the northerly passage to and from the Straits of Mackinac."[^3] Congress appropriated $90,000 in 1926, and a wharf and storehouse were built at Cozy Point on the north shore to serve the work.[^5][^6]

Four concrete caissons, each weighing about 500 tons, were made at the United States Engineers' plant at Milwaukee.[^9] On 2 July 1927 the Milwaukee Leader reported that the lighthouse vessels *Sumac* and *Hyacinth* had begun what their district superintendent, Capt. Charles H. Hubbard, called "the biggest and longest towing job ever undertaken."[^7] By 5 July the tow was reported off High Island, 15 miles south of the shoal.[^8] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin records that the subfoundation was sunk at the site on 24 July 1927 in 25 feet (7.6 m) of water, the four caissons, each 54 feet long, 20 feet wide and 21 feet deep, forming a hollow square 74 feet outside and 34 feet inside.[^9] The concrete cap was poured in October and November, and when work stopped for the winter on 3 December a temporary light and steam fog signal were left on the unfinished structure; work resumed on 19 April 1928.[^5][^6]

The finished station has a basement inside the crib holding the machinery and four bedrooms for the keepers, a main deck 20 feet (6 m) above the lake with a boat room and a common room, and a three-story tower carrying a third-order Fresnel lens with its focal plane at 69 feet (21 m).[^5][^13] The Bulletin reports that the light, air diaphone, fog signal and radiobeacon were put in commission on 6 October 1928 and that Lightship No. 98 was withdrawn on the same date.[^10] The reported cost was $261,765 as of 31 July 1929 in one account and about $262,000 in another.[^5][^11] A summary of the fiscal year ended June 30, 1929 says the station was "nearly completed" and was placed in commission during that year.[^12]

The lightship's last summer on station brought two collisions. The Bulletin says she was struck by passing steamers on two occasions in thick weather, on 8 July (about $2,500 damage) and on 4 August (about $250), while her fog signal and radiobeacon were working, and that a steamer passed between the lightship and the new crib, on the wrong side of the lightship.[^10] Lighthouse Friends names that steamer as the *Sonora*.[^5]

## The keepers

The lightship had a crew of its own, and the Bulletin names some of them in its notices of appointments. Andrew Gallagher became mate of No. 55 in 1913 in place of Alfred Hanson, and resigned in 1914; T. E. Graham was appointed engineer in 1913 in place of Charles Clausen; and in 1917 Thorwald Berutsen was reinstated as master in place of Thomas Green.[^14][^15][^16]

On the tower, the Society's archive lists a head keeper and two assistants at the start, Paul A. Walters, Ardell E. Pierce and Paul Johnecheck. Johnecheck, listed as second assistant from 1928 to 1942, has the longest single stint on the roster.[^13] Theodore Grosskopf was head keeper from 1933 to 1939 and Guy L. Gordon from 1940 to 1945.[^13] William A. Keller is listed at four ranks in turn: third assistant from 1939, second assistant in 1942, first assistant until 1945, then head keeper from 1945 to 1947.[^13] The station's owners name four keepers on duty in November 1940: Gordon, Keller, Henry Rocheleau (spelled Richileau on their page) and Johnecheck.[^6]

Lighthouse Friends reports that the men were typically taken off in mid-December, with an automated winter light shown until the crew returned in spring.[^5] Civilian keepers gave way to Coast Guard personnel after the Lighthouse Service was merged into the Coast Guard in 1939, and the archive's roster lists Coast Guard crew at the station from at least 1950, with names filling the 1960s and running into the 1970s.[^6][^13]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In early December 1929, spray from the lake froze on the new station in a coating several inches thick that blocked every exit. The crew was shut in for three days, with plenty of supplies, according to Lighthouse Friends and the owners' history.[^5][^6]

The Armistice Day storm of 11 November 1940 flooded the station. The keeper's log, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, records on the morning of 12 November: "What a night we had." and later that day "Have been 27 hours without any fire."[^5] Portholes were broken out and the flood left the machinery out of commission, according to the owners' history and Lighthouse Friends.[^5][^6] In the log as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, the south door of the engine room tore away at 6:30 on the evening of 11 November, with the wind at 80 miles per hour.[^5] Terry Pepper's account gives winds of 126 mph at the shoal, and both his page and the owners' history report seven inches (18 cm) of ice on the structure.[^11][^6] The owners' history says a lighthouse tender reached the station on 14 November.[^6]

On the night of 19 April 1970, waves broke over the tower, smashed portholes on the east side and flooded the engine room and sleeping quarters. The five-man crew had a gas stove and an emergency radio. The cutter *Sundew* stood by for about eight hours and took the crew off on the afternoon of 20 April, and an inspection found no structural damage.[^6]

On 9 September 1993 the 1,000-foot (305 m) freighter *Indiana Harbor* ran into the station at full speed in clear weather. The owners' history agrees on the date and the full-speed approach in clear conditions.[^6] Lighthouse Friends reports a 50-square-foot hole in the ship, repairs costing over $1.9 million, and about $100,000 of damage to the lighthouse.[^5] Pepper's page dates the collision to 1995.[^11]

## After the keepers

In summer 1976 the Coast Guard cutter *Buckthorn* automated the station, and the last crew was taken to Station Charlevoix. The windows were later bricked up and the walls sprayed with gunite.[^6][^11] The third-order lens was replaced by a small solar-powered acrylic optic; the owners' history and Lighthouse Friends date that to 1985, while Pepper's page suggests it happened with automation in 1976.[^6][^5][^11] The owners say the old lens went on exhibit at the Michigan Historical Museum in Lansing.[^6]

The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 6 September 2005.[^6] The Coast Guard declared it excess property in May 2014, no custodian came forward under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, and in an online General Services Administration auction that closed on 9 November 2017 the winning bidder was Lansing Shoals Light Station, Inc.[^6] The quitclaim deed of 24 May 2018 conveyed the building only; the Coast Guard kept the light.[^6]

## Today

The light is still an active aid to navigation, flashing white every ten seconds, and the Coast Guard owns and services it.[^1] The nonprofit Lansing Shoals Light Station, Inc. owns the building and aims to renovate and maintain it; the State of Michigan owns the lake bottom beneath.[^1] The station is six miles from shore, with no utilities on the pier, and everything arrives by boat.[^6] The Society's archive lists it as not open to the public.[^13] It is a working offshore aid on private property; do not land on the crib without arrangement with its owners and the Coast Guard.
