Isles of Shoals Light stands on White Island, the southernmost of the Isles of Shoals, a group of nine islands about six miles off the coast of New Hampshire and Maine.[^1] The first light went up in 1820 or 1821, depending on the source. The archive and LighthouseFriends date the brick tower that stands there today to 1859.[^2][^1]

## The place

The island is mostly bare rock, and the lighthouse stands on its steep southern side, about eighty feet (24 m) above the water.[^1] An inspector who examined the light in 1842 called it highly important as a reference point for leaving Portsmouth harbor, and as a mark for clearing the York ledges.[^1]

Celia Thaxter, who grew up on the island, remembered arriving there at sunset in autumn, when she was "scarcely five years old."[^3] SeacoastHistory gives her age as four in 1839.[^9] The lighthouse "looked down on us like some tall, black-capped giant."[^3]

Thaxter also recorded an islander's memory of an older loss. A woman from Star Island "remembered the wreck of the Sagunto, in 1813," and had picked up embroidered vests and "work-bags" along the shores.[^3]

## Building the light

The light was authorized by an act approved 1 May 1820. Its title gives the purpose: "to authorize the erection of a light-house on one of the isles of shoals near Portsmouth, New Hampshire."[^4]

LighthouseFriends dates the first stone lighthouse and its dwelling to 1820. It gives the tower a height of forty feet (12 m), with a lantern about ten feet (3 m) higher.[^1]

Putnam's history of the lights of the United States describes the tower differently, as a rubble-stone tower eighty-seven feet (27 m) high. He says its revolving light showed "alternate red, blue, and white flashes."[^5]

Nathaniel Hawthorne, who visited in September 1852, wrote: "We ascended into the lantern, which is eighty-seven feet high."[^6]

The heights do not agree, and this history does not settle them. LighthouseFriends' forty feet (12 m) plus a lantern comes to about fifty feet (15 m). Putnam gives the tower eighty-seven feet (27 m), and Hawthorne gives the lantern the same height.[^1][^5][^6]

In 1841 a new lantern and lighting apparatus were fitted at a cost of $3,600, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] Thaxter described the lamps of the old lantern: "The lantern, with its fifteen lamps, ten golden and five red."[^3]

The Lighthouse Society's archive records a second-order Fresnel lens installed in 1855 and a tower established in 1859.[^2] LighthouseFriends also puts the brick tower in 1859.[^1]

Thaxter, writing in 1873, put the change later. The old white lighthouse had been taken away "about ten years ago," and "a new, perpendicular brick tower built in its place."[^3] Counting back from her book gives roughly 1863.[^3] The sources do not agree on the year.

LighthouseFriends says an 1875 report found the old stone dwelling decayed and barely habitable, and that a wood-framed duplex was finished two years later.[^1] Mineral oil replaced lard oil in 1883, the same account says.[^1] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 records: "A brick oil house was built."[^7]

## The keepers

The archive's roster for the station has 166 lines. The first is Clement Jackson, principal keeper from 1820 to 1824.[^2]

Thomas B. Laighton was keeper in the early years. The archive lists him as principal keeper from 1839 to 1841 and again from 1843 to 1849.[^2][^8]

Before he came to the island, LighthouseFriends says Laighton lost the 1839 election for governor of New Hampshire. SeacoastHistory describes him as a New Hampshire senator and former newspaper editor, who took up the isolated post on White Island.[^1][^9]

SeacoastHistory says that in 1839 Laighton left for the island with his wife Eliza, his daughter Celia, then four, and a newborn son, Oscar, and that he never returned.[^9] LighthouseFriends says he had vowed never to set foot on the mainland again, and that the household did most of the actual keeping while he went on attending to business there.[^1] The two accounts do not agree.

Thaxter remembered that, as she grew older, she was allowed to kindle the lamps sometimes herself.[^3] She later became a poet, and SeacoastHistory calls her the "Poet Queen" of the Shoals.[^9]

LighthouseFriends says Laighton left the post in 1849, after building a hotel on Appledore, and moved his family into it.[^1] The archive lists L. H. D. Shepherd as principal keeper from 1849 to 1853.[^2]

Nathaniel Hawthorne visited in September 1852, while Shepherd was keeper, LighthouseFriends says.[^1] In his notebook Hawthorne describes the keeper's garden: "On landing, we found the keeper peeling his harvest of onions, which he had gathered prematurely, because the insects were eating them."[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Thaxter dated one storm: "During a storm in 1839, while living at White Island, we were startled by the heavy booming of guns through the roar of the tempest."[^3] The family watched a large vessel driven toward the coast. Afterward they learned that she was the brig Pocahontas, homeward bound from Spain, and that all her crew were lost.[^3] Thaxter's memoir is the only record of this loss used here.

Thaxter does not date the night when a storm from the southeast carried the covered walk between the tower and the house "down the gorge and dragged out into the raging sea."[^3] Another passage describes a storm from the southeast in which "the sea broke into the windows of that eastern chamber where the walnuts lay."[^3]

LighthouseFriends tells of a March gale, with no year given, when the acting keeper John Downs was at the light. A sailor from a grounded Russian brig was lowered from her bowsprit to reach the keeper's house. Downs, a friend and the keeper then saved the brig's crew by using themselves as an anchor for a line thrown to the vessel.[^1]

Fog signals were a long-running concern.[^1][^10] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1896 records that the signal on White Island "is a bell."[^10] Its report for 1901 says a better signal "would be most useful at White Island, one of the Isles of Shoals," and that the Board had recommended one "in its annual report for 1897" and "repeated it in each annual report since."[^11] That report then says the Board "has now become convinced" that "the most desirable point in the region of the Isles of Shoals for a fog-signal" is Boon Island, and recommends a signal there.[^11]

LighthouseFriends says an automated fog bell went in during 1905, and that an air siren was placed on the island afterward.[^1]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1986, according to the archive.[^2] LighthouseFriends says Coast Guard personnel left the island that year, after a three-month automation process.[^1]

Later the Fresnel lens was replaced with a modern beacon.[^1] The archive lists the current optic as a solar-powered LED unit.[^2]

In 1993 the island passed to the New Hampshire state parks system.[^1] By the start of the new millennium the station had fallen into disrepair.[^1] Students at North Hampton School, the Lighthouse Kids, raised money for the work, and in April 2003 they won a $250,000 Save America's Treasures grant.[^1] In 2005 the tower was repaired and re-stuccoed, and the dwelling got a new roof.[^1]

A storm on 19 April 2007 destroyed the covered walkway, the foghorn and the solar panels.[^1] The archive records the walkway as rebuilt in 2011.[^2] From 2012 the Lighthouse Kids ran a stewardship programme that lets resident volunteers look after the buildings and welcome visitors.[^1]

## Today

The archive lists the owner as the State of New Hampshire and the station as not open to the public.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the state parks division owns it, that the grounds are open while the dwelling and tower are closed, and that cruise boats pass the light.[^1]
