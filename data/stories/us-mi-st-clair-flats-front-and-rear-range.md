Two brick range towers stand in Lake St. Clair off Harsens Island, Michigan. LighthouseFriends puts them 0.7 miles west of the island's southern tip; the volunteer group's history places them just off its southeastern tip.[^1][^2] They were built to guide ships through the South Channel of the St. Clair River delta, were first lit in 1859, and were discontinued in 1934. A volunteer group now owns them and works to keep them standing.[^1][^2]

## The place

The delta was a bottleneck. Lieutenant G. J. Pendergrast, who examined the lakes for lighthouse sites in 1837, described the head of Lake St. Clair as "very shoal, particularly at the mouth of St. Clair river, where extensive flats make out," as LighthouseFriends quotes his report. The flats were, he wrote, "regarded as the most vexatious impediments" that mariners had to meet.[^1] Pendergrast added that vessels had been known to take two and even three weeks to get over them.[^1] LighthouseFriends adds that ships which did not dare to enter the river at night had to anchor and wait for daylight in what is still called Anchor Bay.[^1]

The river reached the lake through seven main mouths, and the range was built to mark the South Channel, the one most used.[^1] Congress paid for the foundations in stages: $10,000 in 1853 "for making the foundation of two light-houses," and $20,000 more in August 1854.[^1] A new canal was cut across the flats, and its own range lights were put in operation in 1871. The older range stayed in use, and it is a separate station from the canal lights.[^1]

## Building the light

The towers were built between 1855 and 1859, the Save Our South Channel Lights organization says.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives 1 November 1859 as the date they were first put in operation.[^1] The rear tower was built of yellow brick on a pier, with a fourth-order fixed white light 37 feet (11 m) above the pier. LighthouseFriends calls it cylindrical, but the Society's record gives its shape as conical.[^1][^3] The front tower, 1,000 feet (305 m) southwest of it, also had yellow brick and carried a sixth-order Fresnel lens.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society record gives the original fourth-order lens as installed in 1859.[^3]

After the lights were completed, the engineer in charge of their construction noted that there was not enough money left for a suspension footbridge between them, which "would enable the keeper to perform his duties more regularly in stormy weather."[^1]

The structures settled early. In 1867 the Lighthouse Board said the timber crib under the front light had settled some, and by 1869 both towers were "slightly cracked" and the dwelling's plaster was coming off.[^1] A survey in 1873 found the front tower out of plumb. Congress allocated $10,000 in June 1874 for "rebuilding the crib and tower at the Saint Clair Flats lightstation," and LighthouseFriends says the crib and front tower were rebuilt that year.[^1] The Save Our South Channel Lights history says the front light "began to lean in 1875" and was then dismantled and rebuilt, so the two accounts disagree.[^2] In 1889 the rear crib was repaired.[^1] In 1899 the Board reported materials delivered to repair the keeper's dwelling and rebuild the boathouse, and said the rear crib's superstructure had been rebuilt from the first course of timber above the water line. The front beacon was repointed.[^4][^1]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists the head keepers and assistants from 1859 to 1907. The first head keeper was Michael Jackson, from 1859 to 1861.[^3] Stephen A. Warner rose through the ranks: he was an assistant in 1869 to 1872 and again in 1876 to 1878, and head keeper from 1878 to 1895.[^3]

Caroline M. Warner was first assistant from 1882 to 1892, the only woman the roster names on the station.[^3] The roster also has four other people named Warner, between 1865 and 1895. The roster does not say how they were related.[^3]

Jabez Geer was an assistant in 1872 to 1873 and head keeper from 1873 to 1878.[^3] The roster's last head keeper, George Sherman, served from 1905 to 1907, and the last first assistant named was Henry Blanchard, in 1907.[^3]

## After the keepers

The range was discontinued at the close of navigation in 1907.[^1][^3] It was re-established on 27 August 1915, with the front light showing a white flash every second and the rear light flashing alternately.[^1] A list in the Commissioner of Lighthouses' annual report volume covering 1915 to 1922 includes "St. Clair Flats Range, Mich. (2 lights)."[^5] By 1921, LighthouseFriends says, only small craft and tugs towing rafts used the old channel, which had only twelve feet (3.7 m) of water.[^1]

The lights were discontinued again in 1934. LighthouseFriends says the dwelling at the rear light was torn down around that time; the volunteer group's history dates its demolition to the early 1930s.[^1][^2] A volunteer, Chuck Brockman, helped form Save Our South Channel Lights in 1989 to protect and restore the towers.[^1] A temporary seawall and limestone fill went round the front tower in 1990, and a permanent seawall was finished in 1996.[^1][^2] In 2005 the organization awarded a $599,000 contract to build a new foundation around the rear tower and rehabilitate it.[^1]

## Today

Save Our South Channel Lights owns the lights, and the towers are closed. LighthouseFriends says they are best seen from the water, and that a distant view is also possible from Harsens Island.[^1] The organization's history says the front tower is again leaning and in very bad condition from erosion at its base, and that the rear light also needs major foundation work.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that the front tower is once again an active aid to navigation, showing a white flash every four seconds.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record, which describes the rear range, does not list it as an active aid.[^3] The lights are on the National Register of Historic Places, according to the organization.[^2]

Do not climb or land on the structures. To see them from close up, go by water, and ask the organization about arranging a trip.[^1][^2]
