Shaganash Island Light is a square-tapered wooden tower, 9.4 m (30.5 ft) tall, on a low rock called Island No. 10, on the north shore of Lake Superior in Ontario. It stands just west of Shaganash Island, about 55 km from Thunder Bay.[^1][^3][^4]

## The place

Vessels running along the north shore could stay out in the lake, or take Shaganash Channel, which Lighthouse Friends describes as "an inside route somewhat protected by a string of islands."[^1] The Department of Marine built the light on Island No. 10 to guide mariners along that channel.[^1] The Canadian Register of Historic Places says the tower was built to serve both as a coastal light for Thunder Bay's harbour and as a range light "identifying safe passage through a rock-strewn channel."[^4] The Register adds that the light supported the silver mining, pulpwood and fishing industries of the region.[^4]

## Building the light

The lighthouse went up in 1910.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says it was built by day labour "at a cost of $2,484.90," and that it exhibited a fixed white light from a fifth-order lens.[^1] The Department's report for fiscal 1910-11 says that "A lighthouse has been erected on the western end of island" No. 10, and that its illuminating apparatus "is dioptric of the 5th order."[^7]

Lighthouse Friends notes that the tower was not on Shaganash Island itself, though it "was known as Shaganash Island Lighthouse."[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends says that Alexander Clark and F. S. Oatt, the first two keepers, "minded the light for only a few months before William I. Fairall was placed in charge of the station in 1912."[^1] The Department's keeper table for fiscal 1910-11 also lists an Alex. Clark against Shaganash, which agrees with the first keeper's name.[^7]

In June 1912 the keeper was given a hand-operated foghorn to answer steamers in thick weather.[^1] In 1912 Raymond Smiley Spears set off "in a rowboat" on a trip that would take him around the shores of all the Great Lakes. He saw the light from a distance one evening before he called on Fairall. He wrote, as Lighthouse Friends quotes him, that he could not recall anything that "seemed so lonesome as that light there on Great Shaganash Island."[^1]

Fairall was born on the Isle of Wight. He "died at the lighthouse on April 10, 1921," at the age of seventy-six.[^1] Lighthouse Friends then names George G. Weeks (1921 to 1923) and Andrew McGuinness (1923 to 1926), then lists other keepers, down to Peter Belanger, who is listed from 1943 with no end date.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The original lighthouse burned down on 30 November 1921.[^8] The Department's report for the fiscal year 1921-22 records: "On November 30 Shaganash lighthouse was burned down."[^8] Lighthouse Friends gives the same date.[^1] The Canadian Lighthouses of Lake Superior group also says the first lighthouse and tower "burnt down in 1921."[^5] None of these sources says how the fire started.

A U.S. Lake Survey notice of 23 March 1922 told mariners that a "temporary fixed white oil light" would be shown from a lantern on a pole until further notice, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it.[^1] The Department's report for 1922 to 1923 lists the works as "Erection of temporary light and shed replacing lighthouse destroyed by fire."[^9]

## After the keepers

The Department built a replacement in 1923.[^10] Its report for 1923 to 1924 says "work was started on a lighthouse and dwelling on Shaganash island," and that the work was "completed on November 7."[^10] Lighthouse Friends says the Department chose "to build a separate square, pyramidal tower" with a rectangular keeper's dwelling a short distance away, rather than one combined building like the one that burned.[^1]

The sources disagree on the date of the present tower. The Register says "Built in 1922," and the federal designation release says "The current lighthouse was built in 1922."[^4][^2] The Department's report puts the building work between 10 July and 7 November 1923.[^10]

The light stopped being staffed in 1954.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the hand foghorn was discontinued that year and the light changed from fixed to flashing white.[^1] The Canadian Lighthouses of Lake Superior group says the light "was automated in 1954."[^5]

## Today

The Coast Guard list gives the light as flashing white every four seconds, 11.0 m above the water, with a range of 5 nautical miles. It calls the light a "Private aid" that runs seasonally.[^6]

The Canadian Lighthouses of Lake Superior group now owns the light. It says the lighthouse property was "transferred from the Government of Canada as a surplus light."[^5] Parks Canada announced the designation of the light as a heritage lighthouse under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act on 14 December 2023.[^2] The light lies within the Lake Superior National Marine Conservation Area.[^4]

The light is best seen by boat.[^1] The group calls it "a popular destination for recreational kayakers," and says the tower's interior "is available to tour only when CLLS staff or volunteers are present."[^5] Visitors should go by boat and should not climb the tower unless the group's staff or volunteers are there to open it.
