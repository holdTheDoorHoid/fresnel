Alligator Reef Light is an iron skeleton tower standing in open water on the outer edge of the Florida reef, 3.5 miles (5.6 km) south of Upper Matecumbe Key at Islamorada, in Monroe County.[^1] It was lit in 1873 on a reef named for a Navy schooner that was lost there, and the keepers lived in a house built into the frame of the tower itself.[^2]

## The place

The reef takes its name from the USS *Alligator*, a Navy schooner that ran aground on it. LighthouseFriends dates the loss to November 1821 and says the crew set the wreck afire so that pirates could not claim her; the Associated Press gives 1822.[^2][^3]

The first mark was an iron daymarker raised in 1852 under Lieutenant James Totten of the Army Coast Survey, one of fifteen put up along the reefs.[^2] In 1855 the seventh district's inspector, M. Carrington Watkins, told the Light-House Board that four vessels had been wrecked on or near Alligator Reef in the previous four months, as LighthouseFriends quotes his reply.[^2] Congress did not fund a light that year, and the Board renewed its request in 1867.[^2] By its 1869 argument, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, the reef sat roughly midway between the Carysfort Reef and Sombrero Key lights, 61 nautical miles apart, leaving "an unlighted space of upwards of thirty miles" for a navigator.[^2] The Lighthouse Service's own 1937 history says that after the Civil War several years went to repairs, and that it was not until 1873 that another important light was provided on the Florida Reefs, at Alligator Reef.[^4]

## Building the light

Congress voted $100,000 on 15 July 1870 to begin work. Indian Key, four miles from the reef, became the base, with a wharf, storage, lodging and a cistern for the labourers.[^2] The ironwork was forged by Paulding Kemble of Cold Spring, New York, and shipped to Indian Key.[^2]

The reef was levelled and nine cast-iron disks were laid on the coral: eight at the corners of an octagon fifty feet across and one at its centre. The piles that pass through them were twenty-six feet long and twelve inches thick, driven by a steam pile driver whose hammer weighed two thousand pounds; each blow sank a pile about an inch.[^2] A one-story square dwelling was built on a platform high above the water, and a spiral stair joined it to the lantern.[^2]

The light went into service on 25 November 1873 after $185,000 had been spent.[^2] The Light-House Board's 1894 report gives the same cost, and describes the tower as standing in five feet of water within a quarter of a mile of deep water.[^5] Sources differ on its height: the Board's report calls the frame 135½ feet high, while the National Register nomination calls the lighthouse 148 feet tall, with the light itself 136 feet above the water.[^5][^1]

The first lens was a revolving first-order Fresnel, showing flashes five seconds apart with every sixth one red; in July 1880 ruby panels were added so that every third flash was red.[^2] Red sectors were fitted and the light's character changed on 20 September 1891, so that the red fell on the nearby dangerous water.[^6][^5] By 1931 the light showed a group of four white flashes every 15 seconds, the fourth step in the numbered series that ran along the Florida Reefs, one flash at Hillsboro Inlet up to five at Sombrero Key.[^7][^4] New mineral-oil lamps replaced lard oil in 1884, and an oil room under the dwelling held a year's supply, about 2,206 gallons.[^2][^5]

## The keepers

The station had a head keeper and two assistants.[^2] In 1894 the head keeper was paid $820 a year, the first assistant $510 and the second $490.[^5] The US Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the keeper registers, lists George R. Billberry as head keeper from 1873 to 1888, then Charles A. Roberts, Miguel Fabal (1888-1890), Edgar J. Russell (1890-1907), William T. Stran (1907-1917), Thomas M. Kelly (1917-1932) and Jones A. Pervis (1933-1943).[^8] Assistants came and went much faster: the archive shows many serving only months in a post.[^8]

Fabal's career was traced by the Lighthouse Service Bulletin when he died on 8 November 1924. He joined in November 1882 as second assistant keeper, resigned as keeper of Alligator Reef on 31 December 1890, and came back in 1892 as first assistant at Sombrero Key, where he was promoted to keeper in 1903 and stayed until he retired on 1 October 1919.[^9][^8]

Management passed from the Lighthouse Service to the Coast Guard in 1939.[^1] The archive lists Coast Guard crew here into the 1960s, among them Richard Gooravin (1950-1953).[^8] LighthouseFriends quotes Gooravin's recollection of the 1950s: the water around the tower was usually calm and clear, with barracudas cruising near the structure, and a keeper with a spear could take lobster or snapper from the back porch.[^2]

In 1929 the Bulletin printed a short remark from the keeper at Alligator Reef, whom it does not name, on the radio the Service was then supplying to remote stations. He wrote that it was "grand to enjoy church sermons and all the good lectures and grand music in the lone hours of the night."[^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 1 August 1918 the first assistant keeper, Richard C. Roberts, took the station boat to a disabled aeroplane that had fallen into the water about 10 miles from the light. The Bulletin says he towed it some 4 miles toward land before a naval boat took over, and that he "displayed great courage in diving into the shark-infested waters" in an effort to reach two men reported to be inside or on top of the machine.[^11][^8] The Bulletin does not say what became of the two men.[^11]

A hurricane described in the Bulletin's 1926 volume carried away Alligator Reef's entire landing platform. Repairs here, about $7,000, were counted among the largest single items of that storm's damage on the Florida Reefs.[^12]

LighthouseFriends calls the Labor Day hurricane of early September 1935 the most devastating storm to strike the station. The National Register nomination says the decking on the first tier was washed away.[^1] LighthouseFriends prints the report of the keeper, Jones A. Pervis. He wrote that the barometer fell from a normal 29.98 to as low as 27.35 by 9:30 that night, and that at 6:30 p.m., as he lit the lamp, the lantern glass began to break; the lens "was completely wrecked." The rowboat was washed away and the station launch lost, and he called it the worst hurricane of his eleven years of service.[^2] A temporary lantern arrived on 5 September and the light was burning again that night.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's account, which survives only as a badly damaged scan, says a vessel left Key West for Alligator Reef after the storm, where the lighthouse had been reported damaged and the light out of commission.[^13]

## After the keepers

The resident crew left when the light was automated in 1963.[^1][^2] Afterwards the dwelling's partition walls and floors were stripped out, leaving one large room around the stair cylinder.[^1] The first-order lens was removed in 1985 and replaced with a 190-millimetre acrylic lens, which gave way in 1997 to the present VRB-25 rotating beacon and a radar beacon.[^1] The foundation was repaired in 1949 and again in 1961 and 1976, when concrete was placed under disks that erosion had undermined.[^1]

LighthouseFriends dates the end of the old light to 2015, when a thirty-foot tower with an automated light was built nearby to take over the work; the Associated Press says only that the lighthouse stayed lit until about 2013.[^2][^14] The Coast Guard declared the tower excess on 1 February 2019, and in 2021 the Interior Secretary approved its transfer to Friends of the Pool, Inc. under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act.[^2][^3]

## Today

The tower is owned by the Islamorada nonprofit, which took title in late 2021 and now works as Save Alligator Lighthouse.[^14] In October 2023 it switched on a new solar-powered light, ending about ten years of darkness. An engineering study put the work needed to save the structure at about six years and $5 million to $6 million, and some $500,000 had been raised by then, including $215,000 from the Monroe County Tourist Development Council.[^14] The group's own site gives 2026 as the forecast for finishing the project.[^15] The group also runs an annual eight-mile swim to the tower and back that raises money for collegiate scholarships.[^3]

The light stands offshore and is reached only by boat. The US Lighthouse Society archive lists it as not open to the public, and nothing here invites anyone to climb it. LighthouseFriends says it is the most readily visible of the Florida reef lights from the Overseas Highway near Islamorada, and an Associated Press photograph from September 2021 shows boats with snorkelers anchored around it.[^8][^2][^3]
