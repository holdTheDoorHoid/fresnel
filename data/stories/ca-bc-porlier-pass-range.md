Porlier Pass Range Lights are a pair of range towers, one on Race Point and one on Virago Point at the north end of Galiano Island, that line ships up through a short, tide-swept pass between Galiano and Valdes islands in British Columbia's Southern Gulf Islands.[^1][^2] The Department of Marine's annual report says the range light buildings were put in operation on 15 November 1902.[^3] Frank Fagan Allison, known as "Sticks," kept them from 1902 until 1942.[^4][^5][^1] The station was de-staffed in 1996.[^1][^2]

## The place

Porlier Pass is about a mile long, and CoastView calls it "strewn with submerged rocks."[^2] Tidal currents of four to nine knots make overfalls and whirlpools, especially at the northern entrance.[^2] The Department's 1908 report gives the maximum strength of the current in these passes as "five to nine knots," and says traffic through Active and Porlier passes "is largely handled by" tugs that time their trips to slack water.[^6] Lighthouse Friends says mariners often braved the pass to save time, even though the B.C. Coast Pilot warned against it.[^1]

Ladysmith, on the eastern shore of Vancouver Island, was founded in 1898 by James Dunsmuir, owner of the Wellington Colliery Company.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says steamers soon carried its coal through Porlier Pass, and that in 1901 barge ferries loaded with railway cars began regular trips between Vancouver and Ladysmith.[^1]

In late 1901 the superintendent of the Esquimalt and Nanaimo Railway joined the master of the Ladysmith-Vancouver ferry and several pilots and masters in petitioning the Department of Marine for range lights at the north end of Galiano Island.[^2] That November the Department had also set a fairway buoy off the east entrance to the pass.[^7]

More than twenty years later the Department was resurveying the pass. In the fiscal year 1923-24 "a beginning was made for the resurvey of Porlier pass."[^8]

## Building the light

Lighthouse Friends says day labourers, under the supervision of Thomas Tubman, began the work the following year, 1902.[^1] The Department's report, signed in December 1902, says the range light buildings "were put in operation on November 15, 1902."[^3]

The front tower stood on bare rock at Race Point, 15 m (50 ft) back from the water's edge and 2 m (6 ft) above high water.[^3] It was a square wooden building with an octagonal lantern.[^1][^2] Its height from base to the ventilator on the lantern was 7.3 m (24 ft).[^3] The report describes the front light as fixed white and catoptric, and gives its visibility as "9 miles in the line of range."[^3] The rear tower stood on the first point south-west of Race Point, 38 m (125 ft) back from the water's edge, and 366 m (1,200 ft) from the front tower on a bearing of S 5° E.[^3] Lighthouse Friends gives the rear tower as 9.4 m (31 ft) tall, and its light as dioptric.[^1]

The first keeper was not given a house.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says Allison lived in a "twelve-by-sixteen-foot shack" left by the carpenters who built the towers.[^1] The Department authorized a dwelling at the station in 1906.[^10] In 1907 it had a four-roomed cottage for the keeper built by contract, at a price of $725, and a water cistern added by day labour at a cost of $185.50.[^11]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends says James Gaudin, the marine agent at Victoria, planned to hire a local man, Peter Sit-who-latza, at $25 a month. The local Member of Parliament, Ralph Smith, insisted that Allison get the job, and Allison was appointed.[^1] The keepers' table in the Department's report printed in 1910 lists "F. Allison" at "Portier Pass" with an appointment date of 15 November 1902.[^4] Its 1903 list gives the name as "Allison, Frank Fagan,"[^5] and its list for fiscal 1911-12 has "F. F. Allison" at the Porlier pass range.[^15]

Lighthouse Friends says Allison's real name was Francis, and that he was hurt in a mine explosion in 1901. He would have a limp for the rest of his life, and the nickname came from a small boy he befriended in hospital.[^1] Before he was provided a hand fog signal, the same history says, he beat a five-gallon oil can with a stick whenever a steamer's whistle sounded in the pass.[^1]

In 1907 he brought home his new bride, Mathilda Georgeson, a granddaughter of "Scotty" Georgeson, the keeper at Active Pass. Lighthouse Friends says Allison lost her just before the Christmas holiday of 1915.[^1] Lighthouse Friends also says that each Christmas Eve the family prepared two large Christmas puddings and set off for the reserve in a rowboat with two jugs of brown-sugar sauce.[^1]

In 1922 Allison married Elizabeth Gear, a war widow. Lighthouse Friends says the couple separated after 1929, and that the marine agent nearly dismissed him more than once.[^1] He kept the light until 1942, when he turned seventy-six.[^1] Two of his daughters married men who later served at Porlier Pass.[^1] Lighthouse Friends lists nine more keepers from 1941 to 1996, the last of them in post when the station was de-staffed.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The paddle steamer *Del Norte* was lost near Canoe Reef in 1868.[^2] The Underwater Archaeological Society of British Columbia says that she entered the pass in dense fog, that Captain Windsor ordered her to reverse, and that a storm then swept her off a rock and into 10 m (33 ft) of water on 10 November 1868.[^12]

CoastView gives the sinking as 11 November, says a southeast gale sank her in over ten fathoms (about 18 m), and spells the captain's name Winsor.[^2] The two accounts disagree on the date and the depth, and they spell the captain's name differently.[^2][^12]

In 1949 the steel tug *Point Grey* struck Virago Rock. CoastView gives the date as 26 February 1949, says she was visible above water until 1963, and says she now lies in 13 m (43 ft) of water southeast of the rock.[^2] The heritage register describes her as the only surviving steel tug built in British Columbia for service in the province's waters, and says her stern lies intact but upside down.[^13]

In the second half of December 1921, a Canadian Pacific Railway transfer barge went ashore at Porlier Pass. The Pacific Salvage Company's list of its work for the year ended 31 March 1922 records the grounding.[^14] Lighthouse Friends says Allison also rescued people endangered in the pass, though it gives no dates.[^1]

## After the keepers

The front tower was removed in February 1996. A cylindrical fibreglass tower replaced it, and the station was de-staffed two months later.[^1] CoastView says the station was automated in 1996, when the Race Point tower was replaced.[^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard's list describes the front light as a white cylindrical tower on Race Point. It shows a fixed yellow light (listed as "F Y"), with a focal height of 6.8 m, a range of 10 nautical miles, and operates around the clock.[^9] The rear light, on Virago Point, is also listed as fixed yellow, with a focal height of 10.3 m and the same range.[^1][^9] The 1902 report describes fixed white lights, and the sources do not say when the colour changed.[^3]

Lighthouse Friends reports that pieces of the original front tower were put in storage by Alistair Ross, founder of the Galiano Museum, and that plans were drawn for a museum on donated land. It hoped enough money would be raised to build the museum and restore the tower.[^1]

The land around the lights is owned by the Penelakut Tribe. Lighthouse Friends says permission to cross their property should be obtained before anyone visits the lights by land, and that the front light is not easily reached from the rear.[^1]
