At Port Dover, where the Lynn River meets Lake Erie, a small white wooden tower with a red lantern stands near the end of the west pier.[^3][^5] The present tower dates, on the Department of Marine's account, from 1904 to 1905.[^5]

## The place

The village of Port Dover grew up where the Lynn River meets Lake Erie. Its first settlement was called Dover Mills, after the mills on the river, and American troops burned it on 15 May 1814 during the War of 1812. Israel Powell started a new settlement at the river mouth in 1835, but the population grew significantly only after a road was opened to Hamilton.[^1] Norfolk County's heritage office says the government took over the harbour in 1842 and began improvements, including a lighthouse, soon afterwards.[^2]

Later traffic gave the harbour a second reason for a light. A railway reached Port Dover in 1875, and on 17 August 1895 the first cross-lake rail car ferry arrived from Conneaut, Ohio, with a load of coal for Hamilton.[^1]

## Building the light

The sources do not agree on when the first light was built. The Norfolk County heritage office says about 1843, Lighthouse Friends says 1845, and the Canadian Register of Historic Places says about 1846.[^1][^2][^3] They also differ on its end. Lighthouse Friends gives 5 November 1848, when the tower burned by accident and the keeper hoisted two globe lamps on a pole for the rest of the shipping season.[^1] Norfolk's heritage office says only that it burned in the late 1840s and was rebuilt the following year.[^2] The Register says the replacement went up about 1850 and is the tower still standing.[^3] The Department of Marine's reports, below, describe a tower built on the site in 1904 to 1905.

In its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1904, the Department announced that the front range lighthouse near the outer end of the west pier "is being taken down, to be replaced by a new and improved tower."[^4] The next year's report records the result: a front range lighthouse "built near the outer end of the west pier at Port Dover, on the site of the old lighthouse," an enclosed wooden building, square in plan, with sloping sides, painted white and topped with a red iron lantern.[^5] Lighthouse Friends adds, quoting the Department, a fixed white seventh-order light 10.4 m (34 ft) above the lake, visible 11 miles, burning petroleum vapour under an incandescent mantle, built by day labour for $1,155.87.[^1] While the work went on, a lens lantern was hoisted on a temporary pole 50 feet (15 m) toward the lake.[^1]

The tower was a front range light because it worked with a second, taller light behind the village. In 1897 the Department put up a skeleton steel frame 13.7 m (45 ft) high, painted red, on an unused street. Its fixed red light was first shown on 11 June 1897, and the aim was to line up with the pier light and lead the car ferries between Port Dover and Conneaut along the best channel to the railway wharf. It cost $386.65.[^1] Lighthouse Friends reports that the rear light has since been discontinued.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists the keepers as William Fifield (at least 1849 to 1860), William Carlisle (from 1863), Henry Morgan (at least 1866 to 1888), Crosbie Morgan (1888 to 1891), John A. Innis (1891 to 1894), Andrew Innis (1894 to 1897) and Silas L. Butler (1897 to 1924).[^1] The sources used here give nothing more about any of them than these names and years.

Butler is the one the Department's reports confirm. The 1909 report's table of light-keepers lists Silas L. Butler at Port Dover, appointed 15 June 1897 at a salary of $345 a year.[^6] The Department's lists of keepers name him at Port Dover again in the report for 1905 as well, so he held the post while the old tower was taken down and the present one built (the 1905 list is on p. 252).[^8]

## After the keepers

The range lights were electrified in 1923 and both made fixed red; their colour was changed to green in 1936. A hand-operated foghorn was set up near the outer end of the pier in 1927, and in 1940 a tyfon horn sounding every twenty seconds replaced it, in the front tower.[^1] The Maple Leaf article reports that in 1986 the tower was moved 4.6 m (15 ft) along the pier, after the Coast Guard said it was no longer needed.[^7]

When the federal government declared the lighthouse surplus, two Port Dover women, Sheila Whiteley and Marg Creighton, alerted the community in 2012. The local waterfront preservation association worked up a maintenance plan with Norfolk County, and Fisheries and Oceans approved it with a $25,000 grant.[^7] Lighthouse Friends dates the county's acceptance of ownership to 2014, while the Port Dover Maple Leaf article reproduced by the preservation association says the county took ownership in 2015.[^1][^7] County council declared the lighthouse a heritage landmark in February 2016.[^7]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by Norfolk County, according to the 2016 article; Lighthouse Friends' page still says the Canadian Coast Guard owns it.[^7][^1] Lighthouse Friends describes it as still active, showing a flashing green light.[^1] It is listed in the Canadian Register of Historic Places, which calls it an emblem of Port Dover's maritime character and an important tourist attraction.[^3] Lighthouse Friends reports that the tower is closed while the grounds are open, and that the pier is reached from the harbour parking lot at the end of Harbor Street.[^1]
