The Matane light stands on a cliff just west of the Matane River, on the north shore of the Gaspé Peninsula.[^1] A wooden light was first lit there on 1 October 1873. The tower standing now is a 20 m (67 ft) cylinder put into service on 10 November 1907. The light was switched off in 1951. The tower and keeper's house now belong to the city, and the site holds a museum and a tourist office.[^1][^2][^3][^7]

## The place

At Matane the St. Lawrence is about 29 nautical miles (33 miles in the source) wide, and often foggy in certain seasons. The first light went up on a cliff about 1.6 km (a mile in the source) west of the Matane River, to help light the coast between Pointe-au-Père and Cap-Chat.[^1] A local history page says the government's chief engineer recommended a light here in 1860, and it gives 1869 to 1875 as a time when more than a hundred ships were lost between Quebec and Gaspé.[^4] The page's wreck figure covers the region, not Matane itself. The 1860 recommendation rests on that page alone.

## Building the light

The first tower was wood, attached to a keeper's house. LighthouseFriends gives it as 8.5 m (28 ft) tall, with a focal plane of 20 m (65 ft), and it showed a fixed red light that could be seen for about 9 nautical miles (10 statute miles in the source).[^1] The total cost, including the lantern and lighting apparatus, was $2,860.56.[^1] Keeper François-Xavier Dionne was appointed on 19 September 1873 at $200 a year, and lit the light on 1 October.[^1]

Erosion forced the first move. LighthouseFriends dates it to 1894, when the tower was shifted 30 m (100 ft) inland; Lire la Mer says it was moved in the summer of 1884.[^1][^4]

In 1906 the Department of Marine had a reinforced concrete tower built at Matane, to receive a third-order light that was then waiting at Quebec.[^6] The new light was put into operation on 10 November 1907, when the old light was discontinued. The 1908 report describes the new tower as 20 m (67 ft) from base to vane, painted red, with an iron lantern also painted red.[^7] LighthouseFriends says it was painted white sometime between 1908 and 1917.[^1] LighthouseFriends names the Steel Concrete Company of Montreal as builder, and says the lens came from Cape Norman in Newfoundland.[^1] The Marine report and the Quebec record both call the tower reinforced concrete, but the Quebec record also describes cast-iron panels bolted together and covered with concrete, and Rowlett calls the tower cast iron.[^7][^3][^2]

In 1904 the Commissioner of Lights had Matane, Martin River and Little Metis changed to bright red, "to render them more distinctive."[^8]

## The keepers

In 1877 the keeper complained about the cold, and LighthouseFriends says he had to rent a house in Matane for the winter.[^1] Lire la Mer says Dionne was suspended on 16 May 1879, after several days away from his work.[^5]

From 1879 the keeper was also the telegraph operator, paid $50 a year. From 1884 he ran the semaphore flags as well.[^5] Lire la Mer, quoting a local monograph, names Madame Banville as the woman who ran the tower's telegraph for many years.[^5] Octave Desjardins, the second keeper, was appointed at 48 with a salary of $250, and served until 1897.[^5] LighthouseFriends says that in 1899 Desjardins took away the outbuildings he had put up at his own expense, and his successor, Joseph Banville, had to build his own stable and barn.[^1]

Joseph Banville appears in the Department's keepers' table for 1901.[^9] Lire la Mer says he retired on 1 July 1926, aged 74, after 29 years and five months.[^5] Jules-André-Octave Morin (1926 to 1936) had been a career soldier who fought in the First World War. André Rioux (1936 to 1937) is reported to have been a veteran of the same war.[^5]

Camille McKinnon was the last keeper. LighthouseFriends gives his years as 1937 to 1951, though its own text says 1935 to 1951. Lire la Mer's heading gives 1937 to 1957.[^1][^5] The McKinnon family's memories, printed by Lire la Mer, describe 56 steps to carry the supplies up to the lantern, a weight that took about six hours to run down, and a bell that rang when a lamp went out. The nearest neighbour was half a kilometre away, and the children walked a kilometre and a half to school.[^5] The same family remembered that a few days before the Second World War began, a large ship that was identified as coming from Germany was boarded by the Royal Canadian Mounted Police off the coast near the light.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 7 December 1905 the icebreaker C.G.S. *Montcalm*, on work for the Department, logged "Matane light abeam, four miles off, light N.E. wind with light snow" at 12.20 a.m.[^10]

In December 1946 a Quebec Airways plane came down on the river ice, a little east of Matane according to Lire la Mer.[^5] The light was not then operating, and the keeper got it running quickly to help the search. The survivors were found north-west of Les Méchins.[^5] LighthouseFriends places the crash north-west of Les Méchins and credits the keeper with helping to rescue the crew and passengers.[^1] No newspaper consulted confirms the crash.

Lire la Mer also says that some years earlier the light's strength had helped Captain Heppell, whose boat had drifted. The page gives no date.[^5]

## After the keepers

The light was discontinued on 21 June 1951, when a light on the breakwater took over and no keeper was needed.[^4][^3]

LighthouseFriends says A. Vezina built the keeper's house in 1909 for $4,750.21. Lire la Mer dates the present house to 1910.[^1][^4]

LighthouseFriends says the light was deactivated in 1951 and sold to the Historical Society of Matane for one dollar, and that the municipality took over ten years later. The Rowlett directory says the society bought it in 1952 and the city took it over in 1962.[^1][^2]

The Quebec register lists the tower as a heritage building since 2014, and cites a 2014 document from the city.[^3] Lire la Mer reports a full renovation in 2017.[^4] The site closed for restoration in spring 2019 and reopened that summer.[^2]

## Today

The city owns the tower and house. The keeper's dwelling holds the tourist office, and the site also has a small museum. Rowlett names the Musée maritime de Matane as site manager.[^1][^2] The tower is open in season, but the two guides disagree on hours. LighthouseFriends gives Tuesday to Saturday from early June to mid-October. Rowlett gives guided tours from late June to late September, with a fee to climb.[^1][^2] Check the hours before going. The lighthouse stands on Route 132, at 968 Avenue du Phare Ouest.[^1]
