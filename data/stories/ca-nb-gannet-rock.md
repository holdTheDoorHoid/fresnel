Gannet Rock sits about 13 km (8 miles) south of Grand Manan, on the western side of the entrance to the Bay of Fundy. A light has shone from its tower since December 1831. The keepers lived on the rock itself.[^1] The Canadian Coast Guard runs the light now, on solar power since 2002, and the grounds and tower are closed to visitors.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The rock is about 12 m (40 ft) high and some 91 m (300 ft) long at high tide.[^1] It is named for the black and white gannets that once nested there. Murr Ledges, a cluster of navigational hazards, lie between it and Machias Seal Island, and the Old Proprietor, a dangerous rock, lies to the northeast.[^1]

In 1824 a bill was brought before the New Brunswick government for a revolving light on one of the islands or rocks near the southeast coast of Grand Manan.[^1] According to LighthouseFriends, the commissioners who chose the site left rafts of logs on several rocks and ledges over a winter to find out which were safe to build on.[^1] In 1830 the commissioners gave £1,000 for a floating light off the Old Proprietor. If that could not be done, the money was to go towards a lighthouse on Gannet Rock.[^1]

## Building the light

Thomas Reed and John Cunningham were paid £31 for surveying the site. Crawford, Gray & Purvis were paid £630 for the tower and keeper's dwelling, and David Hogg £155 for the lantern.[^1] The federal heritage statement names John Cunningham as the tower's designer.[^2] LighthouseFriends records him only as one of the two men paid for the survey, so the two sources disagree about his part.[^1][^2]

The tower was an octagon of heavy hewn timber, shingled and painted with black and white vertical stripes.[^2] It stood 12.6 m (41 ft 6 in) from base to vane.[^1] The first lantern was fitted with red glass. LighthouseFriends says the red glass greatly reduced the light's power, and in 1843 the light was changed to eleven seconds of white light followed by nine seconds of darkness. A shade raised and lowered by a weight-driven mechanism made the flashes, and the mechanism had to be wound every six hours.[^1]

In 1840 the station was given a signal gun, and a chute was cut into the rock so the keeper could launch and haul up his boat.[^1] In 1845 a granite wall 3.7 m (12 ft) high was built round the tower for £1,426, to make the station "perfectly secure."[^1]

A new lantern and a fourth-order lens were bought in 1866 for $2,598, but rough seas kept them from being landed that year. The new light was lit on 1 August 1867, and the keeper wrote in his journal, "We lit the new light and were well satisfied with it."[^1]

The Department's reports record the later works in order. In the fiscal year to 30 June 1900 the sea wall was largely rebuilt, and a new tramway 20 m (65 ft) long was laid from the landing to the lighthouse, at a total cost of $314.[^4] In the fiscal year to 30 June 1902 the light's pattern was changed so that it showed "bright 30 seconds," with "the total period being 40 seconds."[^6] In the fiscal year to 30 June 1904 the illuminant was changed from coal oil to a three-mantle incandescent petroleum vapour light.[^7]

In the fiscal year to 30 June 1905 the tower was raised. The octagonal wooden shaft was set on a concrete wall 3.7 m (12 ft) high and capped with a circular iron lantern painted red. The Department gives the height from base to vane as 27.4 m (90 ft), and a second-order lens gave a double quick flashing light.[^8] The Department's keeper table in the same volume gives Coleman Grant Dalzell's station as "Gannet Rock and Explosive Signal Station," with an appointment date of 1 July 1904.[^9]

In the fiscal year to 30 June 1906 a new dwelling was under construction, and the outbuildings were being improved.[^10] In the fiscal year to 30 June 1907 a new fog-alarm building was put up to house a 5-inch diaphone. Its machinery came from the Canadian Fog Signal Company of Toronto at a price of $9,245.[^11] By the fiscal year to 30 June 1908 the building was complete. It stood "about 40 feet from the dwelling" (about 12 m), and its horn, 11.3 m (37 ft) above high water, pointed due south.[^12] In 1911 a telephone cable was laid from Gannet Rock to Little Wood Island.[^13]

The Department's report for 1912–13 lists a January 1913 trip to the Bay of Fundy by Mr. Fraser, made in connection with a new departmental depot and "the extensive improvements required to Gannet rock and Machias Seal island."[^14] The report for the fiscal year 1913–14 says that Mr. J. Henderson was in charge of extensive repairs at Gannet Rock and Machias Seal Island from 16 May to 27 November 1913.[^15] In the fiscal year 1921–22 three new piers were placed under the lower end of the tramway.[^18]

## The keepers

Captain Thomas Lamb was the first keeper. He received £165 a year, out of which he had to pay an assistant, and he moved to Quaco Head in 1835.[^1] His successor, E. G. Miller, drowned in 1837 "while rowing back to the station" after fetching fresh water from nearby Kent Island.[^1]

Jonathon Kent was keeper when the inspector Lauchlan Donaldson visited in 1839. Donaldson called the place "a fearful place in storms." After a gale in the previous winter, several shingles had been washed off the lower tower, and LighthouseFriends says that only the iron braces and chains fitted in 1838 had kept it from being swept away. Donaldson recommended a cut-stone wall at least 3.7 m (12 ft) high round the tower, and an increase in Kent's salary.[^1]

Walter B. McLaughlin was appointed in 1853. LighthouseFriends' text says he spent the next twenty-seven years on the rock, which would run to 1880, but its own roster ends his service in 1879.[^1] His journal survives in extracts that LighthouseFriends prints. In July 1856 he recorded the first reshingling of the tower and dwelling since 1831.[^1] In October 1871 he noticed "a strong smell of burning buildings" and wrote that some large city, perhaps New York or Boston, must be burning. A boat a week later brought news of the burning of Chicago.[^1] In 1876 he wrote to the Marine agent in Saint John that an iron spindle, 9.4 m (31 ft) high, on the Old Proprietor was "worth $10,000 a year to navigation in the Bay of Fundy."[^1] In 1880 he was transferred to the newly built Southwest Head Lighthouse on Grand Manan, and Oliver A. Kent, who had been an assistant at Gannet Rock for many years, succeeded him.[^1] A house of refuge for the keepers was built in 1884 as a precaution against fire on the rock.[^1]

Lincoln Harvey was keeper from 1897 to 1904. His daughter Elsie lived on the rock with him from 1898 to 1904.[^1] In her late nineties she recalled a wire from the fog signal to the house, so that her father could set off the charges by pushing a button, though he still had to go out every hour or so to reload the gun.[^1] One day a freak wave struck the rock. The dwelling's door was open, and the seawater that came in moved the heavy stove and rose two feet inside the house.[^1]

Coleman Grant Dalzell followed in 1904 and stayed until 1911.[^9][^1] Allen Wilson was keeper from 1912 to 1919, and LighthouseFriends says he took in the crew of the *Dornfontein* in 1918.[^1] In August 1930 or 1931 work began on a new keeper's dwelling. Keeper Donald Wilson's family was told on a Friday to move out of the house and into the tower, and by the Monday a crew had begun tearing the old dwelling down. Twelve workmen shared the tower with the keepers until November.[^1] The roster lists every keeper that LighthouseFriends names, down to Barry S. Bagley, who left in April 1996.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In October 1869 the Saxby Gale struck the Bay of Fundy. LighthouseFriends quotes the Department's report for 1870 as saying that the station's buildings would have been "completely swept away" had the wind not veered during the height of the gale. Repairs costing $253 were quickly carried out.[^1]

In 1883 the three-masted schooner *Gertrude E. Smith*, bound from Rockland to Windsor, ran ashore on the rock. All hands were saved, and Keeper Kent looked after the captain's wife and daughter until they could be picked up.[^1]

On the morning of 12 March 1899 the new Allan liner *Castilian*, on her maiden voyage, went ashore at Gannet Rock light at 4.30 a.m. A Halifax dispatch described the grounding as taking place "at low tide, in a dense fog, her compasses being deranged." Fifty passengers and the crew were all saved, and they reached Yarmouth on tugs that evening.[^19]

On 27 June 1901 the Yarmouth life-boat, under Albert Cain with a crew of six, went to the barquentine *Argentine*, of Pictou, stranded on the Gannet Rock ledge. The crew helped refloat her and bring her into Yarmouth harbour, and each man was granted an honorarium of $6.[^5]

On 19 May 1913 the steamer *Gerald Turnbull* ran ashore on Gannet Rock ledge. The volunteer crew from Baker Cove, Nova Scotia, went out and stood by her. The life-saving report says that "as the crew declined to abandon the ship the life-boat returned to her station."[^16] The Yarmouth port warden's return, printed in the same Department report, lists the steamer as "stranded on Gannet rock dry ledge and was total loss."[^17]

On Friday 2 August 1918 a submarine looted the lumber schooner *Dornfontein* and set her afire at the mouth of the Bay of Fundy. The *New-York Tribune* called her "the first Canadian victim" of the raider, and reported that she was towed into port "still burning."[^20] LighthouseFriends adds that her crew was put into dories and reached Gannet Rock the next day, where Keeper Allen Wilson took them in.[^1]

## After the keepers

Over a two-year period from 1967 a temporary tower showed a light while the old lantern and Fresnel lens were removed, and a new lantern holding a modern beacon was installed.[^1] The second-order lens is now on display in the Grand Manan Museum.[^1]

The last keeper, Barry S. Bagley, left in April 1996. In 2002 the Coast Guard solarized the light and gutted the attached dwelling, which was in poor repair.[^1]

## Today

The current Coast Guard list gives a black and white octagonal tower 22.9 m (75 ft) high, with two lights, North and South. Each shows an occulting white light every three seconds, and both run year round.[^3]

The heritage statement on the Canadian Register calls Gannet Rock "the most isolated light station on the Fundy coast" and says the tower is "the second-oldest wooden tower within Canada."[^2] The Coast Guard owns the station. The grounds and tower are closed, and LighthouseFriends advises seeing the rock from the water.[^1]
