Horton Point Light stands on the north side of eastern Long Island, in Southold, New York. Its granite and brick tower and attached keeper's house were lit in October 1857 with a third-order Fresnel lens.[^1][^3][^4] In 1933 an automatic green light on a skeleton tower beside it took over, and the old tower went dark until it was relit in 1990.[^1][^4] Part of the keeper's house is now a nautical museum.[^2]

## The place

The tower and keeper's house stand on the "Cliff Lot" of Barnabas Horton's original 1640 land grant.[^2] The point had a bad name before there was a light. The museum says the sandbar and glacial rocks off the point claimed 13 shipwrecks, and that the waters there were called "Dead Man's Cove" because of the many wrecks before the light was lit.[^1]

In 1756 George Washington, riding across Long Island to Boston, met a man named Ezra L'Hommedieu in Southold, and the two discussed the suitability of Horton Point and Montauk Point for a light, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] The museum says the lighthouse was commissioned in 1790, during Washington's presidency. No money came for it until the 1850s.[^1][^3] A Lieutenant George Bache noted in 1838 that a buoy on the shoal would be prudent, since "vessels not unfrequently ground here."[^3] The first letter asking for a fixed light came in 1853. The museum calls its writer William Brown, "Master of the steamer Bay State," while LighthouseFriends describes him only as a local ship captain of the same name.[^1][^3]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $4,000 on 3 August 1854.[^1] The Light-House Board deemed that inadequate, and a further $3,500 came on 18 August 1856.[^1] The Board first offered $500 for four acres of the point, then $550 for eight. The Payne family accepted the $550 in October 1855.[^1] Charles Payne, the museum says, was a retired whaler, and he and his wife Hannah were the owners.[^1]

LighthouseFriends describes William Sinclair, a Scottish immigrant, as supervising the work.[^3] The museum says he had been an engineer at the Brooklyn Navy Yard, and that he oversaw and documented the construction from 19 May 1857.[^1] His first entry notes the arrival of 90,000 bricks on 16 May. They landed at Southold harbor, where many sank into the soft sand and mud.[^1] The cornerstone was laid on 9 June, and the masons finished the tower and dwelling by 25 September. On 5 October the third-order lantern was raised.[^1]

The sources differ on the first lighting. The museum says the light was first lit on 15 October 1857.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it was first shown on 12 October 1857.[^3] The museum gives the total cost as $12,412, of which $3,875 was labor, $6,437 materials and $2,100 the lens and apparatus.[^1] LighthouseFriends names the lens maker as L. Sautter & Company.[^3]

The light first burned sperm whale oil in an Argand lamp, which the museum says was very costly. Lard oil replaced it in 1863, kerosene in 1883, and an incandescent oil-vapor lamp, first lit on 28 January 1907.[^1] The dwelling changed too. Between 1865 and 1870 a second story was added over the enclosed piazza to give the assistant keeper sleeping quarters.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes major renovations in 1870, including a cast-iron lantern deck and Portland cement over the badly decayed brick.[^3] The museum's visitor page gives the tower as 58 feet (18 m) tall, and LighthouseFriends gives 55 feet (17 m).[^2][^3] The sources give the focal height differently. The museum says 110 feet (34 m) above sea level, and the Lighthouse Society's record gives 103 feet (31 m) above mean high water.[^1][^4]

## The keepers

William Sinclair was appointed the first keeper on 4 June 1857 at a salary of $400 a year.[^1][^3] The Lighthouse Society's record lists eight principal keepers between 1857 and 1933. After Sinclair came Albert J. Tillinghast (1861–1866), Barnabas Pike (1866–1869), Theron W. Squires (1869–1871), Daniel Goldsmith (1871–1877), George S. Prince (1877–1896), Robert Ebbitts (1896–1919) and George Ehrhardt (1919–1933).[^4] LighthouseFriends says nine head keepers served, but its own list names eight.[^3] The same record lists twelve first assistants. George S. Prince, first assistant from 1871 to 1877, became head keeper in 1877.[^4]

Keepers kept a log of what they saw.[^1] The entry for October 1860 counted the vessels seen passing that month: "one ship, 37 barks, 57 brigs, 489 schooners, 353 sloops, 41 steamers for a total of 979 vessels." The listed figures add up to 978, one fewer than the stated total.[^1]

George Prince had served in the Civil War, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] He was made head keeper in 1877 in place of Daniel Goldsmith.[^3] In early 1896 a warrant was issued for his arrest over a serenade given to two newlyweds. Later that year he was dismissed for "drunkenness and neglect of duty."[^3]

Robert Ebbitts came from Cedar Point Lighthouse in 1896, and LighthouseFriends calls him Canadian-born.[^3] In 1903, after he had painted the tower stair, he was going up a ladder to light the lamp when a rung broke. He fell thirty-five feet (11 m), broke a leg in two places and crushed his ankle.[^3] He asked that Stella Maria Prince, George Prince's daughter, be appointed acting keeper while he recovered.[^3] The archive lists her as acting principal keeper for 1903 to 1904.[^4] The museum says she officially held the federal title of acting assistant keeper from June 1903 through November 1904, and that she lived at the station for 34 of her 60 years.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it lasted a few months, while Ebbitts recovered.[^3] In 2023 the Coast Guard added her to its official list of women lighthouse keepers, 120 years after she served, the museum notes.[^1]

Ebbitts kept the post until 1919.[^4] In 1907 inspectors admonished him for leaving "greasy rags in the lantern and watch rooms," a fire hazard, and the Board warned him again in 1908.[^3] The Light-House Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1896 lists Horton Point among the stations where repairs "more or less extensive" were made.[^6]

George Ehrhardt followed in 1919.[^4] The museum says he stayed on in the dwelling, commuting to the Shinnecock Lighthouse, and retired from there in 1935. The archive gives his service at Horton Point as ending in 1933.[^1][^4] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for fiscal year 1922 lists "Horton Point Light Station, repairs to dwelling, $3,157" among the construction and repair work completed that year.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Before the light, the point was dangerous.[^1] The museum counts 13 shipwrecks on the sandbar and glacial rocks off the point, without giving dates or naming the vessels.[^1]

The museum says the 1938 hurricane blew a large part of the southwest roof off the dwelling, and that a piece of it was found a quarter of a mile (400 m) away. Keeper Ehrhardt and his wife then left for civilian quarters.[^1]

During the Second World War the light in the skeleton tower was put out until after VJ Day.[^1] The two sources differ on who manned the tower then. The museum says the military occupied the lighthouse as a landmark and spotter tower.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a Civilian Defense Corps manned it as a lookout.[^3] In the Korean conflict, US Ground Observers reached the tower by a temporary exterior staircase, so that civilian observers would not disturb military personnel billeted in the dwelling.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1933 a skeleton tower was erected 50 feet (15 m) north of the old one, and on 30 June 1933 a revolving green electric light was lit there.[^1] The museum gives the tower's height as 50 feet (15 m), and LighthouseFriends gives 40 feet (12 m).[^1][^3] The keeper's log for 18 July 1933 says: "New Light lit for the first time. The old light in the Tower out for the first time in 80 years."[^1] The Riverhead County Review of 27 July 1933, as the Southold Historical Museum quotes it, described the new light as an automatic type that needed no lighthouse keeper.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes the same passage without naming the paper.[^3] The old Fresnel lens was taken out and sent to Maryland for storage, and the museum says it was lost there.[^1] LighthouseFriends, however, says the museum displays a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^3]

The sources disagree about the transfer of the property. The museum says the Southold Park District acquired the property from the Department of Commerce for $1.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the district leased it later in 1933, and that the town received the site officially in 1937. The government kept the right to take it back if needed, and did so shortly after the United States entered the war.[^3]

The buildings then sat unused for years.[^1] The museum says the iron balcony was removed for scrap during those years, and that its ten bronze drain gargoyles disappeared.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the neglected lighthouse was almost razed before the Southold Historical Society and a Park District commissioner stepped in.[^3] A 1976 bicentennial project put $40,000 into wiring, heating and plumbing, and the Nautical Museum opened in July 1977.[^1]

The tower and lantern room were restored from early 1988.[^1] The Coast Guard re-commissioned the tower on 9 June 1990, and the light was lit again.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the skeleton tower was removed that day.[^3] A 1993 agreement gave the museum perpetual occupancy of the tower and museum space, so long as it runs the exhibition. The Coast Guard operates the light and the Park District keeps the grounds.[^1] The National Register asset record for the lighthouse is dated 21 October 1994.[^5]

## Today

The light in the tower is now a VRB-25 rotating beacon, a green light that charts list as flashing every ten seconds.[^1] The museum says the lamp is green because it would be to port of any ship heading to New York harbor by way of Long Island Sound.[^1]

The museum says the lighthouse is open to visitors on Saturdays and Sundays from 11:30 a.m. to 4 p.m., from Memorial Day weekend to 20 September.[^2] Admission is $5 a person or $10 a family, and the Park District lot charges $5 to people who are not district residents.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open daily from 8 a.m. to dusk, and gives museum hours of weekends from 11:30 a.m. to 4 p.m. between July 3 and mid-September. Its figures differ from the museum's, so check the museum before you go.[^3][^2]
