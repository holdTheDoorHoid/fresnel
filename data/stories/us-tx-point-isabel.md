Point Isabel Lighthouse is a brick tower on a bluff on the mainland, in the city of Port Isabel, Cameron County, at the southern tip of the Texas Gulf Coast.[^1][^2][^4] It was first lit in March 1853 to guide ships through the pass to the port.[^1][^2] It was discontinued in 1888, relit in 1895, and abandoned as a light in 1905.[^1][^2] The state has owned it since 1950 as a historic site.[^1][^2]

## The place

Ships bound for the port came in through Brazos Santiago Pass, which separates Padre Island from Brazos Island and opens into the Laguna Madre.[^1] Buoys marked the pass in 1846, but Texans wanted a lighthouse at their southernmost port.[^1] Local mariners favored the bluff at Point Isabel over the low barrier island, which they said flooded.[^1] The point seemed to be in federal possession already, though the government never secured its title (see below).[^1] General Zachary Taylor set up Fort Polk there during the war with Mexico, and after the war the government kept the fort for the customs service.[^1]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says Texas's representatives in Congress obtained $15,000 for a lighthouse in 1850.[^1] The customs collector at the port, John S. Rhea, recommended a masonry tower built by local contractors, and John E. Garrey of Brownsville was given the contract to build the brick tower.[^1] Work began in December 1851, and the tower was nearly finished by September 1852.[^1] The ship *Brownsville* carried the spiral stair and lantern room from New York, but was wrecked on the voyage.[^1] That delayed the tower's completion until early spring 1853.[^1]

The Texas Historical Commission says construction was under way by 1851, and that the brick tower was completed two years later, in 1853.[^2] Its site page gives 1852 as the year the lighthouse was built.[^3] The commission says the tower was "topped by a stationary white light that could be seen for almost 16 miles." It gives that distance in miles without saying which kind.[^2]

LighthouseFriends gives the first showing as 20 March 1853, with fifteen lamps behind 21-inch reflectors.[^1] A notice in the *New York Herald* of 19 April 1853, as the commission quotes it, said the light and the beacon at the entrance to Brazos harbor "were lighted 20th ult., 1853."[^2]

The second keeper's dwelling was finished in 1855.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a third-order Fresnel lens, lit by a single lamp, came two years after that.[^1] A new lantern room was installed in 1881.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes the Lighthouse Board's 1897 annual report: the lantern was "in a dilapidated condition," and in rain the lens and lamps could not be kept dry, because the lantern leaked in every direction.[^1]

The land under the light was the real difficulty.[^1] The government had held the point since the Mexican War but never secured title.[^1] In 1886 James B. Wells of Brownsville told the Lighthouse Board that he owned the land, and in 1887 he sued the keeper for trespass.[^1] The Board discontinued the light on 15 May 1888, deciding that the screwpile light at Brazos Santiago, on which work had begun in 1878, could serve navigation in the area alone.[^1]

Congress then appropriated $8,000 "to reestablish the light at this place" by an act of 2 March 1889.[^5] The Board's report for 1891–92 says the United States attorney had "commenced proceedings in condemnation" to obtain title.[^5] In 1893 the attorney was "still engaged in securing title" to the land.[^6] A year later the Board expected that "it is probable that title will be vested in the United States" and the light reestablished.[^7] The owner settled for $5,000, and the light came back on 15 July 1895.[^1][^8] The Board's list of lights shows it as "A third-order light, July 15,1895."[^1][^8]

## The keepers

Peter Rouche was the first official keeper, but his tenure lasted only six months.[^1] The archive's roster lists him for 1853 alone.[^4] The second keeper, John H. B. Ham, first lived in a dwelling left over from Fort Polk.[^1] After an inspector from Galveston judged it inadequate, a new one was built.[^1] Ham died in 1860.[^1]

His wife, Hannah Ham, had been serving as assistant keeper.[^1] She took over as head keeper and served until the tower was deactivated during the Civil War.[^1] The roster lists her twice: as first assistant from 1853 to 1860, and as head keeper from 1860 to 1861.[^4]

After the war Benjamin Bergreen was keeper when the light was reactivated on 22 February 1866.[^1] The roster lists Bergreen from 1866 to 1868 and Frank Meyer from 1868 to 1869.[^4] Henry Wildman was head keeper from 1869 to 1884, and George Saunders was first assistant from 1866 to 1884.[^4] Herman Schrieber, whom LighthouseFriends spells Schribers, was head keeper from 1884 to 1888.[^4][^1]

The last head keeper on the roster is William Egly, who had been first assistant from 1884 to 1888 and then head keeper from 1895 to 1905.[^4] James Hill was first assistant from 1899 to 1905.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Civil War came to the point.[^2] Confederate troops held the area early in the war, and Federal troops took control in 1863.[^2] Both sides used the tower as an observation post.[^2] After the lens was removed from the tower, Confederate soldiers used the tower as a lookout.[^1]

In May 1863 a Union ship entered the harbor to engage blockade-runners.[^1] The Confederates set a charge inside the tower and blew out the glass of the lantern room.[^1] Five months later a Union force returned, and the retreating Confederates exploded more powder in the tower.[^1] The tower's door was blown off, the brickwork was cracked, and the clockwork that turned the lens was damaged.[^1] Union officers asked the Lighthouse Board to repair the tower and return it to service, but the war delayed the work for years.[^1]

## After the keepers

The tower was decommissioned in 1905.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the railroad had drawn shipping away from Brazos Santiago, and that the lens was shipped to Mobile, Alabama.[^1] The commission says the tower was abandoned permanently that year.[^2]

The Board kept the property for two more decades.[^1] In 1927 a district superintendent recommended that it be sold.[^1] It went to J. S. Ford of Brownsville for $2,760.[^1] Two months later the Port Isabel Town Company bought the lighthouse from Ford for $5,000 to promote the newly renamed city.[^1] The bluff around the tower was lowered, leaving the lighthouse on a small mound, and the town promoters went bankrupt in the Depression.[^1]

In 1950 Mr. and Mrs. Lon C. Hill Jr. and the Port Isabel Realty Company donated the lighthouse and its buildings to the state as a historic site.[^2] The formal opening of the park was held on 26 April 1952.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the light returned to operation as a private aid to navigation on 27 January 1955.[^1] The commission says the lighthouse "does not operate as a navigational beacon and has not since 1905." The two accounts disagree, and this story does not choose between them.[^1][^2]

The commission says the keeper's cottage was replicated in the 1990s.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the full restoration and the replica dwelling to 2000.[^1] The lighthouse closed for renovation in October 2016 and reopened on 2 January 2018.[^1]

Between 16 and 17 November 2022 a reproduction of a third-order Fresnel lens was installed.[^2] A celebration followed on 9 December 2022.[^2] The lens is illuminated nightly to a soft glow, and it will be fixed, not rotating.[^2] The commission's site page says that, after 117 years without a light, the lens was fitted.[^3] LighthouseFriends also says the lighthouse had been "inactive for 117 years" before the 2022 ceremony.[^1]

## Today

The tower stands in a small state park.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls it Texas's smallest state park.[^1] The commission says that of the 16 lighthouses built along the Texas coast, only Port Isabel is now open to the public.[^2] Visitors can climb 75 winding stairs and three short ladders.[^3] Visitors can again reach the catwalk around the lantern room, which had been closed for safety reasons since 2014.[^1] The reconstructed keeper's cottage serves as a visitor center and museum.[^3]
