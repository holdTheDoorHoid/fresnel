Cap Blanc is a headland at Percé, in the Gaspésie region of Quebec, where a lighthouse was built in 1874 to mark the entrance to the Percé roadstead.[^1][^2][^3] The first light was a square wooden tower. In 1915 a reinforced-concrete tower replaced it, and the sources do not agree on when the light was put out.[^1][^2][^4]

## The place

The Quebec heritage register says the cape takes its name from the pale colour of its rocks.[^2] The register also says the light marks the passage between Bonaventure Island and the mainland, and that it is one of a large number of sites on the gulf and estuary of the St. Lawrence that were marked for the first time in the second half of the nineteenth century.[^2] LighthouseFriends places the Rocher Percé, which it says Samuel Champlain named in 1604, about 2.4 km (one and a half miles) north of the cape.[^3]

## Building the light

In September 1873 the Department bought a piece of land from Charles Bourget, a farmer, for sixty piastres in Canadian currency, according to the magazine.[^1] On 18 October 1873 the Department engaged Bourget as keeper at $100 a year.[^1]

Joseph Octave Boucher of Percé got the contract to build the tower. The magazine gives its cost as $1,429.47, and LighthouseFriends gives $1,429.27.[^1][^3] The light was first shown on 6 November 1874.[^1][^3]

The magazine quotes the Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1874 as describing a fixed white catoptric light 42 m (138 ft) above the water, likely visible beyond 13 miles, shown from a square wooden tower painted white and 6.1 m (20 ft) high.[^1] The register gives the first tower as about 8.9 m (29 ft) high.[^2] The sources do not reconcile the two heights.[^1][^2]

The Department equipped the light with a hand fog horn in 1877.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends says Bourget's salary was raised from $100 to $125 in 1878.[^3] New lamps and large reflectors were installed in 1895.[^1][^3]

The station was the tower and nothing more. The register says it never had a keeper's house, a fog-signal shelter or a shed, and the magazine says the keepers lived close by.[^2][^1]

In 1915 the wooden tower gave way to an octagonal reinforced-concrete tower, painted white with a red lantern.[^1][^3] The magazine says the contract went to Joseph Antoine Boucher of Percé, son of the builder of the first tower, and that the work ran from 14 January to 14 July 1915.[^1][^2] The register says this seems to have been the first tower in the gulf to be replaced in this way.[^2] LighthouseFriends, however, names the builder as Joseph-Octave Boucher of Percé, not his son.[^3]

The new tower is 11.6 m (38 ft) high, and its light is 46.9 m (154 ft) above high water.[^2] The magazine gives the same height.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the tower's height as 24 ft (7.3 m).[^3] The sources do not agree on the height. It flashed white every five seconds, with a range of 15 miles.[^1]

The sources disagree on the cost of the 1915 work. The register and the magazine give $4,483.20 for the tower, its lighting and the inspection together.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends gives $1,845 for the tower and $555 for the lighting and inspection.[^3]

## The keepers

Charles Bourget kept the light from its first lighting in 1874 until he died in December 1892.[^1][^3] The magazine says his work was to tend the fire, sound the hand fog horn in fog, and look after the building. It says he kept the post for 18 years.[^1]

After his death two of his sons took over in turn. Florian Bourget kept the light from 1893 to 1918.[^1] The Department's table of appointments for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1909 names Florian Bourget with an appointment date of 18 March 1893.[^7] The 1915 table gives the same date for a keeper at Percé under the initial "P."[^5] The 1908 list of lightkeepers also gives Florian Bourget at Percé.[^6] The magazine says Florian kept a daily logbook, in which he noted the boats passing offshore, the fuel used and the temperatures.[^1]

The magazine gives 1919 as the year Florian died, although its own table ends his service in 1918. It says C. T. Bourget took the post for a year.[^1] Joseph Horace Bourget, Florian's son, kept the light for two years after he came back from the First World War.[^1] Philias Bourget, Charles's other son, kept it from 1922 to 1931.[^1][^3] Daniel Hayden kept the light from 1931 to at least 1936.[^1][^3]

Léon Bourget, Philias's son, was the last keeper, from 1937 to 1965.[^1][^2] The register says he lived outside the station's area. The magazine says he gave 29 years of his life to the light.[^2][^1]

## After the keepers

The magazine says the light was automated in 1965.[^1] The register records repairs in 1955, and says the light has not been operational since 1956.[^2] The Les amis des phares du Saint-Laurent page gives the same 1956 date.[^4] The magazine says the light ceased its work in 1997.[^1] So three dates bear on the end of the light: 1956, when the register and the Les amis des phares page say it stopped operating; 1965, when the magazine says it was automated; and 1997, when the magazine says it ceased its functions. The register's own keeper dates also run to 1965.[^2]

When he built the concrete tower in 1915, Joseph Antoine Boucher took part of the old wooden structure to his own land to use as a shed, and the magazine says the shed still stands.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the base of the original tower remains standing today, on privately owned land.[^3]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light. The grounds and the tower are closed.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is surrounded by private land marked with No Trespassing signs, and that the nearby campground seems to tolerate visitors who want a view of it.[^3] Keep off that land.

The magazine describes the tower as abandoned since 1997 and in poor condition.[^1] It also describes the climb from inside to the top as dangerous.[^1] Do not climb the tower.
