Annisquam Harbor Light stands on Wigwam Point, at the north entrance to the Annisquam River, in the village of Annisquam in Gloucester, Essex County, Massachusetts.[^1][^2] Its 41-foot (12.5 m) brick tower dates from 1897. A light has marked the point since 1801, and the station is one of the oldest in the state.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The river was a hazard in its own right. The nomination describes "long sandbars and a rocky shore along the Annisquam River" as dangerous obstacles for a navigator, and says the light protects the mariner from the eastern side of the north entrance.[^1] The Life-Saving Service's report for 1881 describes a schooner that struck Squam Bar, a mile southwest of the station it calls No. 2, Second District, at Annisquam.[^8]

Congress authorized a light on Wigwam Point by an act of 29 April 1800.[^1][^2] The land was ceded to the federal government, and Gustavus Griffin sold six and a half acres to the United States in October 1800 for $140.[^1][^2]

## Building the light

The first tower, of 1801, was wooden and octagonal. LighthouseFriends says it cost $2,000 and showed a fixed white light forty feet (12 m) above the water.[^1][^2]

By 1842 the station was failing. Civil Engineer I. W. P. Lewis inspected it that year, called the whole establishment "very dilapidated and ruinous," and recommended a complete rebuild.[^2] George Day, the keeper, gave a statement for Lewis's report to Congress in 1843. LighthouseFriends prints it: "The frame of the tower is rotted in all its parts." Day doubted that the tower would survive the next storm, and he noted that a walk from the house to the tower had been swept away by a heavy sea two years earlier.[^2]

It took the government another eight years to rebuild. By 1851 a new, white, octagonal tower forty feet (12 m) high stood on the site, and the keeper's dwelling had been repaired.[^2] In 1857 the light received a fifth-order Fresnel lens, turned by clockwork.[^2]

The walkway from the house to the tower has two dates. The nomination describes a 75-foot (23 m) wood walk built in 1814 for $1,000, which was replaced in kind in 1978.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a 109-foot (33 m) covered walkway was built in 1867.[^1][^2]

The present tower was built in 1897 on the old stone foundations.[^1][^2] The Light-House Board's report for 1896 to 1897 records the move: the fifth-order light went into a temporary tower on 23 April 1897, and was re-established in the new tower on 13 July 1897.[^6] The dwelling, also dating from 1801, was substantially altered at the same time.[^1] The Board's report for 1900 records a change of the tower's color on 16 October 1899, without saying what the new color was.[^7]

## The keepers

James Day was the first keeper, from 1801 to 1805, at a salary of $200 a year.[^2][^3] When he fell ill in 1805, his son George, who had been helping him, became keeper. George held the post until 1850.[^2][^3]

In 1842, when Lewis came, George Day was seventy-two and still keeper. His salary had risen to $350 a year.[^2]

The Digital Archive roster lists Mary Phipps as principal keeper in 1871, between Octavius Phipps and Arthur G. Moore. She is the only woman's name on the roster of principal keepers at Annisquam.[^3]

Dennison Hooper kept the light from 1872 to 1894. His son Edward was born at the station in 1879, and in later life Edward wrote to the historian Edward Rowe Snow about his years there.[^2][^3] The keeper kept several cows, and their milk was sold in the village.[^2]

John W. Davis was keeper from 1894 to 1931.[^3] LighthouseFriends says his wife, Ida, lived through many changes during their years there.[^2][^3] It says that in 1907 the station was connected to the city water supply and to the telephone service, and that in 1922 it received a fourth-order lens in place of the fifth-order one.[^2][^3][^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1884 the Life-Saving Service's report describes a wreck on Coffin's Beach, near the Davis Neck station. The station was closed for the summer, and the three men from the wreck were landed by the Annisquam keeper with one of the Massachusetts Humane Society's boats, before aid could have come from the station.[^4]

On 26 September 1888 a violent northeast gale drove a coal-laden schooner ashore on Coffin's Beach. A party of fishermen brought the Humane Society surf-boat kept at the Annisquam light to the scene, and volunteers, including a member of the station crew, launched it and rescued six men and the captain's wife.[^5] The schooner's name is printed differently in the two sources that describe the wreck, so this story does not give one.

The Life-Saving Service's account says the vessel "broke up" two months later, and that "all but a small portion of her cargo was saved."[^5] LighthouseFriends, which draws on letters by Edward Hooper, has a different story. It says volunteers carried the boat two miles to the beach, and that the schooner was a total loss.[^2] The Life-Saving Service puts the boat three-quarters of a mile from the wreck.[^5]

In May 1895 Davis went out in a thirteen-foot (4 m) dory to collect driftwood after a storm, and his boat capsized. He was in the water for more than half an hour before two fishermen reached him.[^2] His wife had sent their oldest son to the Life-Saving station, and the crew took the keeper to the light, where he was revived after several hours under a doctor's care.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1922 the fifth-order lens was removed and a fourth-order lens installed. The nomination adds that the light's source changed "from oil to electric" that year.[^1]

A fog horn was established in 1931. LighthouseFriends says it was to operate only from 15 October to 15 May, so as not to disturb the summer residents, and that by 1949 it was back in use in the summer, during daylight hours.[^2] The nomination's chronology says the fog signal had been installed by 1965. It describes a recently added one on the exposed rock behind the tower.[^1]

The light was completely automated in 1974, and its last keeper was relieved.[^2][^3] When the Coast Guard deactivated the foghorn with the automation, local fishermen demanded its return, and several thousand people signed a petition. In 1975 a switch for the foghorn was placed in the local police station.[^2]

In 1980 the Coast Guard deeded two parcels, 4.9 acres in all, to the Norwood Heights Trust, with an easement for access to the light, and kept 1.3 acres.[^1] The nomination's description also says the 4.9 acres were disposed of in 1972, so its own dates disagree.[^1] Later, the tower's metal landing beams, which salt air had rusted, were replaced along with about 3,000 bricks, the old glass-block windows and the roof of the 1801 keeper's house. The repairs were concluded in August 2000.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light and the 1.3 acres around it, and the grounds, dwelling and tower are closed.[^2] The keeper's dwelling houses a Coast Guard family.[^2]

The directions to the light run through Norwood Heights, which the nomination describes as a private housing development.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the small parking area near the light was closed in 2012, and that a visitor was told the neighborhood was private.[^2] Stay off the private streets and the closed grounds.
