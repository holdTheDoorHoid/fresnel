Point Lepreau Lighthouse is a red-and-white octagonal tower on a headland of the Bay of Fundy, in southern New Brunswick, roughly halfway between Saint John and St. Andrews.[^1][^8] A light has shone from the point since 1831.[^1][^2] The tower there now is the third on the site.[^1][^2] It stands on the grounds of a nuclear generating station, and the public is not admitted.[^1][^2]

## The place

The point juts some distance south into the Bay of Fundy, and LighthouseFriends describes it as a significant obstacle for coastwise shipping.[^1] The province's commissioners of lighthouses first put forward a light there as early as 1827, with plans and estimates laid before the House of Assembly.[^1] The money came only in 1831, when £500 was granted to cover construction.[^1]

## Building the light

The expenditure list for the building shows what it cost. J. C. Waterbury was paid £343 for the keeper's dwelling and the lighthouse, and Harris & Allen received £165 for two lanterns.[^1] Daniel Pettingell began to be paid as keeper on 1 September 1831.[^1]

Surveyor L. Donaldson's report of 1839 found two lights in the one tower.[^1] The upper light had five lamps in 58 cm (23 in) reflectors, and the lower light had five lamps in 38 cm (15 in) reflectors.[^1] Donaldson saw that from five or six miles the two merged into one.[^1] In 1840 the lower light was moved to the ground floor, 8.5 m (28 ft) from the upper.[^1]

In 1848 the keeper was given guns and powder, so that he could answer the bells and whistles of ships.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes the practice: a steamer leaving Saint John in fog ran for a set time and then sounded, and the answering gun at the point let it run on with a new departure.[^1]

A steam fog signal went in during 1869.[^1] The whistle, its building, a dwelling for the engineer and a well cost $3,702.[^1] J. Christy was the first engineer, at $400 a year.[^1] In 1873 new lamps and 46 cm and 56 cm (18 and 22 in) reflectors were installed.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the light could then be seen 24 miles away, as far as Head Harbour and the north end of Grand Manan.[^1] The new lamps burned nearly twice the oil of the old ones.[^1]

In 1885 a frame fog-alarm building went up on the point in front of the tower, at $1,987, to house an improved Champion foghorn.[^1] The old steam whistle was kept as a backup.[^1] The lower light was discontinued in November 1886, because the new building blocked it.[^1]

The fog signal changed again in the early 1900s. The Marine Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1905 says a diaphone had replaced the fog horn at this station.[^3] LighthouseFriends adds that the building had to be enlarged to take it.[^1] The report for the year ended 30 June 1906 records that the diaphone's blasts were changed.[^4] The next Marine report, for the nine months ended 31 March 1907, records an inspection on 18 September 1906: the plant was in fairly good condition, the diaphone had been renewed, and the plant had been changed to run on low-pressure air.[^5]

Later work included an electric lighting plant in 1913 and a triple flash reflector on the tower in 1918, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] A new keeper's dwelling, costing $5,975, was built at the station.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates it to 1921. The Marine report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1922 records a new lightkeeper's dwelling at the same cost.[^7]

A wind station at Point Lepreau also appears in the Marine report for the fiscal year 1914-15. Its anemograph records were sent in weekly to be tabulated and analysed, and the report says the station "gives most useful and satisfactory results."[^6]

## The keepers

Daniel Pettingell kept the light from 1831 until 1854.[^1] Donaldson, the surveyor, commended his work in words that LighthouseFriends quotes: Pettingell had made Point Lepreau "from being one of the worst stations, probably the best belonging to the Province."[^1]

George Thomas, Sr., kept the light from 1854 until his death in 1884.[^1] A telegraph station was set up in the keeper's sitting room in 1874.[^1] The keeper's daughter, Miss Thomas, was hired at $150 a year to run it.[^1] She sent a weather report to Saint John twice a day and kept a record of the vessels passing the point.[^1] A $475 addition to the dwelling followed the next year, and LighthouseFriends notes that the family was large.[^1]

In 1878 John H. Harding, the Marine Agent at Saint John, called at the station.[^1] In his report to Ottawa, which LighthouseFriends quotes, he called Thomas "a thoroughly intelligent and practical man."[^1] Thomas had kept the silver-wash reflectors bright, though the other keepers given the wash had given up before it worked.[^1]

George W. Thomas, the keeper's son, was appointed in his father's stead and kept the light until 1905.[^1] Robert L. Belding followed, from 1905 to 1914, then W. M. Snyder, from 1914 to at least 1937.[^1] LighthouseFriends names no keeper between 1937 and 1944.[^1] Donald Wilson kept the light from 1944 to 1959, and Keith E. Wright from 1959 to 1980.[^1]

The fog alarm had its own engineers.[^1] William A. Gallant ran it from 1871 to 1897. Frank Frawley had it from 1897 to 1904, and again from 1905 to 1923.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In January 1898 a fire at the station claimed the lighthouse, the fog alarm and thirty tons of coal.[^1][^2] The Lighthouse Directory also dates the loss of the first lighthouse to January 1898.[^2] A Chance anchor lens was set on a mast as a temporary light, and a replacement fog alarm, built on the old foundation, began operating on 9 March.[^1]

Francis Cassidy built the new octagonal wooden tower for $1,325, and it went into operation on 1 October 1899.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that some of his work was so poor it had to be done over before inspectors would pass it.[^1]

In a storm in December 1956, LighthouseFriends says, lightning struck the lighthouse and totally destroyed it.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory says the replacement wooden tower also burned after being struck by lightning in the same month.[^2]

## After the keepers

The station was de-staffed in 1980, and the station's other buildings have been taken down since.[^1] The concrete tower built after the loss has eight sides and red-and-white horizontal bands, like the one before it.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates it to 1958, and the Lighthouse Directory gives 1959.[^1][^2]

A short distance inland, the Point Lepreau Generating Station began operating in 1983.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the plant made the lighthouse harder to reach, especially after 9/11.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard's list of lights gives the light a white flash every five seconds, from a focal height of 25.5 m (84 ft), with a range of 14 nautical miles.[^8] The tower is 17.6 m (58 ft) high.[^8] The Lighthouse Directory gives a white flash every three seconds. This story follows the Coast Guard's own list.[^2][^8]

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse.[^1] The site and the tower are closed.[^1][^2] The Lighthouse Directory names New Brunswick Power as the site manager.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is not accessible to the public, though visits may be arranged through the generating station.[^1]
