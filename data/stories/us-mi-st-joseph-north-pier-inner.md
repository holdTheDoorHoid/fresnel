The inner light on St. Joseph's North Pier is a steel tower of 1907.[^1][^2][^3] It stands beside an outer light of 1906, and the two were built as a range to guide ships into the St. Joseph River from Lake Michigan.[^1][^2][^3] The city has owned both lights since 2013.[^4]

## The place

The St. Joseph River meets Lake Michigan here.[^3] Work on piers to make the river mouth easier to enter began in 1836, and by 1844 the project had cost $121,113, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] Its planners boasted that St. Joseph would "become an important place, probably second only to Chicago, on this lake."[^3]

The Board's report for 1891 described the port as "about midway between Chicago and Grand Haven, and has an extensive lake commerce."[^5] The first lighthouse, built in 1832, was on the mainland rather than on the pier, LighthouseFriends says.[^1][^3] The Lighthouse Service's archive gives 1846 as the year the station was established, which does not match the 1832 date.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $5,000 for a light at St. Joseph on 3 March 1831.[^3] John Scott won the $2,700 contract for its rubblestone tower and dwelling, and Winslow Lewis received $500 to install eleven of his lamps.[^3] The stone tower was replaced in 1859 by a two-story frame dwelling with a square tower, and its light was changed from white to red in 1864.[^3]

The pier lights came in stages. Congress appropriated $3,000 for a pierhead beacon on 15 July 1870, and it was built on the south pier that year.[^3] In 1881 it moved to the outer end of the north pier, and a new open-frame tower went up there in 1885.[^3] A 1,500-pound fog bell was added in 1887.[^3] The mainland light had been given an assistant keeper in 1876, to help with two lights. It was discontinued on 18 March 1886 and re-established on 10 July 1889.[^3] In the same year a conduit light was run out along the pier to form range lights for entering the river.[^3]

Congress appropriated $5,000 for a steam fog signal in June 1896, and a ten-inch steam whistle went into service on 1 February 1897.[^3] A second assistant keeper was added to help with the extra work.[^3] After the War Department extended the north pier, the range lights and whistle were moved 1,000 feet (300 m) lakeward in 1904.[^3]

The present range dates from 1906 and 1907.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends describes the front tower as a steel frame covered with metal sheets, tapering to a nine-sided lantern, with a fifth-order lens.[^3] The Michigan Lighthouse Conservancy instead gives the outer light as a cast-iron cylinder 35 feet (11 m) tall, first lit in 1906.[^1] The sources disagree about the outer tower's shape and material. Both agree that the inner light is a steel tower of 1907.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends describes it as a 24-foot-square (7 m) steel structure with an octagonal tower and a fourth-order lens, and the Conservancy gives the tower 57 feet (17 m).[^1][^3] A keepers' duplex with seven rooms in each of two apartments went up at the inner end of the pier in 1908.[^3]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Service's archive lists six head keepers at the pier between 1906 and 1941: Edward Mallette, George J. Cornell, Ferdinand Ollhoff, Charles S. Grenell, Owen C. McCauley and Charles S. Carlson.[^2] Eleven first assistants and one second assistant are also listed.[^2] For most of them the archive's dates are the only ones available.

Daniel R. Platt had the longest tenure.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he began in 1883 as head keeper of the mainland and pierhead lights and served until the mainland light was permanently deactivated in 1919.[^3]

George J. Cornell was keeper in 1913, when a launch was in danger of stranding near the station.[^9] On 14 July 1913, the Lighthouse Service Bulletin says, Cornell assisted in saving the launch Wolverine from stranding. He got a line to the boat as it drifted past the station, and then helped the life-savers bring it into harbor.[^9]

Owen C. McCauley, who was in charge of the pierhead lights from 1931 to 1936, was among those who tried to save the old mainland lighthouse before it was razed in 1955, LighthouseFriends says.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Board's 1891 report recorded that in thick weather steamers found it hard to get into the harbor, because "the sound of the bell at the light-tower is ineffective."[^5] In the year reported, the bell struck by machinery was in operation 285 hours.[^6]

On 13 November 1894 a storm damaged the elevated conduit from which the front range light was shown.[^7] The light was moved to a temporary post at the outer end of the conduit the next day, the Board's report for 1895 says.[^7]

The Board's report for 1899 records a new spar to replace a broken one, "for a light to locate the wreck of the steamer City of Duluth."[^8]

The severe Great Lakes storm of 8 to 10 November 1913 damaged the St. Joseph Pierhead Light Station.[^10] The Service's Bulletin puts the damage there at about $6,500, and names the station among several on Lake Michigan that were hit.[^10] In 1914 the Service began work on a new sea wall of reinforced-concrete piles, meant to replace walls put up about two years before.[^11]

## After the keepers

The old mainland light stayed in service until 1919, when an acetylene light went up on a red skeletal tower on the south pier.[^3] The old lighthouse was sold to the City of St. Joseph in 1936.[^3] It later housed offices for several charities, then was razed in 1955 to make room for a parking lot, LighthouseFriends says.[^3]

A diaphone fog signal was installed in the rear tower in 1933.[^3] The Michigan Lighthouse Conservancy records that the range lights were approved for listing on the National Register of Historic Places on 9 November 2005.[^1]

## Today

The city acquired the lights from the federal government in 2013.[^4] After a public fundraising campaign through the Lighthouse Forever Fund, they were rehabilitated and returned to their circa early-1932 configuration, the city says.[^4]

The North Pier is owned and operated by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, the city says.[^4] The lights are still active aids to navigation, and the Coast Guard operates the beacons, the city says.[^4] The Lighthouse Service archive's record, however, marks the station as not an active aid to navigation.[^2] The lights are reached through Tiscornia Beach.[^4] Each summer the Inner Light is open for tours on Saturdays, weather permitting, run by St. Joe Today.[^4] The catwalk that links the lights to the shore is on the National Register too.[^4]
