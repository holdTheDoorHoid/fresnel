The Burlington Canal front range light is a white square tower with a green top at the outer end of the south pier of the canal that links Lake Ontario with Hamilton Harbour.[^8][^1] Its front range was set in alignment with the stone main light to guide boats into the canal, and it is a working light today.[^2][^8]

## The place

A low sandy barrier, known as the Beach, separates Burlington Bay from the western end of Lake Ontario.[^1] A canal through it was proposed in 1823, and the bay was renamed Hamilton Harbour in 1919.[^2] The wooden piers of the canal were a hazard. Steamers rubbed against them, and sparks from their smokestacks could set them on fire.[^2][^1]

## Building the light

The canal's first lights were put up in the 1832 season.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates a new front range, a "square, wooden tower that stood fourteen feet tall", to 1845, when it was set at the end of the pier.[^1] The Beach Canal Lighthouse Group dates a wooden front light at the end of the western south pier to 1856.[^2] The two dates disagree.

The Department's annual report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1905 records a new front range, "built near the outer end of the south pier".[^3] It describes the tower as "an open frame steel skeleton tower, square in plan, with sloping sides", painted red, 11 m (36 ft) high, carrying a "fixed white dioptric light, of the seventh order" 12 m (39 ft) above the lake and visible 11 miles.[^3] The report says the steel tower "was supplied by Messrs. Gould, Shapley & Muir, of Brantford, Ont., and cost $356".[^3] The erection was done by days' labour under the superintendence of W. K. Morris, at a cost of $523.56.[^3] Lighthouse Friends gives the same firm and the same $356 for the tower.[^1]

The next tower, a concrete one, was built in 1909.[^5][^1] The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1910 says that "Mr. E. P. Jennings was engaged in 1909 in erecting a reinforced concrete lighthouse on the pier at Burlington, Ont."[^5] Lighthouse Friends describes it as 12.5 m (41 ft) tall and octagonal, with an octagonal iron lantern.[^1] The same report says the "range lights on masts have been discontinued, and a new tower built", with a "dioptric 4th order 360° lens, showing a fixed red light".[^5]

The works list for that year has a "1-inch single diaphone plant, with a 3-h.p. gasoline engine" at Burlington beach. Its machinery was furnished under general contract by the Can. Fog Signal Co., Toronto, and the installation is a separate item in the list.[^5] Lighthouse Friends says the diaphone was installed inside the new tower.[^1] The report places it at Burlington beach, so the two sources disagree on where it stood.[^5][^1]

The light and fog signal were electrified in 1922.[^2][^1] A works list for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1923 has "Installation of electric lights and electrically driven fog alarm" at Burlington, without saying which tower it meant.[^7]

In 1939 the concrete tower was replaced by the present four-sided tapered tower.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the 1939 tower carried a fixed green light and an electrically operated tyfon horn.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends gives one list of keepers for the canal lights and does not divide it between the main light and the front range.[^1]

George Thompson was a ferryman before he was made keeper in 1861. He had to put out fires on the piers on several occasions, and he retired in 1875 after his health failed.[^2][^1] Captain Thomas Campbell was keeper from 1875 to 1905, at a salary of $300 a year.[^1]

Thomas Lundy was keeper from 1905 to 1924, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] The Department's keepers' table for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1914 lists "Burlington bay, lights and fog alarm" with "T. Lundy" as its keeper.[^6]

After Lundy came A. W. Cripps, from 1924 to at least 1937, and Carl J. Van Cleaf, from 1941 to at least 1951.[^1] Lighthouse Friends names Bob Campbell, Harry Dunn and Mike Gushulak among the keepers of the 1960s, and gives Peter Coletti as keeper from 1968 to 1987.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

At 4 p.m. on 18 July 1856 the steamship *Ranger* caught a pier on fire as it passed through the canal. High winds spread the fire and destroyed the old octagonal lighthouse and other nearby structures.[^1]

In October 1900 Captain Campbell was at the end of the south pier, waiting through heavy fog for the steamer *Macassa*, when he heard a crash and saw the outer pierhead light go out.[^1] A duck had crashed through a pane of the lantern room and overturned the lamp. A small fire started, and Campbell put it out.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says a newspaper account reported that he meant to have the duck stuffed.[^1]

A storm in early December 1907 "heavily damaged the piers at the entrance to Burlington Bay", according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1908 lists a "Burlington beach, temporary pole light" among its minor repairs that year, at $162.20.[^4] The report does not mention the storm. Lighthouse Friends describes temporary range lights set on the north pier while the south pier was rebuilt.[^1]

## After the keepers

Peter Coletti kept the canal lights until 1987, when, according to Lighthouse Friends, they were automated and the keeper's dwelling was left vacant.[^1] The Beach Canal Lighthouse Group gives the automation as 1990.[^2]

In 2003 two local residents approached the Department of Fisheries and Oceans about whether the lighthouse and keeper's dwelling could be preserved. The group that formed from that effort became the Beach Canal Lighthouse Group.[^1][^2] In December 2021 the stone main light was designated a heritage lighthouse, with ownership to pass to the Hamilton Oshawa Ports Authority.[^1]

## Today

The front range is an active navigation light, operated seasonally.[^8] The Coast Guard list gives an isophase green light, on and off in equal spells every four seconds, from a focal height of 14.5 m (48 ft), visible 10 nautical miles. The tower is 13.1 m (43 ft) tall.[^8]

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed, and that the outer end of the pier can be reached from the parking area near the southern end of the lift bridge.[^1] The tower is closed to visitors and is a working aid to navigation, so it should be seen from outside and not entered.[^1][^8]
