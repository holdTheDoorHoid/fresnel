Ballenas Island Light is a white octagonal concrete tower on the north point of North Ballenas Island, in the Strait of Georgia off Parksville, British Columbia.[^8][^9] It is a Canadian Coast Guard light, and its grounds and tower are closed to visitors; the island can be reached only by boat.[^1][^9]

## The place

The Spanish explorer José María Narváez named the islands Islas de las Ballenas, the Whale Islands, in July 1791, after he encountered a pod of whales near them.[^1] The British Admiralty's charts had called them Ballinac.[^1] The Department of Marine's report printed in 1902 still used that spelling, and the one printed in 1908 used Ballenas.[^2][^4] LighthouseFriends says the Geographic Board of Canada corrected the name in 1905.[^1]

The light was meant for shipping. In 1898 Captain Smythe of the Royal Navy survey ship Egeria recommended a light and a fog alarm on the islands, because more and more vessels were using the Inside Passage.[^1]

## Building the light

The marine agent at Victoria, James Gaudin, endorsed the proposal. After Ottawa approved it, a crew was sent out in May 1900 to choose a site.[^1] The crew picked a knoll on the south-east part of South Ballenas Island, where a square, pyramidal tower of wood was built. LighthouseFriends gives its height as 10 m (33 ft) to the vane of the lantern, with the light 30 m (100 ft) above high water. It showed a fixed white for twenty seconds, then an eclipse of ten.[^1]

The Department's report printed in 1902 records that on 15 September 1901 hand foghorns were established at five Pacific lightstations, Ballinac Islands among them, to be used in answer to the fog signals of vessels heard from the station.[^2]

The fog alarm came later. In 1907 the local Member of Parliament, joined by shipping interests, petitioned for an improved light and fog signal.[^1] The report for the nine months ended 31 March 1907, printed in 1908, says a fog alarm was being built at the station by day labour under the superintendence of G. H. Frost, the cost to date being $1,200.[^4]

The report for the fiscal year ending 31 March 1908, printed in 1909, says the fog-alarm building was completed during that year, with a dwelling for the engineer. The diaphone plant was supplied by the Canadian Fog Signal Company of Toronto for $1,900. The report describes the fog-alarm building as a rectangular wooden structure and the keeper's dwelling as a framed wooden building.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the work started in 1907 and the buildings were finished by the end of 1908.[^1]

In 1912 the wooden tower was taken down on the south island and rebuilt near the fog alarm on North Ballenas.[^1][^9] The concrete tower that stands now was finished in 1917, roughly 400 yards north of the wooden one.[^1] The Department's annual report for the fiscal year 1916-17, printed in 1918, lists a reinforced concrete tower, built by day labour, and a triple-flash long-focus reflector against the entry for Ballenas Island.[^6]

The 1916 season brought more work to the island. The government steamer Estevan landed construction supplies there, and the steamer Newington landed materials and machinery for the lighthouse during the summer.[^11] On 4 August the chartered steamer Leehro landed the district engineer, workmen and construction materials.[^7]

## The keepers

The first keeper of record was William Henry Brown. The Department's list of lightkeepers printed in 1903 gives his station as Ballinac Island and his appointment as 3 October 1901.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he was appointed when the light was completed in 1900. The two dates do not agree, and this story does not settle it.[^1][^3]

In 1905 police at Nanaimo told the marine agent that Brown was being held in the city jail.[^1] His wife, Maggie, then had him committed to the asylum at New Westminster. He came back to the station for a month, was committed again the next year, and was never released.[^1] Maggie looked after the light as needed until Brown's nephew, Wilhelm Betait, arrived from Germany and was put in charge.[^1]

LighthouseFriends is not consistent about the family. In one passage Betait is Brown's nephew. In another, Maggie later married Betait, a German immigrant who had anglicised his name to William Brown.[^1] The page does not reconcile the two accounts.

In the autumn of 1912 Arthur Broughton Gurney left Pine Island with his wife and three children, after repeatedly pressing the marine agent for a station that was less remote.[^1][^10] When the First World War began he enlisted. LighthouseFriends says an order-in-council let government employees who enlisted keep their full salary, and that Gurney left the station to his wife, Anna.[^1] The Department's payroll listed her as keeper from 1917 to 1919. Gurney was listed again from 1920 to 1921, when the family was transferred to Active Pass.[^1]

Gurney had proposed doing without the assistant that the Department required at a fog-alarm station. The agent at Victoria reminded him that every station with a fog alarm had an assistant paid by the keeper, since no single man could run a horn for stretches of up to a week while keeping the light.[^1]

## After the keepers

The station was de-staffed on 1 September 1996, and the last keeper, Richard Wood, and his wife were moved to Cape Scott.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory describes the light as automated, and says the status of the keeper's house is not clear.[^9]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard lists the light as a white flash every ten seconds, 21.3 m (70 ft) above the water, with a nominal range of 17 nautical miles.[^8]

The Lighthouse Directory says the island is privately owned except for the light station, and that the site and tower are closed. It is reached only by boat.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the grounds and tower are closed, and suggests seeing the light from the water, or from the shore at Parksville.[^1] Because the island is private and the site is closed, visitors should not land there.
