Tarpaulin Cove Light stands on the west side of the cove on Naushon Island, the largest of the Elizabeth Islands, between Buzzard's Bay and Vineyard Sound.[^1] Naushon is private, and the public may not land there.[^8][^9] The tower's date is disputed. The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 describes a brick tower built that year on the old foundation, while the nomination and a 2024 federal notice give 1856.[^3][^1][^9]

## The place

The Cuttyhunk Historical Society calls Vineyard Sound the world's second busiest shipping passage of its era.[^10] LighthouseFriends says it ranked second only to the English Channel, and that it was one of the most dangerous passages, with fog, strong currents and sudden bad weather.[^8]

A private light was kept at the cove before any federal light. LighthouseFriends says Zaccheus Lumbert, a former Nantucket resident who kept a tavern there, "privately established a light in 1759." He sold the tavern in 1764, and tavern keepers carried on the light until 1817.[^8] The Cuttyhunk Historical Society also gives 1759 for the original light.[^10] The Lighthouse Society's place record gives 1759 as the year the station was established.[^2]

## Building the light

The Commonwealth of Massachusetts ceded the land in June 1816, the nomination says.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the federal government spent $216 on a site on the southwest side of the cove in 1817 and began a rubblestone tower costing $6,062.11. A letter-writing campaign organized by James Bowdoin, a proprietor on the island, held up the work, and LighthouseFriends says it effectively delayed action until after his death.[^8] The nomination says the light was first manned in 1817.[^1]

John Hayden, the first keeper, was there when the new tower showed its fixed white light in October 1817, LighthouseFriends says.[^8] He stayed for thirty-five years.[^8][^2]

Hayden's annual log, as LighthouseFriends describes it, listed one year's use of 195 gallons of summer oil, 130 gallons of winter oil, 133 chimneys and more than 1,200 wicks.[^8]

In 1842 Hayden gave his account for the inspection report of I. W. P. Lewis. He said the establishment "was not faithfully built in the first instance," that the plaster had fallen off, and that the chimneys were all smoky. LighthouseFriends quotes him.[^8]

General context, not a record of this station: the Board's general rules for keepers in 1864 counted a failure to give timely notice of the want of oil, wicks or chimneys as a neglect of duty.[^11]

In 1856 a fifth-order Fresnel lens was bought from Sautter and Company of Paris and installed in place of nine lamps, LighthouseFriends says.[^8] On 7 April 1870 the light's characteristic was changed from fixed white to fixed white punctuated every thirty seconds by a bright flash.[^8]

The nomination's chronology gives 1856 for a white brick tower 38 feet (12 m) high and 19 feet (6 m) across, but its description calls the tower 78 feet (24 m), cylindrical and brick.[^1] A 2024 federal notice describes a tower 38 feet (12 m) tall, built in 1856.[^9] The nomination also notes a remodeling in 1891.[^1]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 gives a different sequence. The old tower of rubble masonry, built in 1817, was demolished. A brick tower 28 feet (9 m) high, with iron stairs, an iron deck and a new fourth-order lantern, was built on the same foundation.[^3] The Cuttyhunk Historical Society dates the present lighthouse to 1891.[^10] LighthouseFriends gives 28 feet for the tower.[^8] The Board's entry is the official record of the rebuilding; the other dates stand as the sources give them.

While the new tower was built, the light was shown from a temporary structure, from 25 April to 30 June 1891.[^4] The same entry says the station was given a 1,200-pound (544 kg) bell, struck by machinery.[^3]

## The keepers

The roster lists John Hayden (1817 to 1852) first. Nathan Clifford, Jr. (1853 to 1861) was keeper when the 1856 lens was bought.[^2][^8]

Richard Norton got the post in 1871, LighthouseFriends says, after the Confederate raider Alabama sank the square-rigger he had captained and partly owned. The lighthouse job was given as partial compensation for his loss.[^8]

Frank S. Carson kept the light from 1886 to 1910, and Carl D. Hill from 1916 to 1920, according to the roster.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for 1920 names the keeper as "Carl P. Hill."[^12]

Frank A. Davis was keeper from 1920 to 1925 on the roster, and he was at the light on 31 October 1924.[^2][^7] Tolman W. Spencer is listed from 1925 to 1941, but LighthouseFriends says he had been keeper since 1928.[^2][^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 31 October 1924 a schooner caught fire and burned to the water's edge near the light, after an explosion aboard, the Bulletin says.[^7] Keeper Frank A. Davis assisted the two men aboard and got a doctor from Woods Hole to treat the men, who were badly injured.[^7]

LighthouseFriends adds that Davis brought the two burned men into his kitchen, where his wife Olive tried to care for them while he went for the doctor. The men died, LighthouseFriends says. It also says that while her husband was away, Olive Davis tended the light.[^8]

The great New England hurricane of 21 September 1938 destroyed the fog-bell tower, which was not rebuilt.[^8][^1]

## After the keepers

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 records that the boathouse was moved to a better site on the west side of the cove, refloored, underpinned and protected by a retaining wall.[^5] The 1914 Bulletin lists Tarpaulin Cove among the lights changed from oil to incandescent oil vapor.[^6] LighthouseFriends says an oil house was added in 1903.[^8]

The light was automated in 1941.[^2] LighthouseFriends says mariners were told it would be automated about 30 September 1941, and the Cuttyhunk Historical Society says the Coast Guard automated it that year.[^8][^10]

The nomination says the keepers' dwelling and other buildings had deteriorated and were demolished in 1962. It records a candlepower reduction in 1958 from 4,500 to 700, while LighthouseFriends gives 7,500 to 700 for the same change.[^1][^8] The nomination also records a fourth-order cut-glass Fresnel-type lens installed in 1967.[^1]

In 2001 the Coast Guard issued the Cuttyhunk Historical Society a 25-year license to care for the site and structure.[^10]

In July 2024 the Coast Guard declared the light excess to its needs. The General Services Administration offered it at no cost to eligible entities, with 60 days to submit letters of interest.[^8][^9]

## Today

The lantern now holds "a solar powered 300 mm optic light," according to the 2024 notice.[^9] The Cuttyhunk Historical Society maintains the tower under its license.[^10] Whether the light has been transferred since the 2024 offer is not recorded in these sources.

LighthouseFriends says the grounds and tower are closed, apart from an annual one-day open house, usually held in the first week or two of August and sponsored by the Cuttyhunk Historical Society.[^8]

Naushon is a private island with no paved roads, and the public is not allowed to land on it.[^9][^8] LighthouseFriends says viewing the light is difficult, since no public cruises pass close to Tarpaulin Cove.[^8]
