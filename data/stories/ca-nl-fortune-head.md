Fortune Head Light stands on a cape 1.6 km (1 mile) west of the town of Fortune, at the southwestern edge of the Burin Peninsula in Newfoundland and Labrador. The cliffs under it are also the Fortune Head Ecological Reserve, where the rocks mark the boundary between the Precambrian and the Cambrian.[^1]

## The place

Fortune is a town on Fortune Bay. Its harbour began as a barachois, a lagoon partly shut off by a sandbar, which made the entrance hard to use. A government dredge worked there for six months in 1906 to improve access and deepen the harbour.[^2]

The headland is also a scientific reference site. The reserve was a provisional reserve in 1990 and became a full ecological reserve in 1992. In that year the International Union of Geological Sciences designated Fortune Head as the best example in the world of the Precambrian-Cambrian boundary, a Global Stratotype Section and Point.[^1]

## Building the light

Fortune Harbour had a light first. LHF says that in 1911 a square, wooden lighthouse, painted white, was set on the eastern pier in the harbour, and that W. King was its first keeper.[^2] The Newfoundland estimates for 1912-13 carry a "Fortune Harbor-Keeper" with a salary of $300.[^3]

LHF gives 15 June 1954 as the date a lighthouse and fog alarm station was established on Fortune Head, about 1.6 km from the town. Maxwell G. Thornhill, who had been the town engineer, became the first head keeper, on a starting salary of $2,520 a year.[^2] The Lighthouse Directory gives 1911 as the year the Fortune Head station was established.[^4] The two accounts cannot both be right about the headland.

## The keepers

LHF says that Thornhill and the assistant keeper, Benjamin Monster, shared six-hour watches and slept on a cot in the fog alarm building. At first there was no road to the station, which had no radio or telephone.[^2]

In 1956 the Department of Transportation built two residences, but the keepers' families still had to live in town, because the children needed to get to school. A 4.8 km (three-mile) dirt road to the station was completed in 1968, and from then the families could live at the station all year.[^2] Thornhill was forced to retire from lightkeeping in 1986.[^2]

## After the keepers

Four years after Thornhill's retirement, in 1990, the station was automated, LHF says. The whistle house, the slipway and two dwellings were demolished, and a new square, skeletal tower was erected for the automatic light.[^2]

The light once revolved and flashed every ten seconds, LHF says. It now flashes every five seconds. The foghorn has kept the same pattern through the years.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard list gives the light as a white flash every five seconds, from a white tower with a red top, 9.9 m (32 ft) high. Its focal height is 23.8 m (78 ft), and it is visible 15 nautical miles. It operates around the clock. The fog signal gives a three-second blast every thirty seconds.[^5]

The reserve is open to walkers, and hiking is its main activity. A maintained gravel road runs to the light station, a navigation aid that the Coast Guard operates. Walkers start from the lighthouse parking area. No entry permit is needed, and all-terrain vehicles are banned in the reserve.[^1]

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and LHF says the grounds are open but the tower is closed.[^2] Rowlett gives the same: site open, tower closed.[^4] Neither source says whether the station buildings are open to visitors.

LHF says the Coast Guard re-staffed the station after automation, with a keeper on site during working hours, and that a small building beside the tower serves as the keeper's office and the reserve's visitor centre. It gives no date for the re-staffing. Rowlett also calls the station staffed.[^2][^4]
