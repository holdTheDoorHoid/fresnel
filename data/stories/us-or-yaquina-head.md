On a basalt headland north of Newport, Oregon, a conical brick tower rises 93 feet (28 m), the tallest lighthouse on the Oregon coast (the nomination puts the headland four miles from Newport, the Oregon Encyclopedia three).[^1][^3] Its first-order Fresnel lens was first lit on 20 August 1873, and a first-order Fresnel lens is still in the lantern today.[^2][^1]

## The place

Yaquina Head is a narrow, rocky headland of basalt that pushes about a mile out from the general line of the coast, a few miles north of the entrance to Yaquina Bay.[^1][^3] The National Register nomination places the light between Heceta Head, 32½ miles to the south, and Cape Meares, about 48 miles to the north.[^1] The light shows 162 feet (49 m) above the sea, and the nomination gives its range as 19 miles.[^1]

The reason for a light here was the growth of trade along the central Oregon coast. Congress appropriated money on 27 March 1866 for harbour lights, buoys and two lighthouses near Yaquina Bay, and the Coast Survey made a survey of the site in 1868.[^1] The small Yaquina Bay light at the river mouth was built in 1871, and the nomination says the new light at Yaquina Head rendered it ineffective; the Bay light was decommissioned in 1874.[^1]

The station's early name was Cape Foulweather Light, and the confusion behind it is itself part of the story. The nomination says that, "Due to an error in geography," materials meant for Cape Foulweather were landed at Yaquina Head, three miles to the south.[^1] The Oregon Encyclopedia disagrees: it says early land-office records sometimes called this headland Cape Foulweather, but that the Lighthouse Board had always intended Yaquina Head, and that the government's own *List of Lights* used the name Yaquina Head from 1896.[^3]

## Building the light

Congress approved $90,000 for the lighthouse in 1871.[^3] The work was supervised by the Army Corps of Engineers and, in both the nomination and the Encyclopedia, went badly.[^1][^3] In the Corps' report for 1872, Major H. M. Robert, the engineer in charge at Portland, wrote that the lighters carrying materials "have been destroyed twice," that the schooner bringing them from San Francisco had been forced to unload most of her freight at Newport and had twice grounded on the bar, and that part of the material had been hauled "six miles over an almost impassable road." He concluded that "About half the time since the work began has been lost on account of the difficulties of transportation."[^1] The Bureau of Land Management says there was no real road at all, only a trail that a horse and wagon could manage partly by way of the beach, and that the lighthouse tender *Shubrick* landed goods in a small cove just south of the headland, from which they were hauled up the bluff.[^2] Steps were cut into the basalt cliff to help carry materials up from the beach.[^1]

The sources differ on when work began and where the parts came from. The nomination says construction began in the fall of 1872, yet it quotes Major Robert dating the work from the autumn of 1871; the BLM also says late 1871.[^1][^2] The nomination says the tower's bricks were made in Portland and shipped by way of San Francisco, while the Oregon Encyclopedia says more than 370,000 bricks came from the Patent Brick Company of San Rafael, California.[^1][^3] The Encyclopedia adds that the double-walled tower copied the design of Pigeon Point Light in California; the nomination says the tower and workroom matched Bodie Island Light in North Carolina (1872), with the same plans for gallery, lantern, base and stairs.[^3][^1] The Encyclopedia dates the keeper's house, a wooden building, to September 1872.[^3]

The lens was a first-order Fresnel made in Paris in 1868 by Barbier & Fenestre, shipped across the Isthmus of Panama and then north to Oregon.[^2] It was unpacked at the station in October 1872.[^3] When the crew prepared for the first lighting, they found that crates holding parts of the lantern had been lost at sea, and the opening waited while replacements were made and shipped.[^3] Keeper Fayette Crosby finally lit the four-wick lard-oil lamp on 20 August 1873, showing a fixed white light.[^2][^3] There was no fog signal; the Encyclopedia says fog was uncommon at the headland.[^3]

## The keepers

The station was run by a head keeper and two assistants.[^3] Fayette S. Crosby, the first head keeper, served from 1873 to 1875, and the archive's roster lists Shadrach L. Wass as head keeper from 1875 to 1886.[^4] LighthouseFriends adds that Crosby had earlier kept Umpqua River Light (1857 to 1860) and served as an assistant at Cape Arago (1869 to 1870).[^5]

Frank M. Plummer is the name most tied to the early decades. The archive shows him as an assistant from 1878 to 1886, acting head keeper from 1886 to 1888, and head keeper from 1888 to 1907.[^4] A newspaper in nearby Toledo, reporting on the visitors in August 1907, called him "Captain Frank M. Plummer," and said he had been in charge "for 30 years and four months," which would put his start earlier than the archive does.[^6] The roster lists four more head keepers up to the mid-1930s: Henry E. Wilson, William Smith, Charles Miller (who had been an assistant from 1918 to 1922) and then Ray E. Dunson.[^4]

John L. Zenor spans the middle of the century. The archive lists him as first assistant from 1932 to 1945 and head keeper from 1945 to 1953.[^4] The National Register nomination, however, says that "one day in 1931 the keeper, John Zenor, recorded 196 tourists," a year before the archive's first date for him.[^1] The last head keeper listed is Loren F. Robb (1964 to 1966), with Harold "Sunny" W. Gore as first assistant over the same years.[^4]

The sources show a steady stream of visitors. In 1907 the Lincoln County Leader reported 2,150 visitors so far that year and judged that the light "probably receives more visitors than any other on the Northwest coast."[^6] In 1913 the Salem *Daily Capital Journal* wrote that visitors were let into the lighthouse on most days of the week and shown around.[^7] The nomination says that as many as 600 people a day combed the grounds in the 1930s and 1940s, and the Oregon Encyclopedia says that by 1938 it was the most visited lighthouse on the West Coast.[^1][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The nomination calls the site exposed and says its weather caused many repairs. The Light-House Board's report for 1879, as quoted in the nomination, records seven days of high winds in March that broke "many panes of glass" in the storm sashes and windows of the keeper's dwelling, stripped twenty running feet of shingles from the oil house and flattened about fifty feet of the old picket fence.[^1] The Encyclopedia says a high fence was built in 1880 to shield the buildings and windows from rocks torn from the cliff by the wind, while the nomination describes an eight-foot closed board fence along the crest of the bluff and says gravel was often driven against the buildings in squalls.[^3][^1] Birds drawn to the light at night often broke panes in the lantern room.[^1]

A tramway to haul supplies from the beach, 3 feet wide and 130 feet long with a geared winch at the top, was built in 1885.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for 1900 records a hot-water heating apparatus installed in the tower and a new 15,000-gallon cistern.[^8] By 1901 the Board was telling Congress that quarters for the three keepers were too small, and the Board's annual requests, repeated from 1901 to 1907, asked for an appropriation of $4,000 for a new building.[^1] A new dwelling was funded by an appropriation of 19 July 1919, with $6,500 allotted; the first bids were rejected as too high, and a modified bid of $6,266 was accepted in 1922.[^9] The nomination dates the new quarters to 1922, and says that a second dwelling replaced the original duplex in 1938.[^1]

During the Second World War the Coast Guard posted seventeen servicemen at Yaquina Head as lookouts for enemy ships and submarines, according to the Oregon Encyclopedia.[^3]

## After the keepers

The station was electrified in the 1930s. The nomination says 1935, with a radio beacon, and that the staff was then cut from three keepers to two; the Oregon Encyclopedia says only "the early 1930s."[^1][^3] A 1,000-watt lamp replaced the oil lamp, and in 1939, the year the Coast Guard took over from the Lighthouse Service, the fixed light was given the flashing signature it keeps now: two seconds on, two off, two on, then fourteen seconds dark.[^2]

The light was automated in 1966, and the last Coast Guard keepers left.[^1][^3] With nobody living there, the dwellings stood empty and were later demolished in 1984, leaving the tower, its attached workroom and a 1926 water reservoir as the main surviving structures.[^1][^3] Most of the lighthouse reservation was sold in 1974, except for 4.52 acres, and the Coast Guard leased the quarters in 1975 to the University of Oregon's marine biology department.[^1]

Congress made the headland the Yaquina Head Outstanding Natural Area in 1980 (Public Law 96-199), managed by the Bureau of Land Management.[^1][^3] The Encyclopedia says the Coast Guard handed the station to the BLM in 1993 and opened it to the public; the BLM opened an interpretive centre in 1997 and spent more than a million dollars restoring the tower and light in 2005.[^3]

## Today

Yaquina Head Light is a working aid to navigation, flashing twice every 20 seconds, with the Coast Guard operating the automated lamp.[^3][^2] The land, the tower and the visitor programme are managed by the BLM as part of the Outstanding Natural Area, and the USLHS archive lists the BLM and Friends of Yaquina Lighthouses as owner/manager.[^2][^10] The BLM says its staff guide visitors through the lighthouse, and that the tower is visible whenever the park is open, fog permitting.[^2] Hours, fees and tour times change, so check the BLM's Yaquina Head page before you go. The BLM also warns that winter weather on this coast can be dangerous: sneaker waves can knock walkers off their feet, and visitors should never turn their backs on the ocean.[^2]
