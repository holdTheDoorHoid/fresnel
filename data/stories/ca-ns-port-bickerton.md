The Port Bickerton light station stands at the west end of Barachois Head, on the east side of the entrance to Port Bickerton harbour on Nova Scotia's east coast.[^1][^3] Its first light, a small harbour light, was put in operation on 10 October 1901.[^4] The station now holds a 1963 light tower beside the 1930 keeper's house, which is a museum.[^1][^2]

## The place

Port Bickerton is a small community on Nova Scotia's east coast.[^1] LighthouseFriends says its first European settlers were fishermen who came from Peggy's Cove in 1840, and that Bickerton Island, on the west side of the harbour entrance, was settled first, followed by Barachois Head on the east side.[^1]

The Department of Marine's report for 1901 records that "a harbour light has been established" at Port Bickerton.[^9]

## Building the light

The light was put in operation on 10 October 1901.[^4] The building was a square wooden tower with sloping sides and a square wooden lantern, all painted white.[^4] It stood 7 m (23 ft) high, about 30 m (100 ft) back from the water's edge on Barachois point, which is the Department's spelling, on land 7.6 m (25 ft) above high water.[^4]

The light was fixed white, 13.1 m (43 ft) above high water, and the Department expected it to be visible 7 miles from all points of approach by water (the report does not say which miles).[^4] Its apparatus was a small pressed-glass lens.[^4] Emery Taylor, of Stillwater, Nova Scotia, built the lighthouse for a contract price of $500.[^4]

## The keepers

Theodore O'Hara was the first keeper, at a salary of $150 a year.[^1] Until the keeper's dwelling was built in 1903, LighthouseFriends says, he rowed to and from the light each day.[^1] The Department's list for the year ended 31 March 1910 gives his appointment as 26 January 1901, at a salary of $205 a year.[^6] He is listed at Port Bickerton in the Department's keeper list for 1908 as well.[^5] LighthouseFriends says O'Hara was still keeper on 28 November 1929, when the Department awarded the contract for a new combined light and dwelling, and it gives his service as running to 1931.[^1]

After O'Hara, Calvin Kaiser served from 1932 to 1937 and Harvey Taylor from 1937 to 1940.[^1] Irvin Beiswanger and then Harold Beiswanger kept the light between 1940 and 1961.[^1] A second keeper was assigned in 1948, when the fog alarm was upgraded, and the assistant's post was eliminated in 1982.[^1] G. Fred Budge, Lamont Lovitt, Edgar Schraeder and Hector Lowe followed, the last of them from 1983 to 1988.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 10 April 1912 the Norwegian-registered vessel Urbye was wrecked near what the report calls "Port Beckerton island".[^7] A formal investigation at Halifax, held on 30 May, found the master at fault and reported him to the Norwegian Government for action.[^7]

Fire took the first lighthouse in the late 1920s, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] Rowlett also says the original tower was destroyed by fire.[^1][^2]

In 1947 a fire began in Fisherman's Harbour, on the other side of the peninsula, and the fire moved west toward the station.[^1] Residents came with seawater to help the keepers hold it back, and the lighthouse suffered only minor roof damage.[^1]

On 28 March 1962 the fog alarm building caught fire, when oil-soaked floorboards ignited while paint was being stripped from them.[^1] The building and its air tanks were destroyed, and no other structure was damaged.[^1] A concrete fog alarm building replaced it.[^1]

## After the keepers

The new combined light and dwelling was built in 1930.[^2] Rowlett describes it as a two-storey wooden keeper's house with a lantern and gallery on its roof.[^2] A diaphone fog alarm came in 1948, and a new one-storey dwelling was built in 1959 by Quemar Co. Ltd. of Bedford, for $21,825.[^1] Commercial power reached the station in 1962, and an electronic horn replaced the diaphone in 1972.[^1]

The lantern was taken off the 1930 building in 1963, and a new light tower went up.[^2] The 1930 building remained in use as the keeper's house until 1988.[^2]

When Keeper Lamont Lovitt retired in 1982, his assistant was promoted to head keeper, and the assistant keeper's post was eliminated.[^1]

In 1983 the 1930 lighthouse was to be offered for sale.[^1] The Deputy Minister of Fisheries asked to use the dwelling for field officers researching hatchery-reared juvenile lobsters released near Bickerton Island, and the request saved the building.[^1] Hector Lowe, the last keeper, left in 1988, after the light had been automated.[^1] Rowlett says the building was then converted into an office for a biological research project.[^2]

In the 1990s the station was named a finalist site for a PCB incinerator.[^1] More than 400 area residents signed a petition against the choice, and local people pressed to turn the 1930 lighthouse into an interpretive centre.[^1]

On 28 June 1997 a local planning body opened the lighthouse as an interpretive centre.[^1][^2] The project included picnic areas and five kilometres of hiking trails.[^1][^2] Ownership of the site passed to the Municipality of the District of St. Mary's. LighthouseFriends dates the transfer to October 2012, while Rowlett says the council asked for it in 2012 and it was transferred on 15 June 2013.[^1][^2]

From 2014 the keeper's house was also run as an artist residence and a weekly vacation rental.[^1][^8]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard maintains the current light, which its list gives as a white flash every three seconds, shown 20.6 m above water with a nominal range of 17 nautical miles.[^3] The list also gives a fog horn that sounds two 3-second blasts in every minute, and it describes the light as year-round.[^3] The tower is 9.2 m high: a white square tower with a red top, beside a white building with a red roof.[^3]

The 1930 lighthouse is a museum.[^2] Rowlett says the museum and tower are open daily from 1 July to 30 September, with an admission fee, and the centre's own site describes it as open on a seasonal basis.[^2][^8]

The current tower is closed to visitors, and the Coast Guard is its operator.[^2] Rowlett gives the Municipality of the District of St. Mary's as owner of the site and the tower, while LighthouseFriends says the new tower is owned by the Coast Guard.[^2][^1] The site is reached from NS 211 in Port Bickerton.[^2]
