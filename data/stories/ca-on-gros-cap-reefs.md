Gros Cap Reefs Light is a concrete light on a sunk crib, on the southwest edge of Gros Cap Reef, where Lake Superior meets the St. Marys River, about 24 km (15 mi) from Sault Ste. Marie, Ontario.[^1][^2] A lightship anchored on the reef from 1923 to 1952, and the light that replaced her came later.[^1][^4]

## The place

Mariners leave Lake Superior and enter the St. Marys River here. LighthouseFriends describes the juncture as defined by Point Iroquois on the American shore and by Gros Cap, "four miles away, on the Canadian shore." At Gros Cap "steep cliffs rise up out of the water to a height of three hundred feet," while "dangerous reefs extend nearly halfway to the American shore."[^1]

The reef was a known hazard long before there was a light. A buoy had marked it since at least 1899. In that year, LighthouseFriends quotes Joseph Ripley of the U.S. Corps of Engineers: "The shoals off Gros Cap are a menace to navigation during foggy and stormy weather."[^1] In 1908 the Lake Carriers' Association "recommended that a lightship and fog signal be established at Gros Cap to replace a red gas buoy," LighthouseFriends says.[^1] Canada's own buoy went onto the reef in 1914, taking the place of an American one that had marked it for years.[^1]

## Building the light

The first answer was a ship. LighthouseFriends says the *St. Julien*, a minesweeper commissioned on 13 November 1917, "was decommissioned in 1920, and like three of her sister ships, was converted into a lightship."[^1] The Sault Ste. Marie library's column calls her a 140-foot vessel; LighthouseFriends gives her length as 130 feet, and neither source says which is right.[^1][^4]

The Department of Marine's report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1923 lists "repairs to Charleton point station, Gros Cap lightship, tower and fog alarm at Little Metis station" among the year's works, and says that "The Gros Cap lightship was also overhauled and painted."[^5] The Lake Carriers' Association put her on station in October 1923, so this work came before she went out.[^1] Her station was described, as LighthouseFriends quotes the association, as "Gros Cap Reefs Light-vessel No. 22." Her light was "a cluster of occulting white electric lights" on the foremast, "elevated 35 feet above the water," and in thick weather a steam whistle sounded "1 blast of 10 seconds duration every minute."[^1] A radiobeacon was added in 1942.[^1] The Department's report for the year ended 31 March 1924 records "Gros Cap lightship Refitting of vessel and machinery repaired and overhauled."[^6]

The light that replaced her came in 1952 and 1953. The sources disagree on when it was lit. Sault Ste. Marie's column says that on 25 August 1952 "what appeared to be the bow of a ship, slowly and methodically, trailed its way up through the Soo Locks." The column says the structure "was headed for the Lake Superior entrance of the St. Mary's River, near Whitefish Bay."[^4] LighthouseFriends says the lightship was pulled from her station in early December 1952, and quotes the *Sault Ste. Marie Evening News* of 9 December 1952, which said the new light "has been in temporary service for approximately three weeks."[^1] LighthouseFriends also says that "when completed in 1953," the new structure was "a concrete structure built atop a crib that was towed to the reef and sunk in place with rocks."[^1] Rowlett describes "a concrete crib that has a knife-edge similar to a ship's prow pointed northwest; this design splits ice floes in the winter."[^2] The column says the crib was "planted approximately six feet into the bottom of the river."[^4]

## The keepers

The lightship carried a regular crew of ten. The library's column says she "housed 10 crew members who were employed for the warmer months while she stood anchored on the reef," and that after the season "she travelled to a winter port in Georgian Bay."[^4] The 1952 *Evening News*, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, said that life aboard was "not unpleasant since there were always regular duties to perform daily," and that "a cribbage game below decks could be arranged almost any time."[^1]

Joseph Griffith had been captain of the lightship for about three years before she was replaced.[^1] The column names "Captain Griffiths along with Bert Oldstrum, Jack Martin and John Galloway" as the captains of the ship during her time at the reef.[^4] It says Captain Griffiths "had orders to take her to Midland" for refit before she went to the waters around Quebec City.[^4] LighthouseFriends says she was sold in 1958 and renamed *Centennial*.[^1]

Later, the light had keepers of its own. LighthouseFriends names only Basil Byers, under "Keepers: Basil Byers (1961 - 1969)."[^1] The library's column names Gavin McKiggan, who "was the lighthouse keeper for 15 years," and Marvin Moreau, who "also served at the Gros Cap lighthouse."[^4] The column reports that McKiggan felt the concrete structure shake from the ice below at times.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Department's casualty list for the year ended 31 March 1908 records that "The tug Philadelphia, of Sault Ste. Marie, stranded at North Gros, Cap. Ont., becoming a total loss," on 3 October 1907.[^7] The entry names North Gros Cap, not the reef, and no source ties that wreck to the light, which did not yet exist. Winter ice is the hazard the library's column describes: "In winter the icy waters could cover the entire structure and create a great amount of pressure."[^4]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the light was "destaffed in the 1980s," after a helipad and a submarine power cable were added.[^1] The library's column says the fog signal has been unmanned since 1987, and that "The lighthouse was quite self-sufficient," with radar and radio beacons.[^4] Rowlett says it "also carries a helipad and a tall triangular radio mast."[^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light, and the tower is closed.[^1][^2] Rowlett says the light is "Accessible only by boat."[^2] The Coast Guard's *List of Lights* gives its characteristic as "Fl W 10s," with a focal height of 19.7 m and a range of 14 nautical miles, and marks it "Seasonal."[^3] Rowlett's directory describes the light differently: a red flash every five seconds, with a focal plane of 18 m (59 ft).[^2] The two sources disagree, and this story does not settle which is current. LighthouseFriends says a distant view is possible from the grounds of Point Iroquois Lighthouse, and that a tour boat "passes near the lighthouse."[^1] Visitors reach the light by boat only.[^2]
