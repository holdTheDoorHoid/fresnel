Toledo Harbor Light stands in western Lake Erie at the outer end of the dredged ship channel into Maumee Bay, about eight miles (13 km) northeast of Toledo Harbor.[^4] It is a Romanesque dwelling of buff pressed brick on a crib-and-concrete pier, with a lantern rising from the middle of its roof, and it was built to house a head keeper and two assistants.[^4][^5]

## The place

By the late 1890s the Government had dredged a straight channel to Toledo Harbor, so that deep-draft lake vessels could reach the port. The Light-House Board's report for fiscal 1897 said this had been done "at great expense" and wrote that a permanent light and fog-signal station to mark the channel's outer end was "now urgently required". It put the cost at no more than $75,000.[^1] According to LighthouseFriends, the new light was to replace the older Turtle Island Lighthouse of 1831 as the mark for the mouth of the bay.[^4]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $37,500 on 1 July 1898 and allowed a contract of up to $75,000. Bids were opened on 5 March 1900, but the lowest was largely in excess of the amount planned for, and all the bids were rejected. An act of 6 June 1900 then authorized a contract at a cost of $100,000.[^2] After the work had been advertised twice, the board's 1901 report says, a contract for $84,700 was made in October 1900, covering the buildings and the foundation crib and due for completion by 31 December 1901.[^3]

Work began in 1901. A temporary post-lantern light was shown at the site on 4 May 1901, and the board's report for that year says the work of setting up a steam fog signal had begun.[^3] By the end of the fiscal year, 30 June 1901, the contractors had completed the crib, sunk it at the site and filled it.[^3] The contractors stopped on 23 November 1901, leaving one man at the site until 5 December to keep a light burning. They returned on 20 March 1902 to set the steel framing for the three-story brick house and its attached fog-signal building. By 9 December 1902 most of the structure was finished, and in 1903 the fog signal and lighting apparatus were installed.[^4] The fog signal that LighthouseFriends describes was a pair of oil engines driving air compressors for a siren, which differs from the steam plant of the 1901 report.[^4][^3]

The lantern held a third-and-a-half-order Fresnel lens made by Barbier & Benard of Paris. It first showed on the night of 23 May 1904.[^4] The USLHS archive gives 1904 as the year the station was established, so 1901 to 1903 is the building period and 1904 the first lighting.[^5]

## The keepers

The USLHS archive, working from the National Archives' keeper registers, lists Delos Hayden as principal keeper from 1903 to 1908, followed by August Gramer (1908-1909) and Charles F. Chapman (1909-1917).[^5] Hayden had come from West Sister Island Lighthouse, according to LighthouseFriends.[^4] Bert A. Dissett, who was first assistant from 1910 to 1917, became head keeper in 1917.[^5] He held the post until 1921, and LighthouseFriends says he won the lighthouse efficiency flag for the best-kept station in the district for 1919.[^4] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1918 to 1923 confirms an efficiency flag for the station.[^10]

Herman J. Schroeder served the longest. The archive shows him as an assistant, then first assistant, and then principal keeper from 1922 to 1938.[^5] Coast Guard personnel appear in the archive's list from 1947, and the last head keepers are Hal R. Alexander (1965-1966) and Earl M. Bibee (1966).[^5] The roster also lists a Mary F. Hayden as first assistant in 1905.[^5] The sources do not say whether she was related to Delos Hayden; a 1908 newspaper gives the name of his wife as Mary, but no source links the two.[^6][^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

**Winter 1908.** Two newspapers outside Toledo carried the story of Keeper Hayden's death. On Saturday, 8 February, one account says, Hayden and his cousin Joe Bernor set out across the ice to relieve the assistant, McNulty, who had been staying at the light with another man.[^6] The archive does list Joseph McNulty as first assistant in 1907-1908.[^5] Hayden fell ill with pneumonia and died on Thursday, 13 February, "conscious to the last" and leaving "a message for each of his relatives and friends."[^6][^7] Bernor put the body in a cool room and opened the windows, then waited for the ice to become passable. The papers differ on the length of his wait, with one reporting six days and another seven.[^6][^7] The ice around the lighthouse was "soft and mushy and full of air holes", and on Wednesday afternoon he set out for shore without even a pike pole to test it.[^6] The Omaha paper, by contrast, says the ice around the lighthouse was "firm under the snow" when he started, though the trip was still extremely perilous.[^7] He reached Toledo with the news after what the Superior paper called a journey of 19 miles over the ice.[^6] LighthouseFriends calls Bernor Hayden's assistant, but the newspapers describe him as a cousin, and the archive's assistants for the period are other men.[^4][^6][^5]

**November 1908.** LighthouseFriends reports that Keeper Gramer was burned on the arm when a can of alcohol exploded as he started the fog-signal engine. He and Assistant Fred Delaney started for medical help in the station's launch but were lost in dense fog and needed eight hours to reach shore.[^4] The archive lists Fred J. Delaney as second assistant from 1908 to 1909.[^5]

**Rescues.** The 1914 volume of the Lighthouse Service Bulletin records that on 31 October First Assistant Dissett towed to port a disabled launch "with eight men on board" that was in danger of drifting out into the lake in a rising wind. LighthouseFriends dates the rescue to 1915.[^9][^4] In 1924 the Bulletin noted that Keeper Schroeder, on 7 September, had towed the occupants of a disabled power boat to their destination.[^11]

## After the keepers

Gramer's time ended in dispute. LighthouseFriends reports that he was suspended in August 1909 after one of his assistants filed charges, among them that he had threatened the life of assistant William L. Gordon.[^4] A news dispatch of 9 September 1909 says Roscoe House of Buffalo, a Lighthouse Board clerk, went out by tug with orders to take the light by force if necessary and hand it to "Gramer's successor." Gramer, it adds, "offered no resistance" and left on the tug.[^8]

The station was modernised in the 1930s and 1940s. LighthouseFriends reports an air diaphone fog signal on 1 November 1934 and electrification of the light later that month, a radiophone in 1935 and a radiobeacon in 1942.[^4] An electric motor to turn the lens was fitted by 1966, and the last Coast Guard crew left in August 1966.[^4] To discourage vandals, a uniformed mannequin was put in an upper window. It first had a penciled moustache and later a long blonde wig, and the ghost stories of a phantom keeper are usually traced to it.[^4][^14]

The original lens was taken out in the late 1990s.[^4] The Toledo Harbor Lighthouse Preservation Society says it owns the lens, now displayed at the Nature Center at Maumee Bay State Park.[^13] LighthouseFriends dates the society's formation to 2003.[^4] The Secretary of the Interior approved its application to own the lighthouse on 5 October 2006, and LighthouseFriends gives April 2007 for the deed.[^4][^12]

## Today

The light is still an active aid to navigation, and the Coast Guard is still responsible for maintaining it, while the Preservation Society owns the building.[^4][^14] The inside is not open to the public. The society says it can be seen from the shore near the marina, about five miles away, and boat trips during the Toledo Lighthouse Festival have offered a closer view of the outside.[^13][^14] The landing dock was lost to storms (the society's pages date the loss to 2009 and to 2010), and the society says a new dock and lift are close to completion with public access hoped for in 2027.[^13] A 2020 contract of $474,368 funded a first phase focused on the first and second floors, with the third floor, light tower, cellar and annex left for later.[^4] The light stands on a pier in open water and is closed to visitors; it can be seen from shore or on the society's organised trips.[^13][^14]
