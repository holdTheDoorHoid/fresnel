Race Rock Light is a granite house and tower set on a pier of stone and concrete in Long Island Sound, about 8 miles from New London, Connecticut, off the western end of Fishers Island in Suffolk County, New York. It was built in the middle of a tideway, on a ledge that lies under water, and LighthouseFriends says it took seven years to build the foundation and pier before the house could rise.[^1][^2]

## The place

The light marks the Race, the gap through which the tide of the Sound rushes in both directions "with great velocity and force," as the Light-House Board's 1889 description puts it.[^1] LighthouseFriends, drawing on the Board's reports, describes a chain of islands (Fishers, Little Gull, Great Gull and Plum) spanning most of the water between Orient Point on Long Island and Watch Hill in Rhode Island. The deepest opening, just off the western end of Fishers Island near Race Rock, has a current that can reach five knots, and eight vessels are said to have stranded around it in an eight-year stretch of the early 1800s.[^2] The New London Maritime Society adds that the roiling water sits above a canyon of some 300 feet, and gives 1671 as the earliest recorded wreck and the steamship *Atlantic* in 1846, with 45 lives lost, as the worst.[^4]

Marking the rock took several tries. By LighthouseFriends' account, the Lighthouse Board wrote in 1852 that Race Rock was "one of the most dangerous obstructions to navigation on the coast"; buoys were swept off it, and iron spindles lasted only until the ice broke up in spring. Congress granted $7,000 on 3 March 1853, and an iron day-beacon went up in 1856, only to be "thrown down" in 1863 and replaced by a buoy.[^2]

## Building the light

In July 1866 Congress set aside $90,000 for a lighthouse on the rock or on the southwest end of Fishers Island. Plans for a granite tower on the rock were drawn up from soundings taken with an iron rod, but a better apparatus showed that the site was not one boulder but a heap of smaller ones, which ruled out a cofferdam. The Board then designed a broad granite pier carrying a dwelling and tower, so the keepers would live on the structure instead of nearly a mile away on Fishers Island, at an estimated $200,000.[^2] Congress returned the old money to the Treasury in 1870, then provided $10,000 in July of that year and $150,000 the following March.[^2]

The engineer was Francis Hopkinson Smith, with the New London diver Thomas A. Scott as foreman of the work.[^3] By LighthouseFriends' account foundation work began in April 1871, though the Board's 1889 description says the work "was commenced in 1872."[^2][^1] A riprap base of ten thousand tons of stone was finished that November, but outer rocks went missing, and Smith put on diving gear himself to look. He persuaded the Board that a concrete foundation was needed instead, which meant lifting about 1,000 tons of the placed stone when that work began in May 1873.[^2] The Maritime Society says Smith and Scott had been given wrong information about the depth and shape of the sea bottom.[^3] Smith later wrote that this was "my first work of any magnitude, in a 'race' that ran six miles an hour," and that success depended on "the nerve, pluck and loyalty of the men who handle the material."[^3] LighthouseFriends adds that Smith lived on site in a shanty during the working months, and that he vowed not to cut his hair until a certain course of the pier was finished.[^2]

The Light-House Board's description gives the finished foundation as a circular, stepped mass of concrete 9 feet (2.7 m) thick in four layers, its lowest 69 feet (21 m) across and its top 8 inches above low water. On it sits a conical stone pier 30 feet (9 m) high, with cisterns and cellars inside, topped by a granite dwelling with the tower rising from the centre of its front.[^1] The sources differ on some figures. The Board's 1889 text gives the pier 67 feet in diameter at the base and a dwelling "one story and a half high," while LighthouseFriends gives 57 feet and a two-story dwelling.[^1][^2]

The dwelling and tower went up in one season, 1878. Because the first lighting is dated both ways, it is worth being exact: the Light-House Board says the light was first shown "on the night of January 1, 1879," the Maritime Society agrees, and the US Lighthouse Society's archive gives the station's establishment as 1878.[^1][^3][^25] The tower carried a fourth-order flashing light, 68½ feet above the sea, alternately red and white by LighthouseFriends' description.[^1][^2] Inside, the house had two kitchens, two sitting rooms and two dining rooms downstairs and five bedrooms above, to serve a head keeper and two assistants.[^2] The Maritime Society thinks Race Rock was probably the last stone lighthouse built in the United States.[^3]

## The keepers

The first head keeper, Neil Martin, served from 1878 to 1882, and LighthouseFriends says he went on to Stamford Harbor and Penfield Reef.[^25][^2]

One keeper's story from these years is a false obituary. In April 1892 a Waterbury paper reported that Christopher Culver, aged 24 and keeper of the light, "was drowned Saturday evening" trying to reach the station from New London, where he had spent a few days with his wife and child. He had set out in a small rowboat for the lighthouse, eight miles off, in a rough sea, and the boat was seen to upset about two miles from shore.[^15] The next day the same paper corrected itself: Culver "arrived here alive and well," having been unable to reach his station.[^16] LighthouseFriends supplies the rest of the story: he had lowered his sail, rowed to Great Hay Harbor on Fishers Island, and was sheltered at a farm until a steamer brought him back to New London.[^2] The roster shows Culver as head keeper 1891 to 1898, with a short interval under Elmer J. Rathbun, and again from 1898 to 1901.[^25]

A Connecticut paper noted in May 1907 that Arthur W. Blish of Hartford, second assistant at Race Rock, had been appointed first assistant.[^17] LighthouseFriends reports that in March 1908 Blish took second assistant Adolph Hansen toward Fishers Island for shore leave; their skiff capsized in storm-driven surf, and Blish, though exhausted, waded out and pulled the injured Hansen to safety. Hansen resigned after a little over a month on the job.[^2] The archive roster has both men and lists Adolph Obman as his successor.[^25]

The roster also shows very short stints. In 1911, for example, it lists James J. Bryan as first assistant from 1 to 15 September, and several other assistants for periods of days or weeks.[^25] The longest-serving name is William O. Chapel, listed as head keeper from 1943 to 1963.[^25] After 1959 the archive lists Coast Guard crew in rotating one- or two-year stints.[^25]

The Maritime Society tells a darker tale of a keeper named Carroll, appointed in 1880, who is said to have been caught ashore by a January 1885 storm and was "last seen alive rowing out through the powerful waves alone in his small boat."[^22] The archive roster does not match this: it lists a Thomas S. Carroll at Race Rock through 1886.[^25] The two accounts are not reconciled here.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

**Fog.** In 1895 the Light-House Board reported that the station, "built at large expense," would be far more useful with a fog signal. In fog, it wrote, the light-station was "a source of great anxiety to navigators if not a positive danger," and it asked for $3,000.[^5] A second-class siren driven by an oil engine was established on 20 October 1896.[^7] Between 22 March and 7 April 1897 it was disabled, and a bell was struck instead.[^8] The report for the year to June 1900 records a new siren and trumpet being fitted and a duplicate engine bought, and gives the siren about 509 hours of operation.[^9][^10] On 8 June 1901 duplicate oil engines for the fog signal were installed, and the report for the year to June 1901 again gives 509 hours, the same figure as the year before.[^11][^12] LighthouseFriends dates a change to a third-class Daboll trumpet to 1905 and a first-class air siren to 1917.[^2]

**Riprap.** The reports return more than once to the stone heaped around the pier. A contract to place 400 tons of riprap around the pier was let in the Board's 1896 report year.[^6] A Commissioner of Lighthouses estimate printed in the volume's 1918 report listed Race Rock among third-district stations needing riprap and better boat landings, at $16,600, noting that ice had damaged many foundations in the past winter and that "landing in bad weather is both difficult and hazardous."[^13] By 1922 bids were about to be invited for riprap work there and at Little Gull Island.[^14]

**Collisions and rescues.** A newspaper of late September 1908 reported the Fall River liner *Commonwealth* striking and sinking the Norwegian steamer *Volund* in dense fog "off Race Rock"; the *Volund*'s officer, the captain's wife and crew were rescued and taken aboard the liner.[^18] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1931 reports that keeper George H. Tooker, first assistant Ernest F. Witty and second assistant Emanuel Weber were commended by the Department of Commerce. They had climbed out on the sea-swept rocks carrying a line to Captain Hazell of a barge that broke adrift from a tug and was carried ashore in a heavy gale, all of them in imminent danger of being washed away.[^19]

**Gales and ice.** In the New England storm of November 1935 the station boat, lashed at the dock, was damaged beyond use.[^20] A keeper's report of January 1936, reproduced by LighthouseFriends, tells of seas over the dock, the motor boat sheathed in ice up to 20 inches thick on the stern, and the keeper and first assistant spending two days chopping her out.[^2] In March 1936 the steamer *Willboro* grounded close to the lighthouse with a cargo of lumber; the Maritime Society adds that there had been no shipwreck or loss of life near the light since it began working, although the 1908 wire report above places a sinking "off Race Rock."[^3][^18] The Lighthouse Service reported that Race Rock was among the Long Island Sound stations principally damaged by the 1938 hurricane.[^21]

## After the keepers

According to LighthouseFriends, the Coast Guard automated Race Rock and removed its personnel on 5 October 1978, three months short of the centenary of its lighting; a metal box of electronics replaced the keepers.[^2] The fourth-order Fresnel lens gave way to a solar-powered rotating beacon flashing red every ten seconds, and a horn on the platform sounds two blasts every thirty seconds.[^4] In 2004, LighthouseFriends writes, a paranormal-investigation team visited at the Coast Guard's reported request after maintenance crews reported strange occurrences.[^2]

The station is on the National Register of Historic Places (reference 05000347); the Park Service's record is dated 29 April 2005.[^24] In June 2011 the Coast Guard declared it excess, and on 27 June 2013 a deed ceremony transferred it to the New London Maritime Society.[^2]

## Today

The archive lists Race Rock as an active aid to navigation, and the New London Maritime Society has owned it since 2013.[^25][^22] The Society says the Coast Guard named the rusted, unsafe iron railing as the most needed repair, and that a grant paid for a new railing made to the original design, due to be installed in August 2025.[^22] It stands offshore, its tower closed, and is best seen from a boat.[^2] In August 2026 the Society ran a limited set of guided excursions, the first in three years, departing Silver Eel Cove on Fishers Island by landing craft; they were scheduled for a single day, 26 August 2026, and have already passed. Anyone who wants to go ashore on the rock should wait for the Society to announce another date.[^23]
