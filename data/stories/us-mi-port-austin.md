Port Austin Reef Light stands on a pier in Lake Huron about a mile and a half off the tip of Michigan's Thumb, marking a reef at the corner where ships turn into Saginaw Bay.[^2] It was built in the 1870s as an offshore station with its keepers living ashore, rebuilt in brick at the end of the century,[^1] and today belongs to a local volunteer association that runs boat tours.[^3][^14]

## The place

Port Austin takes its name from P. C. Austin, who arrived in 1838 and set up a lumber mill and dock.[^1] By the mid-1870s, more and more vessels were rounding Pointe aux Barques into and out of Saginaw Bay, and the reef off the point was a real hazard to them; Pepper describes most of it as lying just below the surface, with a few small parts awash.[^1] The Port Austin Reef Light Association describes the light as sitting at the southerly side of the entrance to Saginaw Bay, a turning point for northbound and westbound traffic.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $10,000 on 3 March 1873 for a light at Port Austin, and the Eleventh District engineer, Major Godfrey Weitzel, sent a survey crew that year. After two years of failed negotiations with a landowner, a site about 200 feet east was chosen in 1875, and plans for a brick dwelling with an attached tower were approved on 6 September 1875.[^1] Before work began, the Light-House Board persuaded Congress that the danger called for an offshore light on the reef instead. An appropriation of 31 July 1876 allowed the earlier money to be spent on the reef, provided the total cost stayed within $85,000, and the keepers' dwelling would stay on the shore reservation already bought.[^1]

The pier began as an octagonal oak crib, 80 feet (24 m) across, assembled at Tawas, where timber could be had, and then towed to Pointe aux Barques, where it was built up to 10 feet (3 m) in height. Water let into its sealed pockets sank it onto a patch of the reef that had been cleared and levelled. It was bolted to the rock, faced with brick and filled with cement and crushed stone from Grindstone City.[^1] Terry Pepper's account dates the sinking of the crib to Sunday, 12 August 1877 yet says construction began in July 1878; the Association's timeline puts the start of construction in July 1877. The dates cannot all be right, and the accounts do not settle which are.[^1][^3]

Because the $85,000 limit left no room for a masonry tower, the pier carried a timber-framed pyramid 57 feet (17 m) high. Its cast-iron lantern housed a fourth-order Fresnel lens made by Henry Lepaute of Paris, and a clockwork motor turned the lens through a two-minute characteristic: a minute of steady white light, then five red flashes.[^1] The first keepers moved into the shore dwelling on 9 September 1878 and lit the light on 15 September 1878; the finished work cost $81,871.[^1]

Fog signals followed. In 1882 two frame buildings went up on the crib for a pair of steam sirens, and the boilers soon proved short of water, which had to be lifted some 33 feet from the lake; valves were replaced in 1886, a force pump added in 1887 and wooden tanks installed in 1888.[^1] In 1895 the sirens gave way to 10-inch steam whistles.[^1] In the fiscal year ended June 1900 the Light-House Board recorded that the whistle ran some 93 hours and burned about 11 tons of coal.[^4]

By the end of the century the weather had damaged the pier and the frame tower. The Board's report for the year ended June 1899 records a contract for new fog-signal boilers and engines and for materials "for rebuilding the tower and reconstructing the fog signal houses into one building".[^5] The next year's report says the brick buildings were practically finished by the close of the year, the pier top was repaved with brick, a landing crib was rebuilt, a boat crane set and a stairway built, and the lens moved from the old frame tower to the new brick one.[^6] The Board's list of changes gives 12 April 1900 as the date the light was established in the new tower.[^7] The 1901 report describes a square, buff-brick tower four stories high with a living room and kitchen, joined to the fog-signal building, with a cast-iron watch room lined with oak and a lantern at a focal plane of 54 feet (16 m) from the base of the tower.[^8] The station still used the shore dwelling as the keepers' main home.[^1]

## The keepers

The roster lists nine principal keepers between 1878 and 1942.[^9] The first, Charles A. Kimball, came from Pointe aux Barques, where Pepper says he had been first assistant for six years; Aaron Peer was his first assistant, and Pepper names Alonzo Kimball as Charles's brother and second assistant, reporting on 18 October 1878.[^1][^9] A Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1925 noted the retirement of Charles Kimball, then keeper at Marquette, at age 71, after almost 53 years of service that began at Pointe aux Barques on 27 September 1872 and took him through Port Austin Reef and Whitefish Point as keeper.[^10] The Bulletin gives no middle initial, so the match with the first keeper rests on the name and the shared stations.

Two later men stand out for how long they stayed. The archive lists Lauchling McDonald as first assistant from 1906 to 1942, and Frank W. Marshall as principal keeper from 1921 to 1942.[^9] Frank E. Kimball was principal keeper from 1883 to 1894, and Herbert N. Burrows, listed as an assistant from 1889, became principal keeper in 1894.[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 21 February 1917, according to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin of May 1917, first assistant Lauchling McDonald and second assistant Edward C. Towns helped the local Coast Guard rescue three fishermen adrift on the ice near the light station.[^11][^12] The Association says the steamer *Howard M. Hanna Jr.* came to rest just north-east of the light in the great storm of 1913.[^2]

The Lighthouse Service reported that Port Austin Reef and Passage Island were "subjected to extremely cold weather" in late fall and early spring and had had difficulty keeping enough cooling water for their diesel air compressors when the fog signals sounded for long periods, so auxiliary heaters were fitted to cool the circulating water.[^13] Pepper adds that the steam whistles were replaced in 1933 by compressed-air diaphones, which could sound within minutes where the steam units needed up to an hour to build pressure; he also records three feet of concrete added around the pier, giving the work in 1933 in one paragraph and 1937 in the next, and the Association's timeline puts it in 1937.[^1][^3]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over the nation's lights in 1939, a submarine cable brought electric power from shore, and a 25,000-candlepower electric lamp replaced the oil-vapor lamp.[^1][^3] The Fresnel lens was removed in 1942 according to Pepper and the Association's timeline, though the Association's history page says 1941.[^1][^2][^3] The light was automated in 1953.[^1][^3] Afterward the pier decayed: roof, windows and doors were damaged, and the Coast Guard considered replacing the station with a skeleton tower.[^1] Port Austin businessman Louis Shillinger (Lou Schillinger in the Association's spelling) gathered a group of acquaintances into the Port Austin Reef Light Association (PARLA). Pepper puts the Association's first five-year Coast Guard licence in 1988 and the Association's timeline puts it in 1985; the volunteers' early work included clearing the bird droppings and rebuilding the fog-signal roof.[^1][^3]

## Today

The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 15 September 2011, and ownership passed from the Coast Guard to PARLA in 2013.[^3] A 2022 state grant of $600,000 paid for exterior restoration, completed in 2023, followed by interior work finished in 2025.[^3] The light is still an active aid to navigation.[^2][^9] It sits offshore and can be reached only by boat; the Association's 2026 tours ran on Saturdays, with groups limited to six and no children under twelve, and the season has ended.[^14] The Association says a fourth-order Fresnel lens was lit again on 23 May 2026 for a live broadcast.[^3]
