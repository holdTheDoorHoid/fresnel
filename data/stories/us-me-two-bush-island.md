Two Bush Island Light stands on Two Bush Island, off Spruce Head in Knox County, Maine, where Two-Bush Channel leads into West Penobscot Bay.[^1][^2] The Lighthouse Board asked Congress for the light in 1892, and it was first lit on 10 November 1897.[^5][^8]

## The place

Two Bush Island sits at the edge of two channels into West Penobscot Bay.[^5] The Lighthouse Board's report for the fiscal year 1892 described Muscle Ridge Channel as "the shortest and most direct route in or out of the West Bay." It also called the channel narrow, and said that in fog and calms vessels anchoring in the channelway often obstructed it.[^5]

The Board called Two-Bush Channel "the widest and cleanest channel into the West Bay," and placed Two Bush Island "in this latter or more northern turn" of the route.[^5] A four-month count kept at the Whitehead light station in the spring of 1892 showed that "nearly two- thirds as many vessels pass by Two-Bush Channel without light or fog signal, as by Muscle Ridge with both." The Board said this "seems to indicate the need of opening up this channel" to navigators, and proposed "a flashing light and fog signal" on the island's southeastern point.[^5]

The Board's estimate covered a brick tower, a double keeper's dwelling, a boathouse and slip, a bell tower with bell and striking machine, and barn and outbuildings.[^5] It put the total "not exceeding $19,000," including the cost of the island and expense of title.[^5]

## Building the light

The Board's list of sums asked for 1894–95 carries "Two-Bush Island light and fog signal, Maine" at $19,000.[^6] LighthouseFriends gives 18 August 1894 as the day Congress appropriated the money.[^2] Buying the island proved impracticable, so the government took the title by condemnation. The Board's report for 1896 records that, and that the buildings were expected to go up that season.[^7][^2]

The contract went to W. H. Glover Co. of Rockport, for $12,250, LighthouseFriends reports. Its brick tower stood near the southwest end of the island on a stone foundation. A double frame dwelling went up forty feet (12 m) northeast of the tower, and a boathouse at the northwest end of the island.[^2]

The Board's report for 1897 says the station's buildings, "for which contract was made, are well advanced," and records that "the light and fog signal were established on November 10, 1897."[^8]

The lens was a fifth-order revolving Fresnel, and from that night it sent a white flash every five seconds, LighthouseFriends reports.[^1][^2]

The fog signal building was brick. Its clockwork and 1,400-pound weights could toll a fog bell for three and a half hours, LighthouseFriends says, and the 4,188-pound bell was struck two blows every twenty seconds when needed.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive lists the principal keepers from Aldiverd A. Norton (1897 to 1909) to Harry M. Beal (1964), along with the first assistants and a group of Coast Guard names from the 1960s.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls Norton "the first keeper of Two Bush."[^2]

Leland Mann is where the sources part company. The archive lists him as first assistant from 1919 to 1921 and as head keeper from 1921 to 1933.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he came to the island as an assistant in 1919 and was made head keeper in 1927.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin calls him "keeper" in 1922 and 1923, which does not settle the rank.[^3][^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Bulletin for 1922 records that on 13 November Keeper Mann rescued a motor boat, with one man aboard, that was drifting toward the sea before a strong northwest wind. The entry gives no year.[^3] LighthouseFriends dates the rescue to 1922.[^2] The Bulletin for 1923 records that on 2 June Mann "rendered assistance to the occupant of a small boat which was discovered drifting before a northwest wind."[^4]

## After the keepers

The light had a red daymark until the spring of 1914, when it was painted white to improve its visibility, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] LighthouseFriends also reports a fourth-order lens installed in the tower in 1902. The archive records only the 1897 fifth-order optic, so the 1902 change rests on LighthouseFriends alone.[^1][^2]

The station was automated in 1964. On 26 August that year the fog signals and new electric compressors began running under remote control from Whitehead Light, and the last Coast Guard crew left Two Bush Island at 3 p.m. on 25 September.[^1][^2]

LighthouseFriends reports that the Coast Guard allowed the U.S. Army's Green Berets to blow up the keepers' duplex as a demolition exercise in 1970, after bids for removing it exceeded the budget.[^2] The station's fog bell hangs in the belfry of the Spruce Head Community Church.[^2]

Ownership passed to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 1998. The light was converted to solar power in 2000.[^2]

## Today

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service owns the lighthouse and island, which are part of the Maine Coastal Islands National Wildlife Refuge.[^2][^9] The refuge's main job is restoring and managing colonies of nesting seabirds.[^9] The archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are closed during the seabird-nesting season, which runs from April through August, and the tower is closed. It adds that the light is best seen by boat or from the air.[^2]
