Sherwood Point Light is a red-brick keeper's house with a short square tower built into its gable, set on a limestone bluff where Sturgeon Bay meets Green Bay in Door County, Wisconsin. It was lit in 1883, and a hundred years later it was the last light on the Great Lakes to lose its resident crew.[^1][^2]

## The place

Sturgeon Bay grew first on lumber and then on shipbuilding. Vessels bound for its yards had to sail around the whole Door peninsula, so local businessmen formed the Sturgeon Bay and Lake Michigan Ship Canal and Harbor Company, and digging began in 1872.[^3] By 1880 small vessels could use the cut, and the company wanted a light to mark the bay's western approach. The Lighthouse Board agreed to build one if the company bought a site and deeded it to the government.[^3] The Board chose a bluff about 30 feet (9 m) high known as Sherwood Point; Lighthousefriends says it is named for Peter Sherwood, who settled there in 1836.[^4]

Getting title took two years. Lighthousefriends says the owner of record could not be brought to terms, the canal company had the land condemned under a Wisconsin law, paid $125 for it and passed it to the United States.[^4]

## Building the light

The HAER inventory says Congress appropriated $12,000 on 3 March 1881.[^2][^4] Terry Pepper describes a work party of 20 men landing materials from the lighthouse tender *Warrington* at Sawyers Harbor, then hauling them by team over a new road to the site. Crews blasted a cellar from the rock and built a 1,500-gallon brick cistern under the kitchen end.[^3] Lighthousefriends adds that the bricks came from Detroit and were red, unlike the cream Milwaukee brick used at other lights nearby.[^4]

The result was a brick dwelling of six rooms with a square tower in the centre of its north gable.[^3] The HAER inventory measures the house at 25 by 37 feet (7.6 by 11.3 m) and the tower at 26 feet (8 m) to the parapet and 37 feet (11 m) to the top of the ventilator ball.[^2] The inventory gives the focal plane as 61 feet (19 m); Pepper says 60 feet.[^2][^3]

The sources disagree about the lens. Pepper has the district lampist assembling a fourth-order Fresnel lens made in France, showing a fixed white light with a red flash every 60 seconds from three rotating ruby panels driven by a falling weight.[^3] The 1979 inventory, however, reads a fifth-order lens marked "Barbier & Fenestre, Constr. Paris 1880."[^2] Pepper and Lighthousefriends both call the lens taken out in 2002 a fourth-order lens, and this account does not settle the question.[^3][^4]

Work was finished on 28 September, and Henry Stanley, who had kept Eagle Bluff light for about 15 years, was chosen as keeper. He showed the light for the first time on the night of 10 October 1883.[^3]

## The keepers

Stanley's trouble was the clockwork that turned the red panels. Within a short time he was paying a Sturgeon Bay clockmaker to come out and repair it.[^3] On 4 October 1890 the *Door County Advocate*, as quoted by Pepper, said he had "no end of trouble since entering his duties down there to make the thing work at all times, it being frequently necessary for him or an assistant to move the machinery by hand."[^3]

Supplies came a hard way. For seven years they were lightered ashore at Sawyers Harbor and carried along a rough half-mile trail.[^3] A $100 appropriation in 1890 bought land for an approach to the station, and the deed was returned by the Door County register on 27 August 1891.[^6] (Pepper dates the appropriation 1899, but the Light-House Board's report says the act was approved on 30 August 1890.)[^3][^6]

Stanley's niece Minnie Hesh came to live at the station after her parents died. Lighthousefriends says she helped run the light and wrote many of the log entries; she married William Cochems, a local man, in August 1889.[^4] When Cochems's hardware store failed in the Panic of 1893 Stanley hired him as a laborer, and in 1894 the Cochemses moved into the station. Pepper says Stanley, then 70, had persuaded the district inspector that he needed an assistant.[^4][^3] (Lighthousefriends instead has Cochems promoted to assistant in May 1895, when Stanley fell ill.[^4]) Stanley died on 13 October 1895 and Cochems became keeper. Stanley's widow Katharine was made assistant, which Lighthousefriends calls the nearest thing to a pension she could get; she spent winters with friends in Sturgeon Bay.[^3][^4][^1]

Pepper says the lampist replaced the whole lens, pedestal and rotating gear on 23 March 1898 with the apparatus from Passage Island light and a new ball-bearing mechanism. Katharine resigned on 24 June and Minnie took the assistant's post.[^3] The Cochemses could stay all year, and Minnie served until she collapsed and died at the station on 17 August 1928.[^4][^3] William put up a plaque in the garden: "In memory of Minnie Hesh Cochems, Assistant Light-Keeper, 1898 - 1928."[^3] No assistant was appointed after her, and William retired on 1 July 1933 after almost 39 years at the station.[^3]

Conrad Stram came from the Sturgeon Bay Canal light.[^3] From 23 November 1937 he broadcast a weather report twice a day from the station's radio, WWOC, and after the Peshtigo Reef light began work in 1935 he had to visit it each week.[^4][^3] Lighthousefriends reports he won the Efficiency Flag in 1938.[^4] He stayed civilian until 1941, when he joined the Coast Guard, and retired in 1945.[^3]

The station's equipment grew too. The Board added a fog bell on a timber tower in front of the house, struck by machinery and in operation from 1 July 1892; Pepper says it rang a single stroke every 12 seconds and needed winding every four hours.[^5][^3] A report of 1899 records boat rollers provided and trees cut down that had hidden the light.[^7] A brick oil house followed in 1902.[^3] Electricity and running water arrived in the early 1930s.[^4] Pepper says that in 1940 electric horns replaced the bell and the bell tower became a radio room; the HAER inventory records a small addition to the bell tower in 1935, and Lighthousefriends places the radio station in an addition to the tower.[^3][^2][^4]

## After the keepers

Coast Guard crews staffed the station until the fall of 1983, when Sherwood Point was the last Great Lakes light to be automated.[^3][^1] A lighted buoy has since replaced the station's radiobeacon and fog signals.[^4] On 21 October 2002 the Fresnel lens was taken out because its prisms had deteriorated, and a 300 mm acrylic optic took its place; the old lens went to the Door County Maritime Museum.[^3]

## Today

The light is owned by the Coast Guard, and the grounds are closed to the public except on organised open days such as the Door Peninsula Lighthouse Passport Days.[^4][^3] Do not enter the grounds outside those days.
