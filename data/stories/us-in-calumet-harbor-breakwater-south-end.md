The Calumet Harbor Breakwater South End light stands at the far end of a detached breakwater at the entrance to the Calumet River, on the southern shore of Lake Michigan. Its steel skeletal tower was first built in 1907 for Holland, Michigan, and moved to Calumet, where it went into service in 1937.[^1][^2]

## The place

The mouth of the Calumet River is in Illinois, but the breakwaters that shelter the harbor extend into Indiana waters.[^1][^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' annual report for 1920 called Calumet Harbor "one of the most important on Lake Michigan," with about 2,300 arrivals and departures a year.[^3]

A 600-foot (180 m) gap between the old breakwater and the detached one gives ships a direct way from the lake into the river.[^2]

## Building the light

In 1934 plans were begun for two new lights on the detached breakwater, which was then under construction.[^2] The tower placed at the far end was an older one. It had been built in 1907 as the north pierhead light at Holland, Michigan, and Holland replaced it in 1936.[^1]

The tower was moved to Calumet and set up at the southeast end of the detached breakwater. The new light and fog signal were controlled remotely from the breakwater lighthouse, and they went into commission on 4 June 1937.[^2]

Rowlett describes a square steel skeletal tower of 45 feet (13.5 m), with a focal plane of 50 feet (15 m). Its light flashes every five seconds, alternately red and white.[^1]

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive dates the station's establishment to 1935, two years before the light was commissioned.[^4][^2]

## The keepers

The sources name no keeper of its own for the south-end light. Its light and fog signal were controlled remotely from the breakwater lighthouse.[^2] The archive's place record lists no people.[^4]

## After the keepers

The breakwater lighthouse at the end of the older breakwater, which worked this light, was automated on 11 November 1966.[^2]

In 1995 that breakwater lighthouse was torn down over twenty-two days, and a white cylindrical tower with a red band stands in its place. A tower with a green band, of similar design, marks the nearby end of the detached breakwater.[^2]

The south-end light is now solar powered. Its lantern has been removed, and the light is shown from the top of the tower.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light and manages the site, which is closed to the public. It can be reached only by boat, and neither the site nor the tower is open.[^1][^4]

Rowlett lists the light as active, but the Society's place record answers "No" to whether it is an active aid to navigation.[^1][^4]

The fog horn gives a two-second blast every fifteen seconds.[^1]
