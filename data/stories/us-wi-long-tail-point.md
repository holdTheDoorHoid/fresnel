Long Tail Point Light stood on a sand spit in Green Bay, Wisconsin, and it had three lighthouses over the years. LighthouseFriends says only the first, a stone tower of 1848, still stands; the US Lighthouse Society lists the ruins as not existing.[^4][^1]

## The place

Long Tail Point is a sand spit that juts southeast into Green Bay for more than two and a half miles (4 km), starting from a point near the mouth of the Suamico River.[^4] In 1837 Congress appropriated $5,000 for a light at the entrance to Green Bay. A naval officer, Lieutenant G. J. Pendergrast, noted that the lighthouse under construction on Pottawatomie Island served that purpose. Another lieutenant, James T. Homans, recommended Tail Point instead. Congress did not pay for it until 1847, when it appropriated $4,000.[^4]

The fog bell did not work well at this spot. In 1894 the Light-House Board reported it to be "of little or no benefit in its present location," because it was so far from the channel that it could be heard only in the stillest weather.[^5]

## Building the light

Work began in 1847. The limestone came from the eastern shore of Green Bay near Bay Settlement, and it was carried out to the point in hand-poled scows.[^4] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives the tower's material as sandstone, so the sources disagree on the stone.[^1] The tower stood 51.5 feet (15.7 m) from base to ventilator ball, with walls four feet eight inches thick at the base. Nine lamps in fourteen-inch reflectors gave a focal plane of 56 feet (17 m).[^4]

A stone keeper's house of one and a half stories stood nearby.[^4] Peter White, the first keeper, activated the station in 1848, the year Wisconsin became a state.[^4][^1] The US Lighthouse Society gives 1848 for the tower, and LighthouseFriends gives 1848 for its activation, but LighthouseFriends' account of the 1870s calls it the 1849 stone lighthouse.[^1][^4] In 1856, when Thomas Atkinson was keeper, the light was refitted with an octagonal lantern and a sixth-order Fresnel lens.[^4] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1850 as the year the original lens was installed.[^1]

By 1859 high water surrounded the stone tower.[^4] The Board had a new wooden house built on higher ground, on ground-sills that rested on iron piles, and moved the lantern and lens into it.[^4] A fog bell was placed in operation on 5 July 1890.[^4]

In 1894 navigators urged that the bell and the light be moved to the spot marked by a red can buoy.[^5] The Board's report puts that buoy 1,650 yards (1,510 m) from the light, while LighthouseFriends gives 1,050 yards (960 m) southeast as the distance for the new crib.[^5][^4] Congress appropriated $7,500 by an act of 1 July 1898.[^6] Work on the new station began on 15 April 1899, and the Board's report for that year expected the light to be shown the next August.[^6] The Board's report for fiscal 1900 says the light and fog bell were established in the new structure on 1 August 1899, and that the old light and a temporary light on a pile cluster were discontinued.[^7] A different entry in the same report gives 31 July 1899 for the move.[^7]

The third light was a small frame house with a lantern, built on a concrete pier atop a wooden crib offshore. The 1859 house was kept as a dwelling.[^4]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists 26 keeper stints for the station across its three records: three at the first tower, seven at the second and sixteen at the third.[^1][^2][^3] At the first tower the keepers were Peter White (1848 to 1849), John P. Dousman (1849 to 1853) and Thomas Atkinson (1853 to 1859).[^1] At the second light, Flury David, John Ham and William Mitchell served, and George A. Gaylord was keeper from 1880 to 1899.[^2] Carl Witzmann was head keeper at the third light from 1910 to 1936 on the archive's list, although LighthouseFriends dates his appointment as keeper to 1920.[^3][^4]

George Gaylord was a native of Ohio who had sailed the Great Lakes for nearly forty years before he became keeper in 1880. He kept the light for nineteen years and was forced to retire in June 1899. LighthouseFriends says he died roughly two weeks later, at 72.[^4]

Witzmann said the great storm of 1929 was the strongest he had seen in his thirty-one years as keeper.[^4] Andrew Weber was first assistant from 1911 to 1935.[^3] LighthouseFriends says that he and his wife, Alice, raised three children during those years.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A fiscal 1919 Lighthouse Service table lists Carl Witzmann as keeper and A. Weber as first assistant at Tail Point. The scanned table prints its columns separately, so the link between the Tail Point row and the raft entry rests on position. The same table has an entry for three men rescued from a raft on which they had taken refuge after their vessel had capsized and sunk.[^8] LighthouseFriends ties that rescue to the motorboat *G. F. Laviolette*, and says Witzmann and Weber made it.[^4] The 1919 table already calls Witzmann keeper, which does not fit LighthouseFriends' 1920 date.[^8][^4]

In October 1929 a storm flooded the area around Green Bay. LighthouseFriends says the foundation of the keeper's dwelling crumbled, and that Witzmann climbed to the highest ground with a signal lantern. The tug *Greiling* found the signal after three hours and rescued him.[^4]

On 9 April 1973 a storm with winds of up to 70 miles an hour and 12-foot (3.7 m) waves destroyed the 1899 tower.[^4][^3]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1936.[^3][^4] The 1859 house was no longer needed. It was sold to a private buyer on the condition it be removed, because that year the surrounding land became the Long Tail Point Migratory Waterfowl Refuge. While the house was being moved that winter, the trailer it sat on broke through the ice. The house was taken apart on the spot, and its lumber was later used to build a silo on a farm in Suamico.[^4]

In the early 1870s the government gave the stone tower to William Mitchell, a former keeper, who wanted its stone. He gave up because the tower was "impervious to bar and pick."[^4] Later the government planned to demolish the stone tower in the early 1900s, but Commodore Arthur C. Neville and Judge Carlton Merrill petitioned Washington to save it.[^4]

## Today

LighthouseFriends says the first tower still stands on the spit, in the Long Tail Unit of the Green Bay West Shores Wildlife Area. The US Lighthouse Society's ruins record lists the station as not existing.[^4][^1] The Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources owns the site. The grounds are open and the tower is closed, and LighthouseFriends says the remains are best seen by boat or from the air.[^4] A modern light, known as Light 18, marks the tip of the spit.[^4]
