A brown cast-iron house on a cast-iron drum, Ship John Shoal Light stands in about 15 feet (4.6 m) of water in Delaware Bay, roughly three miles south of the mouth of the Cohansey River in Cumberland County, New Jersey.[^1] Its upper works were first put on show at the 1876 Centennial Exposition in Philadelphia, then taken down the bay and bolted onto a foundation that had been waiting for them since 1874.[^1]

## The place

Ships bound for Philadelphia came up Delaware Bay. The National Register nomination reports that by the 1870s more than 7,000 vessels arrived and departed the port each year, the second busiest in the country.[^1] The shoal takes its name from the ship *John*, which ran aground there in 1797 on a voyage to Philadelphia; the nomination and LighthouseFriends both give 1797, while a Delaware Bay keepers' association page gives 1787.[^1][^2][^10]

The Light-House Board's report of 1853 argued that the lights then standing on the bay's shores had been put up "solely with a view to the local trade in each case" and did little for vessels in the main channel.[^1] Congress authorised $30,000 in 1854 for a screw-pile light on the shoal, modelled on the one planned for Cross Ledge. After ice destroyed the Cross Ledge foundation in the winter of 1856, the Board's 1857 report called it "very doubtful" that screw-pile structures there or on Ship John Shoal could resist the ice, and no light followed until a new petition in 1872.[^1] That petition put the cost of a lighthouse at $125,000; Congress voted $50,000 in 1873.[^1]

## Building the light

Building began after New Jersey ceded about 4.9 acres of submerged land to the United States.[^1] The design was a caisson: a cylinder of cast-iron plates, 24 feet (7.3 m) across and 30 feet (9.1 m) tall, filled with concrete.[^1] The nomination dates the sinking of the caisson to 28 October 1874; LighthouseFriends gives 24 October.[^1][^2] A temporary wooden dwelling and lantern went on top, and the light was first shown on 29 November 1874.[^1] In the basement, a carved inscription on a brick platform still reads "Light First Exhibited Nov. 29, 1874."[^1]

The first winter tested the design. On 18 January 1875 the structure shook when ice struck it, and the keepers, fearing for their safety, left. LighthouseFriends quotes the Board's report that they "became alarmed for their safety" and hailed a passing steam tug; the bay was so choked with ice that nobody could reach the caisson until 13 March, when it was found unharmed and the light was shown again.[^2] The nomination gives the date of their return as 13 March 1876; LighthouseFriends gives no year and places it straight after the winter of 1874-75, so the two accounts differ by a year.[^1][^2] More riprap, stone dumped around the base, followed that year; LighthouseFriends puts the amount at 2,000 tons.[^1][^2]

The permanent house was a cast-iron Second Empire design with a mansard roof, one of only two light stations of that style built entirely of iron; the other is Southwest Ledge in Connecticut.[^1] The one meant for Ship John Shoal was shown at the Centennial Exposition, and LighthouseFriends says a keeper lived in it and tended a light there during the fair.[^1][^2] The nomination notes that its sources disagree over whether the Philadelphia building and the one meant for Southwest Ledge were swapped.[^1] The temporary house came off on 15 May, which the nomination places in 1876 and LighthouseFriends in 1877; lightship No. 24 was anchored nearby to show a light meanwhile.[^1][^2] Congress voted $25,000 on 31 July 1876 to finish the work.[^1] The nomination says the new light was first shown on 19 August 1877; LighthouseFriends and a local-history blog give 10 August.[^1][^2][^11] LighthouseFriends reports a fourth-order Fresnel lens by Sautter and a 1,200-pound fog bell struck three times every forty-five seconds.[^2]

The light was altered often. A letter printed in the Board's report for fiscal 1892, from the inspector of the fourth district, says that Ship John Shoal had been a fixed light, "frequently mistaken at first" for the lights of vessels, and that the occultations (eclipses) of the new lights let navigators identify them as soon as they were seen; the Board recorded the change to a fixed light of fifty-seven seconds with three-second eclipses, dated 20 June 1892.[^4] In 1890 the district office had proposed raising the tower 15 or 20 feet and moving to a third-order lens; it was never done.[^1] The 1912 Bulletin lists the station among lights changed to an incandescent oil-vapour lamp.[^12] The Bulletin of 1932 reported electric lamps in the fourth-order lenses and Tyfon air horns in place of the fog bell at Ship John Shoal and at Elbow of Cross Ledge; LighthouseFriends dates the work to 1931.[^8][^2]

## The keepers

The USLHS archive, working from the keeper registers, lists keepers and Coast Guard men from 1874 to 1971.[^3] Charles H. Sullivan was the first principal keeper, 1874 to 1875, with John McBride and Thomas Hawkins as assistants; William H.C. Knowles followed from 1875 to 1882.[^3] LighthouseFriends names Knowles as head keeper when the iron house was lit in 1877.[^2]

Thomas Wright appears in the archive as second assistant from 1876, first assistant from 1878 and principal keeper from 1882 to 1905, so he spent nearly three decades at the station.[^3] Alex Johnson then held the post from 1905 to 1932.[^3] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1915 records that on 2 October Johnson rescued a disabled, leaking launch and "with difficulty" towed it to a safe anchorage; the man aboard "would probably have been drowned" without help.[^7] Linwood Spicer, who served here as second assistant in 1904 and 1905, is reported in the Bulletin of 1930 to have died on 12 November, as keeper of Marcus Hook Range Rear, after nearly 35 years in the fourth district; the notice adds that his father, William E. Spicer, also a keeper, died in 1912 in his 39th year of service.[^3][^9]

LighthouseFriends retells a story, said to be in government records, from about 1903: women were then barred from the station, and a head keeper hid his wife in the coal when the inspector's tender approached; the site itself says it may be "a yarn," and it names no keeper.[^2]

Wendell H. Wells, an assistant from 1923 and principal keeper from 1940 to 1946, told a magazine in 1941, as LighthouseFriends reports, that the crew kept watch in twelve-hour spells, spent 22 days at the light and eight ashore, and welcomed 200 to 300 visitors a year.[^2][^3] The Coast Guard took over the Lighthouse Service's duties in 1938.[^1] In 1971, Henry J. Lindemann, who had joined the Coast Guard in 1953, told a visiting reporter, "You're like a city all your own."[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Ice drove the station's repairs. A 1932 letter from the Bureau of Lighthouses said that apart from repairs, only "the rather frequent placement of additional stone rip-rap" had been needed since the original structure was built; the nomination finds added or requested riprap in 1884, 1888, 1894-1895, 1907 and 1916.[^1] In the Board's report for the year ended June 1894, heavy blocks of 2 to 6 tons were placed about 60 feet (18 m) from the cylinder, with two channels left open for boats, using about 1,669 cubic yards of stone; the next year's report gives 1,670 cubic yards, finished in July 1894.[^5][^6] The nomination found no record of a major ship accident on the shoal after the light was established.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard kept keepers here until 1973, when the station was automated.[^1] In the 1990s the fourth-order Fresnel lens was removed and replaced by a modern optic; by the nomination's account it is held at the Coast Guard Air Station in Atlantic City.[^1] In 1997 a VRB-25 beacon was installed and the light went over to battery power charged by solar panels, flashing every five seconds.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the first solar panels to 1977.[^2] The Coast Guard declared the lighthouse excess to its needs in June 2011. A first auction in 2012 drew a single bid of $30,000; a second closed on 16 November 2012 with a winning bid of $60,200.[^2] A local-history blog says it passed to private owners.[^11]

## Today

The lighthouse is privately owned and closed to the public; it is reached only by boat and is best seen from the water, and LighthouseFriends lists summer cruises that pass it.[^2] It remains an active aid to navigation.[^1] The original fog bell is in a museum in Greenwich, New Jersey, which the nomination calls the Cumberland County Historical Society and LighthouseFriends the John DuBois Maritime Museum.[^1][^2]
