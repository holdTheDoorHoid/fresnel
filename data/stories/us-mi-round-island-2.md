Round Island Light stands on a point at the northern side of Round Island, in the Straits of Mackinac, Michigan, where ships pass between that island and Mackinac Island.[^1] It was first shown on 15 May 1896 and was deactivated in 1947.[^4][^6] It is now powered by solar cells, and the Forest Service and a friends group look after it.[^6][^7]

## The place

Ships in the Straits could use the south channel past Bois Blanc Island or the north channel between Round Island and Mackinac Island.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the narrow passage in the north channel, less than a mile wide, "remained dark," and that the Lighthouse Board asked for a light there in 1867 and again in later years.[^1] The Board's request of 1890, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, was for "a light so placed as to enable vessels to hold a close and safe course between the two" islands.[^1] Round Island had been federal land "since 1853."[^1] The Board recommended Round Island because land on Mackinac Island "would command a high price."[^1]

## Building the light

Congress provided the money by the act of 18 August 1894: "An appropriation of $15,000 was made in the act approved August 18, 1894, for the establishment of a light and steam fog signal on this island."[^3] The Forest Service says the building was put up "under the supervision of Frank Rounds from Detroit." LighthouseFriends adds that Rounds "had worked on Mackinac Island's Grand Hotel."[^6][^1]

The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1896 says that "Contracts were made for the metal work" and that the building was completed. It describes the station as "a substantial two-story brick house with a square brick tower at one corner."[^4] LighthouseFriends gives the tower as 12 feet (3.7 m) square and 51 feet (15.5 m) high, forming the northeast corner of a "two-and-a-half-story, cross-gabled dwelling."[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives the tower's height to the lantern as 45 feet (13.7 m), so the two figures disagree.[^2]

The light was first shown on 15 May 1896. The Board's report says "On May 15, 1896, a light of the fourth order showing fixed white, varied by a red flash every 20 seconds, was established."[^4] The first keeper was William Marshall, who had been head keeper at Spectacle Reef for the previous fifteen years and who "exhibited the light for the first time," according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] The steam fog whistle, a 10-inch whistle, "established October 20, 1896," started running that autumn.[^8][^7]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists 30 lines of keepers and assistants from 1896 to 1947. The table below prints them all. The roster gives "George W. Smith (1911-1924)" as head keeper, but LighthouseFriends ends his service in 1922, the year it reports him reprimanded and transferred (see below).[^2][^1] The Society says the first keeper, William Marshall, "served until 1906," while the roster and LighthouseFriends give 1907.[^7][^2][^1]

LighthouseFriends reports that in 1922 "George W. Smith and his assistant William DeRusha" were reprimanded: "severely reprimanded for gross neglect of duty for not making an immediate report to the proper officer after the discovery of a whisky still on Round Island." It adds that "Both keepers were transferred to other stations at the first available opportunity."[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends reports that during a gale in 1914 the head keeper, George W. Smith, and his assistant, Samuel Massicotte, went out in a rowboat to a disabled motorboat and "took one man to shore."[^1]

The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1899 records that the whistle "was in operation some 167 hours, and consumed about 9 tons of coal."[^5] The Society's history says that on 20 October 1972 a fierce storm "knocked down part of the southwestern corner of the lighthouse."[^7] LighthouseFriends describes the same storm as one that "tore away the lower portion of the lighthouse."[^1]

## After the keepers

The characteristic changed on 15 October 1914 "to a red flash every ten seconds," and on 27 August 1928 "the fog signal was changed to an air diaphone," according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] The Forest Service says that "In 1924 the beacon was automated," and that the Coast Guard "was given responsibility for all lighthouses in 1939."[^6]

When the Round Island Passage light off Mackinac Island was placed in operation in 1948, Round Island Light "became an unattended station."[^1] The Forest Service says it was "deactivated in 1947, and transferred to the United States Forest Service in 1958."[^6] The Society says that "When an automated light was constructed off the shore of Mackinac Island in 1947, the Coast Guard abandoned and decommissioned Round Island Lighthouse."[^7] LighthouseFriends gives a later date for the decommissioning, 1955, so the sources differ.[^1]

The Coast Guard "recommended that the lighthouse be demolished" in the years before the transfer, and "Since the lighthouse was abandoned, the lighthouse was a target for vandals."[^7] The Society says that "If it wasn't for local preservationists, the lighthouse would have met its end," and that "the walls and foundation were restored, from 1974 to 1976."[^7] LighthouseFriends gives the National Register listing as August 1974; the Society gives 3 July 1978.[^1][^7] The Society also records that Boy Scout Troop 323 joined the restoration in 1995, that public tours began in 1996, and that the Society was formed in 2009.[^7] The lighthouse "was featured in the movie Somewhere In Time" in 1980.[^7]

In 1996, the light's centennial year, LighthouseFriends says Round Island Lighthouse "commenced operation as a private navigational aid," and the Society records "the lighthouse being reactivated" that year.[^1][^7]

## Today

The Society says the lighthouse is "currently owned by the U.S Government and assigned to the U.S. Forest Service."[^7] The US Lighthouse Society's record lists it as open to the public and as an active aid to navigation.[^2] The Forest Service says that "Today it is powered by the sun using solar cells and storage batteries."[^6] The record also gives "Year Discontinued: 1947-1996."[^2]

LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] The Forest Service lists the site as "Day use only: open sunrise to sunset."[^6] For learning more, the Forest Service asks people to "please call the Saint Ignace office of the Hiawatha National Forest."[^6] The Society has run an annual open house since 2007.[^7] LighthouseFriends notes that the light can also be seen "on eastbound lighthouse cruises."[^1]
