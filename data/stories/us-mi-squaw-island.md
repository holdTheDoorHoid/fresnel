Squaw Island Light stood on a small island in the Beaver Island group, in northern Lake Michigan.[^2] Its brick tower was finished in September 1892 and first shown in October that year.[^4] The light was discontinued in 1928, after a light station was built on Lansing Shoal about five miles north.[^1][^9]

## The place

LighthouseFriends gives the island as about half a mile long, north to south, and three-eighths of a mile wide.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal years 1891 and 1892 says that Squaw Island "is at the northern end of Lake Michigan," and that it is the northernmost of the Beaver Island group.[^2] The Board's 1887 report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, said that the passage west of Beaver Island was "being generally used by steam-barges from Chicago and Milwaukee."[^1]

The name was shared with a second, separate project. An act approved on 15 February 1893 authorized "a light on Sanders Point, or Squaw Point," to guide ships into Gladstone Harbor, on an extension of Green Bay. The Board's report for that year says that no appropriation had yet been made for it.[^4] The Board's 1894 report lists a "Squaw Point (Gladstone) light, Michigan" at $5,000 among its works.[^11] The sources read here place that light at Gladstone, not on Squaw Island, and do not say whether it was built.

## Building the light

The act of 2 March 1889 authorized "the establishment of a light-house and fog signal on Squaw Island." The Board's report says that a sundry civil act of 3 March 1891 appropriated $25,000 for it.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the entire island was bought for $2,000 in February 1892, and that "the tender Amaranth landed workmen" on the island in May 1892.[^1]

The work was completed on 16 September 1892, and the station was first put in operation on 10 October 1892.[^4] The tower was red brick, "11 feet square at its base."[^3] LighthouseFriends says the fourth-order Fresnel lens "was placed in service on October 10, 1892."[^1]

The fog-signal house held duplicate machinery, with boilers, engines and 10-inch steam whistles.[^3] In the first season, from October 1892 to June 1893, the whistle "was in operation some 130 hours since October, 1892," and it consumed about 9 tons of coal.[^5] The figures rose later. In the year ended 30 June 1899 the whistles "were in operation some 327 hours," and in the year ended 30 June 1900 the whistle "was in operation some 228 hours."[^6][^7]

The station began with two keepers. LighthouseFriends says that "a third was added in 1893," and that the barn was converted into a residence for an assistant keeper in 1895.[^1]

## The keepers

William H. Shields was the first head keeper, from 1892 to 1901. Owen C. McCauley followed him and served until 1928.[^10] Among the first assistants were Joseph A. Martin (1892 to 1894), James O. Gallagher (1894 to 1896), James Wachter (1896 to 1900) and Walter H. Nelson (1901 to 1907). Lucien F. Morden was a second assistant in 1899 and 1900.[^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The keepers closed the station for the winter in December 1900.[^1] On 14 December they set off for Beaver Island in the station's "twenty-two-foot sailboat."[^1] LighthouseFriends says a sudden squall tossed everyone aboard into the lake: Keeper Shields, his wife, his wife's niece, and assistants Owen McCauley and Lucien Morden. The two women died that evening. Morden lost his grip after a few more hours and drowned.[^1]

The Manitowoc Pilot's account, printed on 20 December 1900, says that the party had left the island on the Friday of the week before, at noon, and that a coroner's jury took testimony. It names the dead as the keeper's wife, "wife of Keeper Shields, aged 30 years," a Mrs. Davis of Richmond, Indiana, "aged 23 years," and "Lucien Morden of Montague."[^8] The Pilot calls the second woman "Mrs." Davis. LighthouseFriends calls her the keeper's wife's niece, Lucy Davis.[^1] The sources do not settle which is right.

The steamer Manhattan, bound for Manitowoc with coal, spotted the boat the next morning. It took aboard the two surviving keepers and the bodies of the two women.[^1] Shields remained in the hospital for six months, and his right leg was amputated at the knee. McCauley was able to return home to Beaver Island later that month.[^1] Between 1916 and 1919 McCauley and his assistants were commended five times for helping a disabled vessel near the station.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that the Lansing Shoal light station, with its air diaphone, fog signal and radio beacon, was placed in commission on 6 October 1928.[^9] LighthouseFriends says that once Lansing Shoal was built, the island's light and fog signal were discontinued and the keepers were assigned elsewhere.[^1]

The government tried to dispose of the station in July 1936, and in April 1937 bids for the property were accepted at Milwaukee.[^1] At some point the lighthouse passed into private hands.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that the island and lighthouse were on the market for $3,200,000 in 2013. In 2016 a private owner decided to restore the property, and had a new roof put on the lighthouse and its exterior restored.[^1]

## Today

The island and the lighthouse are privately owned, and the Lighthouse Society's record marks the station as not open to the public.[^10][^1] LighthouseFriends notes that vegetation limits the view from the water.[^1] The fourth-order Fresnel lens is on display at the National Museum of the Great Lakes in Toledo, Ohio.[^1]
