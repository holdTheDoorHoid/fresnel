The Liston Range Front Light stands on the Delaware River at the southern edge of Bayview Beach, in New Castle County, Delaware. It is a two-story Colonial Revival frame house with a tower on its roof, finished in 1908 as the front mark of a pair of range lights that guide ships up the river channel.[^1][^2] The house is private property and closed to visitors.[^2] The light that still shows the range is a Coast Guard light on a steel tower in front of it.[^1][^4]

## The place

A range is a pair of lights, one low and near the water and one higher and farther back. A navigator who sees one directly above the other knows the ship is on the line of the channel. If the ship steers off, "the lights disappear."[^1] The nomination calls the Liston range "what is said to be the longest strait line navigational range in the world, over 17.5 nautical miles," and says it is the first range a ship meets going up the Delaware from Ship John Shoal.[^1]

The range replaced an older pair. LighthouseFriends says that because of Army Corps of Engineers work on the lower river channel, the Port Penn range lights "were discontinued and replaced by the Liston Range Lights."[^2] The nomination places the change in 1904, when the channels were changed and a jetty was built in the river.[^1] A Service bulletin from 1937 gives the year the Liston Range Front and Rear Lights were "established in 1904."[^8]

The government bought the land for the new station on 18 June 1903. The nomination says the seller was Sarah R. Burbridge, and the price was $1,350 for roughly five acres (2 ha).[^1]

## Building the light

A temporary light was lit first. LighthouseFriends says a galvanized-iron headlight lantern on a lantern post was first lit on 25 October 1904, and that a 100-foot (30 m) lantern post took the rear mark the same day.[^2] The nomination describes the temporary front light as "a lantern post carrying a triangular slatted daymark and a galvanized-iron headlight lantern."[^1]

On 7 October 1907 the Light-House Service accepted the low bid of $11,645 from Warner and Blair of Claymont, Delaware. The specifications came from Major C. A. F. Flagler, engineer of the Fourth Lighthouse District, in a 48-page booklet.[^1][^2] The bricks were to be "sound, well made, hard, and of uniform dimensions," LighthouseFriends says, quoting the specifications.[^2]

The house is frame, built of hemlock timbers with hemlock fire breaks between the floors. The nomination says it was built "to withstand harsh weather conditions and fire."[^1] The light was first shown from the lantern room on 28 December 1908, according to the nomination and LighthouseFriends.[^1][^2][^4]

The lamp was a fourth-order range lens behind a clockwork shutter.[^2] The light showed for two seconds and was dark for one.[^1] The nomination describes the mechanism as "like a grandfather clock with chains, weights and ratchets" that ran from the cupola to the basement. "The weights were set from the basement every 24 hours."[^1] The Delaware Bay keepers' group gives the light as 62,000 candlepower, 50 feet (15 m) above mean high water.[^4]

The sources disagree about the lens. LighthouseFriends says a fourth-order range lens made by the Macbeth Company of Pittsburgh was used early on.[^2] The nomination says a fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed in 1916, and that a fourth-order lens now in the lantern room is not the original.[^1] The Service's record gives a second-order range lens installed in 1906.[^3]

The station was lit by kerosene until 1932, when the house and light were electrified. About 1935 indoor plumbing and central heating were put in.[^1] In 1911 the Service built 390 feet (119 m) of reinforced concrete wall at the station, and in 1913 five riprap jetties in timber cribs were added to keep the wall's base from being undermined.[^5]

## The keepers

The Service's roster lists eight principal keepers.[^3] The first, George W. Duncan, had kept the Port Penn Front light from 1885 until it closed in 1904.[^1] The nomination says he kept his home there and commuted each day to tend the temporary Liston light.[^1] He had eleven children, "six boys and five girls."[^1] The nomination says: "On his one day off Keeper Duncan would only permit his girls to maintain the light, much to the delight of his sons."[^1]

The nomination says Duncan never lived in the Liston house. In January 1909 he was transferred to the New Castle Rear Range Light, where he stayed until he retired in 1918.[^1][^2] The Service's roster gives him as principal keeper here from 1904 to 1906, a span the nomination's account does not fit.[^3]

Edward W. Long, from 1906 to 1911, was the first keeper to live in the house.[^1][^3] J. E. Lauer and William J. Salmons followed. The nomination lists the salary for these keepers as "$ 500 per annum," and says the list of keepers may be incomplete: "possibly others."[^1][^3]

Harry E. Spencer, Sr., came to the front light from the rear light. The nomination says he was transferred in 1927, and the roster starts his term in 1928.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends says he received both the inspector's star and the commissioner's star while he was at the station, awards the Bureau gave for efficiency at quarterly inspections.[^2] His son's account, as LighthouseFriends prints it, says the keeper would not allow any fire in the house's fireplaces, so the family shut off half of it in winter, and one winter "the ink was frozen."[^2]

LighthouseFriends says Spencer died at the station in 1943; the nomination puts his death in the early 1940s.[^2][^1] His wife Sophia was appointed lamplighter, and the roster lists her as a principal keeper.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends says she left in 1948; the nomination says the house was closed in 1950.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Service's 1924–1929 index lists "Listons Range Front light station damaged by hurricane."[^6] The Service's 1928 bulletin describes a tropical storm in September that cut earth away from the reservation at the Liston Range Front Light Station.[^7] The sources read for this station name no wreck or rescue tied to the front light.

On 8 December 1941, the day after the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor, a section of a Delaware National Guard coast artillery battery arrived at the site and set its guns up in the front yard. By May 1942 two concrete gun emplacements were finished, with a magazine and a range-finder tower. The Army leased the Odd Fellows clubhouse to the south for its headquarters. The troops were gone by December 1942, and the guns went to Fort Saulsbury. The nomination says the concrete emplacements and the magazine still existed when it was written.[^1]

## After the keepers

The nomination says the Lighthouse Service, established under the Treasury in 1852, was moved to the Department of Commerce in 1890, and that in 1939 the Coast Guard took over.[^1]

In 1952 a white skeleton tower was put up 40 feet (12 m) in front of the house, and the light moved to it.[^1] The nomination says the house was declared in excess to the government's needs in 1953 and put up for sale. LighthouseFriends says it was sold at public auction in 1954 for $8,200.[^1][^2] The house has been in private hands since.

The nomination describes the Coast Guard's steel light, "still in use as an aid to navigation," as dating from the mid-1950s, and DBLHKFA says the Coast Guard operates and maintains it today.[^1][^4] The nomination was prepared in 2002 and 2003 by the owner with the Delaware State Historic Preservation Office, and LighthouseFriends gives January 2004 as the date it was listed.[^1][^2]

## Today

The house sits inside the private community of Bayview Beach.[^2] LighthouseFriends says its grounds, dwelling and tower are closed to visitors, and the best view is from the river or from Route 9.[^2] The granite monuments that mark the line between the Delaware River and the Delaware Bay were raised again in 1983 on circular piers, and they can be seen from the water.[^2]

The nomination says the house keeps much of its original interior, including the chestnut woodwork, the oak fireplace surrounds, the original tile, the staircase and the clockwork chase.[^1]
