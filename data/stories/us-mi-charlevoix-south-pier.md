The Charlevoix South Pier Light stands at the end of the south pier, where the Pine River channel runs from Lake Michigan into Round Lake, in Charlevoix County, Michigan.[^1][^2][^3] The light was first shown on 1 September 1885 at the end of the north pier. It moved to the south pier in 1914, and the steel tower there now dates from 1948.[^2][^3][^8]

## The place

Charlevoix sits between Lake Michigan and Round Lake, and the shallow Pine River joined the two.[^2][^3] Terry Pepper's history of the light says that in the 1860s a wooden dock about 900 feet (274 m) long ran out from the shore north of the river mouth, so that wagons could bring lumber to ships tied alongside.[^2] Pepper says the villagers wanted the shallow river opened to navigation, since that would make one of the most protected harbors on the Great Lakes and would ease the shipping of lumber.[^2]

The river was cut and dredged by hand in 1873.[^2] Wave action along the new mouth would silt the channel up again, so that winter nine wooden cribs were built on the ice and sunk to make a pier north of the river mouth.[^2] In 1884 the Lighthouse Board decided to place a light at the outer end of that north pier, to guide the traffic the improved channel was drawing in.[^2]

## Building the light

The Eleventh District's engineer, Captain Charles E. L. B. Davis, drew on a common pierhead beacon design. The parts were built at the Detroit depot and taken to Charlevoix on the tender *Warrington*.[^2] The beacon was a white timber pyramid about 30 feet (9.2 m) high to the center of its lantern, with an iron lantern and a fixed red fifth-order Fresnel lens.[^1][^2] The light's focal height was 37 feet (11.3 m) above the lake.[^1]

Pepper says Wright Ripley, who had been first assistant at the Port Austin Reef light for two years, was made acting keeper and exhibited the new light on the night of 1 September 1885.[^2][^9] LighthouseFriends gives a different picture. A local publication announced on 3 October 1885 that Ripley had arrived at Charlevoix, and LighthouseFriends concludes that a temporary keeper must have kept the light for its first few weeks.[^3]

Small works followed. An oil shed went up in 1890, a fog bell was installed on the lakeward side of the beacon in 1909, and a brick oil house was erected on shore in 1910.[^2]

In 1914 the piers were extended again and the lights were reorganized. Pepper says the old timber beacon was lifted off the north pier, carried across the river mouth and set up on the end of the newly extended south pier, where it was painted red as a daymark. A 56-foot (17 m) red steel tower with a white light was built at the end of the north pier. The station was renamed the Charlevoix South Pierhead Light Station, and both lights were shown together for the first time on 14 April 1914.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses lists a "Charlevoix North Pierhead" light in the reports of 1915 and 1916, and the Charlevoix South Pierhead Light Station among the works completed in fiscal 1917.[^5]

## The keepers

Pepper calls Ripley the single Charlevoix keeper. His service ended on 15 November 1923, after thirty-eight years at the station.[^2] The archive's roster has him as principal keeper from 1885 to 1923.[^1]

In 1890 the Ninth District inspector visited the station. Ripley told him that wagons driven out along the pier at night to vessels tying up there set up a strong vibration, which made the light flicker and the lamp smoke.[^2]

William H. Shields succeeded him in 1923.[^1] Pepper says Shields had lost a leg in a boating accident at the Squaw Island light station in 1900 and had been on light duty at the Charlevoix depot until he was chosen for the post.[^2] The roster lists Everitt C. Sterritt from 1924 to 1934 and Ernest G. Hutzler from 1935 to 1940.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a keeper was still at the light until 1940.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A severe storm struck the Great Lakes from 8 to 10 November 1913. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for December 1913 lists damage at five light stations in the Twelfth District, Charlevoix among them, that together came to about $6,500. The bulletin does not give Charlevoix's share.[^4]

On 28 February 1929 the keeper used the fog bell to guide three automobile parties across Lake Michigan from Beaver Island through a dense fog. The Bulletin describes this as the first known drive over ice between Charlevoix and Beaver Island. It adds that no record is known of the lake freezing over there, though veteran marine men in that section believed it had frozen from shore to shore in 1887 or 1888.[^6]

The Bulletin does not name the keeper. LighthouseFriends names Ernest G. Hutzler, but the archive roster has Everitt C. Sterritt in post until 1934, and LighthouseFriends' own keeper list runs his service to 1935. The name is not settled.[^1][^3]

## After the keepers

In 1938 the fog bell was removed and replaced by an electric, compressor-powered Type C diaphone. The kerosene lamp in the beacon was replaced by an electric bulb at the same time.[^2]

In 1947 the decision was made to replace the timber beacon, which Pepper says had stood 63 years in the weather. The new steel tower was built in Milwaukee and shipped to Charlevoix in 1948. The old lantern and lens were moved into it, and the old beacon was destroyed.[^2] A 1948 newspaper, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, reported that many of the old beams were badly rotted, and that Coast Guardsmen had often wondered whether the tower would survive a storm.[^3]

Pepper says the fifth-order lens was replaced by a 300 mm acrylic optic in the early 1980s, and that the tower was painted white at about the same time.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the white paint to 1968 and says the old lens is on display at the Coast Guard base in St. Ignace.[^3] The tower was returned to red in 2009.[^8]

The Coast Guard declared the light excess in 2005, and the same year the National Register of Historic Places listed it, on 28 April 2005.[^3][^7] LighthouseFriends says the National Park Service gave the lighthouse to the City of Charlevoix in 2008.[^3]

## Today

The City of Charlevoix owns the light. The Charlevoix Historical Society maintains and preserves it, and the Coast Guard operates the light.[^8]

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists the light as not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes the grounds as open and the tower as closed.[^3] The Historical Society invites visitors to see the light from the Lake Michigan beach and to have their lighthouse passports stamped at its museum.[^8]

Between September and October 2025 the Society had the interior blasted and repainted, and the exterior where it needed the work, at a cost of just over $100,000.[^8] In 2016 the light had been repainted in a coating matched exactly to its original red.[^8] On 8 May 2025 the Michigan State Historic Preservation Office named the Society a recipient of its Governor's Award for Preservation.[^8]
