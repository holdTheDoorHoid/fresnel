Miah Maull Shoal Light is a red-and-gray cast-iron tower that rises straight out of the water of Delaware Bay, about eight miles south of Fortescue, New Jersey. Lit in 1913, it was the last lighthouse of its kind built before concrete took over, and it still shows a light today.[^1][^3]

## The place

The shoal lies just east of the main shipping channel in the bay. The National Register nomination describes it as 800 yards wide and 3,000 yards long, with only 13 feet of water over it, a real hazard for the deeper-draft ships the port was beginning to expect. (Lighthouse Friends gives its length as 1,000 yards.)[^1][^3] It is said to be named for Nehemiah Maull, a Delaware River pilot from Lewes, Delaware, who drowned in 1780 when his ship ran aground on what was then an unnamed shoal.[^3][^10] The nomination says only that the shoal was named for an eighteenth-century Delaware mariner.[^1]

The light was planned as part of the improvement of the Philadelphia ship channel. Congress, in the River and Harbor Act of 1909, called for a channel 35 feet deep and at least 800 feet wide from the Philadelphia and Camden wharves to the mouth of the bay, replacing an older channel 26 feet deep.[^1]

## Building the light

Money came in three steps. The nomination lists appropriations in acts of June 30, 1906, March 4, 1907 and March 4, 1911; the Commissioner of Lighthouses later gave the 1907 and 1911 sums as $35,000 and $30,000.[^1][^2] The design, drawn in 1907-08, called for a cast-iron shell, made in pieces, floated to the site, sunk on a bed of pilings and riprap and filled with concrete.[^1]

The contract for the shell went to the Lynchburg Foundry Company of Virginia in May 1908. The plates were delivered to Wilmington, Delaware, where they were assembled and floated; work at the site began on July 24, 1908. The Coast Guard towed the shell out in August 1909 and the sinking and filling were finished by November.[^1] (The nomination names the Tatnall-Brown Company of Wilmington as the other builder; Lighthouse Friends says the foundry could not afford to continue and that Tatnall Brown took over from June 1909.[^1][^3]) The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported the shell erected and partly filled with concrete in December 1909.[^2] When *Engineering News* described it in June 1910, the shell was 40 feet across at the base and 42 feet 8 inches high, made of 224 cast-iron plates in seven courses.[^1]

Funds then ran short, and the foundation waited for the third appropriation.[^3] The Lighthouse Service's bulletin reported the contract for the superstructure awarded in 1912, construction commenced, and by the end of that year the outside walls complete, including the lantern, with a temporary fixed white light to be shown.[^12] The nomination and the Commissioner's 1915 report both give February 20, 1913 for completion of the superstructure and December 5, 1913 for the permanent fog signal.[^1][^2] The Bulletin for 1914 lists the station as completed, with the fog signal established on December 5.[^5] Lighthouse Friends gives the first lighting of the permanent light as February 15, 1913, with a bell as a temporary fog signal, and a total cost of $104,102; it also says the fourth-order lens was made in Paris in 1912.[^3]

## The keepers

The station was staffed by a head keeper and two assistants. The first head keeper, Henry C. Wingate, is listed at the station for 1913-1914, and a long run of short stints follows in the first years, mostly assistants who came and went within months.[^4] Two keepers stayed for decades. Elmer O. Mitchell was head keeper from 1923 to 1938, and Roy P. MacLauchlan served the station from 1922 until 1945, most of it as first assistant (1925-1941).[^4] Harry T. Wilson was second assistant from 1929 to 1939.[^4] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported Mitchell among the keepers detailed as attendants at an exposition in Philadelphia.[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The most vivid record is of a keeper in trouble. In the last days of 1919 Joseph H. Holland, then a second assistant, left the station in an open gasoline boat to go ashore. The Milford, Delaware, *Chronicle* said the trouble came when he had gone about halfway to the Breakwater, leaving him helpless in the open boat; the paper has him four days and three nights without food or water before a pilot, Captain Charles S. Morris, saw the drifting boat from a steamer on the Thursday evening.[^6] The Philadelphia *Evening Public Ledger* said he had left on the morning of December 30, that the boat had jammed in the ice within eight miles of shore and that he was picked up unconscious by an Italian steamship. By January 9 he had been brought ashore, still unable to eat solid food, but regaining the use of his frozen limbs.[^7] The two papers differ on the day he set out (the *Chronicle* says Monday, December 29) and on who found him; this account does not try to settle that.

In another episode, on May 13, 1929 second assistant Greensbury Dennis recovered a drowned man floating three or four miles from the station.[^8]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over the station in 1939, and the roster shows Coast Guard crews from then on.[^4][^3] Lighthouse Friends describes the later crew as three men working two weeks on and one off.[^3] Between 1959 and 1964 the station was converted to alternating current, and its regular power has since come by submarine cable from Cross Ledge.[^1] The nomination says keepers lived in the quarters until 1975; Lighthouse Friends and the Delaware Bay Lighthouse Keepers & Friends give 1973 for the last crew and automation.[^1][^3][^11]

The light was listed on the National Register of Historic Places (reference 90002188) as a surviving example of the cast-iron and concrete caisson tower.[^1][^3]

## Today

The tower is an active aid to navigation.[^11] The Coast Guard declared it excess in 2011; no preservation group took it on, and the General Services Administration sold it at online auction on August 7, 2015 for $90,000.[^3] It is privately owned and closed to visitors, and the shoal is open water far from shore. It is best seen from a boat; Lighthouse Friends lists summer cruises that pass it.[^3]
