Cape Mudge Lighthouse stands at the southern tip of Quadra Island, British Columbia, where it marks the southern entrance to Discovery Passage on the Inside Passage route between Washington and Alaska.[^1][^2] The first light here was a small wooden house lit in 1898; the octagonal concrete tower that visitors see dates from 1916.[^1][^3]

## The place

Discovery Passage is a shipping route that forms part of the Inside Passage.[^2] A few miles to the north lie the Seymour Narrows, which Lighthouse Friends calls dreaded, and the hazard of Ripple Rock. Lighthouse Friends records that the American gunboat *Saranac* struck Ripple Rock in 1875 and sank, and that the rock was blasted away in 1958.[^1] The station's heritage designation ties it to the Klondike Gold Rush, which sent traffic up the coast.[^2] Lighthouse Friends adds that the point was named Cape Mudge by George Vancouver, and that when he landed nearby in July 1792 he found a large village of Coast Salish people.[^1]

## Building the light

The first lighthouse went up in 1898. It was built by G. H. Frost of Nanaimo for a contract price of $1,225: a square white wooden dwelling with a square wooden lantern on a red hipped roof, showing a fixed white light 10 m (32 ft) above high water.[^1] John W. Davidson lit the seventh-order lens for the first time on 16 September 1898.[^1] The Department of Marine's report for the year ended June 1900 records that large trees threatening the buildings were felled at a cost of $30, and outbuildings costing $44 were put up.[^4]

The lens was upgraded in these years, but the sources do not agree on when. Lighthouse Friends says a fifth-order Fresnel lens replaced the original apparatus in 1908.[^1] The Department's report for 1909-10 lists "the fixed white dioptric 7th order light has been replaced by a 5th order dioptric apparatus", burning petroleum vapour under an incandescent mantle.[^5] A list of installations in the volume before it also mentions a "5th order French lens" at Cape Mudge, so the change falls somewhere in 1908 to 1910.[^6]

A fog signal building with a diaphone was added in 1913, together with a new dwelling and oil shed, according to Lighthouse Friends, which places it after the wreck of the *Cottage City* and the prodding of the Canadian Pacific Railway's coast service.[^1] The present tower followed. A government steamer's report for 1916-17 says it was carrying construction materials to Cape Mudge "where a light tower was in course of erection".[^7] Lighthouse Friends calls the current tower forty-two feet tall, while the Canadian register of historic places describes it as a 12 m (40 ft) tapered, octagonal reinforced-concrete tower, "constructed in 1916", the second lighthouse on the site; the old wooden building was kept on as a dwelling.[^3][^1]

## The keepers

Davidson, described by Lighthouse Friends as a fiercely independent Scotsman, kept the light from 1898 until he and his wife retired in 1918.[^1] He was followed by Herbert Smith, transferred from Nootka. Smith's wife died in the 1919 influenza epidemic, and he and his son were then living alone at the station, and he wrote to the Marine Agent asking for time off to meet a woman coming from England, hoping the agent would understand how a housekeeper would be appreciated. He resigned in 1927 in failing health.[^1] Joe Pettingell came from Sisters Rock with his family; in 1931 he wrote that they preferred being alone to living where "temptation" had caused them trouble, and they stayed until 1943.[^1] Lighthouse Friends calls Cape Mudge a desirable station because it is close to towns, and notes low turnover: Jim Abram served nearly twenty years, retiring in 2004.[^1] The roster below follows Lighthouse Friends' head keeper list; it differs from the same source's narrative on Smith's middle initials (Herbert T. ## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 26 January 1911 the steamer *Cottage City* ran onto a reef about two miles south of the light in blinding snow and fog. Lighthouse Friends says Davidson was working the hand fog horn, and guided the lifeboats in by waving a white flag on the beach.[^1] The passengers walked along the snowy beach to the lighthouse, where the Davidsons fed them and found beds, some in the lantern room, until a steamer collected them the next day.[^1] The Davidsons received fifty dollars from the commander of the Marine Service. Davidson's own comment, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, was "I only did my Duty but I dont want know more wreck while I am hear."[^1] The Department of Marine's annual reports list a government vessel going to the assistance of a vessel ashore near Cape Mudge on 26 January, and the *Cottage City* sunk near Cape Mudge on 26 January; they give the day but not the year, which here rests on Lighthouse Friends.[^8][^9]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard's list of lights now gives a sector light flashing white and red every five seconds, with a fog horn, plus a separate north sector light.[^10] Lighthouse Friends says the station has an automated weather-observing system and a solar-powered light, horn and fog sensor, and that in September 2009 the Coast Guard planned to de-staff Cape Mudge. After protests from keepers and the public the plan was put off pending review.[^1] In 2015 the tower was recognised as a heritage lighthouse, with four associated buildings: a 2000 dwelling, a 1965 dwelling, a 1971 fog alarm building and a 1970 shed.[^3]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard, and Lighthouse Friends lists Toni Adams as head keeper from 2022 to at least 2024.[^1] That site also says the grounds are open and the tower open in spring and summer in good weather; that is a visitor site's statement, so check current access before travelling, since this is an active Coast Guard station.[^1] The fifth-order lens is on display at the Vancouver Maritime Museum.[^1] It is reached by road from the Quathiaski Cove ferry from Campbell River.[^1]
