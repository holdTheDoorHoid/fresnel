Grays Harbor Light is an octagonal brick tower on a sand dune at Westport, Washington, lit on 30 June 1898. It is the work of the architect Carl W. Leick, and the National Register nomination calls it the tallest light tower on the Washington coast.[^1]

## The place

The light stands on Point Chehalis, the sandy spit that forms the southwest side of the entrance to Grays Harbor, in the fishing community of Westport. The station sits on the spit's main dune, about two miles southwest of the spit's northern tip, on the ocean side.[^1] The nomination traces petitions for a harbor light back to 1858 and a territorial plea in 1859 to 1860. It says the Light-House Board recommended a light station on Point Chehalis in 1881 and, renewing the effort in 1882, a lighthouse at Point Hanson, the name once given to the bulge in the spit where the tower now stands.[^1] By 1890, according to the nomination, the harbor had thirteen sawmills exporting 60 million board feet of lumber.[^1] Later came canneries and a fishing fleet, which the nomination says also relied on the coast lights.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress made a first appropriation in 1884, which the nomination gives as $15,000 and LighthouseFriends as $15,500 on 7 July 1884.[^1][^9] The Board decided a small harbor light would not do, and sought money for a larger coast light; the nomination records a $60,000 appropriation for a seacoast light, while LighthouseFriends traces the money in steps (a further $20,000 in 1893 and $39,500 in 1895) and has the Board first eyeing a site on the north side of the harbor at Point Brown.[^1][^9] The land on Point Chehalis had to be condemned. The Light-House Board reported that $500 was paid into the court on 16 December 1896 under a decree of 1 October 1895, while LighthouseFriends dates the payment 16 December 1895.[^3][^9]

Leick finished the plans in 1896 and district officials approved them on 16 October 1896.[^1] The Board's report for the year ended June 1897 describes the work under way: a road to the station and sheds built, about two-thirds of the stone for the base cut, the trestle for the water tank and windmill finished, the well dug and the bulkhead for the fog-signal building done.[^3] Earlier, test borings had found "a good foundation."[^3] The ground-breaking and cornerstone ceremony came on 23 August 1897, with the Reverend J. R. Thompson of Aberdeen laying the stone.[^1]

The work ran late. The nomination says the ironwork arrived nearly three months behind time and the building contractor finished on 26 March 1898; LighthouseFriends puts the metalwork 116 days late (12 October 1897) and gives the same completion date.[^1][^9] The builder is C. S. Erickson in the nomination's cover pages, C. J. Erickson in its narrative, and Charles J. Erickson of Seattle in LighthouseFriends.[^1][^9] Erickson later sued the government for extra labor; the Light-House Board recorded that suit was filed on 19 April 1899 and judgment for him of $4,599.92 given on 29 March 1901.[^6]

The nomination describes a brick octagonal tower that rises 92 feet (28 m), with the cast-iron watch room, service room and lantern bringing the total to 110 feet (34 m), standing on 12 feet (3.7 m) of sandstone. LighthouseFriends gives 107 feet (33 m).[^1][^9] The third-order lens, made in Paris by Henry Lepaute in 1895, is built of prisms around two bull's-eyes and floated on a bath of mercury; the nomination says it was set in the lantern on 10 June 1898.[^1] The light was commissioned in a ceremony on the evening of 30 June 1898.[^1] A 1932 Lighthouse Service Bulletin describes the light as alternating red and white flashes of 180,000 candlepower, from a single 300-watt lamp in the revolving lens, one revolution every 10 seconds.[^8]

The fog signal came later than the light. The sirens that arrived on 23 August 1898 were, in the Board's words, "old machines, much eaten with rust"; the Board reports new sirens received on 26 November 1898, the work of installing them done on 16 February 1899, and the signal in commission on 8 March 1899.[^4] LighthouseFriends instead says the new sirens arrived on 15 February 1899.[^9] From 8 March to the end of June 1899 the first-class siren ran some 33 hours; in the year ended June 1900 it ran about 506 hours and burned about 45 tons of coal.[^4][^5]

## The keepers

Christian Zauner was the first head keeper. The nomination says he was born in Austria, became a citizen and a lighthouse keeper in 1886, served as an assistant at Tillamook Rock and Destruction Island, and came to Grays Harbor as head keeper in 1898; LighthouseFriends instead says he had been the first keeper of Destruction Island.[^1][^9] Both sources say he arrived with his wife Hermine and two daughters; the nomination dates their arrival 14 June 1898.[^1][^9] He stayed until 1925.[^1][^2]

The first assistants listed by the U.S. Lighthouse Society archive are James B. Hermann (1898 to 1906) and, as second assistant, David Crosby (1898 to 1903).[^2] After Zauner, John Wilson, who had been first assistant since 1918, led the station; the nomination says he was acting keeper until superseded in 1926 by "Alvin R. Settles," while the archive lists Arvel A. Settles as head keeper from 1930.[^1][^2] Roy Jacobsen followed in 1935; the nomination says he served only until 1939, the archive says 1945.[^1][^2]

Two details survive from the keepers' work. In the nomination, one keeper wrote to the Light-House Board asking that Sunday visiting hours begin only at noon, because excursion boats did not reach Westport until then; he added that visitors should not be admitted on foggy or rainy days because of the mud and moisture they brought in.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that Zauner described the weight that turned the lens swinging like a pendulum during the San Francisco earthquake of 18 April 1906.[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The fog-signal building of 1898 burned in 1916.[^1] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' estimates do not match that date. His report for the year ended June 1917 still places the steam fog-signal plant in a frame building whose machinery and building were "quite old and in poor condition," and proposes a new fireproof building with an electrically operated siren as soon as funds permit; the same wording recurs in the estimates for 1916 and 1921.[^7] The sources do not explain the difference. The nomination says a replacement was built in 1926 or 1927 (it gives both years), at the water's edge about 2,145 feet (650 m) west of the tower, together with the station's first radio beacon.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the radiobeacon 2 November 1926.[^9]

A series of southwest storms struck the northwest coast from mid-December 1931 into early January 1932, and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin records that destruction of the Grays Harbor fog-signal and radiobeacon plant and control center was prevented by sand bags and other protective measures.[^8]

## After the keepers

Electricity reached the tower in the early 1930s: 1933 in the nomination, 1931 in LighthouseFriends; the 1932 Bulletin calls it "recent."[^1][^8][^9] The same Bulletin says that the complete electrification and centralized control, together with a light motor truck, made it possible to run the light, fog signal and radiobeacon, and the entrance range and its fog signal, with three keepers instead of four.[^8] In 1939, when the Lighthouse Service merged into the Coast Guard, the Coast Guard's Radio Station Westport took over the keepers' quarters, and the effective light station shrank to the tower, its oil houses and the fog-signal plant.[^1] The nomination dates automation to the late 1960s. It says the Coast Guard razed the 1898 quarters after a new lifeboat station opened in 1971, and turned off the Fresnel lens in 1992 in favor of a modern electronic optic on the gallery.[^1]

## Today

The Westport-South Beach Historical Society has owned the lighthouse since 2004, when the Coast Guard transferred it under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act; the Coast Guard keeps an easement to service its light.[^1] When the nomination was prepared in 2008 and 2009, volunteers opened the tower for tours from February through November, and the lighthouse is listed on the National Register of Historic Places.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the original Fresnel lens remains in the lantern and may be lit on special occasions by permission of the Coast Guard, the first such relighting being on 30 June 2015.[^9] The grounds adjoin Westport Light State Park.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the tours are offered by the Westport Maritime Museum, which is the place to check for current hours.[^9]
