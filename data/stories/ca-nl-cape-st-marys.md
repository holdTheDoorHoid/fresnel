A white octagonal tower on a flat, grassy tableland at the tip of the Avalon Peninsula, Cape St. Mary's light looks out over St. Mary's and Placentia bays from a headland of 100 m (330 ft) cliffs. Newfoundland's colonial government lit it in 1860, and its brick tower has since been wrapped in cement, iron and concrete.[^1][^2]

## The place

The cape lies at the south-western tip of the Avalon Peninsula and was once ringed by rich fishing grounds. An old Newfoundland saying held that "Cape St. Mary's pays for all", meaning that one trip there could make up for a poor year.[^1] When the colony's House of Assembly looked into a light in 1858, a committee under Assemblyman George Hogsett heard that a 35 km stretch of coast between the lights at Dodding Head and Cape Pine was dark. Captain Joseph Houlahan, who had fished for forty years, told it that about 2,000 boats from Conception Bay to Fortune Bay used the cape.[^1] The Canadian Register of Historic Places statement of significance describes the light as built to guide fishing and supply boats entering the two bays, and as an aid to ships approaching the Gulf of St. Lawrence from the Atlantic.[^2]

## Building the light

An act passed on 10 May 1858 told the Board of Works to get plans and build the lighthouse. The inspector of lighthouses, Robert Oke, and the contractor, R. F. Sweetman, chose the extreme point of the cape that year.[^1] Heritage documents credit Oke with the design.[^3] It was a circular brick tower with a one-and-a-half-storey frame dwelling on each side, for the keeper and his assistant.[^1]

Getting the equipment ashore took longer than raising the walls. The lantern was ready to be shipped from St. John's in August 1859, and the lighting apparatus, made by the Edinburgh engineers David and Thomas Stevenson, followed in September. Heavy seas stopped both ships landing at the cape, and gales through September and October delayed things further. The lantern room was landed on 11 October and fitted by 3 November. The apparatus was stored for the winter and finally landed on 16 July 1860.[^1] The first light, twelve lamps with matching lenses and reflectors, turned to show white and red alternately every minute. It was lit on 20 September 1860.[^1]

The brick tower needed repeated work. By 1869 it had to be repointed and coated with cement, and the beams and floor beneath the lantern room repaired. In 1881 those beams and the lantern's freestone cornice and drum were replaced with cast iron. Two years later the inspector reported that the cement had peeled off and exposed rotten bricks, and Inspector Nevill recommended encasing the tower in iron.[^1] An iron casing was prepared in 1885 and applied in 1886 with a backing of cement-concrete, painted red like the masonry tower. The inspector expected the yearly repairs to end, and judged that the casing turned a troublesome building into a safe one.[^1] The federal heritage statement dates the concrete and cast-iron sheets to 1885 and an outer concrete shell to 1925, repaired in 1993. Lighthouse Friends instead places the reinforced-concrete encasement, which gave the tower its octagonal shape, in the mid-1950s, when the iron had rusted beyond repair. The sources disagree and the point is not resolved here.[^1][^3]

In 1926 the original lighting apparatus was replaced with a kerosene light that flashed three white flashes every thirty seconds, and the light went electric in 1966.[^1] A new dwelling was built in 1959 using timber hauled in by horses, to replace one of the existing dwellings, and two more houses followed in 1963 with supplies hauled up the cliffs from boats below.[^1] The register of historic places dates the present keeper's and assistant keeper's dwellings to 1965 and an office building to 1970, which does not match the 1959 and 1963 dates; the sources do not explain the difference.[^1][^4]

## The keepers

John Reilly was the first keeper, with William J. Collins as his assistant. In December 1861 Inspector Oke found that Reilly had been away from the station without leave, and the Lighthouse Commissioners fined him.[^1] Reilly retired in 1881 and Collins became head keeper with Thomas Young as assistant. That October Collins reported that Young had "behaved improperly" and Young was summoned to St. John's. The evidence put considerable blame on Collins too, so he was called as well, and the Board reprimanded both and made them pay for the inquiry.[^1] In 1892 it was noted that the pair no longer worked well together. Young was moved to Cape Pine and Patrick Houlihan, then assistant there, came to the cape; he became head keeper when Collins retired in 1894.[^1] Houlihan was awarded the Imperial Service Medal in 1918, by approval of King George V, for twenty-five years as head keeper.[^1] Lighthouse Friends spells the keeper's surname Houlihan in its narrative and Houlahan in its list of keepers.[^1]

The same list gives the later keepers as James Conway (1921 to 1933), Arthur Conway (from 1933), Edward Conway (1947 to 1975) and Vincent Keough (from 1978, to at least 1994).[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse was declared a Recognized Federal Heritage Building in 2007.[^1] The station is a year-round light with a fog horn, and the Coast Guard's list gives a white octagonal tower 12.0 m (39 ft) high, flashing white every 5 seconds from 118.9 m (390 ft) above the water with a range of 21 nautical miles.[^5] Lighthouse Friends reports that the Canadian Coast Guard owns it, with the grounds open and the tower closed. A visitor centre for the Cape St. Mary's Ecological Reserve stands just before the lighthouse, reached by road from St. Bride's.[^1] Fisheries and Oceans Canada has listed Cape St. Mary's among the lightstations of the province's Avalon and Eastern regions.[^6]
