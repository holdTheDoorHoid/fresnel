The Roanoke River Light marked the mouth of the Roanoke River where it enters Albemarle Sound, in Washington County, North Carolina.[^1] It began as a lightship.[^2] A screw-pile lighthouse replaced it in 1866. The house that survives stood in the Sound until 1955 and now stands on the Edenton waterfront, open to visitors since 2014.[^2][^3][^4]

## The place

The lightship was to guide vessels to the river mouth and on up to the port of Plymouth.[^4] William Biddle Shepard petitioned the U.S. House of Representatives on 6 February 1832 for a light station there.[^2] Congress provided $10,000 in 1834 to build a light vessel at the mouth of the river.[^2] The Lighthouse Friends site dates the appropriation to 30 June 1834.[^4] The National Park Service inventory gives 1835 as the year the station was established.[^3] The sources agree on the lightship and differ on the year.

The Commission describes the lightship as a 125-ton, three-masted sailing ship, its whale-oil lamps behind red, green and blue lenses, visible for 13 miles and hung 43 feet above the water.[^2] The Lighthouse Friends site gives the height as 42 feet in 1848.[^4]

During the Civil War the Confederates held the lightship.[^2] The Commission says they kept it until Union forces captured Plymouth in late March 1864, and then scuttled it about 40 miles up the river.[^2] The Lighthouse Friends site dates the Confederate seizure to the Union invasion of 1862 and says the ship was sailed up the river before it was scuttled.[^4] A Lighthouse Service list of lightship stations maintained from 1820 to 1927, printed in the Bulletin that year, gives the station as "Roanoke River (mouth of Roanoke River), N. C." and records it as replaced by the Roanoke River lighthouse.[^13]

## Building the light

The government built a one-and-a-half-story screw-pile lighthouse at the river mouth in 1866.[^2][^4] It was fueled by whale oil and first lit in January 1867.[^2] The Lighthouse Friends site counts it among five screw-pile lights established in North Carolina in 1867.[^4] The USLHS archive gives 1866 as the year the original fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed.[^1]

Ice and fire ended that house. The Commission says a fire damaged the structure in 1885, and that ice on the Sound cut its pilings in February 1885.[^2] The Lighthouse Friends site says a fire on 21 March 1885 destroyed the lighthouse, and that in January 1886 ice severed two of its legs and the house fell into the Sound.[^4] The old station was removed, and some of its usable parts were saved for the new one.[^4]

The Commission says the replacement was authorized in 1886 and built in 1887.[^2] The Lighthouse Friends site, which draws on the Lighthouse Board's 1887 report, says the new station was put in charge of the keepers on 12 February 1887.[^4] It was a square frame house with a tower at one corner to carry the lantern, standing on an iron substructure that rests on 24 wooden piles.[^4]

The records disagree about the date of the house that survives. The USLHS archive and the NPS inventory give 1903 for the tower or the first lighting.[^1][^3] The Commission and the Lighthouse Friends site date the house to 1887.[^2][^4] The sources cited here do not settle which is right.

## The keepers

The USLHS archive lists William B. Clifton as principal keeper from 1899 to 1930.[^1] Before 1899 the archive names only assistants, starting with James W. Gillikin, first assistant from 1885 to 1888.[^1] The Lighthouse Friends site names an earlier head keeper, Thomas Blount Clifton, from 1866 to 1899, and lists assistants from 1867 on, among them Elmer E. Hines (1883 to 1885).[^4] The USLHS roster does not list these earlier keepers, so they rest on the Lighthouse Friends site alone.

The Lighthouse Service's own publications confirm the later keepers. In May 1914 the Bulletin reported that the keeper, William B. Clifton, and the assistant keeper, Joseph W. Cooper, towed a disabled boat to safety.[^7] Cooper served as first assistant from 1906 to 1930 in the archive's roster.[^1] In March 1920 the Bulletin calls him "J. W. Cooper, assistant keeper, Roanoke River Light Station, N. C."[^9] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the year ended 30 June 1921 names Clifton as keeper.[^11]

The archive's roster for the 1930s lists Amasa J. Quidley, Sr. (1930 to 1931) and David E. Quidley (1932 to 1933) as principal keepers, and Julian H. Austin as first assistant from 1930 to 1933.[^1] The Lighthouse Friends site says the station's dwelling housed families for several decades, before keepers began commuting to the light from Plymouth.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In September 1913 the Bulletin reported a recent storm in the vicinity of the North Carolina sounds, and listed the Roanoke River light among the structures damaged in Albemarle Sound.[^5] The index to the Bulletin's first volume files the damage as "Roanoke River, damaged by 1913 hurricane."[^6]

On 8 May 1914 the keeper and his assistant towed a disabled motor boat carrying four people to safety.[^7] The Bulletin wrote that without their timely arrival "the boat would probably have been driven ashore and lost."[^7]

On 27 July 1917 Clifton and Cooper helped float the schooner W. H. French, which had gone ashore near the light.[^8]

On 16 March 1920 Cooper went to the launch Laum, which had grounded about three miles from the station, and got it afloat so it could continue on its way.[^9]

In February 1921 Clifton and a radio operator from the tender Columbine helped rescue men adrift in a disabled launch.[^10]

The Commissioner's report for the year ended 30 June 1921 says that Clifton towed a disabled boat to safety and gave its occupants food and lodging overnight at the station.[^11] The report for the year ended 30 June 1922 says he repaired the engine of a launch that had broken down near the station.[^12]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Friends site says the light was discontinued on 29 June 1933, and that the empty building afterwards hosted Sea Scout troop meetings and card games.[^4] The same site's introduction gives 1941 for the station's discontinuance, as do the USLHS archive and the NPS inventory, and the Commission says the Coast Guard deactivated it that year.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

In 1955 the Coast Guard sold this lighthouse and two other sound lights to Elijah Tate for $10 each.[^2] Tate lost two of the three while trying to move them.[^2] He sold the Roanoke River house to Emmett Wiggins for $10.[^2] The Lighthouse Friends site describes Wiggins as a tugboat operator.[^4] The Commission says Wiggins moved it across Albemarle Sound to Edenton.[^2] The Lighthouse Friends site reports that Wiggins took an old landing craft out to the house, sank it under the structure, pumped the water out so the craft rose beneath the house, cut the last piles, and sailed home to Edenton, a job that took about 36 hours.[^4] Wiggins had bought land at the mouth of Filberts Creek, west of Edenton, in 1946, and he lived in the house from about 1960 until his death in 1995.[^2][^4]

The Port O'Plymouth Museum and the Washington County Roanoke River Commission decided to build a replica of the 1866 light in Plymouth.[^4] Pilings went into a grass field on the river bank in the fall of 2001, and the work took about two years, using plans from the National Archives.[^4] The Lighthouse Friends site also reports that the 1835 lightship was located in 47 feet of water in the Roanoke River in 1999.[^4]

In May 2007 the Edenton Historical Commission bought the lighthouse for $225,000 and paid $75,000 to move it to Colonial Park in Edenton.[^2] The Lighthouse Friends site dates the move to 23 May 2007 and places the park on the downtown waterfront.[^4] The State of North Carolina gave $1.2 million for restoration in 2009.[^4] In 2010 workers preparing the site smelled an odor that was later found to be petroleum, and the Edenton Historical Commission sought permits to set the house over the water instead.[^4] The house was moved onto new pilings in the harbor on 1 May 2012.[^4] Interior restoration began in March 2014.[^4]

## Today

The lighthouse stands on pilings over the water at the Edenton waterfront.[^4] The Lighthouse Friends site says it opened to the public on 15 August 2014 and lists visiting hours of Tuesday through Saturday, 10 a.m. to 4 p.m., with the grounds, dwelling and tower open. Check the hours before you go.[^4]

The Lighthouse Friends site lists the North Carolina Department of Cultural Resources as the owner.[^4] The Commission says it bought the lighthouse in 2007.[^2] Those two accounts do not match, and this story does not settle which one is current.

The lens is also unsettled. The Commission says the Coast Guard gave the Town of Edenton a fourth-order Fresnel lamp.[^2] The Lighthouse Friends site says the Fresnel lens will return to the lighthouse when more funding is found.[^4] A replica of the 1866 light stands in Plymouth.[^4]
