Cox Point Light stands on a point on the eastern shore of Grand Lake, New Brunswick, about 8 km (5 mi) west-southwest of Cumberland Bay. Its present tower is a skeleton tower with a red and white daymark, and the light shows a red flash every four seconds. The light has stood on the point since the late 1860s or early 1870s, but the sources give different years.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

## The place

Grand Lake is New Brunswick's largest freshwater lake.[^9] LighthouseFriends gives its length as about 48 km (30 mi) and its width as about 5 to 14 km (3 to 9 mi).[^1] Robert Cox, a Pennsylvania Loyalist, settled at the point in 1806. By 1898 Cox Point was a settlement with a lighthouse and a population of 25.[^4]

The point was exposed, and LighthouseFriends says ice floes often struck it during the spring freshet.[^1] Steamers also used the lake. In 1875 the steamer *May Queen* left Saint John at 8 a.m. on Wednesdays and Saturdays for Grand Lake and the Salmon River. The trip was 137 km (85 mi) and cost $1.50.[^1]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says $650 was allocated for construction and that the light began operating on 3 May 1871. Michael J. Cox, who owned the land around it, was its first keeper, at $80 a year.[^1] LighthouseFriends' data table and the Fredericton Capital Region page give 1869 as the year the first light was established, and Lighthouse Digest gives 1869 for the earlier wooden tower.[^1][^9][^8] The Lighthouse Directory gives 1873 as the date the station was established.[^2] None of these sources settles the year.

The first lamps were three flat-wick lamps in fifteen-inch (38 cm) reflectors, showing a fixed white light out over the lake. After two years the lighthouse needed more bracing and a window on its south side, to guide vessels into Cumberland Bay.[^1]

The Department of Marine and Fisheries' list of minor repairs for the fiscal year 1901–02 includes the Cox Point light, where the pier was repaired with new timber.[^5] In the report for the fiscal year 1904–05, the Department says a seventh-order lens and lamp replaced the reflectors and lamps, and that the light was a fixed white light visible about 14 km (9 mi).[^6] LighthouseFriends gives the same year for the lens.[^1]

A reinforced concrete tower was built in 1917 at a cost of $985, while Alexander McBaine was keeper.[^1] Lighthouse Digest says it replaced an earlier white square wooden tower built in 1869.[^8] A photo note in the Lighthouse Directory calls the 1917 tower the second lighthouse on the point.[^2] The present skeleton tower dates from 1971.[^2]

## The keepers

Michael J. Cox was the first keeper, from 1871, and LighthouseFriends says he was paid $80 a year for the work.[^1] Micah Y. Cox was appointed second keeper in 1875 and served until his death in 1892.[^1]

When Micah Cox died, the Department gave his widow, Eliza, a gratuity of two months' salary. Because no one else lived close by, it also gave her temporary charge of the light.[^1] She was still tending it in 1897.[^1]

That year Walter Butler wrote to a government official. He argued that, with the Liberals in power, a Conservative such as Eliza Cox should not keep a government job.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Butler had recommended William Barton for the post.[^1] Eliza married later in 1897, and the Deputy Minister of Marine and Fisheries cancelled her temporary appointment because she had remarried.[^1] Her new husband, Alexander McBaine, owned the land the light stood on, and he asked to be made keeper himself.[^1]

The Department told Eliza to hand the light to Barton. She refused to give up control of the light, or to let him cross her land, since the government did not hold a right-of-way there.[^1] In January 1898 the Inspector of Lights, John Kelly, came to install Barton and to arrange the right-of-way. He recommended $200 in all, for the land and back rent.[^1] In an April 1898 letter, Barton agreed to resign, on condition that McBaine be made keeper. The Department agreed.[^1]

LighthouseFriends lists McBaine as keeper from 1898 to at least 1923, and says he kept the light for at least twenty years.[^1] The Department's keepers' table for the fiscal year 1904–05 has a line for "McBaine, Alex" in the same column order as the Cox Point station. The columns are hard to read in the scan, so that match rests on position.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends records damage here only from the spring freshet.[^1] In 1888 it tore the tower from the point, and the tower was quickly replaced. The next year the tower was toppled again. The Department of Marine then raised a protective shore wall around it, to break the force of the ice.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light. Its grounds are open, and the tower is closed.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends and the Fredericton Capital Region both give the road route: from the Trans-Canada Highway at Exit 365, west on Route 10, north at Youngs Cove to Cumberland Bay, then 10.4 km (6.5 mi) west to the point.[^1][^9]

Heights differ. LighthouseFriends and the Fredericton Capital Region give the present tower as 3.5 m (11 ft). The Lighthouse Directory gives 7.5 m (25 ft) on a square pyramidal concrete base.[^1][^9][^2] The current list gives the structure as 3.6 m (12 ft) high, with a red flash every four seconds, a focal height of 9.4 m (31 ft), and a range of 4 nautical miles.[^3]
