Thomas Point Shoal Light is a white wooden cottage with a black lantern on top, standing on iron legs in the open water of the Chesapeake Bay, about a mile and a half off Thomas Point near the mouth of the South River south of Annapolis, Maryland.[^2] Lit in 1875, it is the last unaltered cottage-type screwpile lighthouse still on its original foundation in the United States.[^1] Keepers lived in it, surrounded by water, until the light was automated in 1986.[^1]

## The place

Thomas Point is the southernmost point of the peninsula on which Annapolis sits.[^2] In December 1823 William B. Barney, Naval Officer for the port of Baltimore, wrote to the Treasury's Stephen Pleasonton that shipowners and seamen wanted "a light to be placed at the end of Thomas' Point bar, a few miles below Annapolis." The bar, he said, was "cutting the direct track of vessels bound up or down," and fell away from four feet of water to six or seven fathoms almost at once.[^1] (LighthouseFriends dates the same letter to 1821; the National Historic Landmark nomination cites it as December 22, 1823.[^2][^1])

Congress appropriated $6,500 on May 26, 1824. The government bought a seven-acre parcel on the point from Jeremiah T. Chase for $529.69, and the contract went to John Donahoo of Havre de Grace and Simon Frieze of Port Deposit for $5,626. The first light, shown from 1825, was a round stone tower 30 feet (9 m) high on a bank, with a stone keeper's house of two rooms beside it, lit by 13 lamps with 16-inch reflectors.[^1]

The land would not hold. By 1838 the bank had eroded to within 15 feet (4.6 m) of the tower, and the Lighthouse Establishment noted that rain in windy weather "beats through the old masonry of the tower, flooding the inside of the structure." Winslow Lewis took the tower down and rebuilt it in 1840 behind the dwelling, three feet higher, for $2,000. The nomination notes that Robert de Gast gives a different cost ($2,500) and blames poor workmanship rather than erosion; the nomination itself follows the Establishment's reports, which praise Donahoo and name erosion.[^1] A fifth-order Fresnel lens went in on May 15, 1855 and a fourth-order lens in 1857.[^1] The tower, with its land eroded from about seven acres to roughly two, finally fell on February 28, 1894; the Light-House Board's report for that year says that "The old tower at Thomas Point fell on February 28," with the year reading 1804 in the machine-read text of the scan, an evident scanning error.[^1][^3]

Meanwhile the shoal itself had grown outward. By the 1870s it forced deep-draft vessels to keep farther offshore than the light on the point could usefully reach. In 1872 the Light-House Board wrote that the old station's "present location is such that little use can be made of it at night, and in times of thick or foggy weather it is utterly useless."[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $20,000 for an offshore light on March 3, 1873. The Board would have preferred a concrete-filled caisson that could stand up to the ice, and asked for another $25,000; when that was refused, it settled on a much cheaper screwpile cottage.[^1] LighthouseFriends adds that the choice was influenced by ice damage at Love Point, and that the Board moved to a caisson design before returning to the screwpile plan once another $15,000 was appropriated in March 1875.[^2] Maryland conveyed five acres of submerged land to the federal government on October 28, 1874.[^1]

The station was lit on November 20, 1875, the same day the old tower was discontinued.[^1][^2] It stands on a central wrought-iron screwpile and six around it, each 10 inches (25 cm) across and screwed 11 feet 6 inches (3.5 m) into the bottom, carrying a hexagonal wooden cottage 35 feet (10.7 m) across under an octagonal fourth-order lantern.[^1] The nomination reports an unusual detail: the railings have decorative splat balusters of a pattern it says it knows from no other cottage-type screwpile lighthouse.[^1] The cottage also kept its original privy, built out over the water on the south side of the gallery.[^1]

Sources disagree about the first lens. The nomination and LighthouseFriends say the light opened with a third-and-a-half-order lens showing a red flash every 20 seconds, replaced after ice damage in 1877 by a fourth-order lens by L. Sautter & Co.; the Lighthouse Society's archive record lists the original lens as fourth-order.[^1][^2][^4] LighthouseFriends records that on April 15, 1888 the all-round red flash gave way to a white flash over a 169-degree arc covering the best water to the east, red elsewhere.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive's roster for the station runs to well over a hundred names. The earliest years show how the work was organised: a head keeper and two assistants lived in the cottage, and the same men rose through the ranks without leaving it. Charles Miller served as second assistant (1875 to 1877), first assistant (1877 to 1880) and head keeper (1880 to 1881). Julius Warmkessel came up the same ladder and was head keeper from 1881 to 1886. George F. Culleton began as second assistant in 1881 and was head keeper from 1886 to 1901, the longest tenure among the nineteenth-century head keepers on the roster.[^4] The first head keeper of the new light was Eugene Burchenal (1875 to 1880).[^4]

The archive lists a second assistant only until 1883, apart from a single entry in 1923, so the roster shows two keepers on the station for most of the twentieth century before the Coast Guard crews arrived; it does not say why the third post went.[^4]

Henry F. G. Bryant was assistant keeper in 1924 and 1925, then head keeper from 1925 to 1940. Earl C. Harris was his first assistant from 1926 and took over as head keeper in 1943, serving until 1952.[^4] LighthouseFriends reports that Bryant left in 1940 on reaching the mandatory retirement age of seventy.[^2]

The Annapolis *Evening Capital* recorded a disappearance in 1903. On July 11 the paper reported that Henry Addicks, assistant keeper, had disappeared from the light. Head keeper Daniel A. White had been on vacation since July 4, and Addicks was tending the lamps alone. On the morning of July 10, according to the paper, White looked toward the bay through field glasses and saw the light still burning; he got no response when he signalled. He and his twelve-year-old niece took a boat out, and in the lower room he found the bunk empty and the clothes scattered about. Addicks was described as a former sailor, 43 and unmarried, who had lived at Locust Point.[^5] The inspector, Commander John M. Hawley, visited and gave his opinion that Addicks had drowned himself; all his clothes except the underclothes he wore were in place in the lighthouse, and a letter from a brother in Germany was found among his effects.[^6] By July 24 White said he believed Addicks had killed himself, and that he had heard him say repeatedly that he was tired of life. The body had not been recovered.[^7] The newspapers do not say why White was away, and a later summary that has him ashore for groceries does not match the *Evening Capital*.[^5][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Ice has been the station's recurring enemy. In 1877 (LighthouseFriends says winter, with the lens overturned in January[^2]) the vibration of ice floes was enough to overturn the lens, so that the old shore tower had to be lit again briefly. The screwpiles were pushed about an inch to the foot out of the vertical and a lower brace broke from its fitting, though the cottage was not damaged. The Board then built an ice breaker of three connected screwpiles about 90 feet (27 m) north of the lighthouse.[^1] A socket on the northeast pile was damaged in 1883 and strengthened, and over 1,400 cubic yards of riprap stone were laid around the foundation in 1886, with 200 more in 1887 to replace stone carried off by ice and current.[^1] In 1918 ice broke two piles, damaged others and destroyed the landing ladders.[^1] The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported contracts for ironwork repairs and riprap in 1919, and in the fiscal year 1920 a further 600 tons of stone added to the ice breakers on the north and south sides.[^10]

For those afloat, the lighthouse was a refuge. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded that on the night of May 23, 1916, keeper Sheldon R. Van Houter fed and clothed the occupant of a gasoline launch, the *Rex*, which had reached the station disabled, and next day towed it six miles to Annapolis, where a tug was found.[^8] On September 17, 1928, keeper Bryant and assistant Harris helped seven people in a disabled motor boat, got it to the lighthouse and repaired its propeller so it could go on.[^9] LighthouseFriends describes several other rescues by Bryant, sometimes with Harris, including a boat, the *Bertie Parker*, hoisted on a sling in 1932 so its bent propeller could be straightened with hammers.[^2]

The hurricane of August 23, 1933 hit the station.[^1] The Lighthouse Service reported that Thomas Point Lighthouse "was flooded by heavy seas which carried away next winter's coal supply."[^11] The nomination says nearly a ton of coal washed out of a damaged bin, and quotes Bryant's log for water driven "in every crease and crack"; it records no structural harm.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that Tropical Storm Agnes in 1972 sent waves of 23 feet against the cottage with only minimal damage.[^2]

## After the keepers

By 1964 Thomas Point Shoal was the only manned light station on the Chesapeake Bay apart from Cape Henry.[^1] The nomination describes how the Coast Guard crews lived: four men alternated, each serving 21 days on and seven off, with three at the station at one time; by the 1970s three men served 14 days on and seven off, two living aboard at once. Every Tuesday, when the shift changed, the arriving man brought the week's mail and groceries and the departing man took out the mail and trash. There was no telephone, only a radio; rainwater from the gutters was stored in two 1,400-gallon tanks and topped up by a buoy tender in a drought.[^1] Scott Kaufman, who served there in the early 1980s, commented, in LighthouseFriends' account, that the long hours gave him time to think: "You can just sit out here and think out all the angles."[^2]

The station nearly did not survive. LighthouseFriends reports the Coast Guard said in 1972 that it would be evaluated for cost-effectiveness, at a time when other screwpile lights had simply been set afire and replaced by a low-maintenance structure on the old piles; public protest and election-year politics changed the plan.[^2] The nomination, which places the Coast Guard's demolition announcement in the early 1980s, describes a Lighthouse Foundation formed under Rodney Little of the Maryland Historical Trust, a National Register listing in 1975, and an offer to rent the lighthouse to a civilian that drew at least 68 applications. The Coast Guard chose to automate instead, in 1986, at a cost of $172,000, expecting to cut annual operating costs from $38,000 to $5,000. Glass was replaced with Lexan, the station was alarmed, and the light was fully automated on September 5, 1986.[^1]

Under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000, a group of four partners (the City of Annapolis, Anne Arundel County, the Annapolis Maritime Museum and the Chesapeake Chapter of the U.S. Lighthouse Society) took the lighthouse over in 2004. LighthouseFriends reports that volunteers spent about eleven years and some $500,000 on its restoration, replacing roughly a third of the rotten wrap-around deck, and that tie rods and beams in the metal foundation were replaced in 2019 and 2020 with more than $400,000 in grants and donations.[^2] The Society and its Chesapeake Chapter received a Preserve America Steward Award for the restoration, with the White House letter dated January 12, 2009.[^14]

## Today

The lighthouse is a National Historic Landmark and, in the National Park Service's words, is "still being used for navigational purposes."[^13] LighthouseFriends names the City of Annapolis as owner, leasing it to the Chesapeake Chapter of the U.S. Lighthouse Society.[^2] It is on the water and cannot be reached on your own: visitors go on a guided tour run by the Lighthouse Society, which sails from the dock behind the Annapolis Maritime Museum, 723 Second Street, Annapolis. The 2026 calendar lists departures from May 30 into October. The trip takes about two hours including a docent tour inside, tickets must be reserved, no one under 12 (or under 48 inches or 90 pounds) is taken, and the Society warns that the tour involves vertical steps and strenuous activity.[^12] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is best seen from the water.[^2]
