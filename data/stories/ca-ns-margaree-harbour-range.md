The Margaree Harbour range lights are two small wooden towers on the west shore of Margaree Harbour, on the western coast of Cape Breton, built in 1900. Set one behind the other, they guide a ship coming in from the Gulf of St. Lawrence: when the two lights are in line, the ship is on the channel.[^2][^8]

## The place

The harbour lies where the Margaree River comes out to the Gulf.[^5] Its entrance once ran through a channel blocked by a bar of shifting sand. To improve access, the provincial government built a pier on the southwest side of the entrance, then repaired and extended it in 1876 and 1879.[^5]

## Building the light

In 1880 two small beacon range lights were set on posts about 7.6 m (25 ft) high, 40 m (130 ft) apart. John McFarlane was paid $60 a year to look after them. In 1885 Duncan McFarlane built a small square wooden tower on the outer end of the breakwater for $254, and it went into operation on 10 October 1885.[^5]

In 1900 the Department of Marine and Fisheries put up a new pair of range lights. Its Chief Engineer's report for the year to 30 June 1900 says that "Two new range light buildings were established on the mainland opposite the entrance to the channel leading into the harbour," and that they "consist of two small square wooden towers with sloping sides." The lights "have not yet been put in operation."[^1]

The breakwater light was discontinued on 4 May 1901 and its tower removed.[^2] The report for the year to 30 June 1901 says that "To replace the above discontinued light, two range lights were established" on the south side of the harbour.[^2] It describes the front tower as 6.8 m (22 ft) high, with its red light 23 m (75 ft) above high water. The rear tower is 10 m (33 ft) high, with its light 32 m (105 ft) up and 66 m (215 ft) from the front one.[^2] The register says both towers were built to a standard plan of the Department of Marine and Fisheries, which made the design "inexpensive and easy to build."[^8]

The buildings were erected by the department under the supervision of Mr. A. McLellan, at a cost of $1,062.95.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives the same figure.[^5]

## The keepers

Nicholas Daigle was the first keeper of the front light, and Roderick McLellan the first keeper of the rear. Each was paid $50 a year.[^5] Alexander McFarlane had kept the old breakwater light from 1886 to 1901.[^5]

Miles A. Dunn kept the front light for twenty-two years, from 1903 to 1925. Roderick McLellan kept the rear from 1901 to 1922, and John J. McFarlane took it over and held it until 1958.[^5]

In 1963 the towers were converted to battery power, and official keepers were no longer needed. John L. MacKinnon, Jr., who had kept the front light since 1952, was made caretaker until 1970, when the lights were connected to commercial power.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The harbour had its own wrecks. The schooner Laura sank in the harbour in the spring of 1904. The department let a contract to remove the wreck, at a price of $300.[^3]

On 5 May 1913 the Storm King stranded off Margaree. The preliminary inquiry at Halifax found that her master "did not exercise proper judgment in navigating his vessel." It also found that "the range lights and marks at Margaree are misleading and do not indicate the proper channel."[^4]

In the year to 30 June 1915 the department reported that the wrecked steamer Electra had been removed from the harbour by Captain G. LeBlanc, under contract.[^6]

In its 1911 report the department counted 99 storm-signal display stations, "including two new stations at Margaree harbour and Petite de Grat."[^7] In March 2018 a powerful windstorm tore a hole in the rear tower. The lighthouse group told the Coast Guard, and a repair crew arrived by helicopter the next day to patch it.[^5]

## After the keepers

In 2012 local people formed the Margaree Harbour Heritage Lighthouse Group to protect the lights and apply to own them.[^5] In 2015 the group entered the towers in "This Lighthouse Matters," a crowdfunding competition sponsored by the National Trust for Canada and the Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society. It earned $3,590 in the fundraising part of the contest.[^5] In October 2017 the government removed lead-contaminated soil from around each tower.[^5]

In June 2018 the Coast Guard began a major renovation of the rear tower. The crew found the front tower rotting and did the same work on it.[^5] On 23 May 2019 both towers were recognised as heritage lighthouses under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act.[^8][^9][^10]

## Today

The Coast Guard's current list of lights gives the front light as a fixed yellow light (F Y), 15.2 m above the water, with a range of 7 nautical miles. The rear light is also fixed yellow, with a range of 8 nautical miles. The list marks both as seasonal.[^11] Older accounts describe red lights: the 1901 report does, and so does LighthouseFriends.[^2][^5]

The sources also disagree about who owns the towers. LighthouseFriends says the Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and that its grounds and tower are closed.[^5] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society lists the front light as "Owned by the Margaree Harbour Heritage Lighthouse Group."[^12] LighthouseFriends also describes a planned transfer of the range lights, but no source gives a date for one.[^5]

The register notes that being on it does not make a place open to visitors.[^8] LighthouseFriends suggests seeing the rear light from a distance, from Route 219 just west of the Margaree Harbour Village Road.[^5]
