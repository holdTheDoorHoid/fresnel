Ka Lae Light stands on South Point, at the southern tip of the island of Hawaiʻi, where Rowlett's directory describes the light as "of special interest for marking the southernmost point of the United States."[^1] The Hawaiian name, Ka Lae, simply means "the point."[^1]

## The place

In 1904, six years after Hawaiʻi became a United States territory, the Lighthouse Board took over its aids to navigation, and in 1905 the Board requested money for a new beacon at Ka Lae.[^2] A light for the point had been proposed in 1883, but the Hawaiian government appropriated nothing for it.[^2]

The point was also the site of an ancient Hawaiian temple, a heiau. When the light was proposed, Professor William Tufts Brigham of the Bishop Museum wrote to Governor Frear asking that the heiau not be harmed. Captain C. W. Otwell, the army engineer working with the Lighthouse Service in Hawaiʻi, assured the governor that it would be left alone.[^2]

Otwell's visit in 1907 described the station as "isolated and desolate," a place of "a waste of rough lava rock," with the nearest water and provisions ten miles away.[^2]

## Building the light

The first light was a lens-lantern on a wooden mast 34 feet (10.5 m) tall. It was ready for display on 5 March 1906, and its light, made by incandescent oil vapour, could be seen for nine miles.[^2][^1] Rowlett's directory dates the station's establishment to 1909, so the two accounts differ by three years.[^1]

A small service house stood at the foot of the mast, but the keeper had no accommodation.[^2] In December 1907 the *Hawaiian Gazette* reported that an inter-island steamer was to carry a gang of men to erect a dwelling for the keeper at Kalae.[^5] On 4 December 1908 a proclamation by President Theodore Roosevelt set aside just over ten acres for lighthouse purposes.[^2]

The mast gave way to a 45-foot (13.5 m) steel tower. LighthouseFriends dates the tower to 1928, while Rowlett gives 1929.[^2][^1]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists ten principal keepers from 1906 to 1948.[^6][^2]

John T. Nakai was the first keeper, from 1906 to 1907.[^6][^2] Until a house was built for him, he lived in a small pyramidal dwelling he had put up near the light, LighthouseFriends says.[^2]

Robert I. Reid served from 1907 to 1912. LighthouseFriends gives his name in full as Robert Iana Reid.[^6][^2] William F. Williams Jr. kept the light from 1912 to 1915.[^6]

Harry W. Flint was keeper from 1915 to 1925. He is the keeper whose reports of volcanic smoke, in 1921 and 1924, are set out below.[^6][^2]

James K. Haleamau kept the light from 1925 to 1932, and Frank Williams from 1932 to 1935. John C. Allen is listed for 1937, and James J. Gibson served from 1937 to 1942.[^6] Joseph L. Richardson's service ended in 1947. Henry S. Smith, from 1947 to 1948, is the last keeper the roster names.[^6]

The sources name no woman keeper and no keeper's family member for this light.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A report dated 14 January 1921 reached the Lighthouse Service from the station. The Bulletin printed it as Kīlauea appearing "very active at present," with a strong north-northeast gale that had blown for two weeks.[^3] The station lay more than 35 miles southwest of the crater.[^3]

LighthouseFriends prints the keeper's own words from that winter. Flint wrote that for ten days the station had been "enveloped with smoke," at times so dense that nothing could be seen a mile out.[^2] In May 1924 Flint wrote to the superintendent of lights for Hawaii: "Every night the lens has to be lowered down to clean off the ashes."[^2]

The sources give no wreck or rescue tied to this station.

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service tried wind power at the point. The Bulletin reported that a wind-driven generator had been in operation since November 1928, and that in the first year the auxiliary engine had to run only about 50 hours.[^4] The Bulletin gave the reason as the wind itself: "Due to the superabundance of wind," the generator could charge the batteries at a reduced rate, and it also lit the keeper's dwelling.[^4]

LighthouseFriends says the light was automated in 1949. In 1972 the steel tower was replaced by a 32-foot (10 m) concrete post, which carries the light today.[^2][^1]

From 1987 to 2006 the Kamaoa Wind Farm operated on the point. A newer group of turbines, the Pakini Nui, was completed in 2007 about 1.5 miles from the earlier ones.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light. Rowlett's directory and LighthouseFriends both say the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^2][^1]

To reach the point, leave Highway 11 on the island's southern side, turn south onto South Point Road, and follow it to its end.[^2][^1] A large dish on the point belongs to the Swedish Space Corporation and serves satellite tracking and control.[^2] Solar panels now charge the batteries that run the light.[^2]

Rowlett also says the area is recognized as a National Historic Landmark, without giving a date.[^1]

The area is listed on the National Register of Historic Places as the South Point Complex, with a register record dated 15 October 1966.[^7]
