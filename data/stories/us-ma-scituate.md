Old Scituate Light stands on Cedar Point, at the north side of Scituate Harbor in Plymouth County, Massachusetts. Its granite tower was finished in 1811, and the North and South Rivers Watershed Association calls it the eleventh lighthouse built in the United States.[^7] The tower's own light was discontinued in 1860, and in 1994 it was relit as a private aid to navigation.[^2][^3]

## The place

The harbor is small and sheltered, but getting into it is not simple. The watershed association says that "entering the harbor could be difficult, due to mud flats and shallow water."[^7] The 1981 National Register form calls Scituate "the best harbor of refuge between Marblehead to the north and the Cape Cod Canal to the south."[^1]

LighthouseFriends says that in 1807 the town's selectmen were petitioned by Jesse Dunbar, a shipmaster, and other residents for a lighthouse. Congress appropriated $4,000 for one in 1810.[^2] The register form dates the purchase of 2 3/4 acres for the lighthouse to 1810.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the plot on Cedar Point was instead seized under eminent domain, and that its owner, Benjamin Baker, later denied access across his land and feuded with the first keeper.[^2]

## Building the light

A crew of three from Hingham put up the house, the octagonal split-granite tower, an oil vault and a well for $3,200. LighthouseFriends says they finished in September 1811, two months ahead of schedule.[^2] Simeon Bates was named keeper that December.[^2]

The date of the first light is disputed. Some sources say September 1811. A Notice to Mariners published in January 1812 gives 1 April 1812, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The Boston Mariners' Society had proposed periodically obscuring the Scituate light, to tell it apart from the fixed Boston Light. LighthouseFriends says many mariners were dismayed when Scituate became a fixed light.[^2]

In 1827 complaints from mariners led to a brick extension, about fifteen feet (4.6 m) high, on top of the granite tower, with a new lantern room.[^2][^1] The white light came from the lantern, where seven lamps and reflectors made it. A red light came from windows fifteen feet (4.6 m) lower, where eight more lights shone through red glass.[^2]

In his 1838 report, Lieutenant Edward W. Carpender questioned the need for a double light, and suggested a single red light, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] He also criticized Keeper Zeba Cushing, whose reflectors had long gone unburnished and whose lantern glass was badly smoked. LighthouseFriends says Cushing had been allowed to build an addition to the house at his own expense, and that a tenant there, entrusted with the light's care, was likely responsible for its poor reputation.[^2] In 1841 the light was refitted with plate glass and fourteen-inch reflectors.[^2]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society roster begins with Simeon Bates in 1811. Its entries after 1859 are discussed below.[^3]

Simeon Bates kept the light until his death in 1834, at seventy.[^2] The NSRWA says he, his wife Rachel and their nine children lived in the house attached to the tower.[^7] The Bates family's story from the War of 1812 is the one the sources tell most fully. LighthouseFriends says that in June 1814 British raiders set fire to and looted several ships at Scituate. A few months later, while most of the family was away, Keeper Bates's twenty-one-year-old daughter Rebecca and her younger sister Abigail, with a younger brother, were left to keep the light.[^2] In that account the sisters spotted the British warship La Hogue in the harbor, with barges of troops coming ashore. LighthouseFriends also notes that records place La Hogue elsewhere at the time.[^2] The 1981 form says three children tricked a British landing party by sounding military maneuvers on a fife and drum from behind a hill.[^1] LighthouseFriends and the watershed association put the sisters behind a dense stand of cedar trees instead. The watershed association says the sounds were enough to make the British think a whole regiment was waiting, and that the British withdrew.[^2][^7] LighthouseFriends quotes Rebecca: "Yankee Doodle was my masterpiece."[^2] The fife is still at the keeper's house.[^2] LighthouseFriends concludes that the story is likely true and that the sisters had only confused the name of the ship.[^2] The 1981 form lists the episode in its chronology and calls it "important to Scituate history."[^1] The watershed association calls it local lore.[^7]

Anthony Waterman, from 1851 to 1853, and Alonzo Jones, from 1853 to 1856, follow on the roster.[^3]

James Y. Bates, the grandson of the first keeper, took charge in August 1849, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2][^3] The roster puts him there from 1849 to 1851.[^3] Ebenezer Osborne was keeper from 1840 to 1849.[^3] He wrote complaints about the lamps, in his own words as LighthouseFriends prints them: "The lamps are Hemmenway's patent, and nine out of the eleven are so much burnt, and so very leaky."[^2]

The last keeper of the old light was Thomas Richardson. The roster ends his service in 1859, but LighthouseFriends places him at the light when it was put out in November 1860.[^3][^2] LighthouseFriends, drawing on Edward Rowe Snow, says Richardson made sure his ten-year-old son was there to see the light put out.[^2]

The roster also lists three names for 1900 to 1924, when the old tower had been dark for forty years: John E.O. Prouty (1900 to 1904), Henry F. Stubeck (1904) and John F. Cushman (1904 to 1924).[^3] The archive does not say which light they kept. The breakwater light, first shown in 1891, is one possibility.[^3][^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1842 the engineer I.W.P. Lewis said the light was confusing mariners. Ships entering Boston Harbor in a northeast gale, if they drifted to the southward of the light-house channel, "are often wrecked on the lee shore of Cohasset," LighthouseFriends quotes him saying. He wrote that the Scituate light was "one of the causes of frequent shipwrecks" on those rocks, and had "caused the death of many a brave seaman," because it was so often mistaken for Boston Light.[^2] He also noted that the inside of the tower was "coated with ice in winter, and green mould in summer."[^2]

The lantern was a problem in gales as well. Osborne said it "vibrates so much in a gale of wind" that the glass chimneys were rattled out of their places.[^2]

When the first Minot's Ledge light was destroyed in a storm in April 1851, LighthouseFriends says Scituate briefly regained its importance and received a fourth-order Fresnel lens in 1855. The watershed association says the old light had been taken out of service in 1850 and was put back two years later; the 1981 form says it shared duties with the first Minot's Ledge light in 1850. LighthouseFriends dates the storm to 16 April 1851, and the register form to 17 April.[^2][^7][^1]

## After the keepers

The new Minot's Ledge light, of granite, was lit on 15 November 1860, and the old Scituate light was discontinued. The lantern room and lens came out, and the property was leased.[^2] The register form gives 14 November as the old light's last lighting.[^1]

From 10 June 1891 a light in an eight-day ruby lantern on a mast at the end of the breakwater served the harbor, the Lighthouse Board reported.[^6][^2] An 1890 newspaper article, as LighthouseFriends reports it, says that for several years a small light had hung in the discontinued tower "to guide the local herring fishermen."[^2]

In 1916 the Commissioner of Lighthouses reported that an act of 28 June 1916 authorized the sale of the former reservation to the Town of Scituate, "for maintenance as an historic landmark."[^4] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1917 reports the sale of the old lighthouse and the keeper's dwelling.[^5] LighthouseFriends says residents appealed to Senator Henry Cabot Lodge to save the station from public auction, and raised $4,000 to buy it.[^2] The watershed association gives the same $4,000 price.[^7] The register form records the town's purchase on 17 June 1917 for $1,000, plus $1,500 for land to the southeast, and says the deed requires the town to maintain the property "as an historic landmark."[^1]

In 1930, at the start of the Depression, the town built a new lantern room atop the lighthouse, LighthouseFriends says, declaring that "a community is judged by the condition of its public buildings."[^2] The register form records the tower vacated and vandalized in 1966.[^1] In 1968 a town meeting gave the Scituate Historical Society management of the lighthouse.[^2][^7] The society set aside $6,500 for repairs in 1962, according to the watershed association.[^7]

LighthouseFriends dates the relight to 1994, after 134 years of darkness. The watershed association says a light was shown from land in 1991 and made visible from the sea in 1994, as a private aid to navigation.[^2][^7] In 2004 the society found that the outer course of poor quality bricks in the extension of the tower was badly deteriorated, and with funds from the town the outer layer was replaced.[^2] A stone revetment, planned in 2012, was completed in late 2013.[^2] A live-in caretaker retired in 2008 after twenty-two years. The society then chose a new caretaker family from among 100 applicants.[^2]

A $2 million restoration, backed by a town meeting in April 2022, began in October 2022 with the removal of the old lantern room. A new copper-clad lantern room was lifted into place on 15 November 2023.[^2] The light was relit again on 7 August 2024, National Lighthouse Day.[^2]

## Today

The Scituate Historical Society manages the lighthouse. Ownership is reported differently. LighthouseFriends says the Town of Scituate owns it and the society manages it, and the 1981 form also names the town as owner.[^2][^1] The watershed association and the US Lighthouse Society list the society as owner.[^7][^3]

Outdoor visitors are welcome year-round on the grounds, on the wooden walkway beside the tower and on the stone breakwater behind it. The watershed association says occasional tours are available from the society, and LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is open only during occasional open houses.[^7][^2] The watershed association also says the keeper's house is a private residence.[^7] The US Lighthouse Society lists the light as a private aid to navigation with an FA-250 optic.[^3]
