Point Pinos Lighthouse stands on the tip of the Monterey Peninsula in Pacific Grove, Monterey County, California, marking the southern entrance to Monterey Bay.[^1] It was first lit on 1 February 1855, and the city that now owns it calls it the oldest continuously operating lighthouse on the West Coast.[^2][^3] Two of its best-remembered keepers were women, and its third-order Fresnel lens is still in the lantern room.[^2][^5]

## The place

The point takes its name from Sebastián Vizcaíno, who in 1602 called it Punta de los Pinos, the Point of the Pines.[^3] In 1852 the Secretary of the Treasury ordered seven lights for the California coast, one of them at this "dangerous southern entrance" to the bay, and the government bought 25 acres of the Rancho Punta de los Pinos for the purpose, with 67 more acres added later.[^3] The station's lot did not reach the sea. The Light-House Board's 1893 report renewed a recommendation made in each of the previous four years that the government buy land to the shore, and Lighthouse Friends dates the first request to 1890, with the purpose of landing supplies conveniently.[^4][^2]

## Building the light

Point Pinos was one of the first batch of west-coast lights and followed the common pattern: a story-and-a-half stone dwelling with the tower rising through its middle.[^2][^3] The National Register nomination describes the dwelling as built of locally quarried stone, with an original brick tower, and the Lighthouse Society's archive lists a brick tower on a stone house.[^6][^1] The lens was meant to be second order, but the shipment was late, and the third-order lens made for Fort Point in San Francisco was sent here instead.[^2][^3]

Delivery of the French lens and prisms held up the opening until 1 February 1855.[^3] Major Hartman Bache, who inspected the west-coast lights as engineer, sailed past in late September 1855 by day and by night and judged the site a poor one, because the light was lost to view among the trees as a ship approached Monterey.[^2]

The light burned whale oil at first, then lard oil, then kerosene; the city dates the change to kerosene to 1882, while Lighthouse Friends says mineral-oil lamps arrived in 1883.[^3][^2] An eclipser, a shutter turned by a falling weight, was added in 1912 and cut the beam off to seaward for ten seconds in every thirty until 1940.[^3] In 1913 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin listed Point Pinos among the stations changed to an incandescent oil-vapor lamp.[^7] A brick oil house was built: the Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1900 records it, and the National Register nomination lists an oil storage building of 1899. The Act of 6 June 1900 finally provided $2,000 to buy the land between the lighthouse lot and the ocean.[^6][^8]

## The keepers

The first keeper, Charles Layton, was an English-born soldier who had come to California with the army and moved in with his wife Charlotte and their children before the light was lit.[^9][^10] In November 1855 he joined the sheriff's posse sent after the outlaw Anastacio Garcia and was shot at Salinas. The San Francisco *Alta California*, in a letter from Monterey printed on 24 November 1855, said he died after "great bodily suffering" and was "universely beloved as a citizen and as a man."[^11] That letter says he crawled about six miles before being found; the Heritage Society's retelling says his companions carried him back to Monterey, and the sources also differ on the year of his death (the city's page says 1856).[^11][^10][^3] Charlotte was appointed head keeper after him, a post the Lighthouse Society archive dates 1855 to 1860 and the Heritage Society dates from 4 January 1856.[^1][^9]

Allen L. Luce kept the light from 1871 to 1893.[^1] Robert Louis Stevenson, in an essay on Monterey, wrote that at Point Pinos one would find "the lightkeeper playing the piano, making models and bows and arrows, studying dawn and sunrise in amateur oil-painting." Lighthouse Friends and the city take him to mean Luce.[^12][^2][^3] Luce also cleared a trail through the forest to Monterey that became Lighthouse Avenue, according to the Heritage Society.[^9]

Emily A. Fish took charge in 1893 and kept the light until 1914.[^1] The inspector who proposed her, Henry E. Nichols, wrote that "women are the neatest and best keeper for the smaller lights."[^2] The Lighthouse Society archive lists her as acting keeper before her appointment as head keeper; Lighthouse Friends says she was nicknamed the "Socialite Keeper" for her entertaining, and quotes a 1912 newspaper item saying she had lately received a letter of commendation and a medal inscribed "Efficiency".[^1][^2] The roster also names her successors: John H. Jeffrey (1914-1919), Richard H. Williams (1919-1931), Peter C. Nelson (1931-1938) and Thomas Henderson (1938-1954).[^1] Mary G. Williams is listed as first assistant from 1919; the Heritage Society says she was Richard Williams's wife.[^1][^9] Of Nelson, the Heritage Society says he called the post "cream of the crop" and entertained visitors on the harmonica.[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

At 5:13 on the morning of 18 April 1906 the San Francisco earthquake shook the station. According to Lighthouse Friends, the logbook recorded that the shocks bent the lens's connecting tube and loosened the lens, and news of the damage in San Francisco took two days to arrive.[^2] Sources differ on the tower's fate. Lighthouse Friends says the top five feet were taken down and rebuilt in reinforced concrete; George Putnam's 1917 *Lighthouses and Lightships of the United States* says the tower was so badly injured that it had to be torn down and was rebuilt of reinforced concrete; the National Register nomination says it was reconstructed to the original plans in 1907.[^2][^13][^6] A lens lantern, and later the Fresnel lens itself, showed from a temporary structure while the work went on.[^2]

Fog was the recurring trouble. A 1922 petition said Monterey was a regular port of call for coasting steamers, that crude oil was shipped there, and that the harbor had no protection in fog.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported in 1915 that fogs were frequent and a compressed-air signal was "urgently required."[^14] An electric siren was established in 1925, which Lighthouse Friends places 1,600 feet northwest of the tower.[^15][^2]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service joined the Coast Guard on 1 July 1939.[^5] During the Second World War, the Heritage Society says, a second bedroom in the keeper's house served as a Coast Artillery command post and the lens was lit with occasional blackouts.[^9] The Heritage Society names Trueman E. Cook as the last civilian keeper, to 1960; Coast Guard crews then appear on the roster until 1969 or later.[^1][^9] The light was automated in 1975, and the City of Pacific Grove took on the lighthouse as a museum; the radio beacon and foghorn were deactivated in 1993.[^3][^1] It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1977.[^2] Lighthouse Friends reports that the Coast Guard transferred the lighthouse and nearly seventy acres to the city in August 2006, keeping the beacon and two housing units, and that deck and railing work on the tower ran from 2019 into 2022.[^2]

## Today

Point Pinos Lighthouse is owned by the City of Pacific Grove and is an active Coast Guard aid to navigation; the Coast Guard is responsible for the light and lens.[^3][^5] The city's page lists museum hours of Thursday 1 to 3 pm and Friday to Sunday 11 am to 3 pm, with admission charged, but hours change, so check with the city before going.[^3]
