Pigeon Point Lighthouse is a 115-foot (35 m) brick tower on a bluff in San Mateo County, California, about halfway between the Golden Gate and Monterey Bay.[^1][^2] Its first-order lens was lit at sunset on 15 November 1872, and the light still gives one white flash every ten seconds.[^1]

## The place

The point is named for a ship. On the night of 6 June 1853 the clipper *Carrier Pigeon*, out of Boston and bound for San Francisco, ran aground in fog about 500 feet (150 m) off this shore.[^1] The Monterey Bay National Marine Sanctuary says the captain and crew reached land safely, but that within fifteen minutes there were seven feet of water in the hold.[^3] The ship, valued at $54,000, was sold where she lay for $1,500.[^1]

She was not the last loss. Lighthouse Friends reports that at least three more ships were lost near the point in the 1860s, and quotes an 1868 editorial in the *San Mateo County Gazette* that no other place on that coast had proved "so fatal to navigators."[^4] Getting a light built was slow. In 1868 Congress provided $90,000 for a first-order light at "Point Año Nuevo or vicinity"; disputes over the site and the price of land let most of that money revert to the Treasury, and it had to be appropriated again on 3 March 1871.[^5] The government had already paid $10,000 on 18 May 1870 for 19.5 acres from Loren Coburn and Jeremiah Clarke, including 1.5 acres at the tip of the point and nine more inland for "water privileges".[^5]

## Building the light

The fog signal came first. Its twelve-inch steam whistle first sounded on 10 September 1871, and the tower was lit over fourteen months later; Lighthouse Friends names heavy rain and difficulty assembling the spiral staircase, fabricated in San Francisco, among the causes of delay.[^4] A California newspaper reported in March 1872 that the tower was nearly complete, its stairs, platforms and balcony of iron and its walls of brick, and that the light would stand 115 feet up and be a revolving light of the first order.[^6] The Light-House Board's own 1894 description gives the brick tower as 100 feet high, on a 50-foot bluff, so that the light was 150 feet above the water; State Parks puts the top of the tower at 115 feet and the focal plane of the lens at 100 feet above the ground.[^7][^1]

According to Lighthouse Friends the first-order lens was made in Paris by Henry Lepaute and has 1,008 prisms; State Parks also counts 1,008, while the park's restoration partners say 1,009.[^4][^8][^9] Lighthouse Friends adds that the pedestal and clockwork had first served in the older Cape Hatteras tower and were shipped to Pigeon Point on 11 August 1871.[^4] In November 1872 the district inspector, Captain Charles J. McDougal, examined the station, and the district lampist, Thomas J. Winship, readied the lamp and instructed the keepers; the lamp was lit at sunset on Friday 15 November.[^1]

The Board's 1894 report says the station cost $90,000 to establish.[^7] It also gives the fog signal's pattern: a 12-inch steam whistle, with a 10-inch kept in reserve, sounding a 4-second blast, 7 seconds of silence, another 4-second blast, then 45 seconds of silence.[^7]

## The keepers

By 1894 the station had four keepers, whose pay the Board listed as $800, $600, $550 and $500 a year.[^7] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive names John W. Patterson as the first principal keeper (1872), after he had served as first assistant from 1871, and lists well over a hundred different people on the roster between 1871 and 1980, many of them for only months.[^10]

A few careers show how the ladder worked. Ora O. Newhall appears as third assistant in 1891, second assistant from 1891 to 1893 and first assistant from 1893 to 1898.[^10] James Marner was principal keeper from 1888 to 1896, and John Nixon from 1915 to 1931.[^10] Jesse E. Mygrants was first assistant from 1924 to 1941; Lighthouse Friends says he had asked to leave Point Arguello for a station where his daughters could attend school.[^10][^4]

Families lived on the point. Lighthouse Friends, citing the *Daily Alta California* of 20 October 1878, reports that five-year-old Stewart Munroe, son of assistant keeper John Munroe, was lost over the bluff into the sea while playing with another child near the lighthouse, and that his parents offered a reward to anyone who recovered the body.[^4] The archive lists John Munroe as first assistant from 1875 to 1879.[^10]

The Lighthouse Service also gave the station efficiency awards. Its Bulletin index for 1912 to 1917 records that the station, under keeper Carl E. Reit, received an efficiency pennant, and the index for 1918 to 1923 records an efficiency flag awarded to Pigeon Point and to its keeper, J. Nixon.[^11][^12]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Light-House Board's reports give the fog signal's running time: the steam whistle ran 698 hours in the fiscal year ended 30 June 1893, burning 68 cords of wood, and some 987 hours, burning 83 cords, in the next year's figures.[^7][^13]

In August 1914 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded that Carl E. Reit, Fred Prussman, F. A. Cook and G. B. R. Rogerson, keepers of the station, had helped a gasoline fishing boat that had drifted on the rocks near the light station, pulling it off and hauling it to the beach.[^14][^11]

Lighthouse Friends reports that in the spring of 1933 the keepers were burning old paint off the dwelling's exterior when Mygrants found a nail that stayed hot and, with his ear to the wall, heard fire inside it. A fire truck summoned from Redwood City reached the station in forty-five minutes; the damage stayed on the east side, the Mygrants family's part of the house, and a work crew repaired their apartment over the summer.[^4]

## After the keepers

The Board's reports show work on the grounds. In 1894 it pointed out that a fisherman's shanty and a hay barn stood outside the station fence, close enough that a fire in either would threaten the station, and asked for $5,000 to buy a strip of land about 150 feet wide to the east.[^15] A report in the 1896-97 volume records a 15,000-gallon tank installed for fire protection and piped to the spring and the dwelling.[^16] The Board's 1900 report says a new fog-signal building, begun the year before, was completed; Lighthouse Friends says that building is the one standing today.[^17][^4]

The light went electric in the 1920s: the 1926 Bulletin lists Pigeon Point among lights changed to electric incandescent.[^18] Lighthouse Friends reports a radio beacon with its own Morse signal added in 1943 and the audible fog signal discontinued in 1976.[^4] In 1960 the Coast Guard demolished the original four-family Victorian dwelling and built four ranch-style houses; in 1972 a rotating aerobeacon on the gallery replaced the Fresnel lens, and the station was automated in 1974.[^4] The Lighthouse Society's archive also dates the present keepers' quarters to 1960 and calls them bungalows.[^10] The lighthouse was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1977, and the houses were leased to American Youth Hostels in 1980.[^4]

In December 2001 two pieces of the tower's cast-iron belt course, each weighing about 300 pounds, fell, and the tower has been closed to the public since.[^9] The lighthouse was transferred to the State of California in May 2005, and in November 2011 the lens was taken down and reassembled in the fog signal building.[^4]

## Today

The lighthouse stands in Pigeon Point Light Station State Historic Park, owned by California State Parks.[^4] State Parks says the tower is closed until the structure is fully restored and may still be viewed from the grounds.[^8] A $16 million rehabilitation contract was awarded in December 2023, and construction began in early 2024: January according to the park's cooperating association, February according to State Parks.[^4][^9][^8]

The day-use area is open from 8 a.m. to sunset, and the visitor center and park store open Thursdays to Tuesdays, 10 a.m. to 4 p.m., as staffing permits.[^8] The rocky point around the lighthouse is closed to all visitors to prevent erosion.[^8] The Fresnel lens, on display in the fog signal building, is due to be lit there at the 154th anniversary event on 14 November 2026, and State Parks says it returns to the tower the following year.[^8] A hostel operates beside the lighthouse.[^8]
