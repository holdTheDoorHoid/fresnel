Bunker Island Lighthouse, which a witness in the Department of Marine's 1909 report calls "Bunker island light (Bug)", stands on a reef off the south-west point of Bunker Island and guards the entrance to Yarmouth Harbour, in Yarmouth County on Nova Scotia's southwest shore.[^5][^1][^3][^2] The first light on the reef was lit in 1874, and the square concrete tower that stands there now dates from 1959.[^2]

## The place

Lighthouse Friends places the light just north of Cape Forchu, at the south-west end of Bunker Island.[^1] The channel lies between the light and Sallows Rock, a shoal. The 1909 report says that the deepest water of the channel lay midway between Bug Light and Sallows Rock.[^5]

## Building the light

Francis Ryerson held the contract for a pier and lighthouse off the south-west end of the island, for $2,875. The total cost came to $3,432.18; Lighthouse Friends says the increase came from the difficulty of building the crib-work that supported the tower.[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives the activation date as 16 February 1874. The NSLPS dates the light to 1873 and first lit in 1874, and calls its foundation a caisson, where Lighthouse Friends says crib-work.[^1][^2]

Lighthouse Friends quotes the Department's first description of the beacon. Its foundation was "crib work about 24 feet high", carrying a small dwelling and tower, with a 15-foot boat slung on davits. The keeper lived in a house on shore, but "remains all night, and during rough weather, in the beacon".[^1]

A fog bell was installed in 1885, the same year a formal dwelling was built on the cribwork.[^1][^2] In 1892 the dwelling was moved to a new concrete and steel pier, a steel cylinder 11 m (36 ft) across, filled with concrete, built immediately south of the old one.[^1]

On 15 January 1907 a second light went into service at the north-west end of the island. The Department's report describes it as an enclosed square wooden building with sloping sides, 6.7 m (22 ft) high, showing a fixed red light 11.6 m (38 ft) above high water, which the report gives as visible six miles. The work was done by days' labour, at a cost of $599.12.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says this North End light was a wooden pyramidal tower and is no longer standing.[^1]

Dredging at the mouth of the harbour forced the old light to be demolished and a new one built further inshore.[^1] The Department's 1925 report describes the steam tug John C. Stoneman as "used temporarily as a lightship" while the station was rebuilt. It says that in July 1923 the tug was moved to a new position, and a temporary fairway buoy was placed abreast of the former position of the Bunker island light.[^6] Lighthouse Friends says the Stoneman marked the station until the new light went into operation in April 1924.[^1] The NSLPS gives the second tower as 1924, on a red caisson.[^2]

The NSLPS dates the present square concrete tower on a red caisson to 1959.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says it was erected not long before Jules Cottreau retired.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends says Joshua Doane served as the first keeper, at a salary of $350 a year, and lists him until 1901.[^1] The Department's 1907 keepers' table prints "Doane, F. H." against Bunker Island.[^4] Lighthouse Friends gives Frederick H. Doane as keeper from 1904 to 1930, and B. A. Doane for 1930 to 1931.[^1] The NSLPS list gives "Doane, Mr." for 1930.[^2]

Jules A. Cottreau kept the light from 1931 to 1963 by Lighthouse Friends' account. The NSLPS starts him in 1930.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends says that he and his wife raised six children at the station between 1931 and the early 1960s. The top of the pier where the light stood was only 11 m (36 ft) across, so the children had little room to play. To go to school they crossed to the island and walked more than 3 km (two miles).[^1]

A child of the family told the *Atlantic Advocate* in May 1965, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, that "it was nothing for us to wear a raincoat to bed". The roof leaked so badly that an umbrella or a raincoat was the only way to stay dry.[^1]

After Cottreau the NSLPS list names Earl King (1963 to 1966), David King (1966 to 1967) and Charles Manser (1967 to 1981).[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives the same names.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Department's 1909 report prints an inquiry into the steamer *Cohan*. Her engines were stopped while she rounded Bunker Island light, and the strong current of the ebbing tide "nipped her starboard bow", putting her across the channel onto the edge of Sallows Rock shoal.[^5] The inquiry said the pilot should have known the deepest part of the channel, and found him "blameworthy for not keeping the ship in the deepest water".[^5] The printed passage does not date the grounding.

The same report prints testimony from James Ross, first engineer of the steamer *Prince George*, given at the inquiry into that ship's collision with the barque *Lowwood* in Yarmouth harbour on 30 July 1907. He said that "before coming to Bunker island light (Bug)" the ship came "dead slow", and that the weather was foggy.[^5]

The NSLPS records that hurricane Edna in 1954 caused considerable damage to the second tower.[^2]

## After the keepers

The NSLPS list ends with Charles Manser, who served until 1981.[^2] The light was automated by 1984.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the beacon was left exposed until a lantern room was added in 1984.[^1] The NSLPS records a dioptric lens, an electric lantern and a fog signal in operation by 1992.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the station. Lighthouse Friends reports that the grounds are open and the tower closed, and that visitors reach the light on foot at low tide from a gate on a gravel road.[^1] The Coast Guard's list describes the present light as isophase red, with equal periods of light and dark every four seconds, shown from a white square tower with a red vertical stripe, 9.1 m high.[^3] The tower is closed to visitors and should not be climbed.
