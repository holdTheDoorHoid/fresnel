Point Cabrillo Light stands on a grassy headland about three miles north of Mendocino, California.[^1][^3] Its wooden tower, a 47-foot (14.3 m) octagon with a third-order Fresnel lens, was first lit on 10 June 1909 for the small schooners on a 115-mile stretch of coast that had no light at all.[^1][^3]

## The place

Before 1909 there was no light on the coast between Point Arena and Cape Mendocino, a distance of 115 miles.[^1] The National Register nomination contrasts the larger coastwise ships with the schooners, which "hugged the shoreline—often at great risk and peril."[^1] The timber economy depended on the "doghole" ports and landings where lumber was shipped out, and the lumber needed to rebuild San Francisco after the 1906 earthquake added to the traffic.[^1]

The Lighthouse Board asked for a light and fog signal here in 1904. Lighthouse Friends quotes the Board's reasoning: small craft carrying the coast's trade "must feel their way as best they can close along inshore," and a light would let vessels bound for Mendocino City stand off the coast rather than risk the harbor at night or in fog.[^3] Congress authorized $50,000 for the station in 1906.[^1][^3]

Before the light, the brig *Frolic* ran aground just north of the point on the night of 25 July 1850. She had been loaded with household goods and was bound for San Francisco.[^3] The United States obtained land on the point from David and Margaret Gordon by a deed dated 6 January 1908. The property is 30.43 acres.[^1][^3]

## Building the light

The lowest bid, $21,985, was accepted later in 1908.[^3] It paid for a combined light and fog-signal building, three keepers' dwellings and outbuildings, including a barn, a pump house and a carpenter's and blacksmith's shop.[^3]

The nomination describes the tower as a wood-frame structure, 47 feet (14.3 m) high, attached to a fog-signal building, with a copper-capped lantern.[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives the lens as a third-order Fresnel lens made by Chance Brothers in England.[^3] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report lists a water tank installed at the station for $1,031.[^6] Water was a real concern here: the fog signals ran on steam, and the nomination notes that California summers are dry.[^1]

The lens turned on clockwork. A weight on a chain ran down through the tower, and the keeper cranked it back up every two hours.[^7] The light gave a white flash every ten seconds.[^3][^7]

Electricity reached the station in 1935. The keepers' association says the clockwork was replaced by an electric motor in 1937, while Lighthouse Friends puts electric motors on the lens and the fog signal in 1935.[^7][^3] Inside the fog-signal house, two 18-horsepower engines drove the compressor that fed twin sirens.[^3] The fog signal's signature was two seconds on, two off, two on, then twenty-four seconds off.[^7]

Three two-story wood-frame houses in the Craftsman style are set in a line at right angles to the shore, each inside its own fence. The middle house was built for the head keeper, and the two flanking houses for the assistants.[^1]

## The keepers

Wilhelm Baumgartner was the first head keeper.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says he came to Point Cabrillo from the offshore St. George Reef light. He was unmarried when he arrived. In 1911 he married Lena Seman, the daughter of a Mendocino blacksmith, and he died at the station in 1923, after about fourteen years there.[^3]

The lens was first lit on 10 June 1909 under Baumgartner, according to the keepers' association.[^7] He had invited the neighbors, and between thirty and forty came on a foggy evening. Ethel Bassett, wife of First Assistant Keeper George E. Bassett, prepared a midnight supper.[^3][^7]

The San Francisco Call printed a Notice to Mariners that the light station "was established June 10, 1909."[^5]

In 1913 the Lighthouse Service commended Baumgartner and his assistants, George E. Bassett and Albert J. Scott, for the "efficient manner" in which they installed new distillate tanks and pipes for the fog signal.[^4]

The archive's roster lists ten head keepers, eighteen assistants and sixteen Coast Guard names from 1909 to 1969.[^2] After Baumgartner, Lemuel C. Miner kept the light from 1923 to 1939, and Thomas A. Atkinson from 1939 to 1950.[^2]

William (Bill) Owens was head keeper from 1952 to 1963.[^2] The keepers' association says he had also served at the Point Arena light and was the last civilian keeper here.[^7] Lighthouse Friends says his wife, Cora, came with him from Point Arena in 1952. She described a goat that kept jumping the fence to eat whatever grew in the station's plantings.[^3] Owens retired at a ceremony at the lighthouse on 28 February 1963, which Lighthouse Friends calls the last civilian keeper's retirement on the West Coast.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In February 1960 a storm badly eroded the bluffs around the lighthouse and damaged the lighthouse itself.[^3] As the storm built, according to Lighthouse Friends, waves climbed the cliffs and broke over the lighthouse. Owens lit the light early and then sheltered with the others in the easternmost dwellings, hoping the sea would stop short of them.[^3]

Afterwards the men found the fog-signal building's large door torn off, its siding stripped and its windows broken. The diesel engines and a Kohler generator had been wrenched from their foundations, and several inches of mud covered the engine-room floor. The big lens in the lantern room was unharmed.[^3]

On 5 January 2023 a surge of water came through the western doors and put two feet of water in the lighthouse, knocking over museum exhibits.[^3]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service was absorbed into the Coast Guard in 1939.[^7] The Coast Guard manned the station until the 1970s, when it covered the lens and set a modern rotating beacon on the roof.[^3]

In 1989 the Coast Guard proposed moving the lens to a museum in Virginia; public protest kept it at the station.[^3]

The California State Coastal Conservancy bought the station in 1991, according to Lighthouse Friends. The keepers' association gives 1992 as the year the Conservancy took possession, and says Coast Guard officers and their families lived in the keepers' houses until then.[^3][^7] The station was listed on the National Register in 1991.[^1][^3]

Restoration started in 1995 with the blacksmith shop and oil house. In August 1998 the lens was taken down to be cleaned and refurbished, and a crane lifted the lantern room off the tower. The restored lantern room and lens were back in place by the following spring.[^3] In 2002 California State Parks bought the station for four million dollars.[^3] The keepers' houses were restored in stages. One became a museum in 2005. The head keeper's house opened as the Lighthouse Inn in 2006. The inn ran until early 2010, when the decision was made to offer the rooms as vacation rentals.[^3]

## Today

Point Cabrillo Light Station is a California State Historic Park, owned by the state and managed with the keepers' association.[^3][^9] The park's own page says the grounds are open from sunrise to sunset, and the restored lighthouse is open 365 days a year from 11 a.m. to 4 p.m.[^9] The keepers' association says the main floor of the lighthouse and the first assistant keeper's house are open year-round from 11 a.m. to 4 p.m., and that lens tours go up to the top on set days.[^8] Lighthouse Friends counts the days the tower is open to climbers as only a few each year, for special lens tours.[^3] Visitors walk about half a mile downhill from the parking area to the station.[^8]

The lighthouse is still an active aid to navigation.[^8] Its lens stopped turning on 29 December 2022, when its 1937 motor failed. The Coast Guard rebuilt the motor, and the lens turned again on 2 October 2023.[^3]
