Gull Island Light is a navigation light on a small, rocky island in Lake Superior, just off the northern tip of Michigan Island in Wisconsin's Apostle Islands.[^1][^5] The light was first shown in 1929, and a 2020 post describes it as a navigation light on a steel tower.[^1][^6]

## The place

The island's shoreline is flat and covered with stones.[^6] A Washburn newspaper described it in 1921 as "little more than a mass of sharp crags and rocks."[^4] Ships bound for Ashland from the Keweenaw were the concern.[^1] In 1907 the Lighthouse Board asked Congress for $85,000 for a light and fog signal on Gull Island.[^1] Its reason, as LighthouseFriends quotes the board, was that vessels bound for Ashland from Keweenaw Point "are unable to see Michigan Island light until abreast of it."[^1]

Congress appropriated $2,000 on 27 May 1908 for a survey.[^1] Major Charles Keller, a lighthouse engineer, completed it in September 1908 and recommended a light and fog signal on the northeast end of Michigan Island instead.[^1] The Commissioner of Lighthouses later gave the same conclusion in its annual reports: "the eastern end of Michigan Island is the better site."[^2]

The board kept asking for money for the Michigan Island plan until 1918, when it changed the request to raising the Michigan Island light, adding a fog signal, and "establishing an automated acetylene light on Gull Island."[^1] The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1917 describes the acetylene light on Gull Island as "a better guide to passing in either direction."[^3] Congress did not appropriate the money until 1928.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1929 a skeleton tower topped by an acetylene light was erected on the island's east-central part, at a cost of $4,752.[^1] Its white light flashed every ten seconds from a focal plane of 55 feet (17 m).[^1] LighthouseFriends says it could be seen for up to thirteen miles.[^1] The light was commissioned on 30 September 1929.[^1]

## The keepers

The light was minded by keepers from Michigan Island.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes the log that Keeper Ed Lane kept on the day of commissioning, which begins: "Put Gull Island in commission today. 10:30 AM."[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In May 1921 the steamer Thomas Wilson went aground on Gull Island, in a dense fog.[^4] The Washburn Times of 26 May 1921 says she was bound for Washburn with 8,000 tons of soft coal, and that the fog and the island's lack of a fog signal were blamed for the collision.[^4] LighthouseFriends gives the cargo as 7,800 tons, and calls the steamer a total loss.[^1]

The Washburn Times says the crew took to the lifeboats and made for Michigan Island, half a mile off, for help, and went on from there to Bayfield.[^4] LighthouseFriends names Keeper Edward Lane of the Michigan Island light as the man who "rescued the crew and took them to Bayfield."[^1] The two accounts differ on who brought the crew in.

The same newspaper said the wreck recalled a law passed by Congress 15 years earlier to build a light on Gull Island, after lake captains and the Ashland Chamber of Commerce asked for one.[^4] "It was never built."[^4] The paper also named two earlier accidents on the island: the Robert M. Ireland and the William E. Corey, which were "both dashed against its rocks" within the previous fifteen years.[^4] LighthouseFriends lists the William E. Corey among the steamers wrecked on the island.[^1]

## Today

LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard owns the light, that the tower is closed to visitors, and that the light is best seen by boat.[^1]

A national-lakeshore closure listing covers Gull and Eagle islands: they are closed to protect colonial nesting birds, 500 feet (152 m) seaward from shore, from 15 May to 1 September.[^5] A 2020 post from the Friends of the Apostle Islands National Lakeshore says the islands are closed to the public during the nesting season, and that a navigation light on a steel tower helps boats avoid the island at night.[^6]
