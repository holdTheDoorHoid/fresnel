Green Island Light stood on an island in Green Bay, about five miles off the mouth of the Menominee River, from 1863 until 1956.[^1][^2] Samuel Drew was its first keeper, and his son Frank was head keeper from 1909 to 1929.[^3] The Lighthouse Service's bulletin commended Frank for rescues twice in its 1912 volume.[^4][^5]

## The place

The island lies near the main shipping channel of Green Bay, and shoals reach out from it for some distance.[^1] LighthouseFriends ties the need for a light to the great expansion of the lumber industry at Marinette and Menominee in the 1840s.[^1]

Lumbermen and ship captains first called for a light on Bertraw Bay, north of the Menominee River. Wisconsin's representative Billinghurst took up the call with a different site, Green Island.[^2] LighthouseFriends says Billinghurst presented a petition from the citizens of Wisconsin to the House of Representatives in February 1858, and that Congress appropriated $6,000 for the project on 3 March 1859.[^1] Terry Pepper's history says the Lighthouse Board sent Captain George Gordon Meade of the Corps of Topographical Engineers to Green Bay in August 1861. Meade recommended one light on Green Island to answer both the petition and the request for a light on Bertraw Bay.[^2]

The sources name different owners of the land. LighthouseFriends says a land patent for the island went to Peter McBride on 2 March 1854, and that he was paid $39.96 for the 1.998 acres the station needed.[^1] Pepper says the reservation was bought from Samuel Drew, who by 1867 owned half the island.[^2]

## Building the light

The light was first shown on the night of 1 October 1863. LighthouseFriends cites a Notice to Mariners that gave that date, and Pepper gives the same night.[^1][^2]

The keepers' dwelling was brick, with a short square wooden tower on its roof.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends describes a two-and-a-half-story house of Milwaukee brick, and Pepper describes two stories of Cream City brick.[^1][^2] The USLHS record gives the tower shape as octagonal on a square house, which matches neither account.[^3]

At the top, a cast-iron lantern held a fourth-order Fresnel lens showing a fixed white light.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends puts the focal plane at 55 feet (16.8 m), and Pepper gives a visible range of 14 miles in clear weather.[^1][^2] The sources disagree on the fuel. LighthouseFriends says the keepers were issued lard oil, and Pepper says sperm oil.[^1][^2]

LighthouseFriends describes an accident in 1864. It appears that Samuel Drew, experimenting with a kerosene lamp of his own, tried to put it out by blowing down the chimney. The lamp exploded and set his clothes afire, a second explosion severely damaged the lens and lantern room, and a temporary structure was put up while the tower was repaired.[^1]

## The keepers

Samuel Drew was appointed in 1863 at a salary the sources give differently. LighthouseFriends says $400 a year, and Pepper says $560.[^1][^2]

Samuel and his wife, Mary, farmed much of the island.[^2] Pepper says Samuel owned half of it by 1867, and that the family sold 87 bushels of strawberries in Menekaunee one summer.[^2] Their daughter Anna was born in 1869 and died at three months old. LighthouseFriends says a storm kept the family from taking her to the mainland, and Pepper says the family buried her under a lilac tree on the island.[^1][^2]

Frank, born on the island in 1864, was the seventh child in LighthouseFriends' count and the first of five in Pepper's.[^1][^2] The USLHS roster lists the eldest daughter, Helen Marie Drew, as first assistant from 1867 to 1870, and LighthouseFriends calls her assistant keeper in those years.[^3][^1] Pepper names Mary Drew as assistant keeper in 1867 instead.[^2]

Samuel was transferred to the Menominee Pierhead Light in 1881. Pepper gives the transfer date as 27 May 1881 and says Samuel died in August 1882.[^1][^2][^6] The roster lists nine principal keepers between that transfer and Frank's promotion in 1909, and most of them served only a few years.[^3]

Frank entered the service at Pilot Island in 1899, as LighthouseFriends tells it. The USLHS Pilot Island roster lists him there as second assistant (1899 to 1900) and first assistant (1900 to 1903).[^1][^7] Pepper dates his promotion to first assistant there to 1 April, three months after his appointment. He came back to Green Island in 1903 as first assistant.[^3][^1] Pepper dates that appointment to 31 October 1902, the year his wife Mary died, and says he moved to Marinette to be closer to the rest of his family so he could keep raising his children.[^2]

Frank was promoted to head keeper on 15 July 1909, and his brother George W. Drew became his assistant.[^2][^1][^3] Edward H. Cornell took over in 1929.[^3] Alfred L. Cornell, first assistant from 1919, was head keeper from 1931 to 1933, and LighthouseFriends calls him the last head keeper.[^3][^1]

General context, not this station's record: the Lighthouse Board's 1881 *Instructions to Light-Keepers* said that the keeper "shall take an equal share in all the work and duties of the station" with the assistant keepers.[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In October 1871 the Great Peshtigo Fire swept the region.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it destroyed Peshtigo and then jumped across Green Bay to the Door County peninsula.[^1] Pepper says the smoke was so thick that "the light was virtually invisible on the water," and that Samuel kept the lamp burning day and night.[^2] LighthouseFriends adds that embers fell on the station's shingles, that Keeper Drew and the oldest children fought fires on the island, and that Mary looked after the younger children while keeping the light burning in the tower.[^1]

At 9 p.m. on 8 October 1871 the three-masted schooner George L. Newman ran aground on the shoals off the island.[^1] Pepper says the smoke made the light almost invisible and that there was no fog signal on the island.[^2] Keeper Drew helped the crew to the island, and they stayed about a week while they tried to salvage what they could from the wreck.[^1][^2]

Winter ice was hard on the landing.[^2] Pepper says the winter ice of 1902 wrecked all the shoreline structures, and that the ice destroyed the boathouse and landing again in 1905.[^2] Falling water was also a problem. Pepper says the lake level drop caused the well to run dry in 1893, and the Lighthouse Board's report for that year says the well "was deepened to a depth of 20 feet."[^2][^9]

In 1912 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin commended Frank A. Drew, keeper, and George W. Drew, assistant keeper, for rescuing two men and two women and for going to the aid of others in distress.[^4] A second notice in the same volume commends Frank for going to the rescue of 24 people in a disabled power boat.[^5] LighthouseFriends and Pepper date the rescue of the two men and two women to 1913. LighthouseFriends counts 23 people aided from the gasoline boat Neptune, and Pepper counts 24 passengers.[^1][^2] The Bulletin's notice was printed in 1912, earlier than both dates. The sources do not agree on the year or the number.

On 21 July 1914 the brothers towed a disabled gasoline launch, the Bulletin reports.[^10]

In 1917 the station's lamp was changed to incandescent oil vapor.[^11] The Bulletin lists Green Island among the lights changed that year, and Pepper says the change raised the light to 1,700 candlepower, with a range of visibility of fifteen miles.[^11][^2]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for fiscal 1921 records that "F. A. Drew, keeper of Green Island Light Station, Wisconsin, towed the disabled yacht Vanity" to Menominee.[^12] Pepper places that tow of the Vanity in 1920.[^2]

In fiscal 1922 Frank Drew towed the disabled fishing tug Loyd to Marinette after temporary repairs at the station.[^13] Pepper dates the Loyd job to 1921, and LighthouseFriends gives 1922.[^2][^1]

In December 1925 Frank Drew and his assistant, Alfred Cornell, spent most of the month on the island with no communication with the mainland. LighthouseFriends says they walked the five miles across the frozen bay to Marinette, a trek that took three hours in a gale.[^1]

In October 1928 a plane that failed during a trip across Green Bay glided down to a landing on the island.[^1][^14] The Bulletin says the keeper kept the uninjured passengers overnight and took them on to the Coast Guard station the next day. LighthouseFriends says Frank Drew took the couple to Menominee by boat.[^14][^1] LighthouseFriends names the pilot as Thomas Mitchell, who was travelling with his wife and a dog, and cites a Daily Northwestern story of 18 October 1928.[^1]

On 31 January 1930, LighthouseFriends says, Edward Cornell and his son were at dinner when an explosion blew doors and windows out of the lighthouse.[^1] An inspector blamed a defective gas stove and put the damage at $1,000.[^1]

## After the keepers

Frank Drew retired on 31 March 1929 and died on 2 February 1931, Pepper says.[^2]

The light was automated in 1933.[^3] Pepper says a 1,000-candlepower acetylene lamp with a sun valve took over, switching the light on at dusk and off at dawn without a keeper, and that the building was stripped, boarded up and left to stand empty.[^2][^1][^3] Alfred Cornell was sent to Chambers Island Light, where the USLHS roster lists him as principal keeper from 1933 to 1941.[^1][^15]

In 1955 the Roen Steamship Company bought the island except the lighthouse reservation. The Coast Guard erected a 65-foot (19.8 m) steel skeleton light east of the old station in 1956, and Roen bought the reservation in 1957, with the Coast Guard keeping the right to service the new tower.[^2][^1]

Without supervision or maintenance, vandals wrecked the 1863 house over the following years, Pepper says. A fire then burned the roof and the wooden interior, and the walls crumbled.[^2] The USLHS record calls the station "Damaged by Fire and Vandals; Only Shell Remains."[^3]

In 1999 the Coast Guard commissioned the buoy tender FRANK DREW (WLM-557) at Marinette, named for the keeper. LighthouseFriends gives the commissioning date as 17 June 1999.[^1][^2]

## Today

Only part of the 1863 walls is left, LighthouseFriends says.[^1] Pepper's page, last revised in 2007, describes the 1956 steel light as flashing white every six seconds and gives its range as ten miles.[^2]

The island is privately owned.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says Roen Salvage Company, which it calls the owner of Green Island, announced big plans for the island and lighthouse in 2011, including a proposed $90 million development with condominiums and a marina.[^1] LighthouseFriends notes that the remains can be seen only from the water or from the air.[^1]
