Fire Island Light is a black-and-white banded brick tower of about 168 feet (51 m) on the Atlantic side of a long barrier island off the south shore of Long Island. The Light-House Board called it, in 1894, the light that transatlantic steamers bound for New York generally made first, and the preservation society says it was many European immigrants' first sight of America.[^7][^6]

## The place

Fire Island is a barrier island off the southern coast of Long Island, and the light station sits about 5 miles (8 km) east of the island's western end.[^1] Lighthouse Friends notes that the origin of the island's name is uncertain and lists several theories that guides repeat.[^5]

The hazard was offshore. The National Park Service says that sandbars lie about a quarter mile off the beach, and that approximately 200 known and an estimated 640 unidentified ships have wrecked off Fire Island since the mid-1600s.[^3] After the federal government took over the lights in 1789, Montauk Point (1797) was the first on Long Island and Fire Island, put into service in 1826, the second.[^3] The 1981 National Register nomination describes the station as filling the gap between Montauk Point and Sandy Hook, and calls it the first landfall for ships approaching New York from the Atlantic.[^1]

## Building the light

The first tower went up at the western end of the island, beside Fire Island Inlet. The Park Service describes it as 74 feet (23 m) tall, built of Connecticut River blue split stone, and the keeper's family lived in an adjacent two-story house; there was no bridge, so everything came by boat.[^2][^3] Sources disagree on details: the Park Service's two pages give its focal plane as 80 feet and as 89 feet, and Lighthouse Friends calls the tower 85 feet tall, against the Park Service's 74.[^2][^3][^5] The station preservation society gives 15 November 1826 as the first lighting; the US Lighthouse Society's archive dates the station to 1827.[^6][^4] Lighthouse Friends reports that it cost $9,999.65.[^5] Its first apparatus was eighteen Argand lamps with 15-inch reflectors, turning to flash every 90 seconds; in 1842 it was replaced by fourteen lamps with 21-inch reflectors.[^2]

By the 1850s the tower was judged too short. Congress appropriated $40,000 in 1857 for a new one.[^6] Lighthouse Friends credits Lieutenant J. C. Duane of the Corps of Engineers with the plans and Lieutenant James St. Clair Morton with the construction, and puts the new tower about 200 yards from the old one.[^5] The nomination agrees that the first foundation lies about 200 yards from the present station.[^1] The new tower is a brick tower, 140 feet (43 m) from foot to cornice, with a further 24 feet (7 m) to enclose the watch room and lantern.[^1] It was lit on 1 November 1858 with a first-order Fresnel lens made in France by Henry-Lepaute.[^6][^3] Stone from the first lighthouse and keeper's house went into the terrace the new tower and dwelling stand on, and the dwelling was finished in 1859.[^1][^2] The heights given for the new tower vary with what is measured: the Park Service says the light was at 166 feet above the sea, Lighthouse Friends calls the tower 168 feet tall, and the nomination gives about 168 feet for the focal plane.[^3][^5][^1]

By 1876 the brick tower had a cement wash that made it cream-coloured.[^1] The nomination and the preservation society date the black and white bands to 1891; the Park Service says 1892.[^1][^6][^3] Fuel changed with the century: whale oil until 1867, lard oil until 1884, kerosene until 1907, incandescent oil vapour until 1939, electricity after that.[^1] The preservation society says electric power reached the lighthouse on 20 September 1938, and that a hurricane struck the next day and cut power to the island.[^6] A Western Union telegraph line came in 1878 and a telephone in 1898.[^1]

## The keepers

Early on the keeper was as much landlord as lightkeeper. The archive's roster begins with John M. Isaacs as head keeper from 1826, followed by Felix Dominy in 1835.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says he was better known as an innkeeper than a lightkeeper: a local superintendent complained in 1843 that he put boarders ahead of his lighthouse duties, and he was relieved in 1844 and became a full-time innkeeper on the island and at Bay Shore.[^5] The nomination notes that in the nineteenth century the keeper and assistants served as "mayors" of Fire Island, helped baymen, and kept inns for city visitors seeking a primitive holiday.[^1]

The archive's roster lists assistants from 1850, and shows Benjamin Smith, who was head keeper when the new tower was built, serving from 1853 to 1861.[^4] After him, until Seth Hubbard arrived in 1874, came a series of short terms.[^4] The longest was that of Captain Ezra S. Mott, who was head keeper from 1888 to 1909 and retired because of ill health.[^5][^4] Lighthouse Friends reports that he went to sea at seventeen and had served on the Stratford Shoal lightship and at Stratford Shoal Light before coming to Fire Island, and that in summer 1909 he made twenty trips up and down the tower for some 500 visitors.[^5]

Not every keeper's record was clean. The New York Sun reported on 24 October 1886 that an assistant inspector of the Third Lighthouse district had gone to Fire Island the day before to question Keeper Seth Hubbard, who was accused of letting the light go out and of receiving goods stolen from the wreck of the steamship Oregon; Hubbard's resignation, the paper said, had been in the hands of the district inspector, Commodore Benham, for several days; he wrote that heart trouble kept him from taking charge of the light.[^10] Lighthouse Friends adds the inventory: lace, silk shawls, neckties, silverware, and says Hubbard tendered his resignation when the investigator arrived.[^5] Hubbard was removed on 6 November 1886 together with his son-in-law, first assistant Walter B. Abrams.[^5] Two years later his successor, Charles F. Smith, and first assistant Edward Udall were dismissed after charging each other, Smith with fraudulent vouchers and Udall with disobeying orders.[^5] In 1921 Keeper Frank Oberly was arrested and charged with burgling cottages at nearby Saltaire; Lighthouse Friends reports the charge, not a verdict.[^5]

Daily life reached the family level as well. Lighthouse Friends says that Arthur Miller, first assistant in the early 1930s, drove his children along the beach to school at Ocean Beach and once lost his car to the rising tide when it stuck in the sand.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The 1886 case above is the one wreck the sources tie directly to the keepers. Beyond it, the record is of the sea as a hazard for ships passing: in its report for fiscal 1895 steamship owners and pilots are recorded as petitioning the Light-House Board for a lightship off Fire Island, and the Board's report quoted the number of incoming passengers passing the island from northern Europe as averaging 450,000 a year.[^8] The Board's report for 1896-97 lists Light-Vessel No. 58 off Fire Island, a separate station from the lighthouse.[^12]

Lighthouse Friends reports that one winter night Keeper Mott was startled by thumping on the lantern glass and that wild ducks broke through; in the morning forty dead ducks and three geese lay at the tower's foot.[^5] No date is given.

## After the keepers

The first big change came in the 1890s. The Light-House Board's report for 1894 called Fire Island "the most important light for transatlantic steamers bound for New York" and said it would buy the bivalve electric lens shown at the Chicago exposition of 1893 and install it in place of the existing lens.[^7] The 1895 report lists the preparatory work: a power house and coal shed, two boilers, an engine, a dynamo, and a narrow-gauge railroad from the beach.[^8] Lighthouse Friends says the lens installation was then cancelled, a lightship was used instead, and the bivalve lens went to Navesink, New Jersey in 1898.[^5] The Board's report for fiscal 1900 records experiments in wireless telegraphy at the station by War Department signal officers; the nomination dates them to 1901.[^9][^1]

By 1912 the tower was cracked. Lighthouse Friends says it was banded with iron and steel mesh and coated with cement.[^5] The 1914 *Lighthouse Service Bulletin* reports cement plaster on the brick tower at Fire Island, using 96.66 cubic yards of material.[^11] The Park Service says the original Fresnel lens went out of service in 1933; the preservation society and Lighthouse Friends say it was replaced by the lens from the decommissioned Shinnecock Lighthouse, which turned faster and flashed every 7.5 seconds instead of every minute.[^3][^6][^5] (The society's timeline dates the old lens's display at the Franklin Institute from 1939, not 1933.[^6]) The Lighthouse Service was merged into the Coast Guard in 1939.[^6]

The Coast Guard decommissioned the light on 31 December 1973 (the nomination says 1974), replacing it with a flasher on the Robert Moses State Park water tower.[^6][^1] The tower decayed. The Fire Island Lighthouse Preservation Society formed in 1982 in response to a threat of demolition.[^6] It raised more than $1 million, and the light was relit on 25 May 1986.[^6][^5] The tower reopened to visitors in 1989 and the full restoration was celebrated in 1994.[^5] The society has operated the light as a private aid to navigation since 2006.[^3][^6]

## Today

The station is part of Fire Island National Seashore and is owned by the National Park Service; the society runs visitor services.[^5][^3] The foundation of the first lighthouse can still be seen near the second.[^2] The original Fresnel lens, after being displayed at the Franklin Institute in Philadelphia, came back in 2007 and went on display in a new building in 2011.[^5] The island was reachable only by boat until a bridge opened in 1964, and today visitors park and walk roughly a mile to the tower.[^5] In March 2023 a panel of brick and stucco fell from the base and the tower was closed; the museum and lens building stayed open.[^5] Check the Park Service or the Preservation Society for the current state of tower access before visiting.
