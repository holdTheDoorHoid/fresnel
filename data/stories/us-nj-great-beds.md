Great Beds Light is a black cast-iron foundation topped by a white five-story tower, standing in the shallows of Raritan Bay about a mile off South Amboy, New Jersey. It was lit in 1880 for ships heading up the Arthur Kill and the Raritan River, and a red light still flashes from it every six seconds.[^1]

## The place

The light marks Great Beds Shoal, a shallow patch off the southern shore of Staten Island near where the Raritan River and Arthur Kill shipping channels meet.[^1] The lighthouse takes its name from the oyster beds on which it stands.[^3] The nomination says Perth Amboy and South Amboy developed into busy railway hubs with substantial maritime traffic, and ties that traffic to vessels colliding with the lighthouse.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $34,000 for a lighthouse at Great Beds on 20 June 1878.[^1] The first obstacle was one of land title. The government obtained rights to the submerged land from New Jersey, ordered the iron tower and imported 1,000 barrels of cement, but a slight change of site meant it had to apply again. That led to the discovery that an 1834 agreement between New York and New Jersey made the bottom at both proposed sites New York's, and work stopped until the Light-House Board got title from New York. A later survey of the state line put the site on the New Jersey side.[^1] The station's boundary position stayed uncertain for years: the Lighthouse Board listed it under New Jersey in 1879, New York in 1880 and both states in 1898, according to LighthouseFriends, and later the Lighthouse Service listed it in New Jersey.[^3]

The structure is a cast-iron caisson, 30 feet (9 m) high and filled with concrete, standing in about 11 feet (3.4 m) of water. On it sits a five-story conical iron tower lined with brick and a ten-sided fourth-order lantern.[^1] The National Register nomination puts the tower and lantern together at 47 feet (14 m); LighthouseFriends gives the tower as 42 feet (13 m).[^1][^3] The original lens was a fourth-order Fresnel showing a fixed red light, 57 feet (17 m) above the sea and visible 13 miles in clear weather, from a mineral-oil lamp with a red chimney.[^1] The first keeper, David C. Johnson, was appointed on 3 November 1880, and the light was first shown on 15 November 1880.[^1]

The tower was first painted red with a black lantern, then brown by about 1890, and then white around 1892, which the nomination says made it easier to see against the dark land to the north, west and south.[^1]

## The keepers

The station's early keepers stayed briefly. An assistant keeper's post was filled by John W. Totten, who was removed in February 1881, and then by Joseph F. Morey, whose appointment lasted six months; the assistant's post was abolished on 1 September 1882.[^1] Johnson resigned on 3 October 1882 and George Brennan took over.[^1]

Brennan is recorded in the station logbook as missing on 24 April 1883, and the entry for 15 May reports that his body had been found.[^1] LighthouseFriends, drawing on a New York Times report of 1 May 1883, says he had gone ashore to draw his pay and set out for the light in a rowboat in the evening; the light did not burn that night, and his boat was found upturned on the beach. The page quotes the newspaper as saying the police believed he had been followed and murdered, or else that his boat capsized as he tried to reach the light in an intoxicated state.[^3] His successor, John E. Johnson of Tottenville, Staten Island, lasted only a few months: the log for 25 and 26 August 1883 reports that he had drowned, and he left a widow and four children.[^1] LighthouseFriends, citing the New York Times of 28 August 1883, says he was last seen on duty on the night of 18 August, with his boat moored at the tower and the keys on a table inside.[^3]

After a run of short-term and acting keepers, David C. Johnson returned in 1894 (the nomination prints his name as David J. Johnson); it says the assistant's post was reinstated in his time.[^1][^2] In April 1898 he and his assistant John Anderson were dismissed. LighthouseFriends reports that each had accused the other, and that an investigating officer found that Anderson had used profanity in front of Johnson's family and that Johnson had been careless with government property; neither was reinstated.[^3]

John Osterdahl became keeper in April 1898 and stayed until September 1909. After the station's assistant was transferred to Highland (Navesink) Light in 1902, his wife Olivia became assistant keeper and served until 1909.[^1] Pay at the start was $600 for the keeper and $400 for the assistant. The assistant’s post was abolished again in September 1909, when the keeper was henceforth allowed to bring his family to live at the station; a 1909 letter in the files explained that the light was "close to the shore" and could be kept by one keeper if his family lived with him.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A machine-struck fog bell of 1,227 pounds was added in June 1898, sounding a double strike every 15 seconds according to the Light-House Board's 1898 report; by 1901 the Light List gave one strike every 15 seconds.[^1] LighthouseFriends, quoting a Notice to Mariners, says a single blow.[^3] Vessels kept hitting the tower. The nomination counts no fewer than ten occasions between 1901 and 1914 when barges under tow or other craft struck the station, sometimes tearing away the landing ladder or smashing the davit boat; in one collision the privy fell into the water.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes a collision on 25 January 1906 in which a tug of the Pennsylvania Railroad towing barges struck the tower, and the owners paid $20 to repair the damaged ladder.[^3]

On 4 and 5 October 1918 the munitions plant at Morgan, New Jersey, exploded, and debris fell in the water around the light. Three panes in the lantern broke and the lamp went out six times during the night; Keeper George W. Denton, Jr., stayed in the lantern, relighting it and watching the lens, and later received a commendation.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1918 records that he performed his duty faithfully.[^4] LighthouseFriends reports that his wife Carrie died in hospital ashore on 16 October, and that he resigned in November, writing that the strain of rowing out to the station and keeping the light after his loss was too great.[^3] The roster shows him at the station in 1918 only.[^2]

In 1925 the Bulletin recorded that Keeper E. J. Brunner had, on 21 June, helped two people aboard a sand barge that had broken loose from its moorings at the South Amboy railroad dock.[^5]

In early 1922 the Mayor of Perth Amboy asked Congressman T. Frank Appleby to have the light's visibility improved, saying that in dense fog it could not be seen until the tower was nearly in view. The Lighthouse Service's inquiry concluded that a stronger light would add little in fog or smoke, that changing red to white risked confusion with shore lights, and that the real problem was the light's position relative to the channels.[^1] In 1929 the Third District proposed brightening the fixed red light from 150 to 870 candlepower with an incandescent oil vapor lamp, approved on 16 January 1929.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over the Lighthouse Service in 1939.[^1] The nomination gives 1942 for the removal of the Fresnel lens in favour of a 300-millimetre electric optic, though an earlier section says it was removed when the light was automated in 1945; LighthouseFriends also puts the replacement in 1942.[^1][^3] In 1945 the light was automated and the fog signal discontinued, and the fourth-order lens's present location is unknown.[^1] The nomination also records that the sheds, outhouse, boat davits, flagpole, smokestack and chimney have been removed from the gallery.[^1] The USLHS roster lists keepers through Thomas F. Walker (1935 to 1941).[^2]

## Today

The tower was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2008, and the nomination records the Coast Guard as owner.[^1][^3] The Coast Guard declared it excess in 2010; with no public body taking it on, the General Services Administration auctioned it, and a winning bid of $90,000 was made on 28 June 2011.[^3] LighthouseFriends does not know the present owner and lists the lighthouse as privately owned with the tower closed.[^3] It is reachable only by boat and is not open to visitors; the light is best seen from the water or from the shore, and LighthouseFriends mentions the grounds of the Conference House on Staten Island as a viewing point.[^3]
