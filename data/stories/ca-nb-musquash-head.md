Musquash Head Lighthouse stands on the eastern headland at the mouth of Musquash Harbour, a few kilometres west of Saint John, New Brunswick.[^1] A light has been shown there since 1879.[^1] The hexagonal concrete tower that replaced the first one in 1959 still shows a white flash every three seconds.[^6]

## The place

The cliffs of Musquash Head make up the eastern side of the harbour mouth.[^1] In 1880 an automatic buoy was anchored about 2.4 km (1½ miles) from the lighthouse to mark Split Rock, which Lighthouse Friends says had taken numerous vessels over the years.[^1]

The Commissioner of Lights' report, printed in 1902, records that a bell buoy was set out off Dipper Harbour for the use of fishermen, and that "a similar bell buoy was, at the same time, established off Musquash harbour."[^2]

## Building the light

Lighthouse Friends says Parliament appropriated $2,000 in 1878 for a lighthouse on the headland.[^1] The contract, for $1,890, went to David Currie, a carpenter from Upper Gagetown, to build a keeper's house joined to a square wooden tower about 12 m (40 ft) high.[^1] The total came to $3,271: Currie was paid $2,125, and E. Chanteloup received $921 for the lantern, blinds, chimneys and reflectors.[^1]

The light was first exhibited on 15 February 1879.[^1] Standing 34 m (112 ft) above high water, it shone green toward the open sea and white into the harbour, and Charles P. Hamm was its first keeper.[^1]

The light changed later.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates the switch from a fixed light to a flashing one to before 1912, and notes that a 1918 list of lights described a group of four flashes every thirty seconds.[^1] The tower was first painted all white; by 1918 it had red and white horizontal bands.[^1] In 1912 a Reliance clock took over from the clockwork, and in 1929 a motor-driven revolving apparatus, powered by two 900-watt generating units, was installed.[^1]

The present tower dates from 1959.[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives it as 14.3 m (47 ft) tall; the Coast Guard's list gives the structure as 14.0 m.[^1][^6] Rowlett's directory calls the tower octagonal, against the hexagonal of Lighthouse Friends and the Coast Guard.[^5] A fog horn was added in 1967, after the one at Tiner Point, five kilometres to the east, had been shut down.[^1]

## The keepers

Charles P. Hamm, the first keeper, appears against Musquash in the Department's keeper tables in the reports published in 1906 and 1908, which matches the service dates Lighthouse Friends gives for him.[^3][^4] Lighthouse Friends reproduces a photograph captioned as showing the keeper's wife at a window of the original tower.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends then names J.S. Galbraith (1914 to 1931), G.L. Tyner (1931), H. Cartwright (1931 to at least 1939), Hartley H.G. Wilson (to 1963), Raymond Tiner (1963 to 1978) and George S. Hoar (1978 to 1987).[^1] Only that source names them for this station.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources name no particular wreck or rescue at Musquash Head.[^1][^2] What they do show is the hazard the light was for: the Split Rock buoy, and the bell buoy that the 1902 report records off Musquash harbour.[^1][^2]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says automation came in the early 1980s and the last keeper left in 1987.[^1] The two keepers' dwellings were taken off the site soon afterwards.[^1]

On 21 July 2008 the Department of Fisheries and Oceans handed the lighthouse, with Greens Point lighthouse, to local community groups, at a ceremony in the New Brunswick Museum.[^1] The group that took it on, Musquash Head Lightstation Inc., planned to keep about 12 acres (4.9 ha) around the light and to put the other 98 acres (about 40 ha) in trust with the Nature Conservancy of Canada.[^1] Rowlett's directory gives the same July 2008 transfer and says the Lightstation group later disbanded, after its president left the area. In late 2018 a new group was considering restoration.[^5]

Lighthouse Friends says the light went for years without a caretaker until Explore Lorneville stepped in, and that the tower was painted in the autumn of 2020.[^1]

In June 2021 the Nature Conservancy of Canada announced three land parcels totalling 275 acres (about 111 ha).[^8] One of them, a 92-acre (37 ha) stretch at Musquash Head that includes the lighthouse, came through a partnership with Explore Lorneville, a community group.[^7][^8] Explore Lorneville first asked the public for up to $35,000 to restore the lighthouse, improve nearby trails and put money away for future maintenance.[^8] Around 300 individual donors gave, and a $75,000 donation from the federal Natural Heritage Conservation Program helped.[^8] Global News reports that the community group will keep some of the land around the lighthouse, with the rest going to the Conservancy.[^7]

Ownership of the building is not settled by the sources. Lighthouse Friends' visitor notes still name Musquash Head Lightstation Inc. as the owner, although the same page says that company disbanded.[^1] Rowlett lists the Nature Conservancy of Canada as owner and site manager.[^5] The 2021 news reports do not say who holds title to the building.[^7][^8]

## Today

The Coast Guard's list still gives Musquash Head as a light: a white flash every three seconds, from a "White hexagonal tower with red horizontal band," with its light 35.1 m above the water and visible for 20 nautical miles.[^6] The list also carries the remark "Private aid."[^6]

Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] From Route 1 west of Saint John, King William Road leads to a rough dirt road and a red gate, with parking just short of it; from there a 1.2 km (0.9 mi) walk on a dirt road reaches the lighthouse.[^1] Rowlett describes the hike as about 1.5 km (1 mi) round trip, and the two figures do not agree.[^5]

The Lorneville Link, a 20 km trail opened in 2021, ties the Split Rock trail to Prince of Wales and joins the Black Beach and Five Fathom Hole trails.[^8]
