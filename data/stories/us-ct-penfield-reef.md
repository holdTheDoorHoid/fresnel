Penfield Reef Light stands on a round granite pier in about five feet (1.5 m) of water in Long Island Sound, off Fairfield in Fairfield County, Connecticut, at the end of a shoal that runs out from Fairfield Beach.[^1] A square keeper's house with a mansard roof and a light tower rising through its south side, it was first lit on 16 January 1874 to mark a reef for which Bridgeport maritime interests had been lobbying since 1866.[^1][^2]

## The place

Before the lighthouse, the reef was marked by a can buoy.[^1] According to the National Register nomination, lobbying from Bridgeport maritime interests began in 1866, and the Light-House Board proposed a lighthouse in 1868. A light and a fog signal there would let vessels run directly between Bridgeport's growing harbor and New York City at all hours and in all weather.[^1] LighthouseFriends adds that the steamer *Rip Van Winkle* ran aground on the reef in 1864 with passengers aboard, and quotes a Light-House Board officer in 1867 calling it "the most dangerous locality, during fogs and snow-storms, upon Long Island Sound."[^3]

## Building the light

The design was borrowed. In April 1868 Joseph Lederle, acting lighthouse engineer for the Third District, recommended that the Penfield Reef tower and dwelling "be built on the plan approved by the (Lighthouse) Board for the Hudson River stations."[^1] The foundation was another matter. Floating ice in those latitudes ruled out an iron-pile (screwpile) light, so the Board turned to a circular pier of cut granite, a model taken from the one proposed for Race Rock.[^1]

Money came slowly. The nomination says plans for the foundation could not be approved until after 3 March 1871, when a total of $55,000 was made available.[^1] According to the nomination, a riprap foundation 108 feet (33 m) across was laid from 1871 to 1873, with a pier of granite blocks on top, 18 feet (5.5 m) high and tapering in nine courses from about 48 feet 9 inches to 46 feet 6 inches (14.9 to 14.2 m) across; Johnson's 1889 account dates the work 1872–73 and gives the pier's height as 18 feet.[^1][^4] The dwelling, 28 feet 2 inches (8.6 m) on a side, has a granite first story and a wood-framed second; it went up in 1873.[^1] Inside were a sitting room, a kitchen and a supply room on the first floor and four bedrooms above.[^1] A drawing of the station appeared in the Light-House Board's report for 1874, and a picture of it was shown at the 1876 Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia.[^1]

The light itself was a flashing red, shown 54 feet (16.5 m) above the sea in Arnold Burges Johnson's 1889 description; the nomination gives a focal plane of 51 feet (15.5 m).[^4][^1] The nomination says a fourth-order lens was equipped with clockwork in 1889, made at the Board's lamp shop at Tompkinsville, New York.[^1]

## The keepers

The first head keeper listed by the U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive was George Tomlinson (1874–1876), followed by Augustus W. Eddy (1876–1880). Eddy had served briefly as first assistant in 1876 before taking charge, a path later followed by Elmer V. Newton, assistant from 1906 and head keeper from 1908 to 1914.[^5]

Two women appear in the assistants' list in the 1880s: Pauline J. Jones (1880–1882), under head keeper William Jones, and Jane Martin (1882–1886), under head keeper Neil Martin.[^5] Lighthouse Digest, which gives the second name as "June", says both were married to keepers.[^6] Neil Martin's nine years (1882–1891) and William H. Haynes's seventeen (1891–1908) were the longest head-keeper terms before 1914.[^5]

The station's best-known keeper story belongs to Frederick A. Jordan, head keeper from 1914.[^5] On Friday 22 December 1916 he left the reef in a rowboat for the mainland after his assistant had relieved him. A Norwich newspaper reported on 26 December that he started "a little after noon" for Black Rock harbor and that "a terrific gale set in shortly after 2 o'clock."[^7] The assistant, whom Lighthouse Digest names as Rudolph Iten, saw Jordan's boat swamp and started after him in another boat, but the wind and waves were too strong.[^7][^6] The paper put Jordan's age at 38 and said he left a widow and two children at Black Rock.[^7] Iten later told a reporter it was "a dirty day for weather and a nasty sea was running," as Lighthouse Digest quotes him.[^6] The accounts differ on what followed: the Digest says the body was soon recovered, while LighthouseFriends says it washed ashore on Long Island the next March.[^6][^3] Iten became head keeper in 1917 and served again from 1920 to 1926.[^5] Both sources also record the legend that Jordan's ghost remained at the station; that is lore, not record.[^3][^6]

In the 1920s and 30s the roster is dominated by long-serving assistants: William A. Shackley (second assistant from 1927, first assistant from 1928, head keeper from 1941 to 1946), Edward T. Pastorini (assistant 1928–1933 and again 1942–1946) and William Hardwick (second assistant 1933–1941).[^5] The archive's roster gives George Petzolt as head keeper from 1929 to 1941.[^5] Lighthouse Digest, however, calls Hardwick "keeper" from 1932 and says he had earlier saved seven crewmen of the *Calvin Tompkins* while at Bridgeport Harbor Light, so its titles do not match the register.[^6]

From 1951 the roster is mostly Coast Guard personnel, listed under that heading until 1971.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The fog signal is the best-documented part of the station's working life. The first apparatus was a bell struck by machinery.[^1] Around 1891–92 an engineer who tested a new signal here wrote that he lost the bell's sound at a mile and a half while the new Daboll trumpet, driven by Rider hot-air engines, was still plainly heard at more than nine miles.[^8] Penfield Reef had little room to store fresh water and fuel, which the Board's 1894 report gave as a reason to prefer the hot-air design.[^9] The trumpet ran about 404 hours in the year ended 30 June 1894.[^10] In 1898 oil engines replaced the hot-air engines; the 1899 report says the trumpet was out of service from 7 October 1898 for the change (its own entries give three different dates in November for the restart), and also records 500 tons of riprap added around the foundation.[^11]

On 24 October 1917 a storm damaged a string of lights in the Third District, Penfield Reef among them.[^12]

In 1929 the Lighthouse Service commended William A. Shackley, then first assistant, for a rescue on 19 January: during a heavy sea he left the station at 3 o'clock in the morning, took a captain and his wife off a distressed scow and gave them shelter overnight.[^13]

In October 1930 Edward T. Pastorini, second assistant, was commended for saving three men clinging to an overturned rowboat in a severe storm on 19 October.[^14] A Connecticut newspaper reported the rescue on 20 October: the men had clung to their capsized boat for over two hours in the cold Sound, and the paper credits Shackley and Pastorini, saying Pastorini put out in a rowboat, dragged the three exhausted men aboard and returned to the lighthouse.[^15] The paper called Shackley "the keeper"; the register has him as first assistant at the time.[^15][^5]

Lighthouse Digest adds that Hardwick was at the station in December 1935 when a blizzard drove a Japanese steamer onto the rocks, and that an assistant stayed at the light through the hurricane of 21 September 1938 while Hardwick, on his way out in a skiff, gave up and went back to the mainland.[^6]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard announced plans in 1969 to replace the granite house with a pipe tower; local opposition, with the help of the area's congressman and a state representative, saved it, says LighthouseFriends.[^3] The light was automated in 1971: the nomination says December, while LighthouseFriends gives 4 September and adds that men stayed on three more months while the doors and windows were sealed.[^1][^3] Penfield Reef Lighthouse was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1990 as part of the Operating Lighthouses in Connecticut group.[^1]

Neglect followed. LighthouseFriends reports that in 2000 an inspection found a rotted main beam had tilted the lantern; leaks in the lantern were repaired the next year, and a stainless-steel roof was placed on the lantern room in 2003. The Coast Guard prepared in 2005 to declare the lighthouse excess property, and in 2008 the nonprofit Beacon Preservation was recommended to take it over. The Interior Department rescinded that application in February 2011 because no agreement had been reached with the state over a lease of the submerged land, and Beacon then abandoned its claim. Hurricane Sandy damaged doors, windows and gutter in 2012, and about $1 million of work was completed in 2015.[^3] Since 2011 the lighthouse has repeatedly been offered at online auction; LighthouseFriends records high bids in 2011, 2016, 2021, 2023 and 2025, and says the 2011, 2016, 2021 and 2023 winners did not close.[^3]

## Today

The lighthouse is an active aid to navigation and is reachable only by boat; it is not open to visitors.[^16][^3] The U.S. General Services Administration's 2025 auction notice called it a 51-foot octagonal lighthouse with a 1,568-square-foot keeper's dwelling, and required any buyer to obtain a submerged-land lease from the State of Connecticut.[^16] LighthouseFriends reports that the 2025 bidding closed on 4 September with a high bid of $370,000; the sources do not say whether that sale closed. LighthouseFriends describes the lighthouse as privately owned, while the 2025 GSA notice treats it as a federal sale, so the current owner is not established here.[^3][^16] LighthouseFriends notes that the reef is over a mile long, that walking out at low tide is not considered advisable, and that distant views are possible from the shore at Reef Road in Fairfield; the safest close view is from a boat with a captain who knows the area.[^3]
