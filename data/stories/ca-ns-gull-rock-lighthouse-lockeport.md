Gull Rock Light stands on a wave-swept islet at the entrance to Lockeport Harbour, on Nova Scotia's south coast, about 2.4 km (1.5 miles) off the land.[^2][^6] A light has marked the rock since 1853. The lighthouse on it now dates from 1948 or about 1955, and the sources disagree on which. The Canadian Coast Guard operates it. Visitors can see it from the land, but the rock itself is reached only by boat.[^1][^2][^4]

## The place

The town of Lockeport began as a settlement that the Locke and Churchill families reached from Massachusetts in 1762. It was known as Locke's Island until 1907, when the township became the Town of Lockeport.[^3]

Gull Rock sits among a group of dangerous ledges and rocks, which Lighthouse Friends calls the Ragged Islands, that guard the entrance to the harbour. The rock itself is a wave-swept ledge about 100 m by 38 m.[^1] In 1851 the shipowners of Shelburne County petitioned for a light to mark these dangers and guide ships into the harbour. The government approved money for a lighthouse on the rock.[^1]

The entrance had another mark as well. In January 1904 the Department set a bell buoy at the entrance to Lockeport, replacing an iron can buoy that had been maintained in the same locality.[^6] The buoy was moored about an eighth of a mile south of a rock that the Department's report calls Bull rock. The sources read here do not say whether that is the same rock as Gull Rock.[^6]

Lockeport's own town site names two lights that have long marked the waters around it: Carter Island Light and Gull Rock Light.[^3]

## Building the light

The first lighthouse was finished in 1853. It was a square pyramidal tower with a dwelling in its lower part. A separate building, 6.1 by 4.0 m (20 by 13 ft), held oil and firewood. Six lamps, five of them with reflectors, gave a fixed white light.[^1]

A water tank went in during 1871, so that the keeper no longer had to haul water from the mainland.[^1] In 1882 a new iron lantern room, 2.9 m (9 2/3 ft) across, went on a platform built on top of the tower, with a more powerful set of reflectors on flat-wick lamps.[^1]

A hand fog alarm was used at the station until 1 May 1918, when a compressed-air diaphone was set up.[^1][^2] In 1922 the light got a fourth-order dioptric lens that showed a group occulting white light every 30 seconds.[^2]

Lighthouse Friends says the original tower had deteriorated beyond economical repair, and that a new two-storey lighthouse with a square tower went up in 1948.[^1] The Nova Scotia society puts the replacement at about 1955, and gives 1941 to 1960 as the span of its replacing the 1853 tower.[^2] In 1986 athenite was sprayed on the outside of the walls, which increased their reinforced concrete thickness from 30 cm to nearly 43 cm.[^1]

## The keepers

Samuel K. Hayden was the first keeper, appointed in 1853, and Lighthouse Friends has him in post until at least 1858.[^1]

On 28 October 1873 Samuel K. Hayden Jr., then keeper, and his wife Charlotte drowned when the rowboat they were in capsized on the crossing to town. Samuel was twenty-four, and the couple had married the month before. Lighthouse Friends is the only source read for this account.[^1] His brother William, who saw the boat go over and could not swim to help, took over the light and kept it until 1876.[^1]

Lorenzo D. Orchard was keeper for a long stretch: Lighthouse Friends gives 1877 to 1913. In 1884 he was given a hand fog alarm, which he had to sound in fog in answer to vessels' signals.[^1] The Department's list of lightkeepers for the year to June 1902 places his name against Gull Rock.[^5]

The keepers after him are many. Lighthouse Friends names a string of them, several serving two stints, through to Barry L. Crowell, who served to 1987. Bill Ringer kept the light from 1964 to 1986.[^1][^2] The full list is in the roster, with the years each source gives.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Department's report for the year ended 31 March 1917 has an entry dated 20 June. That June was in 1916, the year before the report's year ends. "On June 20 stranded on Gull rock, N.S.," the entry says. A formal investigation at Halifax on 27 June found the stranding due to careless navigation by the master, and his certificate was suspended for six months.[^7]

The entry gives only "Gull rock, N.S." The report does not say which rock of that name it means, and the text read here does not confirm that it was this one.[^7]

## After the keepers

The station was de-staffed in 1987, and the fog-alarm building, the workshop and the boathouse were removed.[^1] The Nova Scotia society says the station was automated by 1987.[^2] Two skeletal towers now stand nearby, one for the light and one for the foghorn.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends records the longest continuous running of the old-type foghorn as fifty-six days, on Gull Rock.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends says the lighthouse is endangered, because its outside covering is falling away and exposing the brick beneath. A group called Protect Gull Rock Lighthouse has formed to try to save it.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light, and Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] The island is reached only by boat.[^2] The nearest land view is from the end of Western Head Road, past a gate and along a short trail, and Lighthouse Friends says it gives a view of the light on a clear day.[^1]

The Coast Guard's List of Lights gives the present light as a long white flash every 15 seconds, with a focal height of 16.7 m.[^4]
