Wolfe Island Lighthouse stands on Québec Head, the north-eastern point of Wolfe Island, the largest of the Thousand Islands in the St. Lawrence River, Ontario.[^1][^4] It was lit in October 1861 and still shows a white light.[^1][^9] The site is private and closed to visitors, and the light is best seen from the water.[^1][^4]

## The place

Wolfe Island lies between Kingston and Cape Vincent, New York, where Lake Ontario meets the St. Lawrence River.[^1] LighthouseFriends notes that the eastern tip of the island was also called East Point and Hemlock Point.[^1] In 1852 and 1853 the Commissioners of Public Works reported new lights at Fiddler's Elbow and other points between Brockville and Kingston, which let the mail boats run at night.[^3] The next year, in 1856, the Commissioners recorded a proposal to move the Fiddler's Elbow tower to Hemlock Point, on the recommendation of the captains of the steamers.[^3] LighthouseFriends thinks the tower was probably moved, although it says the record is not clear.[^1]

## Building the light

The light on the eastern tip was established on 25 October 1861.[^1] A Superintendent of Lights, Darius Smith, visited in 1877 and described the station as "a square white building, containing a fixed catoptric light, 36 feet high" (about 11 m). The inspection does not say whether the 36 feet is the height of the building or of the light.[^2] The light used three No. 1 flat wick lamps and three 15-inch reflectors. Its lantern was iron, 1.8 m (6 ft) across. The lighthouse, he wrote, "requires painting," and the dwelling needed eight new window sashes.[^2]

By 1908 the light used a 270-degree fifth-order French lens, burning acetylene, and showed white.[^10] The list spells the station "Wolf Island" and prints it under Ontario.

In fiscal 1911-12 the Department erected a 10 m (33 ft) standard wooden tower and lantern here, replacing a worn-out building.[^5] LighthouseFriends dates the tower to 1911 and the Lighthouse Directory gives 1910 for the light; this account uses the report's fiscal year.[^1][^4]

## The keepers

Thomas Kilty and Robert Gillespie were both listed as keepers in 1862 and 1863.[^1] From then until 1885 Robert Gillespie was the sole keeper. LighthouseFriends notes that the change coincides with the building of a keeper's dwelling on the point.[^1]

Keeper Gillespie appears in the 1877 inspection, which says he "has four children, and he keeps his light in good order."[^2]

In August 1882 Gillespie asked a local paper to print a notice. It said he was not dead, and that a candidate's petition for his post "will, therefore, be of no avail." Gillespie was, the notice said, "as well and active as he was 16 years ago."[^1]

Robert Gillespie died in June 1887. The Kingston British Whig printed a tribute two days after his death. It says he had come to Canada in 1840, had been a farmer before taking the post, and had been in charge of the light "for a quarter of a century."[^6] The same tribute places the lighthouse "at Port Metcalfe, foot of Wolfe Island." That does not match the Quebec Point site the 1877 inspector described. This history follows LighthouseFriends in treating the two as the same station, and leaves the place-name difference open.[^1][^6]

His son William Gillespie was appointed in March 1885 and kept the light until 1939.[^7][^8][^1] Before that he had been custom house officer at Port Metcalfe.[^1] LighthouseFriends lists Everett Woodman as keeper in the 1940s and until 1954.[^1]

## After the keepers

The lantern room has since been removed.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory gives the present tower as 6.5 m (21 ft) and describes it as a square pyramidal wood tower with its lantern removed; the Coast Guard list gives its height as 6.4 m. The sources do not date the change.[^4][^9]

A private house near the tower is a replica of the Thomas Point Shoal lighthouse in Maryland. LighthouseFriends reports that the original keeper's dwelling reportedly remains standing farther away.[^1][^4]

LighthouseFriends' keeper list ends with Woodman in 1954, and it gives no date for automation.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard lists the light as a white light with a four-second cycle, shown 11.3 m above the water, with a nominal range of 8 nautical miles.[^9] The Lighthouse Directory describes the cycle as two seconds on and two seconds off.[^4] It is shown from a white tower.[^9]

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and the Lighthouse Directory lists its site manager only as "private".[^1][^4] The road and path leading to the light are private, and the grounds and tower are closed.[^1] LighthouseFriends advises seeing the light by boat.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory notes that the island can be reached by ferry from Kingston, and in summer from Cape Vincent, New York.[^4]
