Old Harbor Light stands in Emmet Park on East Bay Street in Savannah, Georgia. It is a 25-foot (7.6 m) cast-iron post that the federal government erected in 1858 as an aid to navigation on the Savannah River.[^1][^2] By the late 1990s the post had corroded and was found unstable. It was taken apart and repaired in 2000, and relit on 11 January 2001.[^1]

## The place

The light was half of a range, paired with a light on Fig Island.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the range guided vessels past six Revolutionary War ships that the British had scuttled in 1779 to shut out the French.[^1] The marker in the park says the beacon "served for several years as a guide to vessels passing over the hulls of ships that the British scuttled in 1779 to close the harbor to the French naval forces."[^2]

## Building the light

In October 1855 Captain J. F. Gilmer of the Engineers wrote to the Lighthouse Board proposing a harbor beacon at Savannah. He suggested a cast-iron column 25 feet (7.6 m) high, with a lantern and red lights, at a cost of about $2,000, with the city giving the land.[^1]

Congress appropriated $2,000 on 18 August 1856 for a small light on the bay, to guide vessels from the Fig Island light. $1,969.12 was spent in 1857 and 1858 to finish it.[^1] The gas light burned at a focal plane 77 feet (23.5 m) above the river. The Savannah City Gas Company was paid $150 a year to care for it.[^1]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends lists the Savannah City Gas Company, together with two men, as the keepers: James Kearney, 1866 to 1867, and William Ryan, 1867 to 1869.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's Digital Archive lists both men as "Principal Lighthouse Keeper," with the same years.[^3] The sources do not say how the gas company's care of the light and the two men's keeping fit together.

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends quotes the Lighthouse Board's 1869 report as saying the beacon, "being no longer a range," had been discontinued.[^1] The sources do not say when the lamp last burned. In 1879 Congress appropriated $3,000 to move the Fig Island light and set up a companion light on the Exchange Building. On 1 December 1880 a light in the Exchange's cupola became the rear light of the Fig Island range.[^1]

The change followed a shift in the channel.[^1] In January 1881 the *Savannah Morning News* listed the cutting off of a point of Fig Island among two important harbor works, and said an appropriation for beacon lights along the river "is greatly needed."[^4] By March 1881 the same paper described the Fig Island light as "in range of light on the Exchange."[^5]

Years of salty humidity had worked on the iron post, and by the late 1990s it was found to be unstable. Cables were put in place to stabilize it, and the lamp was put out.[^1] The park's garden area was made in 1958, the centennial year of the light, as a project of the Trustees Garden Club.[^2] In early August 2000 the light was taken apart into six parts and repaired. The *Savannah Morning News* and CSX each gave $62,500 toward the work.[^1] It was relit in Emmet Park on 11 January 2001.[^1]

## Today

The light is owned by the City of Savannah.[^1][^3] The Digital Archive's place record marks it as not open to the public, while LighthouseFriends says its grounds are open.[^3][^1] Ship anchors, several of them large, stand beside the cast-iron shaft, and the park overlooks the Savannah River.[^1] A Georgia Historical Commission marker, erected in 1958, stands in the park.[^2]
