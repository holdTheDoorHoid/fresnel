Frying Pan Island Light was a small iron tower on a rock off De Tour Village, Michigan, first lit on the evening of 1 October 1882 to guide ships into the St. Marys River.[^1][^2] The island light went out of use in 1937.[^2][^10] In 1988 the Coast Guard took the tower to its station at Sault Ste. Marie, where it still stands in front of the main building.[^10][^11][^1]

## The place

Frying Pan Island lies just off De Tour Village, about two miles (3.2 km) above the mouth of the St. Marys River.[^1] It is a rock outcropping a few hundred feet around, a scant few feet above the water.[^1] A shoal lay a few hundred yards to the southeast, and the light also worked as a front range with the light on Pipe Island, further upstream.[^1]

LighthouseFriends gives an account of the island's name. Captain William Thorn, it says, was held by wind near the river mouth, went ashore on a nearby island and named it after a frying pan he found there.[^2] None of the other sources used here repeats the story.

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $2,000 for a beacon light on the island in 1879.[^2][^1] In July 1880 a Cheboygan newspaper reported that the money was available "for a small beacon light on Frying Pan island," and that the station would be built once the site was bought.[^4]

The sources disagree on when the island was secured. LighthouseFriends gives 4 January 1881 as the date the deed was finalised.[^2] Terry Pepper says the island was surveyed in 1880 but that clear title could not be obtained until 1882, so construction began that summer.[^1]

The engineer, Major Godfrey Weitzel of the Eleventh District, had only the small appropriation to work with.[^1] The tower was built on that budget: it was about 18 feet (5.5 m) tall, with no brick lining inside, no shelves, no oil building and no dwelling for the keeper, and oil was kept inside the tower itself.[^1] The sources describe the iron differently. Terry Pepper says a wrought and cast iron tower, LighthouseFriends describes quarter-inch steel plates riveted together, and the U.S. Lighthouse Society's record lists steel. That record also gives 15 feet (4.6 m) from base to lantern, a lower figure than the 18 feet (5.5 m) the other two give.[^1][^2][^3]

Work finished late in September 1882.[^1] The light was first shown on the evening of 1 October 1882 as a fixed red light of 160 candlepower, which Terry Pepper says could be seen for ten miles.[^1] The Society's record gives a sixth-order Fresnel lens installed that year. LighthouseFriends names the lens maker as Barbier & Fenestre.[^3][^2]

In July 1884 a crew put up an oil store, 8 by 12 feet (2.4 by 3.7 m), prefabricated at the Detroit depot. To give the keeper some accommodation, one end had a bunk, shelves and a cleaning table.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the bunk was there in case the keeper had to spend the night on the island.[^2] A wooden oil house, ten by twelve feet (3 by 3.7 m), followed in 1890.[^2]

In its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894, the Light-House Board reported that "The color of the tower was changed from brown to white," so that it would work better as a day range with Pipe Island. The report also says that a landing crib and a pier were built.[^6] In its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1897 the Board said that "The Detour Range lights on Frying Pan and Pipe Island should be increased in power." It added that both were then "fixed red of the fifth and sixth order."[^7]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society's record lists six principal keepers, from 1882 to 1921.[^3]

J. W. Church lit the light in October 1882. LighthouseFriends says he resigned the following month.[^2][^3]

Frank Bernard was the keeper from 1882 to 1891 and saw the oil store and oil house built.[^3][^1][^2] In 1891 he moved two miles (3.2 km) upstream to Pipe Island, where LighthouseFriends says he served for nineteen years. The Society's Pipe Island roster also lists him there from 1891 to 1910.[^2][^12]

Joseph Riell took over in 1891 and is listed to 1896.[^3] A list of appointments in a Grand Rapids paper of 7 April 1891 named the new keeper of the light station at Frying Pan Island, though the scanned text garbles the surname.[^5] LighthouseFriends spells the name Riel and says he was a close relative of Louis Riel. It gives no source for that, and no other source used here confirms it.[^2]

Herbert S. Slocum is listed for 1896 alone. William A. Burke followed from 1896 to 1900.[^3]

Nelson R. Abear was keeper from 1900 to 1921, the longest tenure on the roster.[^3][^2] The Light-House Board's report for 1902, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, said the keeper lived in "a sort of shed consisting of but two rooms" with no adequate protection from the weather, and it asked for a dwelling to be built on the island.[^2] The request was for $4,000.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a seven-room, two-story house was finished on the site that year, with a storm house added in 1905.[^2] Terry Pepper writes that he has not been able to establish whether the dwelling was ever built.[^1] The sources disagree, and this story does not settle it.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 22 December 1916 Abear rescued two men from the disabled motorboat J.E.O.[^2] Five days later he went to help the tug Rambler, which was trapped in ice near the light with nine people aboard. He gave them food and then took them, with the mail the vessel carried, to De Tour Village.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the Secretary of Commerce later commended him for saving life or property in conditions of peril.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1920 proposed changing the Pipe Island and Frying Pan Island lights to "nonattended acetylene," under an act of 7 November 1918 that appropriated $80,000 for aids to navigation in the St. Marys River.[^8] The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1921 says that "the work at Pipe Island and Frying Pan Island has been completed."[^9] LighthouseFriends says the light's characteristic changed on 9 April 1921 to a red flash every two seconds, and that the light was soon made unattended.[^2]

In 1935 a new light was set on a coal elevator 700 feet (213 m) south of the iron tower.[^2] The tower's light was discontinued in 1937.[^2][^10] The Michigan lighthouse directory gives the same year, reading "Inactive since 1937."[^10] In 1953 an electric siren fog signal was installed on the island.[^2]

The Society's record gives 1988 as the year the station was discontinued.[^3] The dates answer different questions. 1937 is when the light stopped working, and 1988 is when the tower was taken away.[^2][^10][^11] On 8 August 1988 the Coast Guard removed the tower from the island and took it to its base at Sault Ste. Marie, where it was restored and fitted with new windows.[^2][^11] Terry Pepper says the tower was replaced on the island by an automated pole light at a date he has not been able to fix, and that the old tower was later moved.[^1]

## Today

The tower stands on display at the Coast Guard station at 337 Water Street, Sault Ste. Marie.[^2][^10] Terry Pepper says it was restored and painted, then displayed in front of the main station building.[^1] The lighthouse is owned by the Coast Guard.[^2][^10] LighthouseFriends and the Lighthouse Directory both give the grounds as open and the tower closed.[^2][^10] The Society's record, however, says the station is not open to the public.[^3] The tower stands on Coast Guard property, and visitors should treat the tower itself as closed.[^2][^10]

On the island, a quick-flashing green light on a pole, marked Frying Pan Island, was in place by 2016.[^2]
