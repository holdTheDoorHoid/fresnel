A small island at the southern entrance to Catalina Harbour, on the Bonavista Peninsula side of Trinity Bay, carries a red and white octagonal tower that has marked the way into the bay since 1857, and it still has keepers.[^1][^2][^5]

## The place

Green Island lies off the east coast of Newfoundland, on the southern approach to Catalina Harbour in Trinity Bay.[^2] The Canadian Register of Historic Places says the light warns of the dangers of Flower Rocks and guides vessels into Trinity Bay, and that Catalina was the only community on the shores of the bay in the middle of the 19th century.[^2] In 1844 John V. Nugent, who was travelling the coast inspecting schools, wrote that the harbour entrance was not difficult by day but that its dangers "forbid the attempt to enter by night", as quoted by Lighthouse Friends.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1855 a Mr. Winter brought a bill for a lighthouse on Green Island before the Newfoundland House of Assembly. The next year Robert Oke, the Inspector of Lighthouses, presented a plan and estimate, and the Assembly approved up to £1,200 from the Light Dues. Alexander Smith got the building contract and Blaine Johnston & Co. supplied the lantern.[^1] Oke served as Newfoundland's first inspector of lighthouses from 1855 to 1871, and the register entry for the Lighttower calls the tower one of the best examples of his designs.[^3]

Building materials were landed on the island on 30 July 1856, and Smith handed over the finished lighthouse on 5 November. Bad weather kept the first keeper, Patrick Houlahan, from landing until 9 December, and Oke put off fitting the late-arriving lantern until spring. The permanent light, a fixed white light 28 m (92 ft) above high water, went into service in June 1857.[^1] The first building was a stone tower with a one-and-a-half-storey wooden dwelling built around it, and a domed lantern painted in red and white stripes.[^1]

The register notes that the lighthouse "suffered due to unsuitable materials and construction techniques" at first and was altered and repaired several times.[^2] Lighthouse Friends records the main steps: decayed freestone under the lantern was replaced with brick in 1876, and in 1880 the whole freestone drum was replaced with an iron one carrying a new gallery.[^1]

Later changes reshaped the tower. In 1956 two one-storey keepers' dwellings were built and the old wooden house round the tower was removed. Reinforced concrete was placed around the old stone tower to make the present octagon, still topped by its original domed lantern.[^1] Generators electrified the light in 1967, commercial power arrived two years later, and the keepers went onto a four-person rotation.[^1] The light's characteristic was changed in 1922 from fixed white to a triple white flash every twelve seconds; today the List of Lights gives a single white flash every 3.33 seconds, visible 15 nautical miles.[^1][^5]

Sources disagree about the date of the colony's new status. The heritage character statement says the lighthouse was the first navigational aid built after Newfoundland received full colonial status in 1855, while the register entry for the Lighttower prints the year as 1885.[^4][^3]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists Patrick Houlahan as keeper from 1856 to 1866, then Austin Sheppard (1866 to 1867), Captain Henry Roper (1867 to 1869) and Peter Woods (1869 to 1894).[^1] Peter Martin, who had served as engineer of the fog alarm from 1888, became keeper in 1895 and stayed until 1900, followed by Robert Stevens (1901 to 1908), Abel Stone (1909 to 1910) and T. Edgecombe (from 1911).[^1] The same page also names Ronald House (recorded at least in 1936), John House and Kenneth Sheppard, the last two without dates.[^1]

Water was a long-running problem. Inspector Oke had a well sunk in the summer of 1858. The following year, after the keeper complained about the well water, he recommended a 300-gallon frost-proof tank for water supplied from the mainland. In 1875 gutters and downpipes were fitted to feed a cistern, ending the yearly expense of boating water to the island.[^1] In 1893 an inspector, as quoted by Lighthouse Friends, wrote that the keeper's dwelling was "in a very dirty condition" and that Woods, "now an old man", was growing less attentive to cleanliness.[^1]

Ambrose Price was head keeper from 1967 and was still there at the end of 1984, with Murdock Stewart beside him. When an Ottawa Citizen reporter telephoned on 31 December 1984, the temperature was minus 15 and a northwest blizzard was blowing at 90 km/h; Price said they had nothing to do but sit at the table.[^1] Clayton Dyke served from 1981 and retired in 2011 at 79 after thirty years. He recalled that one night, just about dark, he and another keeper saw a dragger heading for the island: the other keeper waved a flashlight while Dyke went in and sounded the horn, and the vessel made the turn.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The fog signal came in two stages. A building for a Champion fog horn was put up in 1882, and the steam-powered alarm began on 1 March 1883, sounding a five-second blast every forty seconds; the former assistant keeper was promoted to engineer to look after it.[^1] A diaphone replaced it in 1909, in a flat-roofed building 22.9 m (75 ft) east of the tower, and an English-built alarm was installed in 1920.[^1] The List of Lights now gives a horn blast of 4 seconds followed by 56 seconds of silence.[^5]

On 22 May 1954 the longliner *Bright Eye 2* ran aground on Green Island. The keepers rescued three men, but Leonard Little drowned.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard, and Lighthouse Friends reports that it is one of twenty-three Newfoundland and Labrador stations still staffed.[^1] The register entry says four related buildings contribute to the heritage character of the lighthouse.[^2] It was classified a Recognized Federal Heritage Building in 1990.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower closed, but the register says the lighthouse is accessible by boat or plane, so it is a working, staffed station that visitors should treat as closed unless the Coast Guard says otherwise.[^1][^2] The tower can be seen from the end of Seaview Drive in Catalina and from Murphy's Cove Trail in Port Union.[^1]
