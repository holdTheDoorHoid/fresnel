Ninini Point Lighthouse stands on the north side of the entrance to Nawiliwili Bay, on the Hawaiian island of Kauai, near Lihue. Its first light was a fixed white lamp shown from an open wooden tower in 1897. The 72-foot (22 m) concrete tower that stands there today was put into service in 1932, though the archive's record gives 1933.[^1][^2][^3][^6]

## The place

The Lighthouse Service's own review of the Hawaiian lights says: "There were no lights on Kauai until after 1890."[^5] Ninini Point marks the northern side of the entrance to the bay.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the Hawaiian government rented the point from the Lihue Plantation in 1897 as a lighthouse site.[^7] The island has no natural deepwater harbor along its coast, LighthouseFriends notes, so part of Nawiliwili Bay was dredged and sheltered by a breakwater to create a harbor.[^7] By 1932 the Lighthouse Service described the harbor as "now the most important port on the island of Kauai."[^2]

## Building the light

A notice to mariners dated Honolulu, 18 October 1897, announced that fixed white lights had been established on Kauai and on Hawaii. The Ninini Point light was a tower of open wooden frame, topped by an enclosed lamp room painted white. The notice gives the light's elevation as 70 feet (21 m) above the sea, and says it was visible for 10 miles.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes the tower itself as forty feet (12 m) tall, with the light at seventy feet above the sea.[^7]

The Lighthouse Service's account says the lights were run by the Republic of Hawaii from 1894 to 1900, and that the federal government has maintained them since 1904.[^5] The archive's record gives 1904 as the year the station was established.[^6]

The 1897 tower did not last. LighthouseFriends reports that in 1904 the lamp room was taken off and replaced by a small box that carried a lens lantern. In 1906 the old tower was torn down. A mast 33½ feet (10 m) tall, topped by a lens lantern, was first exhibited on 22 December 1906.[^7] The Lighthouse Service's bulletin of 1932 says the oil lantern used at the station dated from "its establishment in 1906."[^2] A 1933 bulletin calls it an oil-wick light, "adequate for the traffic of small boats," but of little use once large vessels began to use the port.[^4]

The Lighthouse Service then wanted a permanent tower. LighthouseFriends reports that in 1929 it asked Congress for $22,000 for a 72-foot (22 m) reinforced concrete tower.[^7] In July 1931 a 36-inch revolving airways beacon was put on a temporary 40-foot (12 m) wooden tower.[^2] The permanent tower was completed by contract at Nawiliwili Harbor, and the bulletin says the light was moved to it on 15 November 1932.[^3] The archive's record gives 1933 as the year the tower was established.[^6]

## The keepers

The archive's roster for the station lists 19 keeper lines from 1897 to at least 1971.[^6] The first, Manuel Souza, served from 1897 to 1903.[^6] LighthouseFriends reports that he and his wife, who had come to Hawaii from Madeira under a plantation contract that Souza bought out, lived at the lighthouse for six years. In 1902 he wrote to the government that his pay was too small for his family, that water cost him 50 cents a bucket, and that he had paid for the station's whitewash himself. He resigned the next year.[^7]

Two men are listed for 1904 alone: Carl Blum and Richard E. Hagemann.[^6] The roster names John McLaughlin for 1904 to 1909, and LighthouseFriends calls him James McLaughlin in its narrative. LighthouseFriends reports that he planted trees around the house and named each one after a Lihue acquaintance, and that he was moved to Oahu in 1909.[^6][^7] After him came John H. Kanekoa (1909 to 1912), Edward L. Miller (1912 to 1915) and Alexander D. Toomey (1915 to 1917).[^6]

Oliver Kua kept the light from 1917 to 1938, the longest stint on the roster with a known end, at 21 years.[^6] LighthouseFriends gives his assignment as 1918 and his retirement as 1939, when the Coast Guard took control of the light.[^7] After him the roster lists William C. Dooley (1939 to 1942), Samuel Anderson (from 1942), Clifford H. Hardy (1945 to 1947), Jack W. McDonald (at least 1950), Ronald E. Cook (at least 1959 to 1961), Anastasio Mendez (1961 to 1964), James L. Brantley (1964), Gary F. Zych (1964 to at least 1965), Charles E. Jones (1967 to 1970), Walter E. Glassco (1970) and Dennis V. Wilson (1970 to at least 1971).[^6]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the light was automated in 1953, but an attendant stayed on to maintain it and seven minor lights on the island.[^7] The archive's roster, by contrast, still names keepers to at least 1971.[^6]

## Today

LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard owns the light, and that the point is just south of the airport at Lihue.[^7] The grounds are open; the tower is closed and should not be climbed.[^7]
