Carmanah Point is a cliff on the southwest coast of Vancouver Island, at the Pacific mouth of the Strait of Juan de Fuca. The light on it was built in 1891 for ships coming in from Asia, and it sits on the stretch of coast known as the Pacific Graveyard.[^1] Keepers lived at the station until 2024, when the Coast Guard moved them out and left the light running on its own.[^13]

## The place

The need for a light here was argued for a long time before anyone paid for it. In 1858 Captain George Richards, surveying the coast aboard HMS *Plumper*, recommended a lighthouse on what he called Point Bonilla to mark the northern side of the strait's entrance, as a landfall for ships from China and Japan.[^1] Action came three decades later, when the Canadian Pacific Railway complained in August 1887 that a ship from Yokohama had lost two days waiting for fog to clear before it could find the strait. The Minister of Marine and Fisheries, George Foster, responded at once, and a lighthouse and fog signal were approved.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends adds a story about the name. Bonilla had been Manuel Quimper's name for the present Carmanah Point in 1790, but Admiralty surveyors in 1846 applied it to the lower point two miles east. According to that account, the 1890 building crew landed their materials in thick fog, found themselves on Carmanah Point when it cleared, and built there rather than move everything.[^1]

## Building the light

The contract went to George H. Frost of Nanaimo, and work began in 1890. Winter westerly gales made landing more materials almost impossible, and work stopped until the next year.[^1] The first tower was 14 m (46 ft) high, with the keeper's dwelling attached; both were painted white, with a red lantern. A fog alarm building stood just below and seaward of the tower. The station cost $15,220.89.[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives the first lighting as 15 September 1891, by William Phillip Daykin, with three white flashes a minute, at 53 m (173 ft) above high water and visible for nineteen miles.[^1]

In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 the station's reservoir was enlarged and its tramway partly rebuilt, at a cost of $357.[^10] Equipment changed in the early twentieth century. A fixed seventh-order light was replaced in 1909 by a fifth-order lens burning petroleum vapour under a mantle, and in 1910 a duplicate diaphone fog signal plant and a new fog alarm building followed, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] The Department of Marine's report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1910 lists, for the British Columbia coast, "a 3-inch duplicate diaphone plant with two 12 h.p. engines", though the OCR of that table does not make clear which station each line belongs to.[^2]

A reinforced concrete tower was planned for years. The report for the year ended 31 March 1921 says that "the work of a new concrete tower at Carmanah point" had been postponed,[^3] and the report for the year ended 31 March 1923 records "Completion of concrete tower started last year" and says the new concrete lighthouse at Carmanah was completed.[^4][^5] The dates fit together: Lighthouse Friends says the present tower went up in 1922, and the departmental reports show it postponed in 1920-21 and finished in the year ended March 1923, which began in April 1922. Lighthouse Friends says the tower is octagonal, 11 m (36 ft) high, built by J. Charlesworth of Hodgson, King & Marble under a $30,000 contract, and that the old lantern and a third-order Fresnel lens made in Paris by Barbier, Benard & Turenne were moved into it.[^1] That account does not explain how a third-order lens fits with the fifth-order apparatus it gives for 1909. Canada's heritage record says the tower, unlike many West Coast light towers of the century's first decade, was not built from a standard design.[^6]

## The keepers

William Phillip Daykin, called Phil, arrived on 17 April 1891 with his wife, five sons and his invalid mother, according to Lighthouse Friends, and stayed 21 years.[^1] A heritage trail site names his wife as Helen Strelley Marriott Daykin and says he kept a diary of every ship sighted from the tower.[^7] The place was hard to supply: the same site says it was for decades the most inaccessible lighthouse on the British Columbia coast, with supplies hauled in on rainforest trails from Clo-oose, or landed at Port Renfrew when storms stopped landing at Clo-oose and then brought in by canoe by First Nations people hired for the job.[^7]

The family's losses were severe. Lighthouse Friends says Daykin lost his wife after surgery, his mother in 1906, and two sons: the eldest, Ted, never returned from a hunting trip, and William fell from the tramway car to the rocks while hauling supplies from the tender.[^1] The heritage trail site gives Ted's trip as a prospecting trip near the Nitinat River in 1893, when he was 16, and William's death as 19 September 1894 after the cable of the tramway broke.[^7] The two accounts differ on the purpose of Ted's trip.

Daykin kept in touch with the world by telegraph, which the winter gales often broke. In January 1898, as the Victoria *Daily Colonist* reported (quoted by Lighthouse Friends), the wire had been down 21 days, and he planned to take carrier pigeons home to send news to Victoria.[^1] In 1903 the *Colonist* reported that he had tested night signalling with the Morse code using a 25-candle-power light, with red glass for the dash (as quoted on a *Colonist* extracts blog).[^8]

After asking for a transfer for nearly ten years, Daykin was moved in 1912 to Victoria, according to Lighthouse Friends. George Woodley lasted six weeks, and Daykin's son Robert S. Daykin served until 1917.[^1] James W. Davies followed until 1924, Thomas A. McNabb 1924 to 1930 (and again in 1944), and a series of keepers up to the 1980s, listed in the roster below.[^1] The sources disagree on the later years: Lighthouse Friends has Jerry K. Etzkorn from 1986,[^1] while the Victoria *Times Colonist* in 2024 said that he and his wife spent 29 years at Carmanah Point between 1979 and 2015.[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 11 January 1896 Daykin walked the old telegraph trail after the tug *Tyee* signalled that it had rescued thirteen survivors of the wrecked *Janet Cowan*. Trees blown down by gales lay across the trail. He found nine more of the crew in an abandoned cabin and brought them back to the station, where his family fed and sheltered them until the *Princess Louise* picked them up.[^1]

By 1913 the Department of Marine had cleared a trail along the coast from Banfield Creek to Carmanah lighthouse, which it judged "adequate protection" for shipwrecked mariners.[^11] In the report for the year ended 31 March 1924 the department recorded that patrol men were hired at Carmanah, Pachena and Cape Beale in November to act as assistant lookouts and walk the coast, and that wireless telephones were fitted at the three lightstations.[^12]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the station was electrified in the late 1960s and the old lens was later broken up, with one fragment remaining.[^1] New dwellings were built in 1917, 1948 and 1957.[^1] The Canadian Coast Guard says the light has been automated since 2003.[^13] Lighthouse Friends credits Jerry Etzkorn with helping hikers on the West Coast Trail with water, first aid and warnings.[^1]

On 31 July 2024 the Coast Guard announced it would end keeper functions at Carmanah Point and Pachena Point. A geotechnical assessment had found that many buildings stood on ground of increased vulnerability, the winch building had been condemned, and the helipad had been condemned because of ground settlement.[^13] A Fisheries and Oceans note of December 2024 says the keepers were relocated to other British Columbia lightstations.[^14] First Nations said they had not been consulted, as the *Times Colonist* reported in August 2024.[^9]

## Today

The light is still on, automated. The 2024 release says the two towers and other heritage buildings stay in place under the care of Fisheries and Oceans Canada while long-term options are assessed, which it expects to be complex and possibly cost-prohibitive.[^13] Canada's register of historic places has a record for the light tower, citing a federal heritage buildings report.[^6] Lighthouse Friends describes the station as owned by the Canadian Coast Guard and in the West Coast Trail unit of Pacific Rim National Park Reserve, with grounds open and the tower closed.[^1] That description predates the 2024 withdrawal of keepers, so anyone planning to walk there should check current conditions with Parks Canada. The trail is demanding and only for prepared hikers.
