A dark red cast-iron cylinder stands in the open water of the northern Chesapeake Bay, 1.3 miles southeast of Sparrows Point, Baltimore County, Maryland. It was built in 1873 as the front light of a pair that guided ships into the new channel to Baltimore, and it had a keeper and an assistant until it was automated in 1964.[^1][^2]

## The place

The Craighill Channel was named for William Price Craighill, an Army engineer and member of the Lighthouse Board who supervised the surveys for widening and deepening it. It forms the first leg of the maintained channel from the bay to the Patapsco River and Baltimore Harbor. The growing importance of Baltimore as a port persuaded Congress to appropriate $50,000 in 1870 to widen it to 500 feet (150 m) and deepen it to 22 feet (6.7 m).[^2]

The channel had no lights for night navigation. The Lighthouse Board wrote in 1871, as the National Register nomination quotes it, that the channel saved large vessels about five miles, avoided the ice that built up in the lower Brewerton Channel, and "is much easier navigated, or would be if range beacons were established."[^2] The nomination explains that, given the channel's position, range lights on shore would have needed very powerful lamps and an extremely tall rear tower, so both were built in the water.[^2]

## Building the light

Both towers were first designed as screwpile structures. The severe ice of the winter of 1872-73 changed that: the Board chose a small caisson for the front light and a granite pier for the rear.[^2][^3] Congress had appropriated $45,000 on 10 June 1872 for the two lights. The nomination says that when work began on the front light in the summer of 1873, builders found no firm bottom within 60 feet (18 m) of the surface, and that this led Congress to add $20,000 on 3 March 1873 and a final $45,000 on 23 June 1874. The March 1873 date falls before the summer start it describes, and the sources do not explain the gap.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives the extra sum as $25,000 in one passage and as $20,000 on 3 March 1873 in another, and puts the total for the pair at $110,000.[^3]

The National Register nomination describes the foundation work in detail. Crews dredged away a ten-foot (3 m) layer of mud and drove wooden piles. A steam-driven circular saw, hung on a hollow wrought-iron shaft inside a frame, cut the pile tops 23 feet (7 m) below low water; LighthouseFriends says 24 feet below the surface. Since the sawing was blind, some piles stood higher than others, and a diver spiked hardwood chocks onto the low ones.[^2][^3] A hardwood crib, built at Havre de Grace, Maryland, from four layers of 12-inch timbers, had the first course of iron cylinder plates bolted to it. In a gale it proved hard to manage, so it was taken to a sheltered harbour at Curtis Creek, where two more courses and three feet of concrete were added.[^2]

The first attempt to sink the caisson over the piles, in October 1873, failed, and 50 tons of riprap had to be taken off again. On the second try it was set in position on 31 October 1873.[^2] A temporary fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed on a temporary wooden structure above it on 20 November 1873, and the ironwork of the tower was finished in March 1874. Some 5,000 tons of stone riprap were piled around the base, with 675 tons more in 1875.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the dwelling to October 1874 and says the keepers lived in the makeshift quarters until the lighthouse was completed in early 1875.[^3] The permanent lens was a fixed fifth-order Fresnel lens, made by "Henry Leapute" of Paris as the nomination spells it.[^2]

The tower is a 25-foot (7.6 m) cast-iron cylinder on a 24-foot (7.3 m) caisson, smaller than most caissons, which usually ran 30 to 35 feet across. It stands in 5 to 7 feet (1.5 to 2 m) of water by the nomination's reckoning; an earlier engineering report gave 15 feet.[^2] The station has two lights, the nomination notes: the range light, a red beacon on the gallery, and a second light in the lantern for general navigation.[^2] The date 1873 is set in an iron pediment above the main door.[^2]

Equipment changed over the years. In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 the station received new-model fifth-order lamps.[^4] A gas-operated fog bell was established in 1923.[^2] The light was electrified in November 1929 with duplicate gasoline-driven generators and storage batteries; the Lighthouse Service Bulletin said the generators ran for 3 or 4 hours from sundown to charge the batteries, and during fog.[^2][^7] The fog signal became an air whistle in October 1932.[^2] A description of the station from March 1938, quoted in the nomination, records a Typhone horn as the fog signal, with a standard 1,000-pound bell as a backup worked by a weight-driven clock whose weight had to be rewound every hour and a half. The station then kept an 18-foot (5.5 m) motor boat with a one-cylinder engine and a 16-foot (4.9 m) skiff on davits.[^2]

## The keepers

The Archive's roster of keepers and assistants runs from 1873 to 1964, and the assistants' posts changed hands often, several of them within a single year.[^1] John B. Lewis had the longest single stint as head keeper in that roster, from 1877 to 1886. Jorgen B. Johansen was head keeper in three stints between 1916 and 1929.[^1] The Chesapeake Chapter of the U.S. Lighthouse Society's timeline says Johansen was awarded the inspector's efficiency star in 1918.[^8]

Inside, the dwelling is small. On the first level, the nomination says, the original 1874 plan shows the 12- by 21-foot (3.7 by 6.4 m) room on the south side as two bedrooms; a spiral wooden stair leads up to a watch room only 14 feet (4.3 m) across, below the lantern. The nomination quotes a keeper of 1955: "It's so small, every time you sneeze, you have to swab the place."[^2] The privy hung from brackets over the side of the gallery.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 22 March 1911, Keeper Henry Wingate and Assistant James L. Lewis launched a boat in a severe gale and brought off two men from a capsized vessel. LighthouseFriends reports that they revived one man and that the other, about 18 years old, died some 40 minutes after reaching the lighthouse. That account is drawn from the inspector's report recommending that both keepers be commended.[^3]

On 19 January 1916, two barges in tow of a tug struck the station. The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported damage of about $230, to be paid by the tug's owners.[^5] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1921, Johansen and Assistant E. C. Tyler towed a disabled motor boat, with its occupants, to the station, and gave them a sail for the trip to port.[^6] LighthouseFriends and the Chesapeake Chapter both say that on 13 September 1926 Johansen pulled four people from the water near the station.[^3][^8]

Winter ice never damaged the station, according to the nomination, though it was abandoned and the light put out on 11 February 1936 because of dangerous ice. It was relit on 24 February.[^2]

## After the keepers

The station was automated on 5 May 1964, and LighthouseFriends calls it one of the last on Chesapeake Bay to lose its Coast Guard crew.[^2][^3] In 1989 the exterior was sandblasted and recoated, at a cost of $27,612.88.[^2]

The Coast Guard owned the station when it was nominated to the National Register of Historic Places (reference 02001420, 2002).[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that Historical Place Preservation, Inc. received title in November 2005 but did not carry out its plans. The government took the lighthouse back and sold it at a General Services Administration auction in 2017 for a high bid of $96,000. The buyers sold it again at auction in December 2023 for $121,900, and in 2024 Richard Cucé of The Lighthouse Centers was announced as owner.[^3]

## Today

The tower is a working aid to navigation, powered by a solar panel on the lantern roof.[^2] The Archive lists it as not open to the public, and it is reached only by boat; it is private property in open water, and the nomination described its interior as in poor condition.[^1][^2]
