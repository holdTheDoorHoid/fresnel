Fisgard Lighthouse is a white tower, built of brick, rising out of the keeper's house on a small rock at the mouth of Esquimalt Harbour, near Victoria in British Columbia. Lit in November 1860, it is the oldest lighthouse on Canada's west coast, and its first keeper was brought out from England with the lantern.[^1][^2]

## The place

The island takes its name from HMS *Fisgard*, a Royal Navy frigate that was on the Pacific station in the 1840s;[^3][^5] the national historic site plaque says Captain G. H. Richards, R.N., recommended the island as the site for the light.[^3] The light was meant to guide ships into Esquimalt Harbour after dark.[^4] The register's statement of significance ties the tower to the years after the Fraser River gold rush of 1858, when traffic grew sharply in the narrow, rocky approaches to the harbour, and to the Royal Navy ships that used Esquimalt.[^2] Parks Canada adds that the light was also an expression of British sovereignty at a moment when American miners were arriving in numbers.[^1]

## Building the light

The tower and dwelling went up in 1859 and 1860 under the British and colonial governments, according to the plaque and the register; LighthouseFriends says construction began in 1860.[^3][^2][^5] Sources also differ on who designed it. LighthouseFriends credits the German-born engineer and architect Herman Otto Tiedeman, with John Wright as the local contractor;[^5] the federal heritage character statement says the design "has been attributed to Joseph Pemberton and John Wright."[^6] Parks Canada puts the cost at £7,000 (about $12,000) and notes that government departments argued over who should pay.[^1]

The tower is round and tapers upward, built of brick on a granite base and capped by a granite slab that carries the lantern. A two-storey brick dwelling is built onto it.[^2][^7] The register notes pointed Gothic windows and brick corbelling below the lantern platform, and a cast iron stair with an open geometric pattern.[^7] Parks Canada says the stair was made in sections in San Francisco.[^1] LighthouseFriends records that Victoria's *Colonist* thought the staircase would "amply repay a visit"; that is a quotation passed on by the website, not read in the paper.[^5]

The lens was a fourth-order Fresnel lens, according to LighthouseFriends, and the station first showed its light on 16 November 1860, a Friday.[^5][^3] Sources do not agree on how tall the tower is: LighthouseFriends gives 14.5 m (48 ft), while the register's description calls it a 17 m (56 ft) tower.[^5][^2]

## The keepers

Parks Canada counts twelve keepers between 1860 and 1928, one of them a woman.[^1] The agreement to build the light required that the first keeper come from England with experience of the work, so that he could train others.[^1] That was George Davies. LighthouseFriends says he signed a one-year contract on Christmas Eve 1859 at £150 a year, and sailed with his wife, Rosina, and children in January 1860 on a voyage of seven months.[^5] Parks Canada says instead that he arrived in August 1860 with his wife, two children and an assistant, and was hired at $650 a year, with house, coal and water supplied.[^1] On the night the light was first lit, LighthouseFriends reports, Davies and his wife kept the watches between them in the log, and he noted the light burned for sixteen hours in fair weather.[^5] In February 1861 Davies moved to the new light at Race Rocks. LighthouseFriends reports that he died there about five years later.[^5]

William Bevis, the third keeper, stayed about eighteen years. Parks Canada says he wrote many letters to the government as the house grew colder and damper: windows and doors no longer fitted, and in storms keeping the weather out became as much work as tending the light.[^1] He died on station in 1879. Parks Canada says his wife and a 16-year-old niece then ran the light for nine months until a replacement came; LighthouseFriends gives a shorter gap, ending when Henry Cogan arrived on 6 January 1880, and adds that the marine agent's proposal to appoint the two women was refused as "against the rules of the Department."[^1][^5] Parks Canada's list names Amelia Bevis as keeper in 1879 to 1880.[^1]

Cogan stayed until 1884 and Joseph Dare followed.[^1][^5] LighthouseFriends tells of Dare's 1898 accident: he was shooting at mink along the shore, slipped, and a load of shot from his own rifle struck his eye. He went back to duty half blind after four months in hospital without pay, and a month later he drowned while rowing home to Fisgard.[^5] Parks Canada's list records only that he drowned in Esquimalt Harbour in 1898.[^1] Parks Canada's list then shows two brief keepers in 1898, W. Cormack and John Davies, followed by Douglas MacKenzie (1898 to 1900) and Andrew Deacon (1900 to 1901).[^1]

Captain George Johnston (spelled Johnson in some lists) held the post from 1901 to 1909. The Marine Department's 1902 list of lightkeepers has him at Fisgard, appointed 30 July 1901.[^1][^8] Josiah Gosse came next: the Department of Marine's 1910 table of lightkeepers lists him at Fisgard, appointed 13 October 1909 at $360 a year.[^9] He stayed nineteen years, and Parks Canada says that by then the keeper could leave the site by day to work elsewhere and needed a second job to make ends meet.[^1] The department's 1910 report records new equipment at the light: "Fisgard. — A 35 mm. Diamond vapour installation replaces the duplex lamp."[^10]

## After the keepers

Parks Canada says Gosse lit the lamp for the last time in 1928, the year Fisgard was automated, and stayed a further month in case of trouble.[^1] The plaque agrees on 1928, but an archaeological bulletin refers to the keeper being dismissed in 1929.[^3][^4] The brick storehouse on the island was levelled in 1952 during construction of a causeway.[^4] Parks Canada puts the causeway at 1951 and says that before it there was little contact between the fort and the lighthouse.[^1]

In 1957 a fire gutted the lighthouse interior. Its cause is unknown and vandalism is suspected. The brick walls acted as a chimney, and only the chequered floor and the iron staircase survived.[^1] The Canadian register says the light was in continuous operation until that fire.[^2] National historic site designation followed: LighthouseFriends says it came in 1960, the centennial year; another summary says 1958.[^5][^11] Parks Canada restored the tower to a Victorian appearance in the 1970s and 1980s and rebuilt the storehouse and boathouse.[^5] The register lists a replica boathouse (1978) and a replica storehouse (1960s).[^2] The heritage character statement dates the restoration of the dwelling's exterior to its appearance about 1862 to the early 1960s.[^6]

## Today

The automated light is still an aid to navigation. The Canadian Coast Guard List of Lights carries it as the Fisgard Sector Light, on the west side of the harbour entrance.[^12] Parks Canada says the original lens remains in the tower.[^1] The site is run by Parks Canada as part of Fort Rodd Hill and Fisgard Lighthouse National Historic Sites. The lighthouse is open on weekends from 16 October to 30 April and daily from 1 May to 15 October, with the grounds open every day; check the current notices, because transport and parking works were scheduled until 1 October 2026.[^13] Inside, exhibits tell the keepers' stories, and a few boards from the chequered floor saved from the 1957 fire were put back in the 2010 exhibit.[^1]
