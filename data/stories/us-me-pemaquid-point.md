Pemaquid Point Light stands on a high promontory at the entrance to Muscongus Bay, in Bristol, Lincoln County, Maine. A white light has shown from the point since 1827. The tower visitors climb today dates from 1835, according to the US Lighthouse Society, the American Lighthouse Foundation and LighthouseFriends, but the 1985 National Register nomination says the light was rebuilt in 1857.[^1][^2][^3][^10] The keepers left in 1934, when the light was automated, and the keeper's house beside the tower has been a museum since 1972.[^3][^10]

## The place

The point is ledge-bound. The 1985 National Register nomination says the ledges run "southwesterly for a third of a mile into the ocean," and calls the light "an extremely important navigational mark" between the entrance to Johns Bay and Muscongus Bay. It also says the point "has been the scene of a number of tragic shipwrecks" because of the long ledge that runs out from its base.[^1]

The nomination also mentions an episode from 1813. On what is now the site of the light, it says, hundreds of people watched a single-ship action on September 5, 1813, between the British brig *Boxer* and the United States brig *Enterprise*. The action took place a short distance from shore, and the *Boxer* was defeated.[^1]

Nearer the light's own history, a lighthouse inspector, I. W. P. Lewis, visited in 1842. LighthouseFriends, which quotes his report, says he found the tower in good condition but doubted that the light was needed. Because it stood close to Franklin Island light, he wrote, it "has often been the cause of sad mistakes and disasters of a fatal character." He recommended cutting it to a single lamp rather than abolishing it.[^3]

## Building the light

An act dated May 18, 1826 provided $4,000 for a rubblestone tower and a keeper's house.[^3] The station's plot was bought for $90 from Samuel and Sarah Martin, descendants of survivors of the *Angel Gabriel*. LighthouseFriends describes the *Angel Gabriel* as a British passenger galleon that went down in an August 1635 hurricane, and calls it the area's most famous shipwreck.[^3] Jeremiah Berry of Thomaston built the structures, which cost $3,503. The light went into service on November 29, 1827.[^3]

The first tower did not last. The American Lighthouse Foundation says its poor construction made a new tower necessary, completed in 1835.[^10] LighthouseFriends says the replacement was a conical granite tower, contracted to Joseph Berry, and that Keeper Isaac Dunham certified the work with the words "a Better Tower and Lantern never was Built in this State."[^3]

The sources disagree on the later dates. The 1985 nomination says the light was "built originally in 1827 and rebuilt in 1857," and describes a square tower 34 feet (10.4 m) high.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives a conical tower established in 1835, 38 feet (11.6 m) from its base to the lantern, with a fourth-order Fresnel lens installed in 1856.[^2] The American Lighthouse Foundation also gives 1856 for the lens.[^10] LighthouseFriends puts the lens in 1857.[^3] The sources do not settle these dates.

The fog signal was reworked in the later 1890s. A Lighthouse Board report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1897, says "an engine house was built of brick and two engines were purchased" at the station.[^6] The report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1899, says the Shipman fog-signal engines "were replaced by a Stevens striking machine."[^7]

The boundary of the light-house land was still unsettled in 1891. That year's report says the location of the boundary line, which "was referred to the United States attorney for settlement on March 8, 1889," "is not yet adjusted."[^4] The next year's report records that "a boundary board fence was built."[^5]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists fourteen head keepers from 1827 to 1934, plus two first assistants in 1898 and 1899.[^2] A few of their stories can be told from the sources.

Isaac Dunham, the first keeper, was forty and from Bath when he took the post in 1827.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends says he had served as a privateer in the War of 1812, then farmed before becoming a keeper at $350 a year. He also patented a method of stopping lamp oil from thickening in winter. LighthouseFriends reports that Congress ordered the Treasury to adopt it in 1837, but that it is not known how widely it was used.[^3] His father, Captain Cornelius Dunham, died at the station in July 1835 and was buried in a small cemetery near the light.[^3]

When Nathaniel Gammage took over in 1837, Dunham had built outbuildings at the station. Dunham asked for and received $1,100 from Gammage for them.[^3] Four years later a political appointment brought Jeremiah Mears in to replace Gammage. Gammage asked to be paid for the outbuildings, but received only a little money from Mears.[^3]

Marcus A. Hanna served four years, from 1869 to 1873.[^2][^3] On March 3, 1873, the keeper at Two Lights in Cape Elizabeth, James Mariner, wrote in that station's logbook that Hanna was coming to take his place "but not with my consent," as LighthouseFriends quotes the entry.[^3] Hanna had been moved, the same account says, after using his influence as a Republican and Civil War veteran to persuade President Grant to transfer him.[^3]

Clarence E. Marr, who had been an assistant keeper at Cuckolds Fog Signal Station, became head keeper here in 1899. He retired in 1922 after twenty-three years. LighthouseFriends quotes a Kennebec Journal account of his retirement, in which summer visitors presented him and his wife with a purse of money and a souvenir book of signatures.[^3]

Herbert E. Robinson succeeded Marr in 1922 and was keeper when the rescue described below took place.[^2] LeRoy S. Elwell was the last keeper, from 1928 until the light was automated in 1934.[^2][^3]

The sources name no woman who kept the light in her own right. Sophronia Lawler, wife of Keeper Joseph Lawler, gave birth to a baby girl at the light in 1868, according to LighthouseFriends, but she is not on the keepers' roster.[^2][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources record few storms by name for this station. The Life-Saving Service's 1888 report lists "Pemaquid Point, (rock 4 miles southwest of)" in a table of places on the coasts of the United States where vessels had stranded during the previous ten years.[^11] That is a list entry, not an account of a particular wreck at the light.

On November 5, 1927, a boy was washed off the rocks near the light into a heavy sea and drowned. The Lighthouse Service's bulletin says keeper Herbert Robinson rescued the boy's aunt and uncle, who had also been washed off the rocks, "this act requiring unusual heroism."[^8]

On August 6, 1930, keeper LeRoy Elwell rescued three people from a capsized sailboat under extremely hazardous conditions. The Secretary of Commerce commended him for it.[^9]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1934, and the US Lighthouse Society and the American Lighthouse Foundation both give that year.[^2][^10] Sources disagree on when the fog bell came down. LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard removed it in 1937; the foundation says it was gone by 1930.[^3][^10]

The 1985 nomination says the keeper's house and surrounding land "was given to the State" in 1934, and describes the grounds as a state park.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that in March 1940 Bristol voters approved buying the property except the tower, which stayed with the Coast Guard, and paid $1,639 over four years.[^3] The two accounts do not fit together, and the sources do not explain the difference. The Pemaquid Group of Artists added an art gallery to the park in 1960, and the keeper's house opened as The Fishermen's Museum in 1972.[^3]

Hurricane Bob destroyed the bell house and the white weight tower in 1991, and the structures were rebuilt the following year.[^3] In May 2000 the Coast Guard licensed the tower to the American Lighthouse Foundation.[^3][^10] Storms in January 2024 knocked in the bell house's eastern, ocean-facing wall, and repairs were completed in the summer of 2024.[^3]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the tower and licenses it to the American Lighthouse Foundation.[^10] The foundation's chapter, the Friends of Pemaquid Point Lighthouse, opens the tower to climbers from Memorial Day to Columbus Day.[^10] The Town of Bristol owns the keeper's house, which holds the Fishermen's Museum, and the town handles wedding bookings.[^3][^10] LighthouseFriends says the tower and the museum are typically open daily from mid-May through Columbus Day, with tower hours of 10 a.m. to 5 p.m. and museum hours of 9 a.m. to 5 p.m.[^3] Those hours come from LighthouseFriends and may have changed, so check the foundation's page before a visit.[^10] The keeper's house also has a one-bedroom apartment for weekly rental through Newcastle Vacation Rentals.[^3] Boat tours out of Boothbay Harbor pass the light.[^3]
