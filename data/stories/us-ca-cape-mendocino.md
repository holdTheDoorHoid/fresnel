Cape Mendocino Light was a first-order light on the headland at the westernmost point of California.[^1] It was first shown on 1 December 1868.[^1][^2] In 1998 the lantern room was lifted off by helicopter, and the rest of the tower was dismantled and carried about 35 miles (56 km) south to Shelter Cove, where it is now a museum.[^1][^12]

## The place

The headland is mountainous. Several rocks lie in the shallow water along this stretch of coast, and LighthouseFriends says they hint that hidden ledges may lie just below the surface.[^1] Sugar Loaf, a 326-foot (99 m) sea stack, stands off the headland, and Blunts Reef lies about three miles out.[^1]

After the original Point Loma light was discontinued in 1891, LighthouseFriends says, Cape Mendocino became the highest lighthouse in the country.[^1] The sources do not agree on its height. The Light-House Board's 1868 notice gives the light as 380 feet (116 m) above mean sea level.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives 422 feet (129 m) above the sea,[^1] and the U.S. Lighthouse Society's record gives 422 feet above mean high water.[^11]

The Light-House Board's report in the volume covering the fiscal years 1891 and 1892 described the land approach as a problem: "The road leading from the county road to this station is in bad condition."[^3]

## Building the light

Building the station began with a loss. In 1867 the lighthouse tender Shubrick struck a rock in fog about thirty miles south of the cape, and all the building materials she carried were lost.[^1] Later one man drowned while bricks were being landed.[^1]

The lantern room, the first-order lens and the material for the iron tower were shipped to Eureka in the summer of 1868. Because a landing through the surf was too dangerous, they went the last forty miles by wagon.[^1]

The Light-House Board's notice of the new light appeared in the *New York Herald* on 6 December 1868. It said that "from and after the 1st day of December" the light would be shown "on the lighthouse tower recently erected on the western slope of Cape Mendocino, Cal." The apparatus was of the first order of Fresnel, "showing a white revolving light; duration of flash, five seconds."[^2]

The first brick duplex for the keepers was rendered uninhabitable by an earthquake in March 1871, before it was two years old. The keepers lived in a shanty until a wooden duplex was finished that November.[^1]

## The keepers

Alfred May was the first head keeper.[^11] LighthouseFriends gives his salary as $1,000 a year and reports that $625 was paid to his wife and two men as assistants.[^1]

Women held assistant posts from the first year. The roster lists "Mrs. G. A. May" as first assistant in 1869, Adeline Corbett as first assistant from 1869 to 1871, Amelia Chism as second assistant from 1871 to 1874, and Mary Marble as second assistant in 1874 and 1875.[^11]

Seth P. Chism was head keeper from 1871 to 1874.[^11] In 1870 Haus Buhne sued him, claiming that he owned the land the light stood on, and seeking the keepers' removal. The first trial ended in a nonsuit, and Buhne appealed. The California Supreme Court decided for the government on 10 October 1872.[^1]

Some keepers stayed for decades. Peter Jensen was head keeper from 1897 to 1921, and Morton M. Palmer from 1924 to 1943.[^11] Paschal Hunter, a first assistant from 1903 to 1911, raised ponies on the station for the stage that ran past the light between Ferndale and Petrolia. He supplied horses to the stage until a gasoline vehicle took over that run.[^1][^11] Roy M. Crockett, from 1943 to 1951, was the last head keeper.[^11]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 24 December 1892 Keeper William C. Price logged a storm that blew much of the roof off the keepers' dwelling and blew the chimneys down. LighthouseFriends quotes the entry, and says the keepers and their families took shelter in the lighthouse and then moved into the barn.[^1][^11]

The Board's report for the fiscal year that ended 30 June 1893, which covers July 1892 to June 1893, says the chimneys on the south side were torn down and rebuilt "to the proper height." It gives no date for that damage.[^4]

The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1895 called the keeper's double dwelling "built twenty-three years ago," and "in a dilapidated condition." It recorded that a contract had been made for its repair.[^5]

In 1881 breakers capsized a boat that was rowing the inspector, Charles J. McDougal, ashore from the tender Manzanita. Three of the men aboard drowned, LighthouseFriends says, including McDougal.[^1]

In 1905 a lightship was stationed off the cape on Blunts Reef. LighthouseFriends says it was placed there to make that hazard easier to see.[^1] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1915 still lists Blunts Reef Light Vessel No. 83 on the coast.[^10] In December 1905 the *San Francisco Call* reported that the lightship had been blown from her station a few days earlier, "when the shank of her mushroom anchor parted." She had since sailed back to her station on the Mendocino coast with a new anchor.[^9]

In July 1905 the *Call* reported that the Norwegian collier *Tricolor* had been lost on the rocks. Captain H. Wold, at Eureka, blamed the lightship for the loss, because he had been listening for its whistle when the collier struck.[^8] The lightship's crew told him their whistle had not started until 4:30 a.m., more than an hour after the *Tricolor* went ashore. The same report says Wold later accepted the blame himself.[^8]

LighthouseFriends says the 1906 earthquake destroyed the wooden oil house.[^1] It also describes the passenger steamer *Bear* running aground nearby in 1916. Five people drowned in the surf, and 150 survivors reached the lightship before they could be taken ashore.[^1] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1916 records the wreck of the *Bear* near the light on 15 June 1916.[^13]

On 22 January 1923 severe shocks struck several light stations in California.[^6] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that at Cape Mendocino "there was a very severe shock at 1.10 a. m." The assistant keeper's quarters were damaged "to some extent," with "the foundation being cracked quite badly," and the walls of the oil house were also cracked.[^6] A second shock on 27 January opened the cracks in the oil house, and part of its south wall fell.[^7]

In October 1926 Keeper Morton M. Palmer saw the passing steam schooner *Everett* on fire and called for help on the station telephone. When a rescue vessel reached her, her crew had been overcome by the fumes. Palmer was credited with saving the lives of those aboard.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took control of the station in 1939.[^1] In 1948 the Fresnel lens was installed in a replica tower on the Humboldt County Fairgrounds in Ferndale.[^1] In 1951 the light was moved from the tower's lantern room to a nearby pole, and Roy M. Crockett, the last head keeper, was transferred to Mile Rocks.[^1]

In July 1960 the two wooden keepers' dwellings and the other wooden buildings were put up for sale to the highest bidder, on condition that they be removed. When no buyer came forward, the wooden structures were burned in January 1962 and the remains pushed over the cliff.[^1] The Blunts Reef lightship was withdrawn in 1971 and replaced by a large navigation buoy.[^1]

In November 1998 an Army National Guard helicopter lifted the lantern room off the tower and carried it south to Shelter Cove. The rest of the tower was numbered, dismantled and trucked to a yard for renovation, then reassembled at Point Delgada in 1999.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the dedication to September 2000 and the public opening to Memorial Day 2001. The society's website says it opened to the public in May 2000.[^1][^12]

The lens was removed from the fairgrounds replica in September 2012 and placed in storage.[^1] The modern light on the cape was discontinued on 29 May 2013 and removed, ending roughly 145 years of a light there.[^1]

## Today

The museum stands at Shelter Cove, in the park the society calls Mal Coombs Park. LighthouseFriends spells the name "Mel Coombs Park."[^12][^1] The society says the lighthouse is operated as an open museum in most months, and that volunteer docents open it to visitors in the summer.[^12] LighthouseFriends gives the public hours as seven days a week, from 11 a.m. to 3 p.m., from Memorial Day to Labor Day, subject to docent availability.[^1]

The sources disagree about ownership. The society's website says the building belongs to Humboldt County and stands on Bureau of Land Management land. LighthouseFriends names the society as the owner.[^12][^1]

At the old site on the cape, LighthouseFriends reports that the wooden buildings were burned and pushed over the cliff in 1962. It does not say what else remains there.[^1] The first-order lens was still in storage at the Ferndale fairgrounds in 2018, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1]
