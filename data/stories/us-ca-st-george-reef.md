St. George Reef Light stands on Northwest Seal Rock, the outermost rock of a reef that reaches west from Point St. George, off Crescent City in Del Norte County, California. Its square granite tower, raised in broken seasons between 1883 and 1891, rises 146 feet (44 m) above mean high water, standing on a stone pier that is itself 70 feet (21 m) high.[^1] The Light-House Board counted the first-order light as established on 20 October 1892.[^2]

## The place

The reef is a cluster of barren rocky islets and sunken rocks, about six and a half nautical miles long and one to one and a half wide, projecting west from Point St. George, which lies midway between Cape Mendocino and Cape Blanco.[^1] Close under the point, inside the reef, a deep, wide channel runs; the Light-House Board's engineer wrote in 1892 that northbound steamers used it, but only in daylight and clear weather.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says Captain George Vancouver named the outcrops Dragon Rocks in 1792.[^4]

The wreck that made the case for a light was the *Brother Jonathan*, lost on the reef in 1865. The National Register nomination counts 166 lives lost; Lighthouse Friends says 244 people were aboard the steam side-wheeler and only nineteen got away in a small craft. The two accounts do not agree on the toll.[^1][^4]

The government moved slowly. The Light-House Board drafted a request in May 1866, and on 8 June 1866 President Andrew Johnson reserved land for a lighthouse on Point St. George; on 9 January 1867 he reserved the rocky islets off the point as well.[^1] In 1875 the Board suggested that, though it would cost more, a light on one of the outlying rocks might serve better, and the Coast Survey replied that the rock's exposure to the full force of the sea would make a foundation there very difficult.[^1] George R. Putnam, writing as Commissioner of Lighthouses in 1917, gave the reason the light went out to the rock: because of the off-lying reefs, vessels had to keep ten miles off the coast in thick weather, so even a powerful light on the point would often not be seen in the haze, and a fog signal at that distance would have been practically useless.[^5] In 1882 Northwest Seal Rock was chosen as the site, and $50,000 was appropriated to begin.[^1]

## Building the light

On 3 April 1883 a working party left San Francisco in the 137-ton schooner *La Ninfa*, chartered as the workmen's quarters at the rock. The party of twenty-five was made up of crew, quarrymen, stonecutters and a blacksmith, and the wrecking steamer *Whitelaw* towed the schooner out.[^3] The *Whitelaw* laid a 12,000-pound mooring and made the schooner fast, but when she came back from Humboldt Bay on 28 April she found the schooner gone and no trace of the big mooring. *La Ninfa* had parted her hawser in a gale; the *Whitelaw* found her on 6 May.[^3] The first landing on the rock was made on 9 May.[^3] With no shelter on the rock, the men crossed from schooner to rock by an overhead line carrying a round platform that hung from an iron ring; it took four to six men at a time, and they jumped to the rock, a trip of about three minutes.[^1] By the end of August that first season the benches around the outline of the pier had been roughly formed by blasting, and space had been blasted out for a storage capacity of 77,000 gallons of fresh water.[^3]

Then the money ran short. The appropriation act of July 7, 1884 contained only $30,000 for the rock, and work there was suspended.[^3] The engineer, Major W. H. Heuer, reckoned in his report that in the first four years only one working season of about a hundred working days had been used on the rock.[^3] Captain A. H. Payson, who had been in charge, observed after he left in 1886 that the work had been needlessly prolonged by the unpredictability of Congress's appropriations.[^1]

In 1887 A. Ballantyne, who had supervised the building of Tillamook Rock Light off Oregon, became superintendent, and in March Congress approved $120,000.[^1] Heuer described the summer as a succession of heavy gales.[^6] The next year the workers got quarters on the rock itself, built on a wharf with room underneath for fifty men, so they no longer had to be carried to and from a vessel.[^6][^1] The sea still reached them. In 1889, Heuer wrote, the men's quarters "were smashed in during a gale about 2 o'clock one morning in May"; no one was injured, though some men were washed out of their bunks.[^6] Spray often washed the mortar off the stone beds before the stones could be set, the nomination adds.[^1]

In 1891 the tower itself rose. The first stone of the tower was set on 13 May and the last on 23 August.[^6] One serious accident happened during the tower's construction: a rigger, letting go a tag line of the big derrick boom, was carried over the pier and killed.[^6] Between 23 August and 29 October the scaffolding came down and the finishing work was done, and all of it was complete by the end of October, but the heavy sea kept the men from leaving the rock until November, and the station was left in the charge of three keepers while the lens was still on its way from Europe.[^6]

The stone came from granite boulders found on the Mad River near Humboldt Bay, nearly a hundred miles from the site, and was dressed at a yard at the bay's entrance.[^7][^5] Each stone was cut to a gauge to fit its place and landed in a rope net, which kept it from being chipped; the face of the tower is made of 1,339 dressed stones.[^7] The pier is an oval 86 feet (26 m) long; the tower above it is square, with a spiral stair in a round projecting turret, and has seven floors. The pier holds the cisterns, engine room, boiler and laundry; the galley is on the third floor, the living quarters on the fourth and fifth, and the lantern room on the seventh.[^1]

The nomination gives the total cost as $704,633.78; Putnam gives $702,000, and says the long time and the cost were due in considerable measure to appropriations being made in amounts too small, at irregular intervals.[^1][^5] A 12-inch steam whistle, in duplicate, began sounding on 1 December 1891.[^8] The nomination says the lens, a first-order Fresnel, was installed in August 1892 and that the light flashed alternately red and white at 12.5-second intervals, visible for 18 miles; Lighthouse Friends says fifteen seconds. The Light-House Board listed the first-order light as established on 20 October 1892, and Putnam gives the same day.[^1][^4][^2][^5] An oil vapor lamp replaced the wick lamp in 1911, compressed-air sirens replaced the steam whistle in 1913, and an air-powered diaphone horn replaced the sirens in 1936.[^1]

## The keepers

The station was authorized five keepers. Three served on the rock at a time and two were ashore; their families lived in Crescent City, about eight miles to the southeast. The nomination says the rota of three months on the rock and two months off was hard to keep because sea conditions often made it impossible to leave at the end of a tour, and that many keepers resigned after one or two months.[^1] A memorandum of 17 April 1918 from the inspector's office in San Francisco argued for the full five. It said the launch trip from the landing to the rock took two men, that the launch was often held on shore for weeks, and that three keepers had to be on the rock to keep a double watch in fog; vacancies and leave, it said, meant there were seldom more than three there.[^1] Lighthouse Friends counts it among the postings in the service that keepers least wanted.[^4] For comparison, Putnam wrote in 1917 that at isolated stations where families could not live, keepers were allowed seventy-two days of leave a year; that is general context for the Lighthouse Service of the time, not a record of this station.[^9]

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists John Olsen as principal keeper from 1891 to 1913.[^10] Lighthouse Friends says Olsen and the assistant John E. Lind had both been part of the construction crew, and that Lind later kept the Battery Point light at Crescent City.[^4] The archive lists Lind as an assistant at the reef from 1892 to 1901.[^10]

A newspaper piece printed in January 1893, in the *Hawaiian Gazette* with no byline, gives an outsider's picture of the new light. It says the men had "six months' supply of provisions always on hand," that no families were allowed at the lighthouse, and that "They are, in fact, four Selkirks for the time being."[^11] It is a weak record of the station: it dates the *Brother Jonathan* wreck to 1863 and counts four men, where the nomination has five keepers with families ashore.[^11][^1]

Georges Roux was at the reef longer than anyone else the sources name. The archive's page for him shows him as third assistant in 1910, second assistant 1911-1913, first assistant 1913-1917, then a year at Point Hueneme, and principal keeper from 1918 until 1939.[^12] The Del Norte Triplicate's notice of his death in December 1939 calls him Captain Georges Roux, aged 62, a native of France with no relatives in the area.[^12] In 1938 the station's original launch landing was given up, because the harbor shoaled after a breakwater was built at Crescent City in the 1930s, and a wharf in the harbor was leased as the landing.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says that in 1937 a long stretch of storms trapped Roux and his assistants in the tower for fifty-nine days, and gives his later recollection that after the first four weeks they had run out of things to say, and that near the end they ate cold food from cans facing away from one another to avoid quarrels.[^4]

Lighthouse Friends also tells a story from Easter 1909: a pig brought to the reef for the holiday fell into the sea, and the assistant John Otto Becker jumped in after it and held it up until a boat could be lowered; the pig lived in the basement until it became Easter dinner. The archive's roster lists John O. Becker as third assistant in 1908-1909. The story comes from Lighthouse Friends alone, and no earlier account of it has been checked.[^4][^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Rain falling on the top of the pier ran through a pipe to the cisterns in the base, and Lighthouse Friends says that rain was the water for both the keepers and the steam whistle.[^1][^4] In September 1892, "owing to scarcity of water," the fog signal was run for 20 minutes with 20 minutes' silence, and it was back in full operation on 16 October.[^13] In the year to June 1893 the whistles ran some 405 hours and burned about 27 tons of coal.[^14] The water ran short again from 1 September to 1 October 1893, when the signal sounded for 5 minutes with 10 minutes' silence.[^15] It was cut back again on 1 September 1894 and further on 10 November, and the Board lists the characteristic as re-established on 26 November.[^16] On 12 November the San Francisco *Morning Call* printed the lighthouse inspector's notice that the signal would run regularly in thick and foggy weather, the water on hand being enough.[^17] The Board nonetheless changed the signal on 1 March 1895 to blasts of 5 seconds with silent intervals of 75 seconds, "on account of the scarcity of water," to save steam.[^18] In the year to June 1895 the whistles ran some 452 hours and used about 35 tons of coal.[^19]

On 17 October 1893, just under a year after the light was first shown, the first assistant keeper, William Erickson, was lost with the station's boat. The Board's report for the year ended June 1894 says "No vestige of man or boat has been discovered."[^20] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's memorial list records that he "Disappeared in station's sailboat."[^21] The nomination says he was trying to land on the rock.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates the loss to October 1895 and says it happened on a trip to Crescent City, but the Board's report fixes the year as 1893.[^4][^20] A new standard 18-foot boat was supplied in January 1894, and davits were put up on a wharf at Crescent City so the keepers could secure the boat while they were ashore.[^20] In 1899 a derrick was erected on a wharf at Crescent City to lift the launch out of the water while the keepers were ashore.[^22]

Lighthouse Friends reports that a storm destroyed a framed blacksmith shop on the pier in 1896.[^4] After what the nomination calls a serious accident in 1901, the boom of the hoisting derrick was replaced by a 90-foot (27 m) boom, long enough to reach beyond a bad eddy and swell that had made landing dangerous; the nomination does not say what the accident was.[^1]

On 7 December 1923 huge seas from the west-northwest broke on the platform, 70 feet above the water, and tore the donkey engine house from its foundations and moved it against the coping of the dock. Steam pipes, water pipes and the hoisting derrick's control levers were broken or badly damaged. The *Lighthouse Service Bulletin* said no damage of this kind had been reported from the station before. The keeper was ashore at the time and could not get back for nearly two weeks, but he spoke every day with the other keepers by the newly installed radiotelephone, gave them directions for temporary repairs and learned when the launch could safely approach the station.[^23] The nomination calls it the worst storm recorded in the station's history.[^1]

The radiotelephone between the reef and Crescent City had gone into commission on 25 October 1923. The *Bulletin* said it was needed because a heavily armored submarine cable between shore and station was impracticable, on account of the heavy seas and the sharp rocky bottom at and near Seal Rock; the last cable laid cost approximately $50,000 in place and was first broken within four months.[^24]

The worst day in the station's history came after the keepers' time. On 5 April 1951, Lighthouse Friends says, five Coast Guardsmen were being lowered to the water in the station's boat when a wave struck it, a cable ring tore loose and all five were thrown into the sea. Three of the five died. Fred Permenter, the officer in charge, who had held the post for less than a month, inflated a raft, threw it out, jumped some twenty feet into the water after it and pulled two men from the sea; he was awarded a Gold Lifesaving Medal.[^4] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's memorial list names Stanley G. Castello among those who drowned that day and also lists a Coast Guard drowning at the reef on 30 January 1965, Donald C. Hale.[^21] Lighthouse Friends adds that a storm in 1952 threw rocks through the lantern-room glass.[^4]

## After the keepers

When the Lighthouse Bureau became part of the Coast Guard in 1939, enlisted personnel took over the lighthouses from the keepers.[^1] The nomination records that the tower's interiors were remodeled in 1955 and much of the original equipment removed.[^1] 

On 13 May 1975 the station was disestablished as a manned unit and stopped serving as an aid to navigation.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says a large navigational buoy was placed near the lighthouse that year. It quotes James W. Sebastian, whom the archive lists as principal keeper until 1975, writing in the station logbook that day: "St. George Reef Light is dark."[^4][^10] The nomination says the first-order lens was taken to the Del Norte County Historical Society Museum in 1975; Lighthouse Friends says it was taken down piece by piece eight years later and reassembled at the museum.[^1][^4]

The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 9 December 1993 as part of the Light Stations of California group. The nomination's argument rests on its being one of two wave-swept towers built on the Pacific Coast, and the listing record set its period of significance at 1883-1940.[^1]

PBS's *Legendary Lighthouses* says Guy Towers formed the St. George Reef Lighthouse Preservation Society in 1986; Lighthouse Friends gives 1988.[^25][^4] Del Norte County, which Lighthouse Friends says obtained the lighthouse from the Bureau of Land Management, leased it to the society, dating the lease to 1996 in one place and 1995 in another.[^4] In spring 2000 the lantern room was lifted off by helicopter for the flight to shore, and as the helicopter approached land it came in too low and the lantern room hit the beach; the dome survived and a local firm built a new lantern room from it, returned to the tower in 2001. The light was shown again as a private aid to navigation on 20 October 2002, failed after a short time, and was lit once more on 10 March 2012.[^4]

## Today

The tower is on a rock six nautical miles offshore. PBS describes it as not accessible to the public,[^25] and Lighthouse Friends describes helicopter tours run by the society as the way in.[^4] Lighthouse Friends lists it as owned by the preservation society, while elsewhere describing a 25-year lease signed with Del Norte County in June 2021, so who holds title is unclear from those pages.[^4] The society has run helicopter tours to the rock, with the tower open during them; the state suspended the flights in 2012 and allowed them to resume in 2017, and they are booked through the society.[^4] These details may have changed, so check with the society before planning anything.

The first-order lens is on display at the Del Norte County Historical Society Museum, 577 H Street, Crescent City.[^4] From the mainland, the tower can be seen from Point St. George, at the end of Radio Road north of Crescent City's airport, where the housing used by the keepers when they were ashore stands beside the parking area.[^4]
