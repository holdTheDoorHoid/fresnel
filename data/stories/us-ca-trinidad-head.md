Trinidad Head is a rounded headland, some 400 feet (120 m) high, that forms the northern side of Trinidad Harbor in Humboldt County, California. On a ledge cut into its ocean side stands a one-story brick tower only 25 feet (7.6 m) tall, set high on a cliff, with a bell house below it that is the only one left in the state.[^1]

## The place

The nomination for the National Register of Historic Places calls the headland the most prominent landform on the California coast north of Cape Mendocino. It says the harbor beside it was, in the decades after statehood, one of the busiest in California, carrying mining equipment, lumber and farm products at a time before railroads or a connected road network.[^1] The Trinidad Museum Society adds that between 1850 and 1865 at least 25 ships sank and at least 274 lives were lost off California's north coast, a figure for the whole coast rather than for this headland.[^2]

Sources disagree about when the light was first asked for. The nomination says federal and local officials had been pressing Congress for a lighthouse here since 1851. LighthouseFriends dates the proposal to 1854 and puts an appropriation in June 1860, while the Trinidad Museum Society says Congress approved $20,000 in 1871.[^1][^3][^2] The sources do agree that the government bought the land in 1866, forty-two acres in the museum's and LighthouseFriends's telling.[^1][^2][^3]

## Building the light

The tower dates from 1871, five years after the land purchase, and the nomination names no architect.[^1] LighthouseFriends says work began in the spring of 1871 with a road cut into the eastern side of the head, and that the tower and a keeper's dwelling about fifty yards from it were finished over the summer and autumn.[^3]

The tower is a four-sided pyramid: a one-story base of brick and cement carrying an iron-and-glass lantern, with a gallery round it. The nomination says it is the only one-story brick pyramidal lighthouse ever built in California.[^1] According to LighthouseFriends, Keeper Jeremiah Kiler lit its revolving fourth-order Fresnel lens for the first time on 1 December 1871, and the Trinidad Museum Society also gives 1 December.[^3][^2] The light showed fixed white, broken once a minute by a red flash, a pattern the Light-House Board's list of lights still printed in 1899.[^3][^4] Putnam's history of the service puts the light 196 feet (60 m) above the water, on a cliff about 175 feet (53 m) high; LighthouseFriends gives 198 feet.[^5][^3]

The station's fog signal came late. In the early 1890s the Light-House Board asked its district officers whether the station needed a fog bell. The answer, dated 14 November (the report volume covers 1891 and 1892), was that it did not: at that height a bell would be inaudible at sea level.[^6] A bell struck by machinery was nonetheless put into operation on 15 July 1898.[^7] The nomination describes a 4,000-pound (1,800 kg) bronze bell hung from a concrete frame, with the clockwork that worked its hammer powered by weights; LighthouseFriends adds that the weights descended the face of the cliff.[^1][^3] The present bell house was built in 1900, replacing a less substantial building put up two years before, and a wooden weight tower followed after the original weights broke loose and fell down the cliff.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for the year to June 1901 records that the cable carrying the weights broke and the weights, lost in the sea, were replaced.[^8] The signal was shut down for repairs from 26 August to 17 September 1900.[^9]

A telephone line to the town of Trinidad, paid for with $250 appropriated on 3 March 1899, was accepted in July 1899 and ran on redwood poles 150 feet apart. The Board found it "of no use" to the service, noted that the telephone company charged $60 a year in rental, and ordered it discontinued on 30 June 1900.[^10]

Some time before the Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1914, the old wick lamp was replaced by an incandescent oil-vapor burner in the tower's six-sided lantern. The new lamp threw false flashes that spoiled the light's character at short range. The cure was two screens of asbestos millboard, painted dead black, hinged to the lantern bars and reaching to the lens and over the top.[^11]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists Jeremiah Kiler as principal keeper from 1871 to 1888.[^12] A Sarah J. Kiler appears as first assistant from 1880 to 1882; the archive does not say how she was related to him.[^12] After a short spell with William A. Henderson as acting keeper, Frederick L. Harrington took charge, listed as acting keeper in 1888 and principal keeper from 1889 to 1916.[^12] LighthouseFriends puts the whole of his service at 1888 to 1916.[^3] In his report on the storm of December 1913, Harrington said he had been stationed at Trinidad Head for 26 years.[^13]

Several others named Harrington appear in the archive's list: a Maude S. Harrington as laborer in 1900, a Josephine I. Harrington as laborer in short stints between 1901 and 1904 and as principal keeper in 1916, and a Clinton E. Harrington as assistant in 1914. The archive does not say how they were related to Frederick.[^12]

Edward Wiborg followed in 1916 and stayed until 1930. Malcolm Cady, first assistant from 1926, then kept the light from 1930 to 1940. Emory Vradenburg was first assistant from 1930 to 1946, serving under Cady and then under Perry S. Hunter, principal keeper from 1940 to 1946.[^12] The Coast Guard took control of the station in 1939, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] For the 1960s the archive lists twenty Coast Guard crew members between 1960 and 1969, alongside a succession of principal keepers.[^12]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sea reached the lantern here at least once. In the Lighthouse Service Bulletin, Keeper Harrington reported that the gale that began on 28 December 1913 blew for a week, and that by 3 p.m. on the 31st the sea was washing over Pilot Rock, which he gave as 103 feet high (LighthouseFriends's transcription of the report prints 93).[^13][^3] He went on:

> "At 4.40 p. m. I was in the tower and had just set the lens in operation and turned to wipe the lantern windows when I observed a sea of unusual height"[^13]

He watched it come in. It struck the bluff, ran up its face and over it until, to him, the solid sea seemed level with where he stood in the lantern; it then fell on the top of the bluff and hit the tower about level with the balcony. The lens stopped revolving and the tower shivered for several seconds, and he had the lens level and turning again within half an hour.[^13] In his 26 years there, he wrote, no sea of that size had come, and only twice had he seen sea or spray go over Pilot Rock.[^13] George Putnam's 1917 history of the service printed the keeper's report and dated the storm December 1913.[^5] LighthouseFriends dates it to December 1914, which the Bulletin and Putnam contradict.[^3]

## After the keepers

Electricity reached the station in 1942, LighthouseFriends says, and in 1947 compressed-air horns replaced the bell and a modern beacon replaced the Fresnel lens.[^3] The nomination agrees on the 1947 horns.[^1] The old lens went into a replica of the tower that the town built in a park overlooking the harbor, in 1948 by the nomination and 1949 by LighthouseFriends.[^1][^3] The original bell hangs beside that replica.[^1] The original keeper's dwelling was torn down in the late 1960s and the Coast Guard built housing for its crews, a four-plex in the nomination's words and a triplex in LighthouseFriends's.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends says the station was automated in 1974, though Coast Guard personnel kept living there until after 2000.[^3]

## Today

The light station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1991 (reference 91001098).[^14] In 2013 an LED beacon replaced the 1947 fifth-order lens, which is now on display at the Trinidad Museum.[^3] A ceremony in May 2014 marked the transfer of the property from the Coast Guard to the Bureau of Land Management, which manages it as part of the California Coastal National Monument.[^3][^15]

The lighthouse is federal property, and the Bureau lists it for visits through scheduled tours. Volunteers of the Trinidad Museum Society open it for tours on the first Saturday of each month from 10 a.m. to noon; the museum's number is 707-677-3883 and the Bureau's Arcata office is 707-825-2300.[^15][^16] LighthouseFriends lists the grounds, dwelling and tower as closed, with the lighthouse open to visitors only on the monthly tour morning, and says a viewing platform on the Trinidad Head trail gives a glimpse of the lantern room.[^3]
