Peggy's Point Lighthouse stands on a barren rock outcrop at the east side of the entrance to St. Margaret's Bay, at Peggy's Cove, Nova Scotia. The first keeper is listed there from 1868. The concrete tower there now was built in 1915, beside the original wooden light.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Peggy's Point marks the east side of the entrance to St. Margaret's Bay.[^2] The small cove just inside it shares the name. LighthouseFriends thinks both names most likely come from the bay itself, with Peggy a nickname for Margaret.[^1] It also gives one story as a claim: that Peggy was the sole survivor of a shipwreck in the area, that she married a cove resident around 1800, and that visitors would come from afar to meet her.[^1]

The register describes a barren rock outcrop with natural vegetation, no visible paths, and no other structures in the immediate vicinity. It also describes the light's prominent position at the mouth of the bay, says the lighthouse was open to public visitation very early in its history, and says it became the focus of the community's tourism.[^3]

## Building the light

In 1860 a petition from George Garrison Sr. and other residents of St. Margaret's Bay asked the House of Assembly for a beacon light at the east point. No immediate action was taken.[^1] On 20 June 1867 the Board of Works awarded a $1,690 contract to Murphy and Lawlor to build a lighthouse on the point.[^1]

The first lighthouse was a wooden building: a dwelling about 8 m (26 ft) square, a storey and a half high, with a lantern on its roof. Edward Horne was hired as its first keeper at $350 a year, and LighthouseFriends lists him from 1868. In 1872 the upper part was finished to make two bedrooms for the keeper and his family.[^1]

LighthouseFriends quotes the Department's report for 1874, which describes a twelve-sided lantern with plate glass. The light was shown from six flat-wick lamps with 12-inch reflectors.[^1]

In 1887 an eight-sided iron lantern room, taken from the West Ironbound Island light, replaced the old lantern room, which was no longer fit for service.[^1]

On 29 March 1897 a fifth-order lens replaced the lamps and reflectors. Less than three months later an explosion in the lantern room damaged the lens. The old reflecting apparatus had to go back in, and a false roof was fitted to keep rain off the light.[^1]

The present tower dates from 1915. The Standard Construction Company of Halifax built it under a $2,456 contract, and a fourth-order lens and a new eight-foot lantern cost a further $1,945.92. The concrete tower stands about 15 m west of the old light, which was kept as the keeper's house. It was placed in operation on 1 August 1915.[^1][^2] The register calls it a relatively early example of reinforced concrete used for a lighthouse.[^3]

## The keepers

Edward Horne was the first keeper. In October 1873 William Crooks Sr. was appointed in his place, Horne having been transferred to Meagher's Beach.[^1] George Swinehammer kept the light from 1883 to 1902.[^1]

Sidney H. Garrison took charge in 1902 and kept the light until 1926. The Department's keeper list for 1905 names him at Peggy Point.[^1][^4] Sidney L. Garrison followed him from 1926 to 1933. LighthouseFriends then lists Nellie G. Garrison as head keeper for 1933 and 1934. She is the only woman on its keeper list for the station. The sources do not say how the Garrisons were related.[^1]

Robert R. Manuel kept the light from 1934 and was keeper and then caretaker. In March 1958 he said he no longer wanted to look after it.[^1]

The light had been on commercial power since 1949. In 1958 a time switch was installed to control the beacon, and the light was made unwatched. Because the power was unreliable, York Manuel was hired as caretaker on 9 April 1958 at $100 a year. He served until November 1966.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 15 April 1881 a heavy southeast gale sent a heavy sea over Peggy's Point. It damaged the lighthouse and its foundation and swept away the oil house with its contents. A new oil house and other repairs cost $545 in all.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the original lighthouse was torn down in 1954, after it had been badly damaged by Hurricane Edna.[^1]

LighthouseFriends also notes that Swissair Flight 111 crashed just offshore in 1998, and that a memorial to the crash stands about a kilometre north-west along the Lighthouse Route.[^1]

## After the keepers

From 1975 to 2009 the tower held a post office in the summer months. Visitors could mail letters and cards there and receive a special cancellation stamp.[^1]

In early 2009 the Department of Fisheries and Oceans said it had no money to paint the tower. After public outcry, contractors were hired. A local painters' union repainted it in 2012 and again in 2017.[^1]

In October 2021 a new accessible viewing platform opened near the lighthouse. Its 1,300 m² deck cost $3.1 million, and it was designed to improve access and discourage visitors from going onto the granite rocks during storms.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard's List of Lights gives the light a red flash every five seconds, with a focal height of 22.0 m and a range of 10 nautical miles. It operates day and night, year round. The list describes the tower as white and octagonal with a red top, 13.2 m high.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives the tower's height as about 9 m (30 ft), which does not match the Coast Guard figure.[^1][^2]

LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1] The register describes the rock around the tower as having no visible paths leading to it.[^3]
