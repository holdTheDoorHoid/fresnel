Warrior Rock Light stands near the northern tip of Sauvie Island, Oregon, just south of St. Helens, close to the junction of the Columbia River, the Lewis River and the Multnomah Channel.[^1][^2] It is still an active light, a flashing white beacon on a concrete tower. Its fog bell, which LighthouseFriends calls the oldest in the Pacific Northwest, has been out of the tower since a 1969 accident and now stands in St. Helens.[^1][^2]

## The place

The point was chosen for where it sits. Its position near that junction made it a good mark for the river traffic bound to and from Portland.[^1]

The name is older than the light. In October 1792 an advance party from George Vancouver's Columbia River expedition landed on a rocky point near the north end of the island. LighthouseFriends says that Lieutenant William Broughton, surrounded by twenty-three canoes of war-clad Chinooks, chose to make peace rather than fight.[^1] The community history says it is unclear whether the lieutenant landed at Warrior Rock or at Warrior Point.[^2]

## Building the light

The government had to take the land. LighthouseFriends says the owner asked a very high price, so the government began condemnation proceedings in 1887, and title came in December 1888.[^1]

The 1889 light was a small two-story building on a sandstone base sixteen feet high. The ground floor held the bell's clockwork, a bed, a table and a stove for the keeper, and the upper floor held the lens lantern under a covered half deck.[^1]

The fog bell had been used elsewhere first. It was cast in Philadelphia in 1855 and used at Cape Disappointment, then moved to West Point on Puget Sound, and it came to Warrior Rock in 1889.[^1][^2] The Daily Morning Astorian of 1 December 1889 reported that the new fog bell at Warrior Rock rang for the first time the Friday before.[^3] In its first season, LighthouseFriends says, the bell sounded once every fifteen seconds for 316 hours. The first keeper's logbook recorded problems with the bell and with a lamp that kept going out.[^1]

## The keepers

Joseph Hayburn was the first keeper. The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster lists him as acting keeper from 1889 to 1891 and as principal keeper from 1891 to 1912.[^8] LighthouseFriends says he had been born in Kentucky, and that at eighteen he went off to fight in the Civil War, at Stones River, according to a 1912 notice of his death.[^1]

The first keepers had no house. LighthouseFriends says they were paid twenty dollars a month to find their own.[^1] In 1912 the Lighthouse Service asked for $2,000 to buy 1.61 acres near the light, and the act of 22 October 1913 provided the money.[^1][^6][^5] The Commissioner's report for 1915 says that "One and one-eighth acres, more or less, were purchased," with the dwelling on them, at a total cost of $2,000.[^5] The two sources disagree on the acreage.

Thomas E. Stanfield (1912–1925) followed.[^8] Florido "Frank" DeRoy came from Heceta Head in 1925,[^1][^8] to a station that LighthouseFriends says had fallen into disrepair under his predecessor. Lighthouse authorities praised him as "one of the best keepers in the district."[^1] When the river was high and the foundation stood in water, DeRoy rode an aerial tram he had rigged from a tree to the tower.[^1][^2] He nicknamed the bell "Black Moria," because its striking gear often broke and he had to ring it by hand for hours.[^1]

In 1935 DeRoy retired on disability after a fall. He had climbed a tree to saw off a lower limb, so that he could see Lewis River Light and judge the approach of fog. When the limb broke, he fell roughly twenty-five feet onto a woodpile.[^1] Edmund N. Cadwell (1935–1939) and Ray I. Loney (1939–1940) followed in the roster.[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A cold wave ran from 14 January to 4 February 1930. LighthouseFriends says the Columbia and Willamette froze below Portland for the first time in sixty years.[^1] DeRoy walked across the ice to St. Helens. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin printed his account, saying he "walked on a smooth ice surface and over deep water from his station to St. Helens."[^7] LighthouseFriends says he made the crossing several times until a tank steamer broke the ice, and that he was then stranded, his rowboat frozen in.[^1]

On the night of 30 March 1930 a barge, the Swan, collided with the steamer Davenport in the river near the light. DeRoy and his son searched the beach and aided eleven people, sheltering them and giving them dry clothes.[^1]

The Board's report for 1897 gives the Warrior Rock fog bell as "in operation some 344 hours." The same list gives the Willamette River fog bell 166 hours and the Ediz Hook, Washington, fog bell 848 hours.[^4]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the light was electrified in 1928, after which the keeper could simply flip a switch to activate the light and fog bell.[^1]

In 1931 the small frame building was replaced by a 28-foot (8.5 m) concrete tower on the same sandstone foundation.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends gives 1931 for the tower, and the local history site dates it to the 1930s.[^2]

On 27 May 1969 a barge struck the tower, damaging the foundation and putting the light and the fog bell out.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard was still debating whether to rebuild when the historic bell was taken down. In the move it fell into the river and cracked badly, and it never went back up.[^1] The community history gives a different order: the Coast Guard rebuilt the tower while the bell was being removed.[^2]

## Today

The tower shows a flashing white light, with no fog signal.[^1] The Coast Guard owns the light.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed, so treat the tower as closed and do not climb it.[^1]

Access is by a short boat ride of about a fifth of a mile from the riverfront marina in St. Helens, or by a three-mile hike from the northern part of Sauvie Island.[^1] A distant view is possible from Dike Road on the Washington side of the river.[^1]

The cracked fog bell is on the north side of the Columbia County Historic Courthouse in St. Helens. A half-scale replica of the original tower also stands on the St. Helens waterfront.[^1][^2]
