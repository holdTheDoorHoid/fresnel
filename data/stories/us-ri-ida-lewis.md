Lime Rock is a small ledge at the southern entrance to Newport Harbor, Rhode Island.[^1] For more than thirty years of the light's working life, the keeper there was a woman. Ida Lewis, the daughter of the first keeper, kept the light for thirty-two or thirty-four years, depending on which source is read.[^1][^15] Newspapers called her the "Grace Darling of America," after the English keeper's daughter who helped rescue the survivors of the *Forfarshire* steamer.[^2]

## The place

The rock lies just west of Fort Adams.[^1] The National Register nomination says the light was "Established in 1854 at the southern entrance to Newport Harbor," and that Lime Rock "was separate from the mainland while the light was active."[^1] The sources disagree on how far out it lies. The nomination calls it "approximately 100 yards off shore," while the *New-York Tribune* of 1869 put it "about 304 yards from the shore."[^1][^2]

The Lime Rock Foundation, a Newport group formed in 2022, exists "to provide funds for the purpose of restoring, maintaining, and preserving" the club's historic buildings.[^4] It dates the building of the lighthouse to 1853 and says that "In 1853, under the authorization of the United States Government, a lighthouse was built on Lime Rock."[^4] That is a year earlier than the 1854 given by the nomination and by the Society's record.[^1][^3]

## Building the light

The first light was a brick tower with a sixth-order Fresnel lens.[^3] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's Archive record for the station gives the original optic as "SIXTH ORDER, FRESNEL," installed in 1854, with the light 28 feet (8.5 m) above the water.[^3]

The keeper's house came later. "Between 1854 and the completion of the present dwelling in 1857 there were no accommodations on the rock for the keeper," the nomination says.[^1] The first keeper was Hosea Lewis, a "veteran ex-revenue pilot, familiar with the coast from Halifax to Norfolk," who "became its keeper on the 8th of December, 1853," according to the 1869 *Tribune*.[^2] That paper says that "a substantial, square, two-story brick house was built," and that "in June, 1857, his family joined him."[^2]

The nomination dates the present tower and dwelling to 1857, while the Archive's record gives 1854 for the tower.[^1][^3] Either way the tower is small. The nomination says it is "Thirteen feet high and four feet square" (about 4 m by 1.2 m), and "the smallest of any light in Rhode Island."[^1]

In its 1901 report, the Light-House Board described its exhibit at the Pan-American Exposition. Among the old-style apparatus it listed was the lens used at Lime Rock, "of which Ida Lewis, the noted female life-saver, has been the keeper for many years."[^8]

## The keepers

Hosea Lewis was soon disabled. The *Tribune* says that "a stroke of paralysis disabled him from all work," and that from then on the family leaned on his eldest daughter, "the main-stay of the family."[^2] The accounts do not agree on when the stroke came. The *Tribune* dates it four months after the family arrived in June 1857.[^2] The nomination says that he "took up residence with his family at Lime Rock on July 29, 1858," and that "Six months later, Captain Lewis was paralyzed by a stroke."[^1] A 2015 National Archives blog post, by an archivist who works on civilian personnel records, says the stroke came "A few months after his appointment."[^5]

The Archive's roster lists Hosea Lewis as principal keeper from 1853 to 1872, and then a "Zoradia Lewis" from 1872 to 1879.[^3] The National Archives post says that after the stroke "his wife, Zorada, and Ida carried out the lighthouse duties in addition to their everyday household chores."[^5] The roster does not say how the 1872 keeper was related to Hosea. The National Archives post calls his wife "Zorada," which points to the same person, but the roster does not confirm it.[^3][^5]

Ida Lewis was appointed keeper in her own right in 1879.[^3][^5] The roster gives "Idawalley Zoradia Lewis" as principal keeper from 1879 to 1911,[^3] and the National Archives post describes her "appointment as the official lighthouse keeper of Lime Rock in 1879."[^5] The 1869 *Tribune* says her name, "Idawalley Zoradia," came from her maternal grandfather, "an estimable but eccentric Block Island physician."[^2]

She married in 1870.[^6] The Providence *Morning Star* printed the marriage notice on 1 November. It gives the date only as "23d instant," which in a paper of that date most likely means 23 October. It names the bride as "Miss Ida Lewis, of Lime Rock," and the groom as a Mr. Wilson of Black Rock, Connecticut.[^6] "Although they separated two years later, Ida used 'Wilson' as her middle name for the rest of her life," the National Archives post says.[^5] Several newspapers after 1900 call her Mrs. Ida Lewis Wilson.[^10][^12] A Providence paper of December 1907 describes "Mrs. Ida Lewis Wilson, keeper of Lime" rowing over to Newport on business.[^12]

The nomination calls her "One of only a few women keepers in the Lighthouse Service," and says she "became well-known for her numerous rescues off Lime Rock."[^1]

After 1911 the Archive's roster lists a "T. Rudolph Lewis" for that year alone, and then Evard Jansen from 1911 to 1927.[^3] The 1869 *Tribune* names a brother, Rudolph, and says that Ida rowed her brothers to school.[^2] No source cited here ties the 1911 roster line to that brother.[^3] The Commissioner of Lighthouses printed "E. Jansen, keeper, Lime Rock Light Station, R. I." in its 1918 report.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The best-documented rescue was on 20 March 1869. The *Tribune* places it "toward the close of a stormy March afternoon," and says the two soldiers were "rescued on the 20th of March."[^2] Sergeant James Adams and Private John McLaughlin, of Fort Adams, had gone into Newport to make some purchases.[^2] The paper says a boy of about fourteen, "scarcely 14 years old," who had got hold of one of the smallest and most unsafe sailboats in the harbor, talked them into letting him carry them back across the harbor.[^2]

"The rain fell that day in blinding torrents," the paper says, and "the gale drove the waves across the harbor." The boat overturned. The nomination describes the two soldiers as men "whose boat had overturned in a sudden gale."[^2][^1] The boy was lost, and "nor has any trace of him since been seen."[^2] The soldiers were "Fast paralyzing with cold and almost bereft of hope" when a small boat came out from the rock, with "only a slender youth, and a still slenderer woman" at the oars.[^2] The woman was Ida. The youth was her brother Hosey, whom the paper gives as twenty years old.[^2] As the youth leaned out to reach the nearest soldier, his sister turned the boat "with a well-timed stroke," and then they pulled both men in. "The Sergeant was able to stagger on shore, but private McLaughlin had to be carried into the light-house, where both of them received the utmost care and kindness, and were safely conveyed to Fort Adams the next day."[^2]

The soldiers later pressed a gift on her. The paper says they "insisted on her accepting a gold watch and chain of Swiss manufacture, valued at $100."[^2] The rescue reached a national audience. The nomination records the *Harper's Weekly* of 31 July 1869, "which featured two engravings of Ida Lewis and her lighthouse."[^1] A 1911 newspaper feature remembers the rescue as one that "occurred on a stormy morning in March of 1869," and says that "It took Ida fifteen minutes to launch her dory." The accounts differ on the time of day and on whether she had help.[^14]

Other rescues followed. The National Archives post says that "In 1877, a sheep jumped from the wharf during a gale," and that when three men went after it and ran into trouble, Ida rescued all four.[^5] The *Tribune* describes what appears to be the same rescue, dated January 1867. The two sources disagree on the year.[^2]

In February 1881 two soldiers broke through the ice near Lime Rock. The Life-Saving Service's award list, printed in its annual report for fiscal 1895, credits her with "the rescue of two soldiers who had broken through the ice near Lime Rock on the afternoon of Feb. 4,1881."[^9]

Her medals are listed in several places. The National Archives post says she was "awarded a silver medal from the Life-Saving Benevolent Association of New York," and that she received "the Gold Lifesaving Medal."[^5] A Providence paper of May 1902 called her "the heroine of Lime Rock light, who has saved eighteen lives."[^10]

In January 1907 the *Morning Tribune* reported that she had saved "Mrs. R. P. Walker of Long Island from Drowning" at a Newport wharf. Mrs. Walker had stepped out of the boat and slipped, and her "foot between the piling of the wharf, while her body went under water," was trapped.[^11] Ida first raised the woman above the water, then began "a tug of war to free the jammed foot."[^11]

The Carnegie Hero Fund gave her a life pension of $30 a month.[^1][^12] The nomination says she was "given a $30 a month pension by Andrew Carnegie,"[^1] and the *News-Democrat* of 17 December 1907 reports that she received a payment on "the first day of Decomber."[^12]

## After the keepers

Ida Lewis Wilson died in October 1911.[^1][^5] The accounts give different days. The nomination says "On October 14, 1911, Ida Lewis died at the age of 69,"[^1] while the National Archives post says she "died at the Lime Rock Light Station on October 24, 1911, at the age of 69."[^5] A Pascoag paper of 27 October mentions the death "this week."[^13] A syndicated feature printed in the Olneyville Times of 24 November was headed "Noted Woman Lighthouse Keeper Dies on Duty."[^14]

The rock and light were renamed for her. The National Archives post says that "In 1924 the Rhode Island legislature renamed Lime Rock as the Ida Lewis Rock."[^5] The nomination says the light was renamed "After her death."[^1] The two accounts give different dates for the renaming.

The light was automated in 1927.[^1] The nomination says that the light and lens were removed and an automatic beacon "mounted on a steel skeletal tower directly in front of the lighthouse" took over.[^1] The following year the lighthouse was sold to the Narragansett Bay Regatta Association, which set up the Ida Lewis Yacht Club there.[^1] The nomination gives 1927 in one place and "The light was discontinued in 1928" in another, so the year the light stopped is not settled.[^1]

The Coast Guard kept the steel beacon going until 1963.[^1] The nomination says it was kept "until November 1, 1963, when it was discontinued and taken down."[^1] In 1933 the lamp she had used was found at the Staten Island depot and returned to Newport "as a loan to the Ida Lewis Yacht Club."[^16] The Bulletin says that when Newport citizens bought Lime Rock, they meant to preserve the buildings that had been her home.[^16]

## Today

Lime Rock is private property.[^1][^3] The National Register nomination (reference 87001700) lists the owner as the yacht club, the present use as a club, and the accessibility as "yes, restricted."[^1] The Archive's record says "Open to Public: No."[^3] The nomination describes a 300-foot wooden pier joining the rock to the mainland, with boat slips on the east side.[^1]

The nomination says the original kerosene lamp "still hangs in the lantern," and that the club "still operates the original lamp kept by Ida Lewis as a privately maintained navigational aid."[^1] The Lime Rock Foundation reports that the cupola that houses the light was restored: "Full restoration of the cupola housing the Lime Rock light, completed in Spring 2023."[^4]

Visitors should treat the rock as a private yacht club. The sources give no public visiting arrangement, and any visit is the club's to offer.[^1][^3]
