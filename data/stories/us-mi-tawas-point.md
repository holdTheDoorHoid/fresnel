Tawas Point Light stands on a long sandy hook that curls into the northern end of Saginaw Bay, in Iosco County, Michigan. It is a story about moving ground: the point kept growing, so the light kept ending up too far from the water, and the Light-House Board built or moved things to chase it.

## The place

Tawas Point, formerly known as Ottawa Point, lies near the northern entrance to Saginaw Bay and shelters the anchorage of Tawas Bay.[^1] The Friends of the lighthouse describe the point as a story of sand moved by the prevailing northeast wind, and say that in 1850 the bay held a few log cabins, some fishermen and farmers, and a Chippewa band led by Chief O-Ta-Was, for whom the point and bay are named.[^2] As the bay filled with sawmills, docks and schooners, they write, the community called the first lighthouse "the little jack-o-lantern" and petitioned Congress for something better.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $5,000 on 28 September 1850 for a lighthouse at Ottawa Point; the thirty-acre site was bought from Daniel S. Ellethorpe for $200, and a rubblestone tower was finished in 1852 and lit at the opening of navigation in 1853, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] That source gives its light as seven lamps in 14-inch reflectors until a fifth-order Fresnel lens replaced them in 1856.[^1] The Tawas Point Lighthouse Friends also give 1853 for the first lighting and describe the tower and keeper's house as the only permanent buildings on the bay, but they say the station was fitted with a fifth-order lens from the start, so the two accounts differ on when the lens arrived.[^2]

The point grew. By 1869, LighthouseFriends reports, the old station needed repairs, but the shore had built southward until the light stood more than a mile inland, and the Board asked for $30,000 for a new light or a set of range lights.[^1] The Friends put the point's end almost a mile from the old light by 1873.[^2] Congress appropriated that sum on 3 March 1875.[^1] The new tower went up on a shoal in about four feet of water, with work beginning on 12 August 1876; the lens was moved from the old tower over the winter and the new light shown at the opening of navigation in 1877.[^1] It is a 67-foot (20 m) conical tower joined by a covered passage to a two-story red-brick dwelling.[^1] The Friends add that it rests on a timber crib with a foundation of cut limestone blocks.[^2] Cribs around the station were extended in 1884 and 1885, filled with rubble and brick taken from the abandoned first lighthouse.[^1][^2]

The lens changed again in the 1890s. The Light-House Board's report of 1891-92 says a new illuminating apparatus was ordered for "Tawas (Ottawa)" that would raise the light to the fourth order and shorten its flashing period from ninety to thirty seconds, and a later entry in the same volume records the change to a fourth-order occulting light on the night of 1 September 1891.[^3] LighthouseFriends also gives 1 September 1891 and says the new light showed twenty-five seconds of white followed by a five-second eclipse, produced by a hood on the lamp raised and lowered by clockwork.[^1] The same source says the keeper had to rewind the weight for that clockwork every five hours.[^1]

Sand kept forcing work at the waterfront. The Board's reports of the mid-1890s record the timber platform around the house and tower rebuilt and the landing dock extended 640 feet into deeper water.[^4] The Friends, who give a different figure, say the wharf was extended 600 feet in the 1890s.[^2] A brick oil house was added in 1898.[^1][^2]

In 1899 the station got a fog signal. Material for the building was delivered to the Detroit depot that year,[^5] and the Board's next report lists a 10-inch steam whistle at Tawas dated 28 September 1899.[^6] LighthouseFriends puts the brick building five-eighths of a mile southwest of the tower, because the point had kept growing (the Friends say a mile), and says it took about forty minutes to raise steam, so keepers had to guess when fog was coming.[^1] The same report carries $5,000 for a keeper's dwelling at Tawas among its estimates of funds wanted.[^6] On the tower, an incandescent oil vapor lamp was introduced in May 1915, and in 1928 an air diaphone replaced the steam whistle, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive's roster names 15 head keepers between 1853 and 1955.[^7] The first, Colin Graham, is listed only for 1853, and Sherman Wheeler is listed from 1853 to 1857.[^7] Local history, as the Friends tell it, says Wheeler was a local farmer, that the town founder Gideon Whittemore stayed at the lighthouse with the Wheelers while looking for timber land, and that Chief O-Ta-Was was a frequent visitor and dinner guest there.[^2] That is local tradition and the Friends do not cite a record for it. LighthouseFriends gives Wheeler's first salary as $350 a year and describes a five-room brick dwelling with an attached kitchen.[^1]

Joshua S. Sadler held the light from 1861 to 1876, the span covering the decision to replace the first tower, and Samuel C. Palmer served from 1887 to 1910, the longest term among the head keepers.[^7] After Palmer came Herbert N. Burrows (1910 to 1923), John F. Brooks (1923 to 1936) Louis A. Dissett (1936 to 1946) and Mathew Storback (1947 to 1953).[^7]

The fog signal created the post of assistant keeper. George Galbraith is listed as first assistant from 1900,[^7] and LighthouseFriends gives his start date as 16 March 1900.[^1] Housing was a long-running problem: in 1908 the Board wrote that the assistant lived in an "unsuitable wooden hut", as LighthouseFriends quotes it.[^1] No new house was built; in 1922 a dwelling was moved from Ecorse Light Station in the Detroit River and remodeled for the assistant, at a cost the Commissioner's report gives as $9,121.[^8] The Friends call it the Double Dwelling and say it was large enough for two families and was rebuilt just north of the lighthouse.[^2]

The keepers' reservation was also a timber experiment. Between 25 and 31 October 1917, the Lighthouse Service planted about 20,000 Norway pines on thirteen acres of the reservation to grow construction timber, and a Bulletin report of 1922 said that where trees had shelter, in pockets and behind sand hillocks, they averaged two to three feet, while losses on exposed knobs had been heavy.[^10] (LighthouseFriends dates the planting to October 1927; the Bulletin's 1917 date is the contemporary source.[^1][^10])

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A life-saving station, No. 4 of the Tenth District, was set up at Ottawa Point in the 1870s; the Friends date it to 1876.[^2] The Life-Saving Service's report for 1879 describes the steamer *Annie Moiles*, towing a raft of lumber, as having "stranded about midnight on a shoal off Ottawa Point"; the crew of Station No. 4 found her in the early morning and went out to her with the supply-boat.[^9] The Friends also record that the schooner *Dolphin*, carrying supplies for Tawas, went aground on the point in a heavy gale in November 1874.[^2] The sources used here do not tie these events to the lighthouse keepers.

## After the keepers

Civilian keepers served until 1953, when Storback went to Presque Isle and a Coast Guardsman, Leon DeRosia, took charge; the light was automated that year.[^1][^2] The Friends say the buildings housed Coast Guard families until 1993, and that the double dwelling was demolished in 2002 after the State of Michigan took over.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the transfer to 2002 and the Friends to 2001.[^1][^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by the Michigan Department of Natural Resources and sits in Tawas Point State Park.[^1] The state restored the interior, with the dwelling's rooms set to different decades, and the upper floor is an apartment where visitors can pay to serve one- or two-week stints as tour-giving keepers.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports the lighthouse open from May to mid-October, with tower tours on set days; check the park for current hours.[^1]

The Coast Guard announced in October 2015 that it would move the light to a modern beacon on a tower that carries a fog signal half a mile away, and take out the fourth-order lens.[^1] The Friends write that the light was removed from the tower in September 2016 and a modern optic now stands near the current end of the point.[^2] LighthouseFriends records a preference to keep the lens in the tower, and the lens status in the tower is not confirmed here.[^1]
