Chicamacomico Life-Saving Station stands beside Highway 12 in Rodanthe, on Hatteras Island in North Carolina's Dare County.[^1] It was commissioned on 4 December 1874, and the National Park Service calls it the first Life-Saving Service station staffed in the state.[^2] Crews worked this stretch of the Outer Banks for eighty years,[^3] and in August 1918 one of them took a motor surfboat into a burning sea to bring 42 sailors off a torpedoed British tanker.[^2][^26] Two station houses and outbuildings survive as a museum.[^2]

## The station

By 1874 the Life-Saving Service had ten new stations under way in its Sixth District, which covered Virginia and North Carolina: three in Virginia and seven in North Carolina.[^4] Work at Chicamacomico did not begin smoothly. The site was public land with no deeded owner; the contractor, James H. Boyle, had a crew of twenty there by mid-March 1874, and on the 19th the foreman and his nineteen men quit and went home, the association says, over Boyle's neglect of their comfort and food and a shortage of good lumber.[^4] On 23 April the contract for Chicamacomico, Oregon Inlet and Nags Head went to a builder named D. Simpson, and by the third week of October all ten stations were finished.[^4]

The association describes the first seven North Carolina stations, Chicamacomico among them, as timber-frame houses with frames milled at New Bern, built to withstand storms.[^4][^2] Downstairs were a boat room and a "day room" that served as kitchen, dining room and sitting room; upstairs the keeper had an office and quarters, and there was sleeping space for the crew and for rescued sailors. The roof carried an open lookout platform and a flagstaff for signalling, and a drill pole was put up for practice with the beach apparatus.[^4]

Chicamacomico was commissioned on 4 December 1874 with Benjamin Pugh as keeper.[^4] The Service's 1879 list gives it as Station No. 18 of the Sixth District, five miles south of New Inlet, at about 35° 35′ 30″ N, 75° 27′ 30″ W.[^5]

Outbuildings followed: an oil house in 1888, a cookhouse and cart shed in 1892, and a boathouse completed by May 1897.[^4] In the hurricane of August 1899, the association's account says, the tide came all the way round the station and the building was in danger of being washed away; at 6 p.m. the cookhouse door blew off, and the boathouse ended up thirty feet (9 m) from its foundation. The station itself, already condemned, leaked.[^4] A new station house was under construction in 1910 and was finished in 1911 to replace what the U.S. Life-Saving Service Heritage Association, citing the Service's annual reports, calls a "worn-out antiquated building".[^3] In February 1912 a superintendent of construction recommended razing the old house and reusing its materials for a beach boathouse, but it was kept, and once the new station was in use it was called the "boathouse".[^4][^3]

## The surfmen

On most Atlantic stations of the period, according to Sumner Kimball's 1912 account of the Service, the regular crew was six men, with a seventh added each 1 December, and the keeper chose them from experienced surfmen living near the station.[^6] The same account describes the routine of such stations in general: each night watch was kept by two surfmen who set out along the shore in opposite directions, carrying red Coston hand lights to burn on finding a vessel in trouble, and the week ran from beach-apparatus drill on Mondays and Thursdays and boat practice on Tuesdays to resuscitation drill on Fridays and cleaning on Saturdays.[^7]

Benjamin Pugh, the first keeper, stayed until 1879, when he was appointed to another station.[^4] The Service's 1876 list of stations and keepers has Benjamin S. Pugh in the row for Chicamicomico, station 9 of the Sixth District.[^8] His successor was Little Banister Midgett, "Capt. Ban" in the association's history and L. B. Midgett or L. Banister Midgett in the Service's own reports.[^4][^9][^10] The Service's 1882 report names "Keeper Midgett" in charge of Station No. 18 in October 1881.[^18]

In 1888, the association says, Midgett was ill and Josiah Wescott (Westcott in some reports) replaced him.[^4] The Service's reports show Wescott in charge at Chicamacomico in 1891, 1892 and October 1893, and L. B. Midgett keeping New Inlet, the next station north, in 1891 and 1893.[^11][^12][^13] By June 1894 Wescott was keeper at New Inlet,[^14] and the association has Midgett back at Chicamacomico that year, recovered.[^4] He was keeper there in April 1898,[^15] and a letter of thanks in the 1904 report is addressed to Keepers Josiah H. Westcott and L. Banister Midgett and the crews of the New Inlet and Chicamacomico stations, in that order;[^10] in 1906 a ship's master addressed Midgett outright as keeper of Chicamacomico.[^16] In October 1907, when the barge Saxon was wrecked near Gull Shoal, that station's keeper telephoned "Keeper Midgett, at Chicamacomico" for a team to haul his apparatus.[^17] The reports consulted do not say who kept the station between 1908 and 1915, when the association says John Allen Midgett Jr. was appointed captain (keeper).[^4]

## Wrecks and rescues

**The Thomas J. Lancaster, 5 October 1881.** The Philadelphia schooner had left Boston on 22 September with ice for Savannah and thirteen people aboard, among them the captain's wife and three children, and she stranded about three and a half miles north of the station between three and four in the morning.[^18] Benjamin O'Neal, standing in for the No. 1 surfman, who was home sick, found her on the northern patrol: "It was about 4 a.m.; the weather was misty and thick," and as soon as he made out a vessel ashore he burned his red light and started for the station.[^18] The crew set out with the mortar-cart and a volunteer, Stanley Midgett, who had been hauling wood to the station; by the time the south patrolman and his horse joined them they were eight men and three horses, at times knee-deep in water, and the Signal Service operator at Hatteras testified to a wind of sixty-four miles an hour at 5 a.m.[^18] It took four shots to put a line over the jib-boom, and the exhausted men aboard could not haul the whip line off against the current.[^18] A seaman jumped overboard and swam for shore, and the keeper met him in the breakers and landed him; a second man jumped and drowned, and the keeper, who had tried to reach him, "staggered and fell on the beach completely exhausted and taken with a severe chill" on coming out of the water.[^18] Having rested, he set out about 7 p.m. with four men and four horses to bring up the surfboat; they kept a fire on the beach all night, and at about eight the next morning the boat got through and took off the survivors.[^18] Six people were brought ashore.[^18] The captain had fallen from the bowsprit at sundown, and a child among the survivors died that night at the station; the report says the life-saving men made coffins for the dead at their own expense and buried them.[^18]

**The Mary L. Vankirk, 5 February 1882.** Four months later the crew saw a water-logged schooner heading for the land with "her colors in the rigging, union down"; her five men had decided to beach her rather than risk capsizing.[^19] The schooner came so close that the keeper, wading out waist-deep, could throw a heaving line to the men in the rigging, and all five were landed within fifteen minutes of the hawser being set up.[^19]

**The Strathairly, 24 March 1891.** The Service called the loss of this British steamer "the most disastrous wreck of the year".[^11] The Strathairly, of Newcastle, bound from Santiago, Cuba, with iron ore, ran ashore in a dense fog about twenty minutes before five in the morning, four or five hundred yards (365 to 457 m) from the beach and a mile and a quarter south of the station.[^11] She sounded her whistle, and the patrolman on the south beat answered with a red Coston light and hurried back with the alarm.[^11] Keeper Wescott telephoned Gull Shoal; the New Inlet keeper, four or five miles north, heard the bell on the line, sprang from bed to catch the message and set out with his crew without waiting to be asked.[^11] Nothing could be seen of the steamer until ten o'clock, and when the fog thinned she was found to have broken in two.[^11] Shot after shot fell short or the line parted in the longshore current, and shortly after three in the afternoon every shot-line had been used.[^11] At twenty minutes to five, twelve hours after she struck, the sailors began jumping into the sea, each in a life-belt, and surfmen and neighbours waded in and dragged sixteen men out; ten were dead.[^11] Of her crew of twenty-six, nineteen were lost and seven saved.[^11] The dead were placed in boxes the life-savers made and buried near the station on the morning of the 26th.[^11] The district inspector wrote that the loss of life was "in no way attributable to neglect or inefficiency on the part of the life-saving crews".[^11]

**The Samuel W. Tilton and the George L. Fessenden, 1898.** On 17 February the four-masted schooner Samuel W. Tilton, carrying phosphate rock from Charleston to Baltimore, stranded near the station, and her master, mate and steward wrote that "the life car far excels anything we have yet seen for saving life".[^20] Her managing owner, Captain W. C. Smith, was handed down the beach from station to station until Chicamacomico's crew carried him out to the wreck in their surfboat.[^20]

On Tuesday 26 April Surfman E. S. Midgett was the first to see the schooner Fessenden, heading toward New Inlet, partly disabled after a southeast gale near Cape Lookout.[^15] She anchored about four miles out, and Keeper Midgett "set his code flags to inquire whether she wanted aid"; no notice was taken.[^15] By Wednesday morning a northeast gale was blowing and her cables parted.[^15] She struck the outer bar about a mile north of the station at 8.50 and fetched up some 250 yards (230 m) from the beach.[^15] The Chicamacomico crew was at the wreck within ten minutes, and two volunteers from the neighbourhood, C. P. and A. F. Midgett, helped in the surf.[^15] The first shot of the Lyle gun laid its line across the jib-boom, where the crew had taken refuge, but the hull, which one witness called "rotten as a pear", broke up within about twenty minutes of stranding.[^15] Three men were saved and four lost; one of the three, who to every outward appearance seemed beyond resuscitation, was revived by the Service's Directions for Restoring the Apparently Drowned, in which the crews were drilled.[^15] The Service's own verdict was that all on board would have been saved had the master shown "a willingness to accept aid or advice from the keepers of the life-saving stations before it was too late".[^15]

**The hurricane of August 1899.** On the 16th the Gull Shoal keeper telephoned Little Kinnakeet and Chicamacomico, asking "keepers Hooper and Midgett" to bring their crews to the three-masted schooner Aaron Reppard, which was dragging toward the breakers; where she lay, the report says, "no life-saving ordnance in the world could reach her".[^21] Two Chicamacomico surfmen were among seven chosen to wade into the surf in cork jackets with shot lines, and three men who reached the water alive were pulled out of it; five aboard died.[^21][^22] On the 18th the crew had a wreck of its own.[^23] The schooner Minnie Bergen had sprung a leak and anchored three miles offshore; her master, seeing he could not keep her afloat, slipped her cables and let her drift onto the beach "in order to save the lives on board".[^23] The Lyle gun reached her at 300 yards (275 m) and the crew of seven were hauled ashore in the breeches buoy, "none too soon" as the seas broke over the wreck.[^23] The master stayed at the station until the 30th, when, having sold the part of the oil cargo that had washed ashore, he went home.[^23] In a letter of 29 August he thanked the keeper and crew, and the Women's National Relief Association for the clothing: "Heaven bless these noble-hearted women!"[^24]

**The Clyde, 9 March 1906.** The British ship Clyde, of London, in ballast from Barbados to New York, ran ashore at 5 a.m. in thick weather and a fresh northwest wind.[^25][^16] The first shot of the Lyle gun dropped the line directly over the ship, then some 300 yards (275 m) from shore, and with the New Inlet and Gull Shoal crews arriving to help, the breeches buoy made its last trip by 9.30 and the last of her thirty-five men was ashore.[^25] The master wrote that Captain Midgett "promptly turned out with his crew upon seeing my signal of distress".[^16]

**The Mirlo, 16 August 1918.** By 1918 the Life-Saving Service had been part of the Coast Guard for three years.[^4] That afternoon Surfman Leroy Midgett, on watch in the lookout, saw the British tanker Mirlo, 6,997 tons with a crew of 51, torpedoed off Wimble Shoals by the German submarine U-117.[^26] The association gives the time as 4.40 p.m.; Midgett's report, as a South Carolina newspaper printed it, says 4.30, when "a great mass of water" shot up in the air.[^26][^28] Keeper John Allen Midgett called out all hands, and within three minutes the horses were hitched to the wagon carrying motor surfboat No. 1046, which went the 600 yards (550 m) to the beach at a trot.[^26] The breakers were 18 to 20 feet (about 6 m) high, and in the association's account the boat filled and had to be waded back to the beach three times; on the fourth try, at about 5 p.m., it got through.[^26] The association adds that retellings differ on details such as how many launches it took and whether oars or the motor were used.[^26]

About five miles out they met the tanker's port lifeboat, whose master, William Williams, asked them to look for his other two boats, one capsized and one intact, and told them to go into the fire if they saw an opening.[^26] Midgett took the surfboat around the wreck to its lee side, where he found water clear of burning fuel; through a gap in the smoke between two columns of flame the crew made out a capsized lifeboat.[^26] They took six exhausted, burned men off it, and the heat was enough to blister the surfboat's paint.[^26] The boat had carried fifteen men, and nine had disappeared.[^26] The surfmen then found the third lifeboat, with nineteen men, overloaded and drifting about nine miles southeast of the station, and took it in tow.[^26] After dark they landed the six rescued men through the surf; four trips through the breakers were made before the last of the 42 survivors was ashore at about 9 p.m., and Midgett signed the station log at 11.[^26] Captain Williams wrote that the rescuers had done "one of the bravest deeds which I have ever seen".[^26] Midgett's report was released by the Navy Department and printed in newspapers at the end of August and in early September.[^27][^28]

The six men honoured for the rescue were Captain Midgett and five of his crew: Zion S. Midgett, Arthur V. Midgett, Prochorus L. O'Neal, Clarence E. Midgett and Leroy S. Midgett.[^26] Accounts of the honours differ: the association has British gold lifesaving medals for all six on 8 November 1921, a Board of Trade silver cup for the captain, and Grand Crosses of the American Cross of Honor at Manteo on 23 July 1930, while Wikipedia puts the cup in 1918 and a US Gold Lifesaving Medal six years later.[^26][^29]

## After the Life-Saving Service

In January 1915 the Life-Saving Service merged with the Revenue Cutter Service to form the Coast Guard, and Chicamacomico stayed in service as a Coast Guard station until 1954.[^1] From the afternoon of 22 April 1918 the old 1874 house was rolled 960 yards (about 880 m) on rollers to stand abreast of the 1911 station, a job that took a month.[^4]

Captain John Allen Midgett Jr. died after a car accident, but the sources differ on when: the association says he was killed in December 1938 on a trip to Norfolk to buy Christmas presents, while Wikipedia, citing the Dictionary of North Carolina Biography, says he was injured late in 1937 and died on 9 February 1938.[^4][^29] Levene Midgett took over as captain and stayed until the station was decommissioned in May 1954.[^4][^3]

The buildings were deeded to Mrs. Lurania Midgett on 5 December 1967, and the General Services Administration disposed of them in 1968, according to the U.S. Life-Saving Service Heritage Association.[^3] The Chicamacomico Historical Association was founded in 1974 at the direction of Woodrow Edwards, an artist and community activist, the author David Stick and community activist Carolista Baum,[^30] and the station was added to the National Register of Historic Places on 12 December 1976.[^1] The 1874 and 1911 houses have been restored as museums,[^3] though the association's own page lists total restoration of the 1874 house among its plans.[^30]

## Today

The station is a museum run by the Chicamacomico Historical Association, a private non-profit that owns and operates the site and the museum shop, at 23645 N.C. Highway 12 in Rodanthe.[^30][^4] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists it as open to the public,[^31] and the association's page mentions admission fees,[^30] so check its current hours and prices before you go. The National Park Service describes two station houses and five outbuildings, including cook houses, a stable and a beehive cistern, with period rescue equipment and the surfboat, No. 1046, used in the Mirlo rescue.[^2] Wikipedia says the beach apparatus drill is re-enacted every Thursday in the summer season, with the Lyle gun fired and a live "victim" rescued from a simulated wreck; check with the association for current drill days.[^1]
