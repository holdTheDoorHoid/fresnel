Dunkirk Light stands on Point Gratiot, a bluff on the Lake Erie shore of Chautauqua County, New York. A lighthouse has been here since the 1820s, and the third-order Fresnel lens in the present tower is still shown to the lake from the same point.[^1][^2] The red-brick keeper's house beside it, built in the High Victorian Gothic style, is now a veterans' museum.[^2][^3]

## The place

Dunkirk lies on Lake Erie's south shore. According to LighthouseFriends, the town came close in the 1820s to being the western end of the Erie Canal, which went to Buffalo instead, and it was for a time an important port as the western terminus of the Erie Railroad.[^1] The light sits on a bluff about twenty feet high at Point Gratiot, just west of the town.[^1]

The land was a gift. Walter Smith, a Dunkirk citizen, gave the ground for the first lighthouse, and a local man named Jesse Peck was its contractor; the tower was finished in 1826.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's archive dates the station to 1827, the year its first keeper appears in the keeper registers.[^2]

## Building the light

Smith also made a ten-year arrangement with the federal government to provide a keeper and supplies for the light, and he tried to burn natural gas in it. The gas came from Fredonia, two miles away and some 150 feet higher; according to LighthouseFriends the lighter-than-air gas would not travel down the wooden pipeline, so the lamps burned whale oil instead.[^1]

The light was darkened for a time in 1838, on the thought that a pierhead beacon built at the harbour mouth the year before was enough. LighthouseFriends reports that the decision was soon questioned: the tower was the most easily seen light on Lake Erie's south shore and, paired with the beacon, gave a range for a difficult stretch of the channel. The light was restored.[^1] In 1857 the station received a new lantern and a third-order Fresnel lens made in Paris by Henry-Lepaute; the fixed lens was fitted with three panels on chariot wheels that circled it to make a flash.[^1]

By 1868 the Light-House Board described the station as in poor condition. As LighthouseFriends quotes the report, "The main tower is cracked," and the beacon tower leaked. Repairs followed, but by 1874 the board warned that "large sections of the outer shell may fall off at any moment," and asked for $15,000 to rebuild.[^1] Money came in March 1875. A temporary tower with a sixth-order lens went up in April, the old tower was gone by 2 June, and ground was broken for the new tower and dwelling on 16 June.[^1]

Sources disagree on the date of the new light. LighthouseFriends says it was first shown on 1 July 1876; Rowlett's Lighthouse Directory lists the tower as 1875, and the US Lighthouse Society's archive gives 1876 for the tower and 1875 for the keeper's quarters.[^1][^2][^4] They also disagree on what the tower is made of: LighthouseFriends calls it limestone, the archive records rubblestone encased in brick, and Rowlett says its brick veneer surrounds an older rubblestone tower.[^1][^2][^4] It is square, 61 feet (18.5 m) tall, with the light 82 feet (25 m) above the water.[^2][^4] The archive dates the oil house to 1875 as well.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists nineteen terms as head keeper between 1827 and 1961, two of them second stints by the same men, and nine assistant stints between 1867 and 1928.[^2] Walter Smith kept the light himself until 1831. His successor, Abraham Day, came with a story that LighthouseFriends tells: as a boy he had been a drummer in Andrew Jackson's army, and Jackson brought him home to the Hermitage and raised him. Two years into Jackson's presidency Day asked for a lighthouse post. Barcelona and Buffalo were considered first, but popular veterans already served there, so Dunkirk was chosen.[^1] He stayed ten years, to 1841.[^2]

Later keepers had shorter and longer stays. John J. Cassity served two separate terms, 1845 to 1849 and 1853 to 1861.[^2] Benjamin F. Averill, keeper from 1873 to 1884, was challenged when the vessel *Yosemite* ran onto a shoal in 1873; its captain said the light was not burning that morning, and Averill insisted that it was and, according to LighthouseFriends, had four witnesses.[^1][^2] On 1 May 1885 his successor, as LighthouseFriends quotes the station log, wrote that he "would Resign," giving a cause that the keeper wrote as "Drunkness." The archive's roster, however, shows Robert T. Taggert (spelled so there) as keeper until 1887.[^1][^2]

Peter J. Dempsey was an assistant from 1885 and then head keeper from 1887 to 1902.[^2] John T. McDonough followed him, after serving as assistant from 1888, and kept the light until 1928. Francis D. Arnold then moved up from assistant, a post he had held since 1909, and kept the light until 1950.[^2] William R. Gannon (1950 to 1956) and Arthur G. Bauman (1957 to 1961) are the last keepers the roster names.[^2]

LighthouseFriends ties the assistant's post to the harbour: the keepers also tended the pierhead light, reached by an elevated walkway about 900 feet (275 m) long, and that, it says, is why an assistant was assigned.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Storms damaged the pierhead light at the harbour more than once in the 1890s. The Light-House Board reported that the winter gales of 1889 and 1890 had broken part of the pier that carried it.[^5] In the winter of 1895 the beacon was lost altogether. The report for the year ended 30 June 1896 says that "the foundation crib of this beacon was wrecked and the beacon carried away" in a severe storm on 31 December 1895, while a table in the same volume gives the date as 12 December.[^6][^7] A temporary wooden beacon was put up on the end of the west breakwater and shown from the opening of navigation in 1896.[^6] A new foundation pier followed in 1899 and an enclosed pierhead beacon in 1900.[^8][^9] In 1901 the board noted that the pierhead beacon "was twice repaired where damaged by storms."[^10]

Wrecks come into the station's story from LighthouseFriends. The steamer *Erie* burned in the waters near Dunkirk in 1841 with a loss of around 150 lives, most of them German and Swiss immigrants.[^1] The *Dean Richmond*, a wooden cargo ship, sank near Dunkirk in 1893 while trying to enter the harbour; flour from her washed ashore, and keeper Dempsey's family dried several sacks of it and baked bread and cakes.[^1]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1928 recorded that Arnold, with the help of his son, brought ashore on 23 July a man who had fallen over the cliff near the station and was badly hurt.[^11]

## After the keepers

The light was electrified in 1923, and electricity reached the harbour beacon in 1935; with that the assistant keeper's post was dropped and the assistant's house was leased to local families. During the Second World War and for a short time afterwards Coast Guard personnel lived in the dwelling, and the assistant's house was later demolished.[^1] The station's barn was destroyed by fire and rebuilt at a cost of $1,757, in the work reported for the 1922 fiscal year.[^12]

The date the keepers left is given differently: the archive's roster ends in 1961, while LighthouseFriends and Rowlett put the end of resident keepers around 1962.[^1][^2][^4] After that the Coast Guard kept up the light but the empty dwelling began to decay, until a group of veterans and citizens signed a 25-year lease on the property and made it a memorial park and museum.[^1][^4] In July 2000, at the light's 125th anniversary, ownership of the tower passed to the local preservation society.[^4]

## Today

The station is run by the Dunkirk Lighthouse and Veterans Park Museum, which describes the light as both a working light and a museum.[^4][^13] The keeper's house holds the museum. The grounds also hold several lights moved here, among them a Buffalo "bottle" light and the skeleton tower that stood at the Dunkirk harbour entrance from 1939.[^1][^4] The grounds, dwelling and tower are open in season, May through October, with shorter hours outside July and August; the museum's telephone number is the way to confirm times.[^1][^4]

Storm waves in the summer of 2010 exposed the foundations of the 1827 lighthouse, and an archaeological dig followed that year.[^1][^4] The bluff is still eroding; in 2026, Rowlett reports, the museum was seeking a $50,000 grant for a study of how to control it.[^4]
