Lamb Island Lighthouse stands on a small island off the western entrance to Nipigon Bay, on the north shore of Lake Superior. It was among the first lighthouses built on the lake, put in operation on 15 May 1877, and it is still active.[^1][^2][^6]

## The place

Nipigon Bay is protected by a string of islands. Of five ship channels leading into the bay from Lake Superior, only two are well suited to large vessels.[^1] The entrance used by vessels from the west "is bounded by Fluor Island and Lamb Island."[^1] The heritage statement says the light was "a coastal light for the large vessels going in and out of Thunder Bay," and that the lights of the 1870s were built to serve marine traffic "caused by the construction of the Canadian Pacific Railway."[^2]

The survey section of the Department's annual report for 1907–08 says that about 15 May Captain Fred Anderson, with the steamer Bayfield, resumed operations on Lake Superior, working on the outside coast between Lamb Island and Jackfish Bay. The work made "very fair progress, but was troubled by fog." That passage describes the coast near the island, not the light itself.[^3]

## Building the light

In 1875 the Department of Marine contracted Joseph White to build lights on Battle Island and Lamb Island.[^1] The work was expected to finish in 1876, but "unexpected difficulties encountered by the contractor" delayed the lights until 1877. On 7 August 1876 the superintendent of lights above Montreal, Darius Smith, landed a lantern room on Lamb Island, with other supplies needed to finish the lighthouse.[^1]

LighthouseFriends quotes the Department's report for 1877, which describes a square tower 14 m (46 ft) high from base of structure to vane of lantern. The light, the report says, "should be seen in clear weather from a distance of 17 miles," and "This lighthouse was put in operation on the 15th May last."[^1] The two lights together cost $9,405.26, according to the same report.[^1]

Due to continued leaks, the lighthouse was in a "bad state of repair" in 1896, and it was "practically rebuilt at a cost of $818.46."[^1]

The Department's report for 1909–10 records the change of lens. "The catoptric light heretofore shown has been replaced by a 4th order 360° French lens and a 35 mm. Diamond vapour installation with adjustable iron stand."[^4]

## The keepers

John Michaelson was appointed keeper on 7 September 1876, at $300 a year.[^1] The inspection report of 1878, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, noted that "John Michelson is the keeper; he has a family of ten." The name is spelled two ways in the sources, and this story uses the spelling of the appointment entry.[^1]

LighthouseFriends prints a roster that runs from Michaelson in 1877 to Charles Lorne Gibson, whose service is given as at least 1975 to 1989.[^1] Andrew Alexander has the longest stretch in that roster, from 1897 to 1931.[^1] Government tables also place him at the station. The 1906–07 table lists him at Lamb Island, appointed 26 April 1897 at $500.[^7] The 1909–10 salary table lists Andrew Alexander at Lamb Island at $555 a year.[^4] The 1911–12 table lists him at the same station, appointed 26 April 1897, at $680.[^5]

Arthur J. Newman took charge of the light in 1937.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that he "died of a heart attack at Lamb Island Lighthouse during a storm on November 28, 1951."[^1] A small forestry department aircraft landed his son, Arthur Newman Jr., on the island the next day, and LighthouseFriends says he kept the light for the rest of the season.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The one storm the sources read for this station that ties to the light is the storm of November 1951 in which Arthur Newman died, described above. The sources read here name no shipwreck tied to the light.[^1]

## After the keepers

On 20 September 1928 an air diaphone began operation "in a building nearer the shore of Lamb Island."[^1] In 1930 the light was changed from fixed white to a flash every 25 seconds.[^1]

The original lighthouse was replaced in 1961. "A forty-foot-tall, square, skeletal tower replaced the original lighthouse in 1961," and a new keeper's dwelling was built on the island that year.[^1][^6] The heritage statement says that until the light was de-staffed in 1990, its staff "was on hand to provide assistance to any mariners in distress."[^2] The Lighthouse Directory says the 2-storey wood keeper's house and several other buildings remain of the original light station.[^6] The light was designated a heritage lighthouse in 2015.[^1][^6]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse. Grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1] The island is accessible only by boat.[^6] The light shows a white flash every five seconds from a focal plane of 30 m (99 ft).[^6] The Lighthouse Directory describes the tower as a 12 m (40 ft) square pyramidal steel skeletal tower.[^6]
