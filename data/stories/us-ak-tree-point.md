Tree Point Light stands on the Southeast Alaska mainland, on the east side of the southern entrance to the Revillagigedo Channel, about four and a quarter miles north of Cape Fox.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls it the only lighthouse built on mainland Alaska, and the National Register nomination ties the station to the Klondike gold rush, which brought Congress to fund lights along the busy Inside Passage route.[^1][^12] Its concrete Art Deco building of 1935 carries the lantern house built for the first tower of 1904, and the station has been an automated minor light since 1969.[^1]

## The place

The station was meant to mark the way in from the open Pacific through the Dixon Entrance and into the Revillagigedo Channel.[^1] The National Register nomination describes the water around it: treacherous rocks lie to the south, near Cape Fox, the Lord Islands and Port Tongass, while to the west the channel is deep.[^1] The same document gives the only wreck it found nearby, the steam tug *Vigilant*, which ran into the rocks at Cape Fox in August 1903 when the helmsman fell asleep, and it says that no major wrecks or rescues were identified for the station itself.[^1]

The point had people before it had a light. The nomination places a major Tongass Tlingit village about eight miles from the station, east of Cape Fox, and reports shell midden near the boathouse that indicates Native American use of the spot.[^1] An executive order of 4 January 1901 set aside 679 acres on the point as a lighthouse reservation, according to LighthouseFriends.[^12]

The reason for the money was the gold rush. The nomination says that the Lighthouse Board had pressed Congress from 1890 for a light and fog-signal station at the southern end of the Tongass Narrows (the nomination names Mary Island) and was ignored until the Klondike brought attention to Alaska and the number of wrecks climbed.[^1] In its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901, the Board recorded that in March 1901 it had decided to continue establishing stations in Alaskan waters in a set order; Tree Point, southeastern Alaska, was on that list at an estimated $36,213.[^3]

## Building the light

The Lighthouse Board approved construction on 24 April 1903, according to LighthouseFriends.[^12] The nomination says the original station was built over two seasons, 1903 and 1904, and lit on 30 April 1904.[^1] The Ketchikan *Mining Journal* of 9 April 1904, quoted in the nomination, reported that the lighthouse was "now ready for the installation of the light."[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's archive dates the station's establishment to 1903, which fits the start of construction.[^2] LighthouseFriends says a small fire damaged the lighthouse two weeks after it was first lit and put it out of service briefly.[^12]

The first station was an eight-sided fog-signal and engine building with the light tower rising through its middle, plus three residences, two oil houses, a barn, a dam and water supply, and a boathouse with a tramway.[^1] The lantern was built for a third-order Fresnel lens and showed a fixed white light 86 feet 6 inches (26 m) above mean tide.[^1] The nomination says the light had an 11-degree red sector to the south, covering the Lord Rocks; LighthouseFriends instead says the red sector was added on 1 October 1906.[^1][^12] The fog signal was an oil-burning air siren that gave a 3-second blast followed by 27 seconds of silence.[^1]

The keepers' houses were not beside the tower. Unlike most other Alaskan stations, the nomination says, the topography put the housing compound some distance from the light, behind a knoll, and a tramway about 2,300 feet (700 m) long linked the buildings.[^1] The water supply gave trouble. The nomination notes that the original dam and piping apparently did not meet the station's needs, and the Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for 1918 said the water was subject to interruption through freezing and "not considered healthful," proposing a pipe line to a lake about a mile away.[^1][^4]

The lens changed in the years that followed. The nomination says a fourth-order Fresnel lens made by Henry LaPaute of Paris, in an occulting unit, replaced the third-order lens sometime between 1910 and 1918, while LighthouseFriends gives 1913 to 1918.[^1][^12] A 1919 issue of the Lighthouse Service Bulletin separately lists Tree Point among lights whose illuminant had been changed from oil to acetylene, and from fixed white to flashing white.[^6] The sources do not say how these changes fit together, so the history of the optic between 1910 and 1919 stays unsettled here.

By the 1930s the wooden buildings were failing. The Lighthouse Service reported in 1937 that the main wooden building at Tree Point had been beyond economical repair and was replaced in 1935 by a reinforced-concrete building.[^11] The nomination says Edward W. Laird designed it under Assistant District Superintendent Dwight W. Chase, though it adds that records suggest Chase had more of a hand in the inspiration than Laird recognized; it counts the building among six replacement light and fog-signal buildings that Laird designed or supervised in Alaska between 1931 and 1940.[^1] LighthouseFriends says excavation began in November 1933, that a wooden trestle was built so the old lantern room could be slid across to the new tower, and that the finished building cost $47,481.[^12] The nomination agrees that the lantern house on the 1935 tower is the one installed on the 1904 tower.[^1] The 1937 report adds that the landing, exposed to a swell through Dixon Entrance, was improved by a wharf and hoisting gear in place of an inclined tram.[^11]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists keepers at Tree Point from 1904 until the Coast Guard left in 1969, under head keeper, first assistant and second assistant.[^2] The first assistant in 1904 was Edward J. Lawrence, and the second was Charles B. Bohm.[^2] The archive's line for the first head keeper, Jacob C. La Bryn, is garbled ("1000-1000"); LighthouseFriends gives 1904 to 1908.[^2][^12]

Tree Point was one of the larger stations, with three family residences, and the nomination says it had a school whose teacher was the wife of one of the keepers.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that in the 1930s the Territory of Alaska provided a teacher for the children there.[^12]

John C. Johnson came up through the ranks at the station: the archive shows him as second assistant in 1910, first assistant from 1910 to 1914, and head keeper from 1915 to 1922.[^2] In the Commissioner of Lighthouses' list of services for 1922, Johnson and his assistant Baard J. Lervick are credited with towing the disabled gas boat *Ray*, with two men aboard, into harbor.[^5] Lervick had himself risen from second assistant (1917) to first assistant (1918) and became head keeper in 1922.[^2] The Bulletin's list of efficiency flags for 1922, awarded in each district to the station showing the highest general efficiency, includes Tree Point, and its index names Johnson as the keeper when it records the award.[^7][^8]

In a Bulletin issue of 1923, the Lighthouse Service described a message that came on 4 September to the Ketchikan radio station by way of the Canadian station on Digby Island, near Prince Rupert, asking that a boat be sent to Tree Point. The wife of the second assistant keeper was very ill and needed treatment in Ketchikan. The weather was stormy and the station's power boat could not be used; the *Fern*, which had been in port and was sent to Tree Point, could not land, anchored for the night, and took her aboard the next morning when the sea had gone down.[^9] The Bulletin noted that the station had no radio equipment but that the second assistant had rigged up some apparatus of his own, which is how he reached Digby Island.[^9] It does not name the family.

In April 1936 Ray I. Loney, the head keeper, and assistants August Waltenberg and Joseph Washburn were credited by the Lighthouse Service with helping to find a lost plane. Having heard radio reports of a plane missing between Prince Rupert and Ketchikan, they reported the exact time and direction of a plane flying over the station, which let a pilot and a Coast Guard cutter find the aircraft on 12 April 1936. The pilot and his wife were found alive after six days of severe cold and no food.[^10] The archive lists Loney as first assistant in 1929 and 1930 and head keeper from 1931 to 1937.[^2]

## After the keepers

Authority over the lighthouses passed to the Coast Guard in 1939.[^1] The archive lists Coast Guard crews at Tree Point from 1943.[^2] According to the nomination, the fog horns were replaced by electric Supertyfon emitters in 1962, and when the station was converted to alternating current in 1963 the fourth-order Fresnel lens was removed.[^1] By 1960 only four Coast Guardsmen lived at the station, in a single house, and two residences were declared surplus and demolished, one in 1960 and one in 1963.[^1]

In 1969 the light station was disestablished and automated, and a 250 mm electronic lens on the outside of the lantern replaced the old optic; the nomination says this reduced the light's visibility from 15 to 9 miles and classed it as a minor light.[^1] Solar power replaced generators in 1987.[^1] In 1974 the Coast Guard let a contract to demolish everything except the light and fog-signal building. The contractor defaulted, and the nomination credits that default with leaving the old compound largely standing.[^1] The National Park Service's National Register record for the station (reference 04001177) is dated 27 October 2004.[^13]

## Today

Tree Point is owned by the Coast Guard, and LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds, dwelling and tower are closed.[^12] LighthouseFriends describes landing by boat or helicopter as a trek across big boulders.[^12] The station is closed to visitors. As of the 2004 nomination, the removed fourth-order lens was on display in Ketchikan; the nomination places it in the Ketchikan museum, called the Tongass Historical Museum.[^1] The nomination records that the original octagonal building was demolished, but its foundation remains beside the 1935 building, along with one keeper's house, the two original oil houses, the boathouse and stretches of the tramway.[^1]
