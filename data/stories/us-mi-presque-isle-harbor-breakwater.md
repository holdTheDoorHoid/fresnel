The Presque Isle Harbor Breakwater Light stands at the outer end of a breakwater on Lake Superior, at the upper harbor of Marquette, in Marquette County, Michigan.[^1] It was established in 1941 and now runs as an automated light, and its red flash can be seen 11 miles (about 9.6 nautical miles) in clear weather.[^1][^2] The 1938 plan had called for an unattended light.[^7]

## The place

The breakwater runs southeast from the shore of Presque Isle, a city park at Marquette. It shelters Presque Isle Harbor, which the nomination also calls the Upper Harbor of the city.[^3] Its job was to shelter the harbor from the rough water of Lake Superior.[^2] Congress appropriated money in 1896 for the Army Corps of Engineers to build it, and the first 1,216 feet (371 m) were complete by about 1900.[^2][^3] The outer end sits about 0.4 mile (0.6 km) from shore, in roughly 25 feet (8 m) of water.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1935 the Corps began a second project. It dredged the port and added a 1,600-foot (488 m) riprap extension that gave the breakwater a dogleg bend.[^2] The extension ended atop a hazardous shoal.[^2] The Polaris Concrete Products Company of Duluth won the contract at $596,000, and the work was completed in 1939.[^2]

In 1938 the Lighthouse Service announced an unattended light for the end of the extension. The same plan called for a new keeper's dwelling and boathouse at Marquette.[^7] The Lighthouse Service's 1939 transfer to the Coast Guard carried the plan forward, and the light was officially established as a federal aid to navigation in 1941.[^2]

The Lake Carriers' Association annual report for 1940 described the planned light before it was built. As LighthouseFriends quotes it, the planned light was a red flasher of 3,000 candlepower on an enclosed steel tower, with a diaphone fog signal that was to sound two blasts every 20 seconds.[^8]

The light is built on a rectangular concrete pier, with an octagonal concrete first story and a three-story steel tower with a cupola on top.[^1][^4] The nomination calls its design Streamlined Moderne, and it is painted white with a red band on the tower.[^1] The whole light is about 55 feet (17 m) from the water to the top of its tower. The steel tower alone is about 30 feet (9 m) tall.[^1][^4]

## The keepers

The sources name no keepers for this light. The USLHS archive's record for it lists no keepers and says there are no keeper's quarters at the station.[^9] The nomination says Coast Guard personnel based at the Marquette Harbor Lighthouse maintained the light until it was automated, around the 1970s.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the light was run by remote control from that station, two miles away.[^7] That harbor station was occupied by Lighthouse Service keepers and later by Coast Guard crews.[^14]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources record no storm damage to the light itself. The breakwater is dangerous in high waves, a monument there warns.[^7] On 4 October 1988, a large wave swept Shane C. Kulchyski, 18, off the breakwater into a harbor of Lake Superior. His friend Kurt A. Buffington, also 18, took off his jacket and boots, ran out onto the breakwater and dived into the 47-degree water to reach him.[^10] Both men went under and did not resurface. They were recovered from the lake the next day, and both had died.[^10] The Mining Journal, reprinting a notice from 30 years earlier, says the two men were walking on the breakwater during a storm when the wave struck.[^11]

## After the keepers

The light was automated around the 1970s.[^6] Its equipment has changed since 1941. The nomination says the original beacon, the diaphone fog signal and a radiobeacon have been removed. A modern optic, an electric horn fog signal and batteries recharged by a solar array have replaced them.[^12] The current optic is a light-emitting diode (LED) marine beacon that signals a red flash every four seconds.[^5] The light was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 2016.[^7] The nomination places it among the second-quarter twentieth-century lighthouses of concrete and steel built for piers and breakwaters.[^13]

## Today

The U.S. Coast Guard owns the light and runs it as an automated aid marking the breakwater's end.[^1] The breakwater can be walked to the light, but the light is not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^7] A monument near the breakwater warns people not to walk the breakwall when the waves are high. Northern Michigan University students and the two men's families and friends gave it as a warning to others.[^7]
