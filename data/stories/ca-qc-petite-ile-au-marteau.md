Petite Île au Marteau Light stands on a small island in the Mingan Archipelago, on Quebec's Côte-Nord, 4.3 km from the port of Havre-Saint-Pierre.[^1] Its first light was a wooden lighthouse, lit in August 1915. Its successors were a concrete tower of 1954–55 and a steel skeleton tower of 1976, and the island now lies inside Mingan Archipelago National Park Reserve.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

## The place

The Quebec heritage record says the site was chosen to help local navigation, which was made up mainly of fishing boats, and that the light marks the entrance to the port of Havre-Saint-Pierre.[^1] The record notes that the waters of the archipelago were known for rich fishing banks but were also hard to navigate (translated from the French by us).[^1] A light had stood earlier in the area. LighthouseFriends says the first lighthouse to serve mariners there was built in 1888 on Île aux Perroquets, the westernmost island of the group.[^3] Early light lists called Petite Île au Marteau "Walrus Island".[^3]

## Building the light

The Department of Marine and Fisheries built the first light in 1915.[^1][^6] Its annual report for the fiscal year 1915–16 lists "Ile au Marteau" among the new aids in Quebec, with an entry for "Erection lighthouse, dwelling, oil".[^6] LighthouseFriends says that S. Menier was paid $3,573 that year for a square dwelling, topped at its centre by a square lantern, and for an oil shed and a boathouse.[^3][^6] The Quebec record says the first light carried a modest 5th-order lens.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Charles Boudreau was hired as keeper, and that the light was first lit on 11 August 1915.[^3]

A fog-alarm building followed. The Quebec record says it was built in 1917, to make up for the light's weakness in bad weather or fog.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates it to 1917 and says it housed a diaphone horn.[^3] The Department's annual report for the fiscal year 1918–19 says new fog-alarm plants were established that year, at Ile au Marteau among other stations.[^7] The sources disagree about the year.

In the fiscal year 1922–23 the Department had two oil storage tanks installed at Isle au Marteau.[^8]

The 1950s brought a change. The Quebec record says mining on the Côte-Nord brought a large rise in shipping, and that the Montreal Shipping Company Limited complained of the light's lack of visibility. The Department answered by building a new, more powerful light on the island, in 1954–55.[^1] That tower was an octagonal reinforced-concrete tower, 12 m (39 ft) high, according to the Quebec record.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends gives its height as 38 feet and says Gulf Maritime Construction Ltd. built it.[^3]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends names five keepers, one after another: Charles Boudreau (1915 to 1933), Grégoire Cyr (1933 to 1955), Léonard Cyr (1955 to 1956), Vital Boudreau (1956 to 1965) and Rosaire Boudreau (1965 to 1987).[^3] The sources do not say which of them was head keeper and which an assistant.

In the early years keepers and their families reached the island over the ice in spring, with dogs and horses carrying the supplies.[^3] Until a separate dwelling was built in 1952, the two keepers and a schoolteacher all lived in the 1915 lighthouse.[^3] The Quebec record says the 1952 house was built for the assistant keeper and his family.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it was for the head keeper.[^3]

From 1934 to 1949 six schoolteachers lived on the island to teach the keepers' children. Lessons were held in a workshop in the fog-alarm building, and were cancelled in fog because the engines were too noisy.[^3]

Before the light was automated, keepers had to light the lamps an hour before sunset.[^3] LighthouseFriends records how one keeper taught his son to judge when to light the lamp: a hand held at arm's length between the sun and the horizon, with four fingers' width fitting in the gap, meant it was time, and each finger's width stood for fifteen minutes.[^3] Until radio reached the island in the 1950s, the station and the mainland signalled with flags. A pink or a blue flag, raised on the mainland, passed on news of a keeper's wife giving birth at the hospital and whether the baby was a girl or a boy.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The fog alarm was built to make up for the light's weakness in bad weather or fog, as the Quebec record describes.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that the light and foghorn here worked with three lights and fog alarms built on the north coast of Anticosti in 1918 and 1919, to guide ships through Jacques Cartier Strait.[^3] Parks Canada says the lighthouse "has prevented many shipwreck since 1915".[^4] No particular wreck or rescue at the island is named in the sources cited here.

## After the keepers

In 1965 a new keeper's dwelling was built and the old one demolished, and two electrical generators were installed in the fog-alarm building that year.[^3] The Quebec record says the keeper's garden and the first light disappeared in 1965.[^1]

In 1976 a steel skeleton tower was erected to carry the light, and the concrete tower was left standing.[^3] The Quebec record ties the 1976 steel structure to the complete automation of the light.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the light was automated in the early 1980s, and that the last resident keepers left the island in 1987.[^3] The sources differ on when the automation was complete.

## Today

The Coast Guard list gives the present light as a white flash every 2.5 seconds, from a 25.3 m (83 ft) skeleton tower, with a focal height of 28.9 m (95 ft) and a range of 12 nautical miles. It marks the light as seasonal.[^5] Routes des phares gives the present light as a 12 m concrete tower of 1954, which does not match the Coast Guard list or LighthouseFriends.[^9]

Parks Canada says the island is part of Mingan Archipelago National Park Reserve. A hiking trail leads to the lighthouse, with a lookout over the cliffs of Grosse île au Marteau. Parks Canada asks walkers to keep to established trails or to shoreline without vegetation, warns that rocks fall near the cliffs, and notes that the rocks are slippery at low tide.[^4] LighthouseFriends names a boat operator for trips to the island, and says the Canadian Coast Guard owns the light: the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^3]

Under Canada's Economic Action Plan, the exteriors of all the station's buildings and the interiors of the newer keeper's house and the foghorn building were restored. LighthouseFriends says completion was announced in October 2010.[^3]
