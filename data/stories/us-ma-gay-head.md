At the western tip of Martha's Vineyard, where the island ends in a wall of coloured clay, a brick tower has marked a dangerous passage since 1856, and a lighthouse of one kind or another has stood here since 1799. By the 2010s the eroding cliff was less than fifty feet (15 m) from its base, and in 2015 the tower was moved inland.[^1][^2]

## The place

The cliffs gave the headland its name. LighthouseFriends reports that the explorer Bartholomew Gosnold sailed past in 1602 and called them Dover Cliffs, and that by the 1660s the place was known as Gay Head for their colours. The Wampanoag people have lived here for thousands of years, and in 1998 the town changed its name to Aquinnah, which that site translates as "end of the island".[^3]

The hazard is offshore. Between Gay Head and the Elizabeth Islands lies Devil's Bridge, a submerged obstruction that runs out from the headland into the channel that ships use through Vineyard Sound.[^3] The first lighthouse was requested in 1796 by a Massachusetts state senator, according to LighthouseFriends; the lighthouse's own history page says Peleg Coffin of Nantucket wrote to his congressman, at a time when whaling had increased the traffic past both islands. The two accounts also differ on the money: LighthouseFriends gives $5,750 approved by Congress in 1798, the lighthouse's history page gives $570.[^3][^4]

## Building the light

The first tower was an octagonal wooden structure on a stone base, with a wooden keeper's house, a barn and an oil store.[^3] The lighthouse's history page says the contract went to Martin Lincoln of Hingham, and the archive of the United States Lighthouse Society lists Lincoln as a contractor for the station.[^4][^12] The date of the first lighting is not settled: LighthouseFriends gives 18 November 1799, the lighthouse's history page 7 November 1799.[^3][^4]

The tower was altered more than once. LighthouseFriends reports that early in the nineteenth century it was lowered by 14 feet (4.3 m) to keep the light out of the fog, that a local blacksmith rebuilt the lantern in 1838 and the tower was lowered another 3 feet (0.9 m), and that in 1844 the tower was moved back about 75 feet (23 m) from the eroding bluff.[^3] The lighthouse's history page names the mover as John Mayhew of Edgartown, at a cost of $386.87.[^4]

In 1842 the engineer I. W. P. Lewis described the station as "decayed in several places". His report included a statement from the keeper, Ellis Skiff, who said his house was "likely to be blown down" and "shakes fearfully with every gale of wind", and that with no cellar the oil was kept in the wooden tower, where in winter he was "often obliged to cut the oil out of the butts solid" and warm it before use. The distance from house to tower was 64 feet (20 m), and in northwest snowstorms, he said, getting between them was difficult (as quoted by LighthouseFriends).[^3]

The replacement came after the Lighthouse Board took over in 1852. LighthouseFriends reports that $30,000 was budgeted in 1854 for a new tower with a first-order lens; the lighthouse's history page says Congress approved the sum that August.[^3][^4] Caleb King is the contractor named by both, and the Society's archive lists him too; the tower is a 51-foot (15.5 m) conical brick tower, which the history page puts at 52 feet.[^3][^4][^12] The date of the tower is itself a small puzzle. The National Register nomination's chronology and the Society's archive give 1856, while a technical amendment to the nomination in 2015 says the tower was built in 1854.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends reconciles the two by giving the lighting date: the lens was put into operation on 1 December 1856.[^3]

The lens was made by the Parisian firm of Henry-Lepaute, and held 1,008 prisms; clockwork that had to be wound every four hours turned it, and its twenty-four flash panels gave a flash every ten seconds.[^3] Ruby-coloured glass added in May 1874 made every fourth flash red.[^3] A visitor who climbed to the gallery and described the scene for *Harper's Magazine* saw "bars of misty light, revolving majestically on the axis of the tower", clouds and stars visible through them (as quoted by LighthouseFriends).[^3]

## The keepers

Ebenezer Skiff kept the light from 1799 to 1828, and his son Ellis took over in 1828 and stayed until 1846.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends calls Ebenezer the first European to live in the town of Gay Head.[^3] His own words survive in a letter of 25 October 1805 to the Secretary of the Treasury, Albert Gallatin, asking for more pay. Clay and ochre, he wrote, "ascend in a Sheet of wind" up the cliff and "catch on the light House Glass", which had to be cleaned on the outside in cold weather, and he had carted "almost the whole of the water used in my family" from nearly a mile away. His salary went from $200 to $250 (as quoted by LighthouseFriends).[^3]

After the Skiffs, the archive of the United States Lighthouse Society lists Samuel H. Flanders (1846-1849 and again 1853-1861), Henry Robinson, Ichabod N. Luce and Calvin C. Adams, and then Horatio N. Pease, who held the station from 1869 to 1890.[^1] Among the assistants, the same archive records Lydia A. Adams as first assistant from 1869 to 1871.[^12]

One keeper's household is recorded in unusual detail. Crosby L. Crocker, assistant from 1886 and head keeper from 1892 to 1920, led a household that, LighthouseFriends says, lost four children in fifteen months. That site and the National Register amendment both attribute deaths at the station to damp in the brick dwelling; the National Register amendment cites a recent keeper's personal communication for this.[^12][^1][^3][^2] The Lighthouse Board had said as much in its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899: the brick double dwelling's cellars and lower rooms were so damp that "mold and mildew gather" on the walls and on household articles, and the house was "too damp and unsanitary" to live in. The Board recommended $6,500 to rebuild it.[^5] A wooden double dwelling replaced the brick one in 1902 according to the Society's archive, the National Register amendment and the lighthouse's history page; the nomination's original chronology gives 1906.[^1][^2][^4]

Wampanoag keepers served at Gay Head in the early twentieth century. According to the Coast Guard Museum, Leonard Vanderhoop, who had helped in the 1884 rescue described below, became assistant keeper in 1892.[^9] Charles W. Vanderhoop was assistant from 1913 to 1919 and head keeper from 1920 to 1933, with Solomon M. "Max" Attaquin as his assistant until 1935 by the archive's dates.[^12][^1] The Coast Guard Museum says that in 1919 Vanderhoop was made keeper of Sankaty Head Light on Nantucket, the first Native American appointed to oversee a federal installation, and that in 1920 he and Attaquin were appointed to Gay Head.[^9] Visitors were part of the job: LighthouseFriends reports 2,600 people signing the register in 1922 and 3,614 in 1929, and says Vanderhoop was retired on disability in 1933, a newspaper attributing it to what it called "visitor-itis".[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The steamer *City of Columbus* was wrecked on Devil's Bridge in January 1884. The Portland *Daily Press* of 21 January 1884 said the vessel "ran upon the rocks known as Devil's Bridge", put the toll at ninety-seven lives in its latest reports, and reported fifteen survivors arriving in Boston on Saturday 19 January. A survivor, John L. Cook, told the paper he had never seen a more cheerful company than the one that embarked on the steamer on the Thursday afternoon.[^6] The date of the wreck itself is not settled: LighthouseFriends gives 19 January at 3:45 a.m., while the newspaper has the steamer embarking on a Thursday and survivors reaching Boston on Saturday 19 January, which does not fit a wreck on the 19th.[^3][^6]

On shore, the response was partly the work of Gay Head's own people. The Coast Guard Museum describes Aquinnah Wampanoag volunteers of the Massachusetts Humane Society launching a surfboat, which capsized on the first attempt and again on the return trip with survivors, but brought everyone ashore.[^9] The *Daily Kennebec Journal* recorded that "a single boat with several Indians in it" came off from Gay Head but could not approach the wreck, and the Portland paper of 22 January described a life boat manned by Indians that was conveying nine bodies and the remaining five survivors.[^8][^7] The Portland paper of the 21st wrote that too much could not be said of the Indians who made up the Humane Society's volunteer crew.[^6]

The lighthouse itself became a refuge. The Portland *Daily Press* found the steamer's purser "in the residence of the lighthouse keeper, his head done up in bandages", and the same paper quoted keeper Horatio M. Pease as doubting that the buoy had shifted.[^6] Communication with Gay Head was cut off at first; the paper noted that "for the first time since the disaster" communication had been held with it, and a tug chartered by correspondents found the sea too high to approach the wharf.[^6] Bodies that people of Gay Head had picked up along the beach were reported to have been placed at the meeting house, a "dilapidated and storm-beaten" church.[^6]

The Life-Saving Service's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1895 recorded that a station had been built and put in operation at Gay Head.[^11]

## After the keepers

The first-order lens left the tower in 1952, after the light was electrified, and went on display at the Martha's Vineyard Historical Society's museum grounds in Edgartown, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] The National Register nomination's chronology puts the replacement of the Fresnel lens by 22-inch lenses in 1961, after the keeper's house was razed.[^2] The same chronology says the light "changed to untended operation" in 1956, while the Society's archive gives 1960 for automation, so the sources disagree on when the last keeper left, though the roster ends in 1956.[^2][^1][^12] The lighthouse's history page says the keeper's cottage was torn down by 1956, where the nomination's chronology has the keeper's house razed in 1961.[^4][^2]

By the 1980s the Coast Guard no longer wanted to maintain the tower. The lighthouse's history page says the Vineyard Environmental Research Institute defended Gay Head, East Chop and Edgartown lights from demolition in congressional hearings in 1984, obtained a 35-year license from the Coast Guard in 1985 and reopened the tower to visitors in 1986; the license passed to the Martha's Vineyard Museum in 1994.[^4]

The cliff then threatened the tower. The history page says Keeper Richard Skidmore found 40 feet of fence gone over the edge in April 2010; LighthouseFriends reports that by December 2012 only about 50 feet remained between tower and cliff.[^4][^3] The National Trust for Historic Preservation listed the light among America's Most Endangered Historic Places in 2013, according to the lighthouse's history page.[^4] The Secretary of the Interior recommended in September 2014 that title go to the Town of Aquinnah, and the National Register's 2015 amendment records the plan to move the tower about 150 feet (46 m) to the east-southeast.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the town received title on 20 February 2015, and that the tower came to rest on its new foundation at 11:10 a.m. on 30 May 2015 after a three-day move of 175 feet (53 m); the lighthouse's history page measures the move at 134 feet (41 m), and says it was relit on 11 August 2015, and that $3.4 million was raised, including more than $2 million from about 1,000 individuals.[^3][^4]

## Today

The tower, 51 feet (15.5 m) tall, stands on Lighthouse Park in Aquinnah, owned by the Town of Aquinnah. The 2015 amendment to the National Register nomination said the deed would include an easement for the Coast Guard to operate and maintain the navigation aid.[^2][^3][^10] The original first-order lens was moved to the Martha's Vineyard Museum, which LighthouseFriends reports reopened in the former Marine Hospital in Vineyard Haven in 2018, in a purpose-built display building.[^3] The lighthouse's own website announced a 2026 season opening on Memorial Day weekend, with limited days until 29 June and daily visits through Labor Day weekend, from 10 a.m. to 4 p.m., with an admission charge; the winter of 2025 saw the lantern deck removed for restoration, and the site says a new lens replaced the one in place since 1989. Check the site before travelling, as it also lists days of early closing.[^10]
