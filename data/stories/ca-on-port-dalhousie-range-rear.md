The Port Dalhousie Range Rear Light, which the City of St. Catharines calls the Inner Range Lighthouse, stands on the east side of the harbour entrance at Port Dalhousie, at the end of Lighthouse Road, where the Welland Canal reached Lake Ontario.[^2][^9] It is an octagonal wooden tower, built in 1898 after lightning burned its predecessor, and it has shown no light since 1988.[^1][^2][^3][^9]

## The place

Port Dalhousie was the Lake Ontario end of the Welland Canal from 1829, when the canal opened, until 1932, when the canal's entrance moved about four kilometres east to Port Weller.[^2] Ships had to find a narrow entrance, and the two range lights were there to guide them in.[^1] The Register describes the pair as a dual-light system for ships on Lake Ontario and for those entering the canal or the port.[^1]

A light was marking the harbour long before the present tower.[^2] LighthouseFriends records that funds for nails and wicking for a Port Dalhousie lighthouse were provided in 1833.[^2]

## Building the light

In 1850 the east pier was capped with a circular wooden lighthouse about 13 m (44 ft) tall.[^2] In 1876 one of its lamps exploded, and the fire badly damaged the lighting apparatus and the lantern room.[^2] In 1878 a square wooden tower was built at the end of a new pier, about 91 m (300 ft) north of the main light, to form a range for the canal.[^2][^1]

By 1893 the timber foundation of the main light was settling.[^2] William H. Noble, the Department's foreman of works, built a new foundation. When the tower was to be moved onto it, the tower was found to be in such poor condition that a new one was needed. The old lantern and lighting apparatus were reused, and the work cost $2,157.51.[^2]

After the 1898 fire, Noble built the present octagonal tower on shore, just east of the pier. LighthouseFriends says it would stand 457 m (1,500 ft) from the outer light and give a better range for entering the canal.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes the site as on shore, just east of the pier, but its map page places the light at the inner end of the east pier, and Rowlett's directory places it at the end of the breakwater.[^2][^9] A fixed red light was shown from the framework until the tower was finished, at a cost of $2,943.19.[^2] The City dates the start of the work to October 1898.[^3] Rowlett's directory gives 1899 for the tower, not 1898.[^9]

The light's character changed in the early 1900s. In the year ended 30 June 1900 the Department reported that the light "still remains fixed red," because the occulting light planned for the station had not been arranged.[^4] On 1 June 1901 the light in the back range tower was changed from fixed red to occulting white, the Department reported the next year.[^5] LighthouseFriends gives the change as 1 June 1902, the day the range lights were electrified.[^2]

LighthouseFriends dates a diaphone fog alarm, installed in the outer lighthouse, to 1908.[^2] The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1908 records a fog-alarm plant installed in the front range lighthouse on the outer end of the east pier.[^7] In the year ended 30 June 1909 the Department repaired the back light pier and renovated the foundations of the tower.[^8]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends lists the keepers with their years. Jonathan Woodall had kept the light for more than twenty years when he retired in 1872, and William Woodall succeeded him. Captain David Hunter kept it from 1879 to 1907, followed by Bernard J. McGrath (1907 to 1912), George Houston (1912 to 1923), L. Houston (1923 to 1924), P. F. Rooney (1924 to at least 1937) and Edward Rooney (at least 1940).[^2]

The Department's statement for the year ended 30 June 1902 lists David Hunter at Port Dalhousie, with an appointment date of 29 October 1879 and a salary of $350.[^6] The City's account of the 1898 fire names Captain David Hunter as the keeper that morning.[^3] The City also says the keeper's home was along Michigan Beach, but the tower had sleeping quarters, a small kitchen and a primitive washroom for a keeper who had to stay inside to keep the light working.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A severe gale in early 1837 destroyed the earlier lighthouse and injured most of the east pier, which then needed about 380 m (1,245 ft) of rebuilding.[^2]

At 8:05 a.m. on 12 August 1898 lightning struck the inner light, and it burned to the ground.[^2][^3] The keeper had gone home for breakfast just before, and he was not hurt.[^3] The steamer *Lakeside* left the harbour and paused to try to put out the flames. Its captain, Wigle, thought of the large amount of oil stored in the tower, and for the safety of his passengers he kept on his course.[^3] Several tugs in the harbour let the fire run, afraid the oil tank would explode.[^3] The lightning had broken the tank open, and the oil burned away until none was left.[^3] There was no explosion.[^3]

## After the keepers

The range lights were automated in 1968.[^2][^3] Transport Canada took the inner light out of service after the 1988 boating season.[^3] Ottawa designated the tower a Recognized Federal Heritage Building in 1990.[^2] Ownership passed to the City of St. Catharines in 1997,[^2][^3] and the Friends of the Port Dalhousie Lighthouse restored the structure from 2000 to 2002.[^3] Rowlett's directory dates an initial restoration to 2002 and 2003, and says the exterior was repaired and repainted in 2010 as the first step of a general restoration.[^9]

## Today

The tower belongs to the City of St. Catharines.[^3] The grounds are open, but the tower is closed to visitors.[^2][^9] The lighthouse is home to the Niagara College Sailing School during the summer months.[^3] The City says the lighthouse is also designated under the Ontario Heritage Act.[^3] The front light on the same pier, the Register says, "continues to serve recreational boaters."[^1]
