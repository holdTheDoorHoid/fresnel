Sandusky Harbor Breakwater Light stands at the outer end of a long jetty that runs out from Cedar Point into Sandusky Bay, on the Lake Erie shore of Ohio.[^1] The Coast Guard owns it, and its tower is closed.[^1]

## The place

The entrance to Sandusky Bay was first flanked by two lighthouses: Marblehead Lighthouse on the west, activated in 1822, and Cedar Point Lighthouse on the east, activated in 1839.[^1] The jetty into the bay was finished in 1914, but no light marked its end until money came for one.[^1] The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1921 described "the proposed light and fog signal at the end of the jetty."[^4]

## Building the light

An act of 17 June 1910 authorized a light and fog signal at the entrance to Sandusky Bay.[^3] The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1920 noted that "no appropriation for the project has been made." The same note said the U.S. Engineer Department had an appropriation for the pierhead at the jetty's outer end, and had awarded the contract for its underwater part.[^3]

Estimates in the reports for the fiscal years ended 30 June 1920, 1921 and 1922 each sought money for "Constructing a light and fog signal at the entrance to" the bay.[^3][^4][^5]

LighthouseFriends says Congress appropriated $50,000 on 28 May 1924 for improving the aids for entering Sandusky Bay. A concrete base went in at the outer end of the jetty, and a steel tower built at the Buffalo lighthouse depot went up there in 1925. That first tower was a twenty-foot (6.1 m) skeletal steel tower with a square lantern room.[^1]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1921 list of "Lights established" includes a Sandusky Pierhead light, "flashing white, lens lantern, acetylene."[^2] LighthouseFriends says a temporary acetylene light was shown from the 1925 tower until the permanent light came on, on 15 March 1928.[^1] The two accounts do not agree on when a light first showed here, and this story does not settle it.

LighthouseFriends gives the permanent light's pattern as four seconds of white followed by a two-second eclipse.[^1] Its focal plane was 66 feet (20 m), and LighthouseFriends says it could be seen for up to fifteen miles, without saying whether statute or nautical miles are meant.[^1] The fog signal was an air diaphone giving a three-second blast every thirty seconds, and it was commissioned on 1 June 1928.[^1] The Bulletin's opening summary for 1929 lists, among the aids in progress, "constructing a lighthouse and installing a fog signal at Sandusky Harbor, Ohio."[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends, citing the *Star Journal* of 5 July 1933, says a sudden northeaster on 2 July 1933 sent more than 100 men fishing on the jetty scrambling for shelter.[^1] Ten men from Cleveland took refuge in the lantern room at the outer end of the jetty. The structure was locked and unmanned.[^1] The waves reached almost forty feet (12 m), and the men had to hold on to the steel supports to avoid drowning. They were stranded through the night.[^1]

By the next morning a Coast Guard boat had come close enough to shoot a line to them, with the lighthouse key attached. The men unlocked the door, lit a lantern and used the tower's private telephone line to reach a keeper on the mainland. That afternoon the Coast Guard took them off and returned them to shore.[^1]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says a submarine cable carried the power and that Cedar Point controlled the light remotely. A double dwelling for the lights' keepers was finished at Cedar Point in 1927.[^1] In 1933 the lantern room was unmanned and locked.[^1]

The Bulletin's 1938 review of the Tenth District says that a very extensive program of reconstruction of lighthouse structures had been carried out in the district since 1910. Among its more important projects, it names the light station at Sandusky, in Ohio.[^7] A separate sentence gives the 1933 to 1936 public-works program only in total: about 25 projects costing roughly $500,000.[^7]

The current tower replaced the 1925 tower in the mid-1990s, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light, and its tower is closed.[^1] The light can be seen from Cedar Point Amusement Park and from the Jet Express ferries to Put-in-Bay.[^1] The tower is closed, and visitors should not try to climb it.
