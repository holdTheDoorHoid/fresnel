Passage Island Light stands on a small island off the north-eastern tip of Isle Royale in Lake Superior, where a stone tower with an attached keepers' house marks a narrow channel between the island and Isle Royale. Lighthouse Friends calls it the most northerly United States lighthouse on the Great Lakes.[^2]

## The place

The light was built for one stretch of water: a channel between Passage Island and the northeast end of Isle Royale, which Lighthouse Friends gives as three and a half miles wide and the National Park Service as 3-1/4 miles.[^2][^3] Lighthouse Friends says it was the most direct route from the lower lakes to Port Arthur, Fort William and the silver mine at Silver Islet, on the Canadian shore. In 1871 the Lighthouse Board asked Congress for $18,000 for a light there, pointing to the "sudden and remarkable increase of travel and traffic" that the silver discoveries had brought, and calling the island "difficult of access" so that anything built there would cost more than at a more accessible point.[^2] The Park Service tells the same story, and says ships "set their course to thread the needle" through the gap, mostly Canadian vessels running between Sault Ste. Marie and Thunder Bay.[^3]

## Building the light

Congress voted $18,000 on 3 March 1875, but made the money conditional on the Canadian government building a light on Colchester Reef in Lake Erie.[^2][^3] Lighthouse Friends says the funds were released in 1880 even though work on the Canadian light had not started; the Park Service says that by 1880 Canada had made enough progress in its preparations for construction here to begin.[^2][^3] In spring 1881 materials were landed on the island, with shanties for the workmen, a boathouse and a crib landing.[^2] The Park Service says the keystone was set in 1881 and that the light was first shown on 1 July 1882, which Lighthouse Friends also gives.[^3][^2]

The design is in what Lighthouse Friends calls a Norman Gothic style, like several other Great Lakes lights, though most of the earlier ones were brick.[^2] At Passage Island it is built of stone: a one-and-a-half-story dwelling with a tower joined to its northwest corner, square at the base and beveled to an octagon higher up.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society archive records the construction material as fieldstone.[^1] Sources differ on heights. Lighthouse Friends gives a tower of about 39 feet (12 m) with the lens 97.5 feet (30 m) above the lake, while the Society's record gives the light as 78 feet (24 m) above the water.[^2][^1] The first lens was a fixed fourth-order Fresnel showing red.[^2][^3]

A 1,500-pound fog bell with a striking mechanism came with the station. Lighthouse Friends suggests the bell "must have proved inadequate", since a ten-inch steam whistle replaced it in a new frame building west of the lighthouse, first sounded on 25 October 1884; an assistant keeper came that year to help run it, and a second assistant the next.[^2] In the Board's reports for the fiscal years ended 30 June 1893 and 30 June 1895 the whistle's running time varied widely: some 282 hours, burning about 21 tons of coal, in the first, and some 755 hours and about 35 tons in the second.[^4][^5] The lens was changed on 24 September 1897 to a fourth-order Barbier and Bernard Fresnel that gave a white flash every ten seconds, and in 1913 the lamp was converted to incandescent oil vapor, raising the light from 5,600 to 50,000 candlepower.[^2] The fog signal became an air diaphone on 10 November 1917.[^2]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster, drawn from the keeper registers, lists James B. Prior as first head keeper in 1882, followed by Richard Singleton (1882-1883) and W. F. Demant (1883-1893).[^1] Lighthouse Friends says Singleton, a man in his seventies, decided to winter on the island. On 14 May 1883 he went to Port Arthur for supplies and three days later was killed by a train there; the steamer *Quebec*, passing the island afterward, found his wife and four children short of food and left them enough to last ten days. Demant took charge on 19 June 1883.[^2] The National Park Service tells a related story differently: it says a keeper it names "W. Dermant" went for supplies at the end of the 1883 season, was kept away by ice until spring, and left his wife and three children to survive the winter by fishing and hunting snowshoe hares.[^6] The NPS account adds that its keeper left his duties soon after the family was rescued, while the roster has Demant in charge until 1893. The two accounts disagree on the man, the family and the season, and neither is backed here by a primary record.

Demant was followed by Alexander Shaw (1893-1907) and John A. McDonald (1908-1915), then Oliver A. St. Andre, Edward B. Gates, Harry W. Thompson, Charles A. Lewis and James Gagnon, among others.[^1] An assistant arrived in 1884 and a second in 1885, and Lighthouse Friends says all three keepers and the head keeper's family lived in the seven-room house.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1921 says Gates had towed a disabled motor boat to shore.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In November 1905 a storm struck while Walter Peters was first assistant to Keeper Shaw. Lighthouse Friends quotes Peters telling a Duluth newspaper that the waves "went so high that all the windows in the kitchen were broken and the floor was flooded," and that the new pump house was swept away.[^2]

The passenger steamer *Monarch* ran aground near Isle Royale and sank in December 1906. A wire report in the Rock Island Argus of 10 December says it happened "yesterday" and that the crew and passengers were rescued, while Lighthouse Friends has the steamer leaving Port Arthur on 6 December and striking rocks that evening.[^8][^2] Lighthouse Friends adds that the survivors lit a signal fire that the island's keepers saw, and that Assistant Keeper Klaas Hamringa rowed over on 9 December; a purser swam out to meet him, and together they flagged down the steamer *Edmonton*, which turned back to Port Arthur for help.[^2] The Hamringa part rests on Lighthouse Friends alone. The Society's roster lists Hamringa as second assistant from 1905 to 1906 and first assistant in 1907-1908.[^1]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 2 January 1919, reviewing the close of navigation on the lakes "during the past month", reports that the keepers were taken off by Canadian authorities and the station closed on 15 December, so in December 1918. It does not say why Canadian authorities were involved.[^9]

On 7 December 1925 the district superintendent in Detroit learned that the wife of Head Keeper Harry Thompson was dying at Marquette. The Bulletin of 1926 records the message, and Lighthouse Friends says radio stations broadcast it and a vessel carried Thompson to Sault Ste. Marie.[^10][^2] In December 1932 (Lighthouse Friends gives the year; the Bulletin reporting it is dated 3 January 1933) the keepers ran the fog signal through a gale in freezing weather, with the Commissioner praising their "heroic efforts in operating their fog signal for four days and three nights." The Bulletin says the head keeper froze his face and ears carrying water across the open yard to the fog-signal building, and names Gagnon as head keeper with Herbert R. Driver and Lawrence E. Lane as his assistants; it adds that the radio broadcast the keeper sent out was picked up by an amateur operator at Mackinaw City, who rebroadcast it to one in Indianapolis, who telegraphed Detroit.[^2][^11] The Bulletin for 1932 also records that the keeper repaired the fog-signal controller with instructions from a radio electrician at Poe Reef, by radiotelephone.[^12]

## After the keepers

A radiobeacon was added in 1927, and electrification followed.[^2] The roster names head keepers through 1947, then Coast Guard crews from 1960 to 1967.[^1] The Park Service says the station was manned as recently as 1957, which does not match the Society's roster, and that it was automated on 20 December 1978; Lighthouse Friends gives the same year.[^3][^2] The Fresnel lens was removed in 1989 and is on display at the Portage Coast Guard Station near Houghton. The Coast Guard declared the station surplus in 1996 and it passed to the National Park Service.[^2]

## Today

The light is within Isle Royale National Park and is an active aid to navigation with a 190 mm modern optic.[^1] Sources differ on access: Lighthouse Friends says the Park Service owns it, the grounds are open and the tower is closed, and that the Park Service runs excursions to it from Rock Harbor; the Park Service's own places page says concession tours travel to the island, but lists the lighthouse as not open to the public and managed by the Coast Guard.[^2][^13] The Society's record says it is open to the public.[^1] Do not count on landing or entering the tower: ask the park about current tours and landing rules before planning a visit.
