Wings Neck Light stands at the end of a point in Pocasset, in the town of Bourne, on the east side of the southern approach to the Cape Cod Canal.[^3][^12] Its first light was shown on 17 September 1849, from a hexagonal wooden tower and lantern at a stone keeper's house.[^3] The tower and dwelling that stand today were built in 1889.[^1] The archive gives 1945 as the year the light was discontinued.[^2]

## The place

The light guarded the eastern entrance to Sandwich Harbor and the Monument River, and commerce passed the point from the port of Wareham and the Pocasset iron foundry.[^1] Congress approved a $5,000 appropriation for a light here in 1837, after merchant captains pressed for one.[^3] Before the work could begin, two men, Commodore J. Ord Creighton and Captain Daniel Turner, wrote to the Navy Commissioners that the nearby Bird Island light, only four miles away, was enough, and that the trade the new light was meant to serve "consists chiefly of wood shallops."[^3]

The Cape Cod Canal opened in 1914 and sent much more shipping past the point.[^3] The 1918 report of the Commissioner of Lighthouses places the station in Buzzards Bay, on the north side of the entrance to the dredged channel that led to the canal.[^6]

## Building the light

The money came slowly. LighthouseFriends reports that the project waited until 1848, when Stephen Pleasonton, the Fifth Auditor of the Treasury, requested a lighthouse on Wings Neck. The original budget was cut by nearly a third, to $3,500.[^3] The finished light cost $3,251, and the contractor was John Vina.[^3]

The light was placed in operation on 17 September 1849 and could be seen for fourteen nautical miles. Its fixed white light was set 38 feet (11.6 m) above the ground.[^3] The archive gives the light's height as 50 feet (15 m) above mean high water.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's digital archive also gives 1849 as the year the station was established.[^2] A lighthouse directory gives 1848 as the year the station was established, and LighthouseFriends dates Pleasonton's request to that year.[^12][^3]

LighthouseFriends, citing the Board's records, says that in 1856 a fourth-order lens replaced the whale-oil lamps and reflectors. The archive's record lists a fifth-order optic as the original.[^3][^2] The two do not agree, and this story does not settle the question. LighthouseFriends, summarizing the Board's entry for 1863, says repairs were finished that year, including a new lantern room.[^3]

LighthouseFriends also reports that a fire did substantial damage in 1878, and that the weight of the lantern was crushing the dwelling's roof.[^3] Funds to rebuild were approved in 1888.[^3]

The tower that stands today dates from 1889. The Massachusetts nomination describes "an 1889 wooden octagonal tower," while the archive gives its shape as hexagonal and pyramidal.[^1][^2] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 records that a covered way had been built from the dwelling to the tower.[^4] A bell tower and a 1,000-pound fog bell arrived in 1902.[^3]

In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1917, the Commissioner of Lighthouses reported that the candlepower of Wings Neck Light "was increased from 180 to 2,900 by installation of oil-vapor apparatus."[^5] The same report said a reed horn would replace the clockwork bell about 1 September 1917. By the 1918 report the light was a fourth-order fixed lens, and the reed horn sounded "a blast of 2 seconds duration followed by a silent interval of 18 seconds."[^6]

Plans for new keepers' dwellings at Brant Point and Wings Neck stalled. The Commissioner's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1920 says the bids for both dwellings were rejected as too expensive, and the report for fiscal 1922 says the work "has been indefinitely postponed."[^7][^8]

In 1923 the Lighthouse Service moved a keeper's house from the Ned's Point light at Mattapoisett, which had been automated, across Buzzards Bay to serve as the assistant keeper's house here.[^1][^12][^3] The move, with a new foundation and a heating system, cost $5,438.[^3] The two sources do not agree on the house's age. The Massachusetts form calls the moved house "an 1870s Keeper's House," while the directory dates it to 1888.[^1][^12]

## The keepers

The first keeper, Edward Doty Lawrence, was appointed in 1849. LighthouseFriends says the appointment was political: his father, David Lawrence of Sandwich, was a Whig who had made large donations to a presidential campaign.[^3] Lawrence was removed in 1854, shortly after the Democrat Franklin Pierce became president.[^3] A newspaper item of 17 February 1854, printed in The Register and quoted by LighthouseFriends, announced his replacement, Samuel Barlow, and called Lawrence "a faithful, capable man."[^3] The archive's roster lists Lawrence again from 1865 until 1887.[^2]

LighthouseFriends quotes the logbook that Lawrence began on 1 January 1872. Its entries are mostly one sentence long, and many are about the weather. One reads: "January 17th some snow fell."[^3]

The archive's roster lists Albert B. Gifford as keeper from 1887 to 1908.[^2] LighthouseFriends, which spells his first name Alfred, says that his wife, Caroline, known as Carry, was Lawrence's daughter and informally became his assistant keeper.[^3] The roster lists Caroline H. Gifford as principal keeper for 1908, the year her husband died.[^2][^3] A local undertaker, Edward D. Nickerson, described the scene he found after learning of Gifford's death in October 1908. As he told it, "One mean and foggy night he died in his house," and Mrs. Gifford kept the light and the fog bell going as he worked on the body, "tending the light and clocking the fog bell."[^3] LighthouseFriends says Carry Gifford remained at her post for two weeks before the next keeper arrived.[^3]

Wallace A. Eldredge was keeper from 1908 to 1921.[^2] A 1916 article quoted by LighthouseFriends praised the housekeeping: "The polished floors and walls and the immaculate ceilings assure the visitor" that the man of the house should share the credit for it with his wife.[^3] With the canal open, a lookout on the point had to watch for vessels approaching from the south, then phone for a tug and a pilot to bring them through.[^3] Eldredge retired in 1921 after thirty-three years in the lighthouse service, and had earned five Efficiency Gold Stars.[^3] His brother-in-law, Benjamin W. Joy, was the first assistant from 1917 to 1922.[^2][^3]

George A. Howard became keeper in 1921 and stayed until 1942.[^2][^3] His brother, William J. Howard, was his assistant from 1925 to 1935.[^2][^3] Russell B. Eastman, who had been the last keeper of Ned's Point and had floated across the bay in the moved dwelling, was first assistant from 1923 to 1925.[^2][^3] The last principal keeper, Javan D. York, served from 1942 to 1943.[^2]

General context, not a record of this station: the 1881 General Instructions to All Light-Keepers began by saying that "The keeper is responsible for the care" of the light and of the station.[^13]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 2 September 1876 a boat carrying two men capsized and drifted ashore near Dry Ledge. The keeper's log says the men had been in the water about four hours, and the keeper wrote: "We gave them what assistance we could."[^3]

In June 1931 a speedboat sank near the light. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports that the keeper, George A. Howard, and his assistant rescued two women and two men "when their speed boat sank near the light station on June 18." The keeper's son called the assistant, who was on watch, and they launched the station boat.[^9] LighthouseFriends gives the date as 19 June.[^3] On 14 July the same year, William J. Howard rescued a party of five whose sailing skiff had capsized near the station.[^10]

The Bulletin's account of the hurricane that swept the North Atlantic coast on 21 September 1938 lists the Wings Neck Light Station among the damaged stations. It says that the station's structures were "rendered so unsafe" that the keeper could not stay, and that the keeper had to use an emergency light.[^11]

## After the keepers

In 1928 a new lens made the light flash instead of showing a fixed beam, and the beacon was converted to electricity in 1934.[^3] The Cleveland Ledge light was activated in 1943, and Wings Neck was automated that year. LighthouseFriends says the fog signal stopped and the light was shown from a skeletal tower instead.[^3] The station's buildings were sold in 1947 for $13,738, and LighthouseFriends says the light stayed active on the point for several more years.[^3] That conflicts with the directory, which dates the light's deactivation to 1945, and with the archive, which gives 1945 as the year the station was discontinued.[^12][^2]

After many years as a private residence, the dwelling was renovated for weekly vacation rental in 2003, and the tower was repaired and repainted in 2014.[^12] In September 2016 the local zoning board ruled that weddings and other special events were not allowed on the site. A court upheld that ruling in late 2018, and the state appeals court agreed in June 2020.[^12] The station is listed in the National Register of Historic Places as part of the Lighthouses of Massachusetts group.[^1]

## Today

Wings Neck Light is private property. LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is privately owned and that its grounds, dwelling and tower are closed.[^3] The dwelling is let by the week, and the directory says the site is open only to guests.[^12] LighthouseFriends notes that No Trespassing signs keep visitors from a close view.[^3] From the edge of the property at the end of Wings Neck Road there is a distant view of the light.[^12] Keep to the public road and do not go onto the property.
