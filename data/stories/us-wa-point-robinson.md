Point Robinson Light stands on the low, flat spit at the eastern tip of Maury Island in Puget Sound, King County, Washington, where the shipping channel makes a sharp turn on its way south to Tacoma. It began in 1885 as a fog-whistle station, and a small concrete lighthouse that replaced its wooden predecessors in 1915 carries a fifth-order Fresnel lens that was still in operation when the National Register nomination was written in 2002.[^1]

## The place

The point is the easternmost end of Maury Island, which a narrow land bridge joins to Vashon Island. The National Register nomination describes the deep channel here as making a "critical 90-degree turn" around a long, low reach of land, one that ships heading for the south Sound had to make safely.[^1] The Light-House Board had planned Point Robinson, Point No Point and West Point as the three main marks along the channel before 1882, so that a vessel could keep two of them in view as it came down the Sound.[^1]

The Board’s report for 1879 first mentioned the place, calling it "next in importance to West Point or Sandy Point" and asking for $7,000 for a 10-inch fog whistle, with Tacoma then the prospective terminus of the Northern Pacific Railroad.[^1] In 1882 the district surveyed the 23-acre site, which the nomination describes as a high bank with a low triangle of land below it holding a salt marsh and pond, edged with drift logs and gravel.[^1] The deed passed to the Board from Mary Elizabeth Webster in 1884 for $1,000.[^1] The nomination dates the deed 27 June 1884; LighthouseFriends gives 27 January 1884, and the two do not agree.[^1][^2]

## Building the light

The station was first a fog-signal station. The nomination says the Board established the "Point Robinson fog-signal" in 1885 with a 12-inch steam whistle, housed in a one-story frame building 36 by 16 feet with an extra fuel room, and fed with rainwater from a shingled shed over two brick cisterns.[^1] A one-and-a-half-story keeper's dwelling, 630 feet to the south, was done by June 1885; work stopped on 3 June, a watchman was left in charge, and the first keeper, Franklin Tucker, was due in the middle of that month.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the first sounding of the whistle to 1 July 1885.[^2]

The ground was soft. In 1886 and 1887 the Board's reports describe filling the lagoon by washing earth down from the bluff and building a large bulkhead around the spit, enclosing about four and a half acres, which was then sown with grass.[^1]

A light came later and its beginnings are unclear. The nomination says the original signal is hard to make out from the evidence, and that by 1890 the Lights of the United States listed a fixed red lens lantern on a white stake, 30 feet above the water.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives a start date of 12 December 1887 for that light.[^2] In 1894 the lantern was raised 5 1/2 feet because the dwelling hid it; in 1897 it moved to a ten-foot-square wooden tower, and about 1907 to a second, open wooden "skeleton" tower, 11 feet square at the base.[^1] The report volume covering 1896 and 1897 records the framing of a new tower for the lens lantern, left unfinished because of "unusually bad weather".[^3]

In 1914 the district inspector asked for a better light, writing that "Mariners have recently requested the establishment of a flashing light here", and that the president of the Puget Sound Navigation Company had urged an improvement.[^1] The result was a reinforced-concrete building with an octagonal tower attached, holding light, fog signal and engines under one roof; the nomination says it was built late in 1914 and put in service in 1915.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1915 lists the light changed to a fourth-order group-flashing white and the fog signal changed from the 12-inch steam whistle to a third-class reed horn.[^4] LighthouseFriends instead calls the signal a Daboll type; sources differ on the type.[^2] The nomination says the lens was replaced after May 1918, when electric power became reliable, by a fifth-order Fresnel lens, although it also calls the fifth-order lens “original (1915)” and the 1915 Bulletin lists a fourth-order light, so the nomination and the Bulletin do not agree on the lens first installed.[^1][^4] The nomination gives 31 feet (9.4 m) from grade to the focal plane, while LighthouseFriends gives a 38-foot (11.6 m) tower; the two may measure different things.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

For its first fifteen years the station had one keeper. Franklin Tucker served 1885 to 1887, followed by Charles H. Davis, who stayed until 1900.[^5][^1] LighthouseFriends says Tucker had been head keeper at New Dungeness and was transferred from Cape Flattery.[^2]

The steam whistle made the work heavy. In the report volume covering 1896 and 1897 the whistle is listed as running "some 2,089 hours" and burning about 27 tons of coal in one year, and 528 hours and about 35 tons in the next.[^3] The Board began asking for a second keeper and a second house in 1897: "Now that there is a fog signal here an additional keeper is much needed", it wrote, because the dwelling was "barely sufficient for the present keeper and his family".[^1] The request was repeated every year until 1905, and Congress voted $5,000 in June 1906; bids came in over the sum, so a working party built "Quarters B", finished in 1908 by the nomination's account and in 1907 by LighthouseFriends.[^1][^2] An assistant, Frank E. Wyman, arrived in 1900, and the roster shows an assistant on the station from then on.[^5]

William S. Denning kept the light from 1913 to 1930, a longer stint than any other head keeper on the roster.[^5] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for fiscal 1916 (July 1915 to June 1916) names Denning as keeper and Samuel B. Morris as assistant keeper at Robinson Point in its table of saving of life and property by Lighthouse Service employees.[^6] The nomination says the two came to the rescue of a family in a disabled boat near the station, without a date; LighthouseFriends places it in 1916.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends starts Denning's term in 1912 where the archive roster has 1913.[^2][^5] LighthouseFriends adds that the family got food and clothing, that the keepers helped repair the launch so they could go on to Tacoma, and that Denning won the district's efficiency flag in 1922.[^2]

Thomas Wyman Albee was assistant keeper in 1919 and returned as head keeper from 1939 to 1944, with Jens O. Pedersen as his assistant.[^5] Ila Albee later wrote a memoir, *Children of the Lighthouse*, about her family's years at five Northwest lights, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The same page tells of the two keepers pushing a stranded young whale off the shoal at the point with long planks.[^2]

The nomination notes that the families also built the small working structures that a station needed: a chicken house and yard, a fruit locker, a paint and carpenter shop, a pump house with a wooden water tank, and a fenced garden.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources give few dated events here. Beyond the boat rescue described above, LighthouseFriends reports that in 1919 Denning and his assistant towed the disabled tug *Mayflower* to safety with the station launch.[^2] In the late 1930s the Works Progress Administration was hired to sluice earth from the hill behind the lighthouse to fill the marsh, which a 1938 note called "a menace to health" because it bred mosquitoes; the crew also built a rock shoreline defence.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1939 the Lighthouse Service was folded into the Coast Guard.[^1] Around 1960 the Coast Guard declared about 11 acres surplus, and they passed to King County for a park.[^1] The Coast Guard automated the light in 1978, and the last resident crews left in the late 1980s.[^1] The roster names Coast Guard crew here into the 1970s.[^5] The Coast Guard installed tall radar and radio towers at the point in 1989 for vessel traffic monitoring, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] In 1997 the Vashon Park District obtained a permit to use the station for museum purposes.[^1] In the early 1990s, LighthouseFriends reports, neighbours who opposed a seafood-processing plant on the point formed the Keepers of Point Robinson and negotiated a lease with the Coast Guard.[^2] The Coast Guard replaced the Fresnel lens as the working light in 2008 with a plastic beacon mounted outside the lantern room; what became of the lens is not stated in the sources read.[^2]

## Today

The station is listed on the National Register of Historic Places (21 April 2004).[^7] The Coast Guard owns it and the Vashon Park District manages it. LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open in daylight, the lighthouse is open for tours on summer Sundays, and the keepers' quarters are rented by the week.[^2] Check the Vashon Park District for current hours.
