Great Point Light stands on the northern tip of Nantucket, at the end of a barrier beach about seven miles long, where Nantucket Sound meets the open Atlantic. The tower visitors see today is a replica, built in 1986 after a storm knocked the 1818 stone tower down.[^1][^2]

## The place

The point is a long strip of sand at the island's northern end. Lighthouse historians describe the water beyond it as one of the busiest areas on the East Coast by the late 1700s, thick with whaling traffic and made hard by shoals and strong, shifting currents.[^2] The earliest recorded request is from 1770, when Nantucket's town leaders formed a committee to ask the Massachusetts General Court for a light here, and the Revolutionary War held the matter up until the Commonwealth passed its resolution in February 1784.[^2]

A Springfield newspaper in 1881 described the place as a light-house that "stands solitary in its hills of sand, and is rarely visited."[^14]

## Building the light

The sources do not agree on the first tower's dates. One account has a wooden tower built quickly after the 1784 resolution and lit that October;[^2] the historical marker beside the replica tower dates the wooden tower to December 1784;[^5] and another account says it was finished the following year.[^3] The United States Lighthouse Society's archive lists the station as established in 1784.[^1]

For its first years the light had no keeper's house. Captain Paul Pinkham, a former whaleman, tended it as the first keeper at $166.66 a year, and the keepers had to reach the point from the town, about seven miles away, on foot or horseback; Pinkham sometimes took a boat to Brant Point for supplies.[^2][^3] After about three years a dwelling was built.[^2] Pinkham also published what was reckoned the best chart of the island and its shoals in 1791.[^3]

The wooden tower burned in 1816. Winslow Lewis, who visited, wrote that it could "hardly admit of a doubt" that it had been set on fire on purpose, though nothing was proved.[^2] The marker gives the fire as November 1816 and says Congress appropriated funds in March 1817.[^5] A stone tower followed, about 60 feet (18 m) high, at a cost given as $7,500 in one account and about $7,400 in another.[^2][^3] The marker says it was built in October 1817; the other accounts say it was finished in 1818.[^5][^2][^3]

LighthouseFriends quotes the 1838 report of Lieutenant Carpender, who counted fourteen lamps with reflectors in the lantern, and the engineer I. W. P. Lewis, who in 1842 judged the illuminating apparatus to be in bad condition.[^2] A third-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps in 1857, when the tower was also lined with brick and an assistant keeper's dwelling was added.[^2][^3] Mineral oil replaced lard oil as the illuminant in August 1882, and in 1889 a new frame double dwelling went up on the old foundation.[^2]

## The keepers

The United States Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the keeper registers, lists more than thirty principal keepers, from Paul Pinkham in 1784 to Antone Syliva, who appears from 1944, together with more than fifty assistants.[^1] Pinkham died in 1799, which ended his service.[^2][^3]

Isolation shaped the job. When Jonathan Coffin became keeper in 1812 the keeper's house burned soon after, forcing him back onto the long daily trip, and he asked for and got a pay increase for the hardship.[^2] An account of the station records that Judah Berry, a former sea captain aged seventy, died at the station in January 1904 after a few months as keeper.[^3] Archived records show Berry as keeper in 1903-1904.[^1]

Archford "Ted" Haskins kept the light from 1937 to 1944; LighthouseFriends calls him the final principal keeper before Coast Guard crews were stationed at the light.[^1][^2] He had earlier been an assistant at Boston Light, and his family arrived at Great Point in thick fog.[^3] His daughter Jeanette later remembered the storms there fondly and said she sometimes helped light the lamp.[^2][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A journal kept at the light between 1863 and 1890 records forty-three shipwrecks in those twenty-seven years, according to two histories of the station. Mariners often mistook Great Point for one of the nearby lightships, and the Light-House Board's 1888 report, as quoted by LighthouseFriends, ordered a red cut placed in the light to cover Cross Rip Shoal.[^2][^3]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin records several rescues by the keepers:

- On 5 February 1917, in a 66-mile gale, Keeper Henry L. Thomas and Assistant Charles Stoll helped the crew of the steamer *A. T. Serrell*, caught in the ice half a mile from shore, who reached land over the drift ice on boards.[^6]
- On 5 February 1918 the keeper sighted the Cross Rip lightship drifting in heavy ice east of Great Round Shoal; the vessel was never recovered, and the Bulletin feared her lost.[^8]
- On 24 April 1920 Keeper John M. Scharff and Assistant Clarence L. Turner helped the crew of a seaplane forced down on the beach near the station.[^9]
- On 9 April 1925 Keeper H. B. Dobbins helped float the stranded schooner *Waltham*.[^10]
- On 15 April 1930 Keeper Theodore L. Chase helped rescue the crew of the burning schooner *Irma A*. Heavy seas kept the Coast Guard's pulling lifeboat from launching, so a power boat was launched on the inside of the beach and the keeper volunteered to take it through the rips.[^11]
- On 12 July 1931 Chase helped the pilot of an amphibian plane, carrying eight passengers, that landed on the beach in heavy surf.[^12] On 23 September that year the fishing vessel *Elizabeth M. Foley* caught fire about two miles from the station and there was an explosion; her crew had put off in a dory that was about to sink when Chase and Assistant Otis E. Walsh reached them in the station boat. The men were lodged at the light.[^13]

One small item from the First World War years also made the Bulletin. In 1917 Keeper Thomas raised good potatoes in the beach sand, where there was no soil, and the Service published the news for other keepers "similarly situated."[^7]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in the 1950s, according to the sources that give a date, and the dwelling was boarded up.[^2][^3] A suspected arson fire destroyed the keeper's house on 23 October 1966.[^2][^4] A modern solar-powered optic replaced the Fresnel lens in 1971.[^2]

Erosion brought the sea steadily closer to the tower. Residents wanted it moved, but the cost, estimated at $450,000, was not found.[^4] The Coast Guard refurbished the tower in 1983.[^2][^4] On the night of 29 March 1984 a severe storm toppled it and cut through the barrier beach, leaving the point briefly an island.[^2][^3][^4]

The Coast Guard first planned to replace it with a modern fibreglass tower, but islanders objected. Federal money, secured with the help of Senator Edward M. Kennedy, paid for a replica instead, put up about 300 yards from the old site.[^2][^4] The marker says the old tower stood 800 feet (about 245 m) east of the new one.[^5] Accounts give the cost as two million dollars appropriated[^2][^3] and slightly over one million dollars spent.[^3][^4] The replica has a reinforced-concrete core faced with rubblestone, including some from the 1818 tower, and stands on a sheet-pile foundation with a thick concrete mat.[^2][^5] D'Entremont and LighthouseFriends give the dedication as 7 September 1986, a day later than planned because of fog, while the marker's heading reads "Rededicated September 6, 1986."[^2][^3][^5] At the ceremony Kennedy declared "Great Point is alive and well again."[^3] Jeanette Haskins Killen, who had lived at the old light as a child, attended.[^3][^4]

The 1818 tower's third-order Fresnel lens is displayed outside the Nantucket Shipwreck and Lifesaving Museum.[^2]

## Today

Great Point Light is an active aid to navigation, flashing white every five seconds, and the station record gives its optic as a solar-powered VRB-25.[^1][^2] The original tower was on the National Register of Historic Places, but the listing was rescinded when the tower was rebuilt.[^16] LighthouseFriends names the Coast Guard as owner, and the Trustees' refuge page calls it the federally owned lighthouse; it stands in the Coskata-Coatue Wildlife Refuge.[^2][^15]

Getting there is not easy. The point lies about seven miles beyond Wauwinet. Access is on foot or by over-sand vehicle, which needs a permit, and the Trustees offer guided tours from which visitors may climb the tower to an observation deck.[^15][^16] Much of the surrounding area is closed because it is a nesting site for the endangered piping plover, and beach conditions change with the tides, so check the Trustees' current rules before going.[^3][^16]
