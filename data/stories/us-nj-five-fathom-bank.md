For more than 130 years a lightship lay at anchor on the open Atlantic off Cape May, New Jersey, marking the way into Delaware Bay. The station was a succession of ships rather than a tower, and in 1972 a horn buoy took over from the last of them.[^1][^2] The official reports leave only glimpses of life aboard, but one night in August 1893, when a cyclone sank the relief lightship then holding the station, is told in the Light-House Board's own words and in a New York newspaper's first reports.[^3][^4]

## The station

The Light-House Board's list for 1858 placed the ship "Sixteen miles from Cape May, N. J., near the shoal called the 'Five-Fathom bank,'" and gave her two reflector lights and a fog bell.[^5] By 1860 she was "Moored in 12 fathoms water" (72 feet, 22 m), and by 1870 her fog signals were a bell, a horn and a gun.[^5]

When the station began is not settled. The Lighthouse Society's place record gives 1839 and cites the 1850 Light List.[^1] Its historical light-list page opens the station's first span of positions in 1837, as does Wikipedia,[^5][^6] while the Delaware Bay Lighthouse Keepers & Friends Association says only that the first lightship was recommended in 1826.[^2] A history of the Service published in 1917 counted just three lightships on outside stations in 1839: Sandy Hook, Five-Fathom Bank and Carysfort Reef, in Florida.[^7]

The ship's position changed over the years. The 1878 Light List put her about 5½ nautical miles from the shoal and the 1893 list about 4½ miles; on 1 September 1894 she was moored afresh in 93 feet (28 m) of water, between one and two miles south-southeast of her former position, and the 1898 list gave her as 6¼ miles south-southeasterly from the shoalest part of the bank, in 90 feet (27 m).[^5][^8]

The lightship also marked one end of a speed-trial course. On the Saturday before 27 March 1893 the new cruiser *New York* made two timed runs between the Five Fathom Bank and Northeast End lightships in her preliminary trial, and the *Indianapolis Journal* headed its report "Fastest Cruiser Afloat."[^9] Northeast End was one of the five lightships that Putnam's 1917 history lists between Delaware Bay and Chesapeake Bay, "from seven to sixteen miles out."[^7]

## The ships

The Society's table of vessels runs: No. 18 (1839 to 1869), No. 37 (1869 to 1876), No. 39 (1876 to 1877), No. 40 (1877 to 1904), No. 79 (1904 to 1924), No. 108 (1924 to 1942 and 1945 to 1970), No. 110 (1970 to 1971) and WLV-189 (1971 to 1972).[^1] Wikipedia's list is the same apart from leaving out No. 39 and adding an unnamed ship for 1837 to 1839, and it warns that "Records for lightvessels are incomplete."[^6] The sources disagree in small ways too: the 1878 Light List gives "No. 39" on the quarters in a year when the Society's table has No. 40 on the station.[^5] The Society's record for the first ship, No. 18, gives her build year as 1839, her years on the bank as 1839 to 1869, then relief duty until she was retired in 1875, and carries the remark "Used for Torpedo Practice."[^10]

No. 40 is on the station from 1877 to 1904 in the Society's table.[^1] She was built at Wilmington, Delaware, in 1875 for $39,200 (the hull alone), of white oak fastened with copper, about 115 feet (35 m) long and 27 feet (8.2 m) in the beam.[^11] Two lanterns, each with eight reflector lamps burning mineral oil, showed her lights, and her fog signals were a 12-inch steam whistle and a 1,000-pound bell.[^11] When the Board showed a model of her at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition, its report gave her crew as 4 officers and 6 men and, for the year ended 30 June 1893, a maintenance cost of $5,867.02, 1,138 gallons of mineral oil burned in her lights, and a steam whistle that ran 451 hours and used 22 tons of coal.[^11]

The reports record trouble with the whistle's boilers. The Board's 1891 report says they failed in December 1890 and the bell was rung in fog until 30 April 1891, though its list of temporary changes dates the substitution of a hand-struck bell to 2 January 1891; No. 40 was then brought in for new boilers, the schooner *Drift* and a whistling buoy marked the station, and the tender *Laurel* towed her back to it on 4 June 1891.[^12] In July 1893 she was taken off the station for repairs; at the Edgemoor depot her yellow-metal sheathing was found "so much worn as to need renewing," and relief ship No. 37 took her place.[^13] The yearly returns give the whistle's running time: 451 hours in the year to June 1893,[^11] 938 hours between 13 September 1893 and 30 June 1894,[^13] 649 in the next year[^8] and 584 in the year to June 1901.[^14]

In June 1897 No. 40 was off the station from the 5th to the 29th, with a red whistling buoy at her moorings.[^15] In July 1899 she was taken off for two new fog-signal boilers and relieved by relief ship No. 16 until she went back on station on 21 August.[^16] In January 1900, the Board reported, she "leaked considerably without apparent cause, and as suddenly stopped," before the source of the leak had been found.[^16] In its report for 1896 the Board repeated a request, made after the loss of No. 37, for a new first-class light-vessel with a steam fog signal for Five-Fathom Bank, priced there at $80,000, so that the vessel then on the bank could become the relief ship the Fourth District lacked.[^17]

No. 79 took the station in 1904.[^1] The 1905 Light List describes a "Flush-deck, steam vessel; two masts, schooner-rigged, no bowsprit," with "A black smokestack and the fog signal between the masts; white pilot house forward."[^5] Her two white lights were fixed, 340 candlepower each in 1915 and 400 in 1917; in 1922 the *Lighthouse Service Bulletin* recorded the light changed from fixed to flashing, oil to acetylene.[^5][^18] Radio fog signals came next. In 1923 the *Bulletin* listed Five Fathom Bank among five light vessels on which radio fog stations were being established, and said the signal for the Five Fathom Bank vessel, "now under construction," would use a tube transmitter sending a continuous wave in place of the older spark set.[^19] George Weiss's 1926 history of the Service dates the station's radio fog signal to 1925.[^20] The 1927 Light List then lists, for the station, an occulting white light every 6 seconds shown from the foremast head, a submarine bell sounding the group "42" every 28 seconds, and a radiobeacon.[^5]

The next ship, No. 108, was built in 1923 and held the station from 1924 to 1942; she then served as an examination vessel while a buoy marked the bank until 1945, and she returned and remained until 1970.[^21][^5] The 1946 Light List describes a red hull with "FIVE-FATHOM" in white on the sides and two masts with circular galleries.[^5] The Light Lists record the signals changing with the decades, from steam horns in 1946 to a two-tone air diaphone in 1950 and a diaphragm horn sounding one 2-second blast every 30 seconds by 1970. The light's rated strength was 3,000 candlepower in 1927, 15,000 in 1931, 13,000 in 1959, 15,000 in 1966 and 17,000 in 1969.[^5] Coast Guard oceanographic reports also give daily water-temperature and salinity observations at the lightship's position, 38°47.3' N 74°34.6' W, for 1956 to 1971.[^22]

## The crews

The earliest names in the sources are two masters of No. 18, taken from the National Archives' registers of lighthouse keepers as recorded in the Lighthouse Society's archive: Isaac P. Merritt, whose service runs from 1838 to 1850, and Wilmore W. Ware, from 1850 to 1853, each paid $700 a year.[^23][^24] Merritt's start falls a year before the Society's date for the station.

In 1893 No. 40 carried 4 officers and 6 men,[^11] and No. 37, the relief ship, had six men aboard when she was lost.[^3][^7] The *Bulletin*'s personnel columns name masters when their posts change. Oliver Clark's service as master of No. 79 was terminated at the close of 17 February 1914, and that year the *Bulletin* recorded Alfred Johansen's promotion from mate of the Overfalls lightship, No. 69, to master of Five Fathom Bank.[^25] He was in command when the crew helped a hydroaeroplane in September 1918 (below).[^26] A personnel list in the 1920 *Bulletin* moves an Alfred Johansen to master of Overfalls No. 69, presumably the same man, and an Alfred Johansen is commanding the bank's ship, by then No. 108, in June 1928 (below); the sources read do not show whether he stayed on the bank in between.[^33][^27] The 1918 columns also record changes among a ship's officers: James Keyes was temporarily appointed mate of No. 79, and David Sawyer temporary assistant engineer in place of J. E. Peterson.[^26] The latest names are from 1939, when the *Bulletin* recorded Ruben M. Donovan's transfer to assistant engineer of No. 108 and the death of her radio operator, John S. Harrison.[^28]

*General context, not this ship:* a history of the Service published in 1917 said that life on a lightship "is somewhat dreary," and that on an exposed station the crew knew that in fog many vessels were headed straight for them and that in a storm, anchored in the open sea, they "may be far from comfortable." The writer was describing the Nantucket Shoals ship.[^7]

## Storms, collisions and rescues

The night the records describe best is 23 to 24 August 1893. Relief lightship No. 37 was holding the bank while No. 40 was under repair when the cyclone of those two days came.[^3] A board of three district inspectors met on 9 October 1893 and examined the two survivors, and the Light-House Board's report summarises the assistant engineer's evidence almost hour by hour.[^3] On the morning of the 23rd, he said, the sea was already very heavy and breaking on the bank; the wind began to blow about 5 p.m. and "kept increasing until about midnight, when it blew the hardest." A sea boarded the ship about 10 p.m. and others "every little while"; at midnight two boats were lost and at 1 a.m. the last one was carried away. About 1:45 a.m., "a tremendous sea boarded her," and she went down sideways to port. The barometer stood at 29 inches at 1:30 a.m., a quarter of an hour before she foundered.[^3] One witness told the board the seas were "regular heavy rollers like breakers on the beach," and that "There were four big ones in succession, and the fourth turned the ship over."[^3]

Four of the six men aboard were lost. The pilot boat *F. C. Knight* picked up Assistant Engineer Stillwell, as the dispatch spells the name, and Seaman Charles Olsen seven miles southeast of Five Fathom Bank, and *The Sun* reported that Stillwell had seen one man drown; another dispatch, from a pilot at Lewes, Delaware, had the lightship sunk and her crew supposed lost, and the paper remarked that only "a mighty blow" could wreck a lightship.[^4] The Board's account is that the engineer went down with the ship, came up holding the main hatch scuttle, and was picked up some sixteen hours later; the seaman clung to a piece of the lamp house, and from daylight to a gaff as well, for some seventeen hours.[^3] The newspaper's scanned text puts the pick-up at 7 a.m., where the Board has about 6 p.m. for the engineer and about 7 p.m. for the seaman.[^4][^3]

The board of officers found that the chain was not veered to its full scope, "as is required by the regulations," and fixed the responsibility on the assistant keeper in charge, who was lost with the ship. The Light-House Board noted that the main hatch scuttle, about 4 by 5 feet (1.2 by 1.5 m), was not lashed down until about 9 p.m. on the 23rd, and set the loss beside the Rattlesnake Shoal lightship, stranded on 27 August with her hatches so well battened that she held "but a few inches of water." It called the foundering "the first instance in the history of the United States Light-House Establishment in which a light-ship has foundered at her moorings."[^3] The steam tug *International*, which the Board's 1893 report says could show electric lights, was chartered at $125 a day and anchored on the station on 26 August, leaving it unmarked for two nights; she stayed until No. 40 went back on 13 September.[^3][^13] On 8 September the Secretary of the Treasury asked Congress for $70,000 to build a replacement.[^3] A 1917 history gives the same toll: of the crew of six, two were rescued after sixteen hours in the water.[^7]

On 2 February 1913, according to a Philadelphia dispatch printed in the *Daily Kennebec Journal* of Augusta, Maine, the four-masted schooner *City of Georgetown*, carrying salt from New York to Savannah, collided early that morning with the Hamburg-American liner *Prinz Oscar* near the Five Fathom Bank light. The dispatch said the schooner sank within eight minutes, that Captain A. J. Slocum and his crew of seven got away in a boat and were taken aboard the liner, and that the liner returned to Philadelphia with two of the schooner's anchors and much of her forward rigging wedged in the hole in her bow. It added that "the big light from Five Fathom bank lightship is said to have been responsible": in its account the schooner was heading toward the Cape Hatteras lightship in the southeast while the liner held a northeast great-circle course for the transatlantic route, and the lightship's light lay between the two ships, so that by the time the lookouts made each other out it was too late.[^29] The Lighthouse Service established a gas buoy on the wreck that year.[^30]

On 6 February 1918 No. 79 was dragged about 2 miles off station and returned the same day under her own power; the *Bulletin*'s index lists the item as "dragged by ice."[^26][^31] On 13 September 1918 Alfred Johansen, commanding No. 79, and other members of her crew assisted a hydroaeroplane carrying two men, one of whom had been killed when the plane fell into the water close to the ship.[^26] On 8 June 1928, with the station 18 miles off Wildwood, No. 108, Alfred Johansen commanding, rescued a Coast Guard warrant officer who had made a forced landing in an amphibian aeroplane near the ship. The ship's motor boat brought him aboard, the plane was towed alongside and pumped out, and the *Bulletin* says her officers and crew were commended.[^27]

## After the lightships

The sources disagree about the last years. The Society's table has No. 110 on the station in 1970 and 1971 and WLV-189 in 1971 and 1972, gives 1972 as the year the station was discontinued, and records No. 108's retirement in 1970 and her scrapping in 1975.[^1][^21] The Delaware Bay Lighthouse Keepers & Friends Association dates the end of the last Five Fathom lightship's service to 1966, says a temporary lightship filled in until a lighted horn buoy went into operation in 1972, and says the last lightship was sunk off Atlantic City to become part of an artificial reef.[^2] Wikipedia says only that a horn buoy replaced the station.[^6] We found no source for the horn buoy's own history.

## Today

The station was a moored ship, not a structure on the bank, and none of the sources read describes anything left at the spot. The 1858 list put the ship sixteen miles from Cape May and the *Bulletin* in 1928 eighteen miles off Wildwood, so the site lies well out at sea and can be reached only by boat; we did not find a current source for what, if anything, marks it now.[^5][^27] The Society lists one of the ships, No. 79, as still in existence. She held the station from 1904 to 1924 and later served at Barnegat until she was retired in 1967, and the Society's remark reads "Camden Museum and Learning Center, Badly Deteriorated"; we have not confirmed her condition or whether she can be visited.[^32]
