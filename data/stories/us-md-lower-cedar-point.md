Lower Cedar Point Light stood on the end of Yates Shoal in the Potomac River, opposite Lower Cedar Point on the Maryland side.[^1] It was a screw-pile house first lit in 1867, and it burned at Christmas 1893 (the Board gives 25 December).[^2][^3][^5] The house was rebuilt on the old iron foundation in 1896,[^8] and a steel skeletal tower stands on that foundation today.[^4]

## The place

Before the screw-pile house, a light-vessel marked the shoal. An act of Congress on 3 March 1837 authorized one at Lower Cedar Point, and LighthouseFriends prints the Board's list of 1845, which gives her as "Built in 1837; tonnage 72."[^5] The Board's list of 1860 says instead that the vessel was built in 1825 and refitted in 1855.[^5] The lists disagree, and the sources read here cannot settle which is right. A new light was first exhibited from her on 25 April 1855.[^5]

The Star reported in 1893 that the light stood in six feet (2 m) of water on the end of Yates' shoal.[^1] A red sector covered Kettle Bottom shoal.[^5] The Board's own papers were not consistent about the state. Its list for 1867 places the station "in Virginia," while its 1891 index lists "Lower Cedar Point, Maryland."[^5][^6]

The war reached the river. Between 19 and 24 April 1861 two light-vessels in the Potomac were "wantonly burned," the Board reported. The Board's report does not name who did it. The Lighthouse Directory says Confederate raiders burned a lightship at this station.[^5][^4] In 1864 two light-vessels were placed back on the Cedar Points, and "A competent guard for their protection has been detailed from the army."[^5]

## Building the light

By the late 1860s the Board preferred to replace light-vessels with permanent structures where it could. Its 1867 report, as LighthouseFriends prints it, aimed "to replace the light-vessels, wherever practicable, by permanent structures."[^5] On 6 August 1867 a light was exhibited from the screw-pile house that replaced her. The vessel was taken to Norfolk, examined, and then placed on the York Spit station.[^5]

In 1895 Congress appropriated $25,000 to reestablish the station and authorized a contract up to $75,000.[^5][^7] The iron foundation came through the fire with only two of its columns damaged.[^5] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1897 calls the station "Reestablished on old foundation."[^8] The new light was exhibited on 5 September 1896 as a fourth-order light.[^5][^8] The new frame house was framed at the Lazaretto depot, and the tender Jessamine towed the parts out in July 1896. It was "a square, white, frame dwelling supporting a lantern."[^5] In October 1896 the red sector was removed, and the light became a fixed white of the fourth order.[^5] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 new model fourth-order lamps were installed.[^9]

## The keepers

The roster has 53 lines: 22 principal keepers and 31 assistants, from 1867 to 1942.[^10] The longest single stint is that of James A. Arnold, principal keeper from 1896 to 1920.[^10] LighthouseFriends dates his start to 1894, so the two records disagree by two years.[^5]

Several men rose from assistant to principal here. Benjamin F. Langley was first assistant from 1867 to 1868 and principal from 1868 to 1869.[^10] Samuel A. Conway was first assistant from 1874 to 1875 and principal from 1875 to 1883.[^10]

In December 1893 the principal keeper was Benjamin R. Grymes, Jr., on the roster for 1893 and 1894.[^10] The Evening Star of 27 December named the keeper "Benjamin R. Grimes. jr.," and his assistant John Field.[^1] John Field is on the roster as first assistant for 1893 and 1894.[^10] The Alexandria Gazette, also on 27 December, named him as Mr. B. R. Grymes.[^3]

In 1930 Edward Farrow became principal keeper, on the roster for 1930 to 1939. The archive's record for Farrow also lists him as principal keeper at Leading Point, in Baltimore Harbor.[^10][^11]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Evening Star of 27 December gave the timing as "early yesterday morning," which would be 26 December. The Star said the keeper and his assistant were believed to have escaped, and that they had "a boat" and were "only half a mile or so distant" from the shore, "provided they were alarmed in time."[^1]

The Gazette had it on Monday night, which was Christmas night.[^3] The keeper, it reported, said that a flue in the light tower had collapsed and set the house on fire.[^3] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 gives 25 December 1893 and says the cause "was not definitely ascertained."[^2][^12] A Star report of 28 December said the burned light had been destroyed by fire at Christmas, and that a letter to the Treasury indicated the tender Violet had carried "the President and Secretaries" to the scene.[^13]

The Bulletins record several rescues by the keepers in the 1920s. In February 1926 William G. Rollinson and his assistant John L. Callis helped float a vessel near the station.[^14] On the night of 19 June 1926 Rollinson rendered assistance to the yacht Elizabeth when it suffered engine trouble, obtained medical aid for a lady on board who had been injured, and accommodated the party at the station overnight.[^15] In August he helped float a schooner that had run aground nearby.[^16]

On 13 July 1929 James W. Simpson, keeper, and Franklin S. Barnes, assistant keeper, helped rescue two young men from drowning near the light station.[^17][^18] Riprap was added against the ice, 774.4 cubic yards in 1906, according to LighthouseFriends.[^5]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the light was automated in 1942.[^5] It also says the light was changed in August 1948 to a white skeleton tower on brow piles.[^5] The Lighthouse Directory has the cottage demolished in 1951, with a steel skeletal tower built on the old foundation.[^4] The two sources disagree on the date of the tower.

## Today

The Directory lists the light as active, with a green flash every 2.5 seconds and a focal plane of 38 feet (12 m).[^4] It is accessible only by boat. It can be seen from Cedar Beach on the Maryland side and from the Harry Nice Bridge.[^4] The site and tower are closed, and the Coast Guard owns the site.[^4]
