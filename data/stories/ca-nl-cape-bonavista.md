Cape Bonavista is the northeastern tip of the Bonavista Peninsula in Newfoundland, and its lighthouse is a wooden house with a stone tower rising through the middle, painted in red and white stripes. Lit in the early 1840s, it was kept for most of a century by one family and then by a man who started out tending its fog alarm.[^1][^2]

## The place

The light stands between Bonavista Bay and Trinity Bay. It was meant to mark the entrances to both bays and to help vessels bound north for Labrador.[^3] By the 1840s the nearby town of Bonavista had about 2,000 people, according to LighthouseFriends, and the colony's Commissioners of Lighthouses judged that a light was needed near the cape.[^1]

## Building the light

In April 1841 the Newfoundland legislature passed an act to provide for a lighthouse on or near Cape Bonavista, and authorised raising £1755 for the work. The commissioners' secretary, Ambrose Shea, went out in July 1841 to choose a site. He weighed Cape L'Argent, Cape Bonavista and Cape Island, a small island just off the cape. Cape Bonavista won because Cape L'Argent was foggier more often and Cape Island would have needed an expensive bridge.[^1]

John Saunders won the £717 building contract. The design came from Trinity House in England, and the building resembled earlier Newfoundland lights at Cape Spear and Harbour Grace Island.[^1] The result is a square, two-storey house about 9 m (30 ft) on a side, built around a central circular tower.[^1] The register entry lists a cut-stone, glass and copper-hooded lantern, a stone foundation and red and white stripes among its defining features.[^2]

The light was meant to open in June 1842, but the commissioners decided the lantern needed a stone parapet instead of a wooden one, and the cut stone had to come from Halifax. It reached St. John's in early September, and bad weather then held it up for weeks.[^1] LighthouseFriends then describes surveyor Patrick Kough leaving for Bonavista on 1 November with the newly appointed keeper, Jeremiah White. It gives no year for that voyage, and its account places a May 1842 completion after it, so the order of events is unclear in that source. Kough found the tower masonry poorly done and judged that it would have to be taken down, and the work was put off until spring. A brick flue was run up from the fireplace so that White could stay in the building, which kept vandals out; they had been breaking in. White was told to keep the seaward shutters closed and show no light, so as not to deceive any vessel sailing that way.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says Kough and Robert Oke of Harbour Grace Island lighthouse oversaw the lantern and apparatus in May 1842 and dates the opening to 11 September 1842.[^1] The Canadian Society for Civil Engineering and the Institution of Civil Engineers, on their commemorative plaque, say Oke installed the apparatus in 1843, and the provincial register and the National Trust of Canada also give 1843 for completion.[^3][^2][^4] Sources therefore disagree on whether the light first showed in 1842 or 1843.

The apparatus was a revolving array of reflectors from Scotland's Bell Rock Lighthouse, bought on Robert Stevenson's advice. The CSCE dates the Bell Rock lighting to 1811, while the 1889 inspector's remark quoted below says 1810.[^3][^1] LighthouseFriends says it had sixteen Argand lamps and gave alternating red and white flashes every two minutes, and that the commissioners put the final cost at £3028, nearly double the estimate. They defended the outlay by saying the colony had "better value" here than at its other lights.[^1]

## The keepers

Jeremiah White emigrated from Ireland to Newfoundland and was fifty-one when he became first keeper. He and his wife Mary had four children: Nicholas, Matthew, Johanna and Thomas.[^1] Each of the three sons later had charge of the light for roughly a decade, and LighthouseFriends lists them in turn: Nicholas from 1867, Matthew from 1878 and Thomas from 1888.[^1] The National Trust says Thomas died in 1895 and was replaced by his son Fred, bringing the family's service at Cape Bonavista to seventy-five years.[^4]

A two-storey mansard-roofed dwelling was added in 1885, joined to the lighthouse by a covered passage; LighthouseFriends says Matthew, then keeper, and his younger brother Thomas, then assistant, much appreciated the extra space.[^1] In 1888 the inspector, John Nevill, reported that Matthew had gone on leave for ill health, and that when rest did not help Thomas was made sole keeper with a salary large enough to pay for the help he needed. Matthew received a retiring allowance, and Nevill called him "a faithful keeper for many years."[^1]

The fog alarm brought another family. A diaphone fog signal was installed in 1913 on Cape Island, joined to the cape by a wooden footbridge, and Hubert Abbott, a Bonavista man aged eighteen, was hired to tend it.[^1][^4] Abbott took charge of the light and the fog alarm in 1923 and served until he retired in 1960.[^1] LighthouseFriends names John Shelley as keeper between 1918 and 1920.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The characteristic was a point of complaint for years. The gap between flashes was cut from two minutes to ninety seconds on 1 October 1860.[^1] In 1889 Nevill wrote that the machine turning the light was "getting very much worn", having run since 1810 at Bell Rock. In 1895 an overhauled apparatus came from Harbour Grace Island to replace it, six Argand burners and reflectors that originally served the Isle of May in Scotland.[^1] The CSCE plaque dates that Isle of May array to 1816.[^3]

In 1919 a storm sent waves over Cape Island. LighthouseFriends reports that the dwelling there was destroyed and the fog alarm damaged, and that Abbott and his family escaped with little more than their lives. The fog alarm was afterwards moved to a spot just south of the lighthouse.[^1]

## After the keepers

The lamps and reflectors were replaced by an electric light in 1962, and a steel skeletal tower was built beside the lighthouse in 1966 to carry an automated light.[^1] The CSCE's site description also gives 1962 for the end of the historic apparatus (its plaque wording misprints 1862) and says the lighthouse served nearly 120 years.[^3] The old building passed to the provincial government and was designated a Provincial Historic Site on 9 April 1970.[^1][^2] From 1975 a crew led by Willis Hicks spent four years restoring it, using mostly local labour, and the 1895 apparatus went back into the lantern room.[^1]

On 3 August 2001 lightning struck the cape repeatedly, including three hits on the lantern room, and started a fire that the fire department put out. The lighting apparatus was out of the lantern for restoration at the time.[^1]

## Today

The old lighthouse is a Provincial Historic Site, run as a museum where guides in period costume lead visitors through the rooms, and the National Trust describes it as an experience of a keeper's day in 1870.[^1][^4] The province's tourism listing gives the season as 16 May to 9 October, with an admission charge, and says visitors can climb the stone tower and see the seal-oil-fuelled reflector apparatus.[^5] The working light is the automated one on the steel tower, and the Canadian Coast Guard's list of lights still carries a Cape Bonavista Light Station and Fog Signal.[^6] Check the opening dates with the site before travelling.
