Two range lights on the Lake Superior mainland were built to guide ships into the harbor behind Grand Island, beside M-28 just east of Christmas, in Alger County, Michigan.[^1][^5] The front light stands at the water's edge on a white cylindrical tower.[^2][^3] The rear light stands on a conical steel tower that was built about 125 yards (114 m) behind the keeper's dwelling, which was later moved away.[^1]

## The place

In 1863 a committee charged with reporting on the Great Lakes lights wrote, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, that the shore between White Fish Point and Copper Harbor had "no other harbor of refuge on this bare, rocky, and inhospitable coast."[^1] The committee said that both entrances to the harbor, east and west, "must be lighted to make them secure." It wanted range lights on Williams Island, to mark the west entrance and "to help mariners avoid shoal ground near the southwest end of Grand Island." The Lighthouse Board chose the mainland instead, just west of the island.[^1]

The range stands on a swampy parcel of 115.5 acres, which the government bought in 1866.[^1] Bay Furnace, a stone blast furnace where pig iron was made between 1870 and 1877, lies just west of the range lights.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $6,000 for "one or two beacon lights" in 1860, after a petition from masters, pilots and owners of vessels navigating Lake Superior.[^1] The sum was deemed insufficient, and on 28 July 1866 Congress provided another $10,000.[^1]

The work was to be done in 1867 by a work party engaged at Gull Rock Light, but the foreman, William Tunbridge, drowned, and the work was postponed to 1868.[^1] During the summer of 1868 a seven-room, one-and-a-half-story dwelling went up for the rear light, with a rectangular tower set on one of its gabled ends, and a wooden tower was built for the front light. The range first displayed its fixed white lights on 15 August 1868.[^1] A Munising visitor guide gives 1867 as the year the range was established, but its page is undated.[^3] The two lights are separated by 375 feet (114 m), with a plank walk between them. A boathouse was built near the front light in 1890, and in 1905 a brick cellar and a 500-gallon oil house of concrete blocks were added.[^1] In 1891 the Lighthouse Board's report records a hoisting winch purchased for the delivery of supplies.[^6]

## The keepers

The archive's roster of keepers lists six principal keepers in order, beginning with Frederick M. Giertz in 1868.[^5] In 1876 Napoleon Beedon and Casper Kuhn swapped station assignments, with Beedon going to Au Sable Lighthouse and Kuhn, who had been its head keeper since it was established in 1874, coming to the range lights.[^1][^5]

LighthouseFriends says Kuhn served nineteen years at the range lights, longer than any other keeper, and died in 1895. The roster's dates, 1876 to 1894, give eighteen years, and the roster ends his stint in 1894.[^1][^5]

Frank Brannock followed in 1895 and served until 1902. Timothy E. Dee came next, and the roster ends his time in 1913.[^5] The roster lists no assistant keepers at this station.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources record the drowning of William Tunbridge, the foreman of the work party, but they do not say where it happened.[^1] No wreck or rescue tied to the range lights appears in the sources for this story.

## After the keepers

In 1913 the range was de-staffed under a plan that put a conical steel tower about 125 yards (114 m) behind the keeper's dwelling to house the rear light.[^1] On 15 August 1913 the illuminant was changed to acetylene, and the characteristics changed too: the front light became a white flash every second, and the rear light alternated on and off each second.[^1] The 1913 Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists the Grand Island Harbor Range among the lights changed from oil to acetylene.[^7] The keeper's dwelling was kept for some time afterward, and then sold and moved to Munising, where it is a residence.[^1]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' list of lights in commission on 30 June 1915, under its acetylene heading, still shows the range with two lights.[^8] In 1920 a white mast, fitted with a slatted daymark and tank house, replaced the wooden front tower.[^1] The range was electrified in 1941.[^1] In 1969 the rear light was discontinued, and the front light was modified and renamed Bay Furnace Directional Light, showing colored sectors instead of a fixed white light.[^1] The present cylindrical tower replaced the front light in 1983.[^1]

## Today

The towers are owned by the Forest Service and managed by the Hiawatha National Forest.[^1] The 2025 Light List gives the front light as Bay Furnace Light, a white flash every 2.5 seconds, 20 feet (6 m) above the water, on a white cylindrical tower.[^2] The undated Munising visitor guide says both lights are now inactive.[^3] The two sources disagree, and the story cannot settle which is right, so check the light's status before relying on it.[^2][^3]

LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the towers closed.[^1] The Munising guide says the Forest Service offers periodic free tours of the rear light in summer, subject to staffing.[^3] Ask the Munising Ranger District before you go. The towers are closed, so do not climb either one.[^1][^3]

The rear light's National Park Service register record gives 26 June 1990 as its date published, and names it the Grand Island Harbor Rear Range Light.[^4]
