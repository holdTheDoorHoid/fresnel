Bloody Point Bar Light is a cast-iron tower on a caisson, standing in about seven feet of water off the southern tip of Kent Island, where it marks the entrance to Eastern Bay on the upper Chesapeake.[^1][^2][^3] It was first lit in 1882. A fire gutted it in 1960, and in 2018 the Coast Guard completed a warning light to replace it.[^2][^3]

## The place

The light sits at the edge of the shipping channel, east-southeast of Kent Point.[^3] A red sector, added in 1888, covers the shoals off Poplar Island, and the station is close to one of the bay's deepest channels, which LighthouseFriends gives as 174 feet.[^2]

Petitions for a light at the point reached the Lighthouse Board, and the Board first proposed one in its report for 1868. Congress did not pay for it until 1881.[^3][^2]

## Building the light

Congress first appropriated funds "for the erection of a lighthouse and fog-bell on Bloody Point Bar, Kent Island, Chesapeake Bay," in the amount of $25,000, on 3 March 1881.[^3] The contract went to Thomas Evans. The plan was similar to that of the Sharps Island light, and the ironwork came from the same supplier.[^3][^2]

Work began on 5 June 1882. Twenty-two piles were driven into the bottom, and the cast-iron cylinder, 30 feet (9 m) across, was lowered into position and filled with more than 700 cubic yards of concrete.[^3][^2] A 37-foot (11 m) cast-iron tower, lined with brick, rose on top of it.[^3]

The fourth-order Fresnel lens was first shown on 1 October 1882.[^3] J. Regester & Sons of Baltimore cast the fog bell that year.[^3][^2]

The structure began to lean early. LighthouseFriends and the Chapter's timeline date the storms that caused it to 1883. The 1996 National Register nomination dates them to 29 February and 3 March 1884.[^2][^4][^3] The sources also disagree on the size of the lean: LighthouseFriends says five feet at the lantern, and the nomination about six degrees.[^2][^3] In November 1884 sand was dredged from under the high side. In spring 1885, 760 tons of larger stones were laid as a scour apron.[^3][^2] LighthouseFriends says the tower has stayed within two degrees of vertical since then. The nomination found it leaning about two degrees south in 1996.[^2][^3]

## The keepers

The archive lists principal keepers from 1882 to 1960, with a long run of assistants and Coast Guard crew.[^1] The first principal keeper was Benjamin F. Lewis (1882 to 1883). Elisha T. Shockley served longest as principal keeper, from 1904 to 1928.[^1]

In 1939 the keepers rescued people from a stranded boat. LighthouseFriends says Keeper Hiram White and his assistant, "Louis Carmen," took in Mrs. Carl J. Hammersla and eight others from her 45-foot (14 m) boat, and the Chapter says nine people in all.[^2][^4] Mrs. Hammersla remarked that the floors and walls were so spotless "you could eat from them."[^2] The archive's roster makes Lewis R. Carman the head keeper that year, with White as first assistant, so the two accounts differ on who was in charge.[^1]

In 1948 a Baltimore Sun article described the station through its keeper, Tom White. The nomination quotes the article. In White's words, "Inside, the lighthouse is made up of five rooms," one of them below the waterline. The nomination describes two radio sets, one on standby and one running day and night, "bringing the latest information on matters concerning navigation in the bay and rivers."[^3] The archive lists Leslie F. Garrish as head keeper from 1947 to 1950. No Tom White appears on its roster.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 says the fog bell's striking machine was replaced by one of improved pattern, and the copper smoke pipe was renewed.[^5][^3] In 1901 the Board recorded soundings around the light and various repairs.[^6]

On 30 April 1960 an electrical short started a fire in the light.[^3][^2][^4] The two Coast Guardsmen on duty tried to fight it, but the smoke drove them out.[^3][^2] LighthouseFriends describes an escape boat that could not clear its davits until a large wave freed it, and says the two reached Tilghman Island safely.[^2] The nomination says they escaped in an outboard motor boat.[^3] LighthouseFriends names the two as Mark Mighall and Heywood Savage. The nomination calls the first Mark Mitchell (Mighall), and the archive lists them as Mark L. Mighell and Haywood H. Savage, both to 1960.[^2][^3][^1]

The nomination says the fire spread toward propane tanks, which exploded shortly afterwards. LighthouseFriends describes a storage room with a 500-gallon fuel tank.[^3][^2] The lens toppled into the blazing interior, and the fire left only a cast-iron shell.[^3][^2]

## After the keepers

Until the fall of 1961 a temporary buoy with a flashing light marked the spot. Then a lamp and lens were installed and the station was automated.[^3][^2]

The 1996 nomination describes the lamp as an incandescent bulb in a 300 mm acrylic lens, and LighthouseFriends says solar panels now power the lens.[^3][^2]

A 1996 National Register evaluation of the station found that the fire had compromised its integrity, so it was not eligible for listing.[^3]

In 2006 the General Services Administration put the light up for auction. A Nevada lawyer, Michael Gabriel, bought it for $100,000.[^2]

In August 2009 Gabriel announced plans to turn a room in each of his offshore lights into a brewery that would use water from a desalination unit. LighthouseFriends says nothing was ever done to rehabilitate or stabilize the lighthouse.[^2]

A large section of the tower had broken away by 2017, exposing the interior, according to a photograph from that year. LighthouseFriends says the damage had happened more than a year earlier.[^2]

In early 2018 the Coast Guard completed a warning light, the Bloody Point Bar Warning Light, because the tower had become too hazardous to keep in service.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is closed to the public and can be reached only by boat.[^1][^3]

LighthouseFriends reports that only a single steel ladder and the exposed cast-iron plates remain. The cantilevered lantern deck is in relatively good condition.[^2]

The 1996 nomination names the Coast Guard's Fifth District as the owner. LighthouseFriends reports the 2006 sale at auction to Michael Gabriel, but none of the sources read says who holds the tower today.[^3][^2]
