Granite Island Light stands on a small granite island in Lake Superior, 12.5 statute miles north of Marquette, Michigan.[^6] Its square stone tower is joined to the keeper's house.[^1] The light was first shown at the opening of navigation in 1869.[^9] Keepers lived on the island until the late 1930s.[^1][^6] The historic tower is now private property, and a separate Coast Guard light stands close beside it.[^1][^7]

## The place

In 1866 the Lighthouse Board asked for a light on the island.[^6] LighthouseFriends quotes the request: the Marquette light "cannot be seen by vessels coming from Portage until they are almost abreast of the light and have passed Granite island, which is directly in their track."[^6] Congress provided the money on 2 March 1867.[^6] The island was privately owned, so the State of Michigan had to condemn it.[^6] Governor Henry H. Crapo signed the patent for the island on 16 April 1868.[^9]

The island was a hazard even after the light.[^9] As the owners' page transcribes it, the Board's report for 1870 says the island lies off the general line of the coast, so that in foggy weather, "in the absence of an efficient fog signal," it is "a serious obstruction to navigation." The Board submitted an estimate for a fog signal that year.[^9]

## Building the light

As the owners' page transcribes it, the Board's report for 1868 describes the difficulty of the landing.[^9] Whatever was landed had to be moved from the steamer to the top of the island, which the Board described as "sixty feet above the water" (about 18 m).[^9] A platform was built ten feet (3 m) above the lake, "from which a track was laid to the summit of the island," where a portable steam engine was placed to haul the materials up.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the newly acquired steam tender *Haze* "delivered the construction materials to Granite Island from Detroit shortly after the opening of the 1868 navigation season."[^6]

The tower was built of rough-cut granite from the Huron Islands, with cut limestone at the corners and as lintels above and below the doors and windows.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the tower stands forty feet tall.[^6] Rowlett's directory gives it as "40 ft (12 m) square cylindrical granite tower," while the USLHS place record gives 34 feet (10.4 m) from base to the centre of the lantern.[^7][^1] The original optic was a fourth-order Fresnel lens, installed in 1869.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the flash panels around the lens turned once in four and a half minutes.[^6] The Board's list of lights for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 gives the characteristic as "Fourth order, fixed white, varied by a red flash every ninety seconds."[^3]

A landing derrick was also put up on the island.[^2] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 records that one "was put up" to help deliver supplies, and that its timber "was framed at the light-house depot in Detroit."[^2] A fog bell from Thunder Bay Island was set up at this station in 1879.[^9] A brick oil house went up in 1905, and a metal fog-signal tower in 1910.[^1][^7]

## The keepers

The roster the USLHS archive prints for the station opens with Isaac P. Bridges as principal keeper, 1868 to 1872.[^1] Bridges drowned on 30 August 1872 on his way home from Garlic Point.[^6][^9] The owners' history page, which transcribes the station's records, says his family "saw him capsize and could not help him."[^9]

Two women appear on the roster.[^1] Rozelia A. Wilson is listed as first assistant from 1873 to 1876.[^1] In 1903 Annie M. Carlson is listed as acting assistant for that one year.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives her name as Anna Carlson, the wife of the Marquette Harbor keeper, who filled in after the assistant drowned until a permanent one was appointed.[^6]

The Wheatleys kept the station, father and son, from 1885 to 1915.[^1] William H. Wheatley was head keeper from 1885 to 1893.[^1] His father, James, "was hired to serve as his son's assistant at Granite Island."[^6] In 1893 William was transferred to Marquette Harbor, and James became head keeper, a post the roster gives him until 1915.[^6][^1] LighthouseFriends says James died in Marquette in 1915.[^6]

The last head keeper was John A. McDonald, on the roster from 1928 to 1937.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls him "John McDonald, the last head keeper of Granite Island Lighthouse."[^6] In 1930 the assistant John W. Kirkendall asked to have his family at the station from 20 June to 1 September.[^9] The recommendation on the request said, "Conditions on Granite Island during that period are very pleasant."[^9] His son Grant spent summers on the island and later recorded his memories. He remembered that "When the Amaranth came, the coal for the furnace had to be carried up the stairs from the dock to the house in canvas bags."[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 30 April 1898 William H. Wheatley, then keeper of the Marquette light, drowned.[^6] LighthouseFriends says his boat "capsized in a squall" while he was fishing north of Marquette.[^6] The owners' history page says he "drowned in a sailboat on his way out to visit his father James Wheatley."[^9] The two accounts differ on how he came to be on the water.

The Life-Saving Service's record for its Marquette station that year describes the next step.[^4] The station's keeper went out to the island "with the steam launch" and "brought ashore the light keeper to take charge of the body of his son, the keeper of the Marquette light, who was drowned April 30."[^4] That record puts the island "10 miles to the northward" of Marquette. LighthouseFriends gives 12.5 statute miles, and Rowlett gives 6 miles (10 km) off Thoney Point.[^4][^6][^7]

On 2 October 1903 the assistant keeper John McMartin took the station's boat from the boathouse and started round toward the island's south side, intending to go to Marquette.[^9] The station's report of the death, dated 5 October and transcribed on the owners' page, reads: "Fresh wind from N.E. Sea caught boat and dashed it against [illegible] of rocks, boat smashed to pieces and Mr. J. McMartin was drowned."[^9] LighthouseFriends says "McMartin plunged into the icy water and drowned."[^6] The log for 6 October records that the steamer *Marigold* "landed a new boat to replace the one McMartin was lost out of."[^9]

## After the keepers

On 26 August 1914, LighthouseFriends says, the light's characteristic changed to "fixed white with a red flash every sixty seconds," and its illuminant changed to oil vapor.[^6] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1914 lists "Lights changed from oil to incandescent oil vapor: Granite Island" among the changes.[^5]

In 1937 the station was automated, with acetylene and "a new characteristic of a white flash every fifteen seconds."[^6] In 1943 the carbon-dioxide fog bell was discontinued, and "the light's characteristic was changed to a white flash every five seconds."[^6] Rowlett's directory says the historic light has been "Inactive since 1939."[^7] The sources therefore disagree about when the old light stopped working.

The NPGallery asset record gives the station's National Register listing as published on 4 August 1983.[^8] LighthouseFriends gives 1969.[^6] The island and light were sold by sealed bid in 1999. LighthouseFriends gives the highest bid, $86,003, as Scott Holman's, and Rowlett's directory describes Holman as a director of the Great Lakes Shipwreck Historical Society.[^6][^7] Restoration followed. "Over the next two-and-a-half years the team transformed the structure," LighthouseFriends says, beginning in 2000.[^6]

## Today

The island and the historic lighthouse are privately owned.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the grounds, dwelling and tower are closed, and that the best views come from the water.[^6] Rowlett's directory says the island is "Accessible only by boat," with the site open and the tower closed.[^7] The two sources disagree about the grounds. The USLHS record marks the station as not open to the public.[^1] Do not land on the island or climb the tower without the owner's permission.

The Coast Guard's light near the old tower is a steel skeletal tower built in 1995.[^7] Rowlett's directory describes it as active, showing a white flash every six seconds.[^7] Rowlett lists that tower separately from the 1869 station, which it describes as inactive.[^7]
