The North Channel Front Range Light stands on the northwest side of Grosse Ile, in the Detroit River, Wayne County, Michigan.[^1][^2] It is an octagonal wooden tower, rebuilt in 1906, and the only surviving structure of a pair of range lights that marked a channel north of the island.[^1][^2] The rear tower was later demolished.[^2] The front tower belongs to the township and is open only by appointment.[^2]

## The place

Grosse Ile is the largest island in the Detroit River and the most populated island in Michigan.[^1] The long spits that run out under water from Fighting Island were a hazard to steamers.[^4] The first range on the island was set up on Hennepin Point in 1891.[^1][^2] The Light-House Board's 1892 report describes that range: "The middle of the channel from Ballards Reef to the south end of Fighting Island is now marked by a range on Grosse Isle."[^3]

In the spring of 1891 the private lights were not put in operation until a few nights after navigation opened. "During those few nights several large vessels met with disaster," the Board reported. The passenger steamer City of Detroit was damaged by "striking a rock just above the Limekiln Crossing," at a point where, the Board said, this "would not have happened if Ballards Reef had then been lighted."[^4] The same report notes that "steamers frequently ground on these spits" at the ends of Fighting Island.[^4]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $2,500 on 5 August 1892 for a second pair of range lights, "to make a range to mark the center of the channel from the foot of Fighting Island to Mamajuda Light."[^5] The Board's 1893 report says "the rear beacon of this range is to be on land," and that "the front beacon is to stand in the water."[^5]

The 1894 report says that "a site for the rear beacon was purchased," and that the bottom for the front beacon, near the shore, "was ceded to the United States by the State of Michigan."[^6] The Board's 1895 report gives the first exhibition: "The two beacon lights of this range, completed in June, 1894, were first exhibited on July 16, 1894."[^7] The 1894 pair took the name North Channel range, and the first pair became the South Channel range.[^1]

During the fiscal year 1895 the open framework of each beacon was enclosed and painted, and a balcony with a hand rail was fitted under the lanterns.[^7] The rear beacon had a small frame building that served as a store and as a shelter for the keeper, "there being no keeper's dwelling here."[^7] In the fiscal year 1896 a brick cistern holding 2,000 gallons was built, and plans were drawn for a keeper's dwelling and a boathouse.[^9]

Congress provided $3,500 for the keeper's dwelling in February 1903.[^1] The dwelling was put up by hired labour beside the rear light, and the Water Trails essay says a larger house was built on the property in 1904.[^1][^2] The front tower was rebuilt on concrete foundations in 1906, and that is the octagonal tower that stands today.[^1]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends names three keepers: Eugene H. Watson (1894 to 1898), Thomas L. Kean (1898 to 1911) and James T. Story (1911 to 1920).[^1] The annual reports cited here do not name Watson or Kean.

Story has the fullest record. He had been in charge of the North Channel range from 1911, stayed on as keeper of the Grosse Isle light, and kept that post until the light was automated in 1920. He then moved to the Windmill Point light, where the Commissioner's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1922 names him as assistant keeper.[^1][^11]

Water Trails says the keeper was in charge of all upkeep at the lights. Oil for the lamps was stored in metal structures near them, and glycerin was used to clean the windows and "keep them from freezing during the winter."[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources read give no dated storm, wreck or rescue at the North Channel range itself. The Board's report for fiscal 1895 does record damage at the neighbouring South Channel range: on 3 September 1894 its rear structure was "blown down," and a temporary structure was erected. That was the other range, not this one.[^8]

## After the keepers

The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported on works in the Fighting Island channel, begun in the fall of 1916 and "completed in June, 1921." Four ranges that had been "formerly attended by keepers" were replaced by unattended acetylene lights. "At Grosse Isle an acetylene light in a fifth-order lens was installed in the old frame tower." The report does not say which Grosse Isle range this was.[^10]

LighthouseFriends dates the automation to 1920, the year Story left. The Commissioner's report gives June 1921 as the completion of the work.[^1][^10] LighthouseFriends also says the rear range was discontinued in 1917.[^1] The Water Trails essay ties the end of the North Channel, and of its rear light, to the opening of the Livingstone Channel in 1912.[^2] The two accounts give different dates for the rear light's end.

Water Trails says the light was put out for good in 1963, and that the Coast Guard removed its Fresnel lens.[^2]

## Today

In 1965 the township bought the light from the Interior Department for $350, with money from the Grosse Ile Historical Society. The Society now maintains the light.[^2] Entry is by appointment only, but the light can easily be viewed from the water.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says the tower cannot easily be seen from land, because of recent housing construction, and that the Coast Guard's 2013 Light List carried it as a "private aid to navigation."[^1] The two accounts do not agree about whether the light has been shown since 1963.
