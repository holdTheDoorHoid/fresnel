The Craighill Channel Upper Range Rear Light is an iron-framed range tower on land at the head of Old Road Bay, near Sparrows Point in Baltimore County, Maryland.[^2] The National Register calls it the Cut-off Channel Range Rear Light Station.[^2] It was first lit on 15 January 1886, and it is still an active aid to navigation.[^2][^4]

## The place

The rear light stands 1.3 miles (2.1 km) northwest of its front partner.[^2] The two lights work together to guide ships into a cut-off channel that runs into the Patapsco River, which cuts several miles off the route to Baltimore.[^2] The cut-off joined the Craighill and Brewerton channels.[^4]

## Building the light

After Congress appropriated $25,000 for range lights at the "new cut-off from Craighill Channel, approaches to Baltimore," arrangements were made to buy a site for the rear beacon at "Holly Grove, Patapsco River."[^2] Work on the beacon and its dwelling began in September 1885.[^2]

The tower is an iron skeleton frame around an inner wooden shaft, covered with corrugated iron, and it rests on stone and brick piers.[^2] The steam launch Nettle carried workmen between Baltimore and the site.[^2] Both beacons were first lighted on 15 January 1886, though the dwellings were not finished. All work was completed by the end of June.[^2]

The sources give the light's height several ways. The nomination cites Board reports for 1885 and 1886 that put the rear range 65 feet (20 m) above the water, and LighthouseFriends gives the same 65 feet. The Chapter's page gives 64 feet (19.5 m) above mean water. The nomination's own description gives 75 feet (23 m) to the light, and the Chapter's timeline gives 74 feet (22.6 m).[^2][^3][^4][^5]

The original lamp was a locomotive headlight that showed white. The nomination says it has since been replaced by a DCB 24 beacon.[^2][^5] A dwelling, storehouse and privy were built beside the light in 1885.[^2]

In 1890 brick walks were laid, and a plank walk ran from the tower to the water's edge.[^2] In 1895 the Board recorded that an iron oil house with room for 55 five-gallon cans was erected at the Cutoff Channel range rear.[^15]

## The keepers

The archive lists three principal keepers: John Peterson (1886 to 1892), Charles A. Green (1892 to 1918) and Albert "Allie" L. Davis (1919 to 1922).[^1]

John Peterson was the first principal keeper of the rear light. The Chesapeake Chapter's biography says he had been first assistant and then principal keeper at Seven Foot Knoll, and that he moved to Maryland Point in 1892 and to Hawkins Point in 1896. It says he died on duty at Hawkins Point in February 1915.[^6] The archive gives his salary as $520 at the start and $560 at the end.[^7]

Charles A. Green came to the rear light from Lazaretto Point in 1892 and retired from it in 1918. The archive's record gives his salary as $560 at the start and $576 at the end.[^8] LighthouseFriends ends his service in 1919.[^3]

Allie Davis served here as principal keeper from 1919 to 1922, when he was transferred. The archive's person record shows him at Hog Island before this post, and at the Annapolis depot from 1922 to 1933.[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 13 December 1919 Davis towed a disabled motor boat, with three people aboard, into Jones Creek.[^10]

In 1921 Davis towed a disabled seaplane into Jones Creek.[^11] The Bulletin's index lists him as keeper of the Cutoff Channel Range Rear who "tows planes."[^12]

In 1915 the Bulletin lists the rear light among those whose illuminant was changed, from acetylene to oil.[^13]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1930 reports that in 1929 a program of electrification took place in the approaches to Baltimore. In June the rear light was electrified, using commercial current carried over a land line, and it now showed a red light of 64,000 candlepower.[^14]

The nomination says the station was manned from its completion until automation in 1929.[^2] The archive and LighthouseFriends end the keepers in 1922, when Davis was transferred.[^1][^3] The Chapter's page says the light kept its keeper until 1933, when it was officially listed as "unmanned."[^4] The three dates do not agree.

The nomination says the keeper's dwelling was torn down in the 1920s.[^2] The gallery that once encircled the tower has been lost, and the windows are covered with plywood.[^2][^3] In 2021 both upper range lights were re-lamped with LED fixtures.[^4]

## Today

The Coast Guard's aid-to-navigation team was responsible for the property when the nomination was reviewed in 2002, and the form lists the property as federally owned.[^2] Access is through Sparrow Point Boulevard, inside the grounds of the Sparrows Point Bethlehem Steel Plant.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the light is best seen from the water.[^3]

The Chapter says the site and tower are closed to the public.[^4] It also says both upper range lights exhibit a fixed red light, 24 hours a day.[^4]
