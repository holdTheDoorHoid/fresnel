Toronto Harbour Light stands at the outer end of the Leslie Street Spit, at Vicki Keith Point, where it marks the eastern entrance to Toronto's harbour.[^1][^3] It is a 12 m (39 ft) tower, white with a red upper part, that shows a red flash every ten seconds.[^2]

## The place

The gap the light marks is the Eastern Gap.[^3] The Lighthouse Directory says a storm created it in 1858 as an entrance to the inner harbour.[^3]

The spit itself is man-made.[^4] In 1959, as the St. Lawrence Seaway opened, the Toronto Harbour Commission began building it out into the lake for "port-related facilities."[^4] By the early 1970s, the park's own history says, "it was clear that a site for port-related facilities wasn't needed." In August 1973 the province made the Toronto and Region Conservation Authority its agent for a proposed aquatic park, which became Tommy Thompson Park.[^4]

## Building the light

Lights were on the gap long before the present tower.[^3] The Lighthouse Directory dates the Eastern Gap range station to 1895, with the front tower dated 1898 and the rear tower 1906.[^3] In the spring of 1900 the Department set a fog bell on the front range tower, on a platform "fixed to the framework on the lake side of the tower," 7.6 m (25 ft) above the water.[^5]

The Department's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1905 records a diaphone fog alarm at the East Gap, put in operation "on the opening of navigation in 1905." It gave one blast of seven seconds every 45 seconds, from a square, white-painted building on the beach "immediately east of the eastern pier." A Toronto contractor, Jesse Tulloch, carried out the building work for $1,808.36.[^6]

In the report printed in 1908, a fourth-order lens with an occulting screen and a petroleum-vapour light was installed at the Eastern Gap.[^7] In the report printed in 1910, a wooden house for the fog alarm engineer went up at the East Gap station, built by contract for $3,295.78.[^8] In the report printed in 1916, a long-focus reflector and lantern box went on a steel tower owned by the Hydro-Electric System, "to form back range light."[^9]

The present tower came in 1974. LighthouseFriends says the Toronto Harbour Commission marked the outer end of the headland that year "with a light mounted atop a hexagonal concrete tower."[^1] The Lighthouse Directory describes a 12 m (40 ft) "octagonal steel tower."[^3] The Coast Guard's list gives a "White hexagonal tower with red upper portion," 12.0 m high.[^2] The sources disagree on the shape and the material. The sources do not say whether the 1974 tower stands on the ground the range towers used.

## The keepers

No keeper is named for the 1974 tower in the sources. The earlier Eastern Gap fog alarm had a keeper.[^10] The keeper statement in the report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1906 (printed 1907) lists "McKelvie, Geo" at the "Eastern Gap Fog Alarm, Toronto."[^10] The statement in the report printed in 1910 lists "George McKelvie" at "Toronto, East Gap."[^11] The report printed in 1913 says the Eastern Gap wind gauge was "in charge of Mr. McKelvie," and that it logged about 176,500 km (109,710 miles) of wind in twelve months.[^12]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the 1912 navigation season, storm signals were displayed at both Queen's Wharf and the Eastern Gap, on sixteen occasions in all. The heaviest gale, on 13 November, reached a maximum of 64 km/h (40 miles per hour), the Meteorological Service reported. The report does not say which station recorded that peak.[^13]

The Department's report for the fiscal year 1916-17, quoting the Toronto Observatory, says the heaviest general gale of that navigation season came on 22 November, "when the wind guage at the Eastern gap recorded west 50 miles per hour." The gale "was very successfully warned."[^14]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Directory says the two Eastern Gap range lights were deactivated in 1973. In 1981 they were moved to the Humber Bay area on the city's southwest side, one to the Mimico Cruising Club and one to the Etobicoke Yacht Club.[^3]

LighthouseFriends says the present tower is maintained in season by the Toronto Port Authority.[^1]

## Today

The City of Toronto owns the light, and the Port Authority operates it.[^1][^3] The Coast Guard lists it as a private aid, seasonal, operating around the clock.[^2]

The park's own site says public access "is restricted to weekends, holidays and weekdays from 4 pm to 9 pm, due to the truck traffic associated with the Port Authority's ongoing filling operations."[^4] Private vehicles are not allowed in the park, and the light is "4.8 km (3 miles) from the park entrance to the lighthouse."[^1] The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1][^3]
