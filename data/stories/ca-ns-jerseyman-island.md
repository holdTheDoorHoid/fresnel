Jerseyman Island light stands on Beach Point, at the north-west entrance to Arichat Harbour on Isle Madame, Nova Scotia.[^1][^2] It has shown a red light there since 1872. Its tower has been replaced twice: in 1950 (1953 in one table) and again in 2017.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Jerseyman Island is a crescent-shaped strip of land across the mouth of Arichat Harbour.[^3] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society describes its seaward side as "ringed with constantly breaking seas", fringed with reefs and shoals.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says the island is really three islands, and that the light was built on the northern tip of the third, which lies north of the other two, to mark Crid Passage, the northwest entrance to Arichat Harbour.[^1] The Preservation Society puts the light at the north end of West Jerseyman Island.[^3]

The island's name is disputed. The Preservation Society says members of the Robin family named it when they came to Isle Madame from Jersey to set up a fish-processing business.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says it was named for the Jersey traders who settled there.[^1] Isle Madame Lighthouses gives the Jersey traders who had established fishing posts there in the late 1700s.[^2] A family-history essay on the Robin company says John and Charles Robin discovered Arichat Harbour in 1764 and named the island "Jerseyman's Island".[^4]

The island was also raided in the American Revolution. In September 1776, according to the same essay, John Paul Jones's boats surprised a fleet of Jersey vessels in the harbours of Arichat and Petit de Grat.[^4] The essay says the raid "ended Robin's business there", at the Robin fishing station on the island.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says John Paul Jones attacked the island, pillaging and burning several stores, and that its inhabitants moved to Isle Madame after the attack.[^1]

## Building the light

The Department of Marine's notice of the new light, as Lighthouse Friends prints it, called it "a new fixed red light, on the catoptric principle".[^1] It had two circular burner lamps with 20-inch reflectors, two flat-wick lamps with 16-inch reflectors, and a square wooden tower painted white.[^1] The light was first shown on 10 July 1872, under a keeper named Boudrot, who was paid $250 a year. The notice gives his initials as C. A.; the keepers' list gives Charles C.[^1] F. S. Cunningham built the tower and its attached dwelling for $1,200.[^1]

The lamps and reflectors stayed in the lantern from 1872 until 1902, about thirty years.[^1][^6] In its report for the year ended 30 June 1901, the Department said the six lamps "are to be replaced by a 7th order dioptric apparatus, which has been ordered from England".[^5] Its next report said a seventh-order dioptric lens "has been installed in the lighthouse, replacing the catoptric apparatus heretofore used".[^6] Lighthouse Friends gives 1902 for the lens as well.[^1] The Preservation Society's light table gives 1922 for the seventh-order lens, but the Department's 1902 report dates it earlier.[^3][^6]

General context, not a record of this station: a catoptric apparatus gathered its light with reflectors, and a dioptric one with lenses.[^7] An 1875 American survey of European lights noted that reflectors were still in use in many lights and were "considered better than lenses for some localities".[^7]

## The keepers

Charles Boudrot resigned after about a year, and Simon Babin took over.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says Babin died in 1877 and his son, Simon Babin Jr., became keeper. Isle Madame Lighthouses gives the elder Babin's term as September 1873 to September 1877, and the younger's as December 1877 to March 1883.[^1][^2]

George Vigneau kept the light from 1883 to 1905. In 1896 he was given a hand foghorn, which he was required to sound in thick weather "to answer signals from vessels".[^1] The Department's keepers' list for 1904 gives his name against Jerseyman Island.[^8]

Alphonse Theriault succeeded him in May 1905 and kept the light until January 1931, about twenty-six years.[^1][^2] The Department's keepers' table for 1911 lists him at Jerseyman Island.[^9] A short-lived keeper, Joseph Martell, served early in 1931. Irving R. Latimer followed that spring and kept the light until 1955.[^1][^2]

Russ Latimer, the keeper's son, later wrote a memoir of his boyhood there, which the Preservation Society has printed. In it the dwelling had "no electricity, telephone, plumbing or radio" when the family arrived, and the keeper's pay is given as $68 a month.[^3] The memoir has the family landing on 1 April 1930 and places the appointment in that year. Lighthouse Friends and Isle Madame Lighthouses both give 1931.[^1][^2][^3]

Abel LeBlanc was keeper from 1955 until the station was de-staffed in 1978.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A gale in August 1873 damaged the lighthouse and washed the oil store off its foundation.[^1] Afterwards cribwork was added around the tower, and it was anchored with cable stays (Isle Madame Lighthouses) or wire rope (Lighthouse Friends).[^1][^2] That is the only storm damage the sources read give a date for. None of the sources read ties a wreck or rescue to the station.

## After the keepers

In 1950 a new tower and a new keeper's house replaced the original station.[^1][^2][^3] The Preservation Society's light table dates the new tower to 1953.[^3] A battery-powered foghorn that ran around the clock was switched on in 1961. Lighthouse Friends says the fishermen appreciated it, while people in town did not.[^1] A submarine cable from the mainland brought commercial power in 1972, "to supply the lighthouse with commercial power".[^1]

The station was de-staffed in 1978.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends says this came after the boathouse was repurposed as an engine room for a diesel generator that would operate the light during power outages.[^1] The keeper's house was removed from the island in 1980. It was floated to Arichat, where it is now a private dwelling.[^2][^3]

By 2017 Lighthouse Friends says the Coast Guard felt the 1950 tower had deteriorated too far and had to be replaced.[^1] The two accounts disagree on how it came down. Lighthouse Friends says the wooden tower "toppled over in August 2017"; Isle Madame Lighthouses says it was demolished early that summer.[^1][^2] A replacement went up in September 2017. Lighthouse Friends describes four sections brought from Dartmouth and lifted into place by a Coast Guard helicopter; Isle Madame Lighthouses calls it a modular, aluminum-framed lighthouse.[^1][^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light.[^1] Its List of Lights shows it as seasonal, with a red flash of one second in each four-second period, a focal height of 7.6 m and a nominal range of 5 nautical miles. The tower, white with a red upper portion, is 7.8 m high.[^10]

Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower closed, and that the light is best seen by boat or from the eastern end of Veterans Memorial Drive in Arichat.[^1] The Preservation Society gives the site's access as "By Boat".[^3]
