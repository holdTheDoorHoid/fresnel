Cape Spencer Light is a concrete light-and-fog-signal building on a small rock off the outer coast of Southeast Alaska, built in the mid-1920s to mark the northerly entrance from the Pacific into the channels of Southeast Alaska.[^2][^8] Keepers and then Coast Guard crews lived on it from 1925 until the station was automated in 1974.[^1][^9]

## The place

LighthouseFriends calls the cape an important turning point for vessels sailing between Southcentral and Southeast Alaska.[^10] The Commissioner of Lighthouses described it in 1916 as "at the entrance to Cross Sound and Icy Strait", the way in for vessels running between Puget Sound ports and Prince William Sound, Seward, Cook Inlet and Kodiak, apart from an occasional freighter taking the outside route.[^3] The National Register nomination places the light station on "the outermost large islet south of Cape Spencer".[^2] LighthouseFriends says Captain George Vancouver named the cape on his 1793-94 exploration of the coast.[^10]

Before there was a lighthouse there was a small automatic lamp. The Lighthouse Service's 1913 notice of new aids lists Cape Spencer, Cross Sound, among the "lights established", a lens lantern burning acetylene.[^5] The US Lighthouse Society archive and the University of Alaska Anchorage finding aid for the station's engineering records both date the first light to 1913; LighthouseFriends says 1912.[^1][^9][^10] The Commissioner's estimates in the 1910s called it "a small unwatched light" and argued that "a large watched light and fog signal should be provided".[^3]

## Building the light

The request ran for years. The Commissioner's annual reports asked for $100,000 for a station "at or near Cape Spencer" in 1916, and for $165,000 by 1922.[^3][^4] In 1923 the Lighthouse Service printed a ship captain's account of why: arriving in fog or snow with no fog signal, a vessel had to stand off to sea until daylight, and in winter, he said, there were only six or seven hours of it. If the weather had not cleared, "it is almost impossible to find the entrance".[^6]

The UAA finding aid says Congress approved funds in 1923 and that work began the following year.[^9] LighthouseFriends adds that the first season, ending in September 1924, went on a landing platform, derrick, boathouse, blacksmith shop, tramway and the excavation for the foundation, and gives the cost as $174,881.[^10] A Bulletin item records the tender *Cedar* discharging materials for "the new light station at Cape Spencer" on 23 June 1925, and coal on 6 September.[^8]

The building is a one-story reinforced-concrete structure on concrete foundations, with a square concrete tower rising 25 feet (7.6 m) from the middle of its flat roof. It held the light, the fog signal and the keepers' quarters under one roof.[^2] Its white light was "110,000 candlepower" and sat about 105 feet (32 m) above the water; later the nomination records it raised to 200,000 and then 500,000 candlepower.[^2][^7] The sources disagree on the flash rate: the Lighthouse Service said in 1925 that it would flash every 10 seconds, with an air fog signal every 30 seconds, whereas LighthouseFriends gives 15 seconds.[^7][^10] The original lens was a third-order Fresnel, according to the US Lighthouse Society archive.[^1]

A Bulletin item from late 1925 called it "a primary light station" that "will perhaps be the most important aid to navigation in Alaska".[^7] The dates of first lighting differ too. One 1925 Bulletin item says the station "went into commission November 20", while the UAA finding aid and LighthouseFriends give 11 December 1925.[^7][^9][^10] The Bulletin's wording, in the same volume, that the station would be in "full commission during December" may reconcile them, but no source says so.[^7]

In July 1926 the station started Alaska's first radiobeacon, sending dashes in thick weather that ships with radio compasses could use at "distances up to 100 miles or more".[^8]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society archive lists George Alexius as the first principal keeper, from 1925 to 1930, with Charles McLeod as first assistant and Michael Connellan as second assistant, both listed as ending in 1926.[^1] The roster shows men moving up one rung at a time. Daniel Kinnaley appears as second assistant in 1928-29, first assistant 1929-32 and principal keeper 1932-34. Niels M. Norgaard rose from second assistant in 1932-33 to principal keeper for 1934-36 and again 1937-38. James H. Scriver went from second assistant in 1934-35 to principal keeper in 1938-40.[^1] The archive's lines overlap in places (two first assistants in 1936-37), which may be errors in the register transcription.[^1]

From 1959 the archive lists the station with Coast Guard crews, most of them there for about a year.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes their recollections. Paul Reager, there in 1946, remembered making a lemon pie from brass polish when supply deliveries were delayed by weather.[^10] Pat O'Brien, at the station in 1963-64, described a night tsunami warning that sent the crew off the rock in a 16-foot (5 m) outboard to an inlet called Dick's Arm; there was no wave.[^10] In 1968 Russell Newberry, who was in charge and supervised three younger men, told a reporter the winds ran 50 to 100 miles an hour and that the sun had last been seen 35 days earlier.[^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

None of the sources cited here ties a wreck to the station, but a January 1926 voyage shows what the light was for. Captain Jeremiah Flynn of the steamer *Alameda*, after "one of the stormiest voyages in his experience", told the Ketchikan *Chronicle* that the weather near the cape was so thick that without "the strong light" his ship would have had great difficulty finding its way to the inside waters, as the Bulletin reported.[^8]

## After the keepers

The station was automated in 1974 and the crew withdrawn.[^9][^10] LighthouseFriends says the Fresnel lens was removed the same year; the lens and its clockwork are now on display at the Alaska State Museum in Juneau.[^10] The light was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in December 1975, on a nomination prepared that March by the Alaska Division of Parks.[^2][^9] The nomination noted that most of the outbuildings, including the derrick, boathouse, blacksmith shop and tramway, still stood.[^2]

## Today

Cape Spencer is still an active aid to navigation, with a solar-powered optic in place of the Fresnel lens.[^1] It is owned by the Coast Guard, and LighthouseFriends reports the grounds, dwelling and tower closed; the US Lighthouse Society archive lists it as not open to the public.[^1][^10] LighthouseFriends places it within Glacier Bay National Park, and its own visit was by chartered flight from Gustavus; the island is Coast Guard property and not a place to land.[^10]
