A low gravel island in the northeast corner of Lake Michigan, seven miles northwest of Cross Village, carries a brick lighthouse, the third light tower on the island; the first light was shown there in 1850. French explorers called the island Isle aux Galets, "Isle of Pebbles"; English speakers wore the name down to Skillagalee, and both names are still used.[^1]

## The place

The island lies between Beaver Island and the Michigan mainland. Lighthousefriends describes it as gravelly and low-lying, with shallow water and dangerous reefs around it.[^1] The Lighthouse Service used both names for the station at first, Lighthousefriends says; from 1889 it used only Skillagalee, and from about 1910 Ile aux Galets again.[^1]

The first formal call for a light was probably a petition from "certain ship-owners on the northern lakes," presented to the House of Representatives in 1844. On 3 March 1849 Congress put $4,000 toward a lighthouse on Skillagalee.[^1]

## Building the light

The first light was finished in 1850. On 26 November of that year Henry B. Miller, the Superintendent and Inspector of Lights on the Northwest Lakes, wrote to the Fifth Auditor of the Treasury that it was "a beautiful red light, distinguishable some fifteen miles," in a letter as quoted by Lighthousefriends.[^1] It was a lantern set on top of a one-and-a-half-story stone keeper's dwelling measuring 20 by 36 feet (6 by 11 m), burning eight lamps with 14-inch reflectors, 25 feet (7.6 m) above the island. A fifth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps in 1858.[^1]

By 1866 the Light-House Board judged the light too low. "The light at Skilligalee is of insufficient elevation and cannot be seen far enough to subserve the wants of navigation," the Board wrote, as Lighthousefriends quotes it, and it asked for $40,000 for a 70-foot tower and a fog signal.[^1] Congress voted the money on 2 March 1867. Work began that year on a 100-foot (30 m) tower of yellow brick with an attached dwelling, stopped for the winter, and resumed in the spring; a third-order Fresnel lens first showed a fixed white light from it on 19 July 1868.[^1]

A fog signal followed. Congress funded a first-class steam siren in June 1874, and it began running in the fall of 1875; a duplicate was added in 1880.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1891 records the next change: because the signal at Skillagalee "was a siren" and "it was thought best to have a steam whistle at that station," the sirens were moved to Beaver Island and 10-inch whistles installed.[^2] The report gives the work as begun on 4 October and finished on 14 November 1890 in one place, and 1 November 1890 as the date the whistles were substituted in another.[^3][^4]

In 1888 the tall tower was taken down. A contract for $6,875 was made on 7 March; a temporary lantern showed from the island on 10 May; and by the end of June the old tower was gone and three courses of the new foundation laid. The old bricks were cleaned and reused with 14,000 new ones, and a new lantern room and a fourth-order Barbier & Fenestre lens came with the work. The new tower is octagonal, joined to the 1868 dwelling by a 12-foot covered passage, and Keeper Vetal Bourissau put it in service on 12 October 1888.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive holds plans titled "Rebuilding the Tower" dated 24 December 1886.[^5] Sources measure the tower differently: Lighthousefriends gives 52 feet (16 m) from base to ventilator ball, the Lighthouse Society's place record gives 52 feet from base to the center of the lantern, and Lighthouse Digest calls it a 59-foot (18 m) tower.[^1][^7][^6] A brick oil house was added in 1902.[^1]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society archive, working from the National Archives' keeper registers, lists keepers at the station from 1850 to 1955.[^7] The early years turned over quickly: five principal keepers between 1850 and 1856 (Elijah B. Pressey, Jacob Burk, Henry J. Tucker, Ambrose Davenport and Alden Hale), then Davenport again until 1861.[^7] Jerome M. Pratt, Sr., served from 1861 to 1868 with his son Jerome M. Pratt, Jr., as first assistant.[^7][^8] In 1869 Nancy Hume appears as first assistant, in the year Robert Hume left as principal keeper; the archive does not say how they were related.[^7]

Patrick J. McCann, keeper from 1869 to 1874, noted two years after the 1868 tower was finished that the island had lost 20 feet on its northwest side and 12 on its southeast, Lighthousefriends reports.[^1]

Then came the Bourissaus. Vetal Bourissau, born 1825, arrived from Bois Blanc Island in 1874 and was principal keeper for 24 years.[^9][^7] His sons served under him: Gabriel as first assistant from 1875, Frank A. as second assistant and then first assistant, and Andrew G. Bourissau as second assistant from 1885 and first assistant from 1888.[^7][^10] Andrew went on to keep Beaver Island and North Manitou Island and returned as principal keeper in 1898, when his father resigned.[^10][^9]

Louis Beloungea took over in 1901 after the Bourissau tragedy described below. He had been an assistant keeper at Waugoshance since 1894 and stayed at Skillagalee until 1924.[^11] Lighthousefriends describes him as an Odawa man.[^1] His son Louis P. Beloungea served as first assistant from 1910 to 1917 and, the archive notes, resigned in 1917 because he had been drafted.[^7][^12] Lighthousefriends says the son then succeeded the father as head keeper; the archive's roster instead shows Frank Lauer as principal keeper from 1924.[^1][^7]

Lauer's career at the station ran longest of any in the roster: second assistant from 1907, first assistant from 1918, and principal keeper from 1924 to 1941.[^7] Daniel A. Backus followed the same ladder, from second assistant in 1930 to principal keeper in 1941, and stayed until 1949.[^7] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' table of employees credited with saving life or property in the fiscal year 1919 names the Ile aux Galets keepers in three entries on pages 62 and 63 (Beloungea with Keller, Beloungea with Lauer, and all three together); in the OCR'd table the services cannot safely be matched to the entries.[^13] Lighthousefriends says Beloungea and Second Assistant August Keller helped the occupant of a motor boat from Squaw Island that year, and that the station won an efficiency flag as the model station of the district for 1923.[^1][^14]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The lake itself was the station's first enemy. Lighthousefriends traces the island's shrinkage to rising water, 15 inches from 1869 to 1870, and says a storm on 4 December 1873 washed away two-thirds of what remained. In 1875 Congress provided $5,000 for protection "around the whole island."[^1]

The fog signal's hours show how often the weather closed in. The Light-House Board counted the sirens in operation 268 hours in the year to June 1891, and the 10-inch whistle 453 hours in the year to June 1893, burning about 7 tons of coal and nearly 14 cords of wood.[^4][^15]

On 19 October 1901 Keeper Andrew G. Bourissau and Second Assistant Keeper Walter E. Grobben drowned in Lake Michigan while trying to sail from the island to the mainland, according to Lighthousefriends; Lighthouse Digest also dates both deaths to October 1901.[^1][^6] The archive's pages for the two men give the death date 19 October 1901, though a comment on each page carries the year 1866, which looks like a typing slip.[^10][^16] Grobben, born in 1882 by the archive's record, had come from North Manitou Island two years before.[^16]

Late-season closing drew attention in the Service's own papers. In 1914 the Lighthouse Service arranged a trial of unwatched acetylene lights at White Shoal, Isle aux Galets and Squaw Point so that the keepers could be taken off the stations for the winter about 10 December while still giving some service to late vessels.[^17] The Bulletin issue of 2 January 1925 says that, as the season closed, four keepers at White Shoal and three at Ile aux Galets were taken on board the Grays Reef lightship on 8 December and carried into Charlevoix; the passage prints no year, but the issue's date points to December 1924.[^18]

Backus's own scare came in June 1946. Lighthousefriends describes him that year as an assistant keeper of more than a decade's standing, though its own list and the archive roster make him principal keeper from 1941. It says his boat's engine failed not far from shore on the way back from the mainland, and he drifted until coastguardsmen picked him up off the southeast tip of Beaver Island; the other keepers, thinking he had stayed ashore because of light fog, had not reported him missing.[^1]

## After the keepers

The fog signal became an air diaphone in 1933, and the light was electrified in 1951. Lighthousefriends dates the change to unattended operation to 1959, with a 175 mm automatic lens replacing the Fresnel lens; Lighthouse Digest also says the Coast Guard removed the last keepers in 1959.[^1][^6] The archive roster names its last keepers in the early 1950s, with Robert H. Erickson to 1955.[^7]

In October 1969 the crew of the Coast Guard cutter Sundew was sent to demolish everything on the island except the 1888 tower. Lighthousefriends says the dwelling's walls proved to be interlocking brick, three bricks thick; the crew dynamited it and burned the debris.[^1][^6] A visitor's account on a Beaver Island website puts the removal in 1943, which does not fit a roster that has keepers into the 1950s.[^7][^19]

## Today

The Coast Guard declared the light excess in June 2011. No qualified non-profit or public body took it under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, and the General Services Administration sold it by online auction that ran from 1 July to 11 September 2015; the high bid was $23,250.[^1][^20] The buyer was required to keep the light under the government's standards for historic properties.[^20]

The station is on the National Register of Historic Places, with 1888 recorded as its significant year.[^21] It is privately owned and the tower is closed. It can be seen from a boat or from the air, and the reefs around the island prevent a close approach by water.[^1]
