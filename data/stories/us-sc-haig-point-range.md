The Haig Point range lights were a pair of white lights at the northeast tip of Daufuskie Island, South Carolina. From 1873 they guided ships into Calibogue Sound, and by the 1920s the Lighthouse Service had discontinued them. The rear light still stands, restored as a guest house in a private community.[^1][^2]

## The place

Calibogue Sound runs between Daufuskie Island and Hilton Head Island. A lightship had marked its entrance from 1855.[^2] In 1867 Congress appropriated funds for a light on Braddock Point to replace that ship, but the Lighthouse Board decided that a pair of range lights on Daufuskie would serve mariners better.[^2] Congress provided $15,000 on 3 March 1871 for two small lights on Daufuskie, and the government acquired five acres at Haig Point from the Pope family for $745.[^2]

The entrance to the sound shifted with the sand.[^2] The 1982 National Register nomination of the Daufuskie Island Historic District describes the light this way: "Haig's Point lighthouse (#1), actually the rear beacon of a pair of fixed white range lights, was constructed in 1873 to guide vessels through the Calibogue Sound."[^1]

Haig Point had been a plantation.[^2] The nomination records "the ruins of ten tabby slave huts" built on it between about 1805 and 1841.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that Union forces, passing Daufuskie after the Battle of Port Royal in 1861, decided the plantation mansion's timbers would be useful for a road. The mansion was soon picked apart and the remains burned.[^2]

## Building the light

In December 1872 James H. Reed of Washington, D.C., contracted to supply the materials, transport them to the island, and provide the labor for the two range lights and a dwelling, for $7,681.[^2] The nomination says Reed erected the two-story frame building that served as both lighthouse and keeper's residence, with its tower on the roof.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the house was constructed on the foundation of the old plantation mansion.[^2]

The front light was a wooden structure set on rails so that it could be moved to follow the sound's entrance.[^2] The rear light used a fifth-order Fresnel lens, and the front light a steamer lens.[^2] The Comers lit the lights for the first time on 1 October 1873.[^2]

The station gained a boathouse and ramp in 1875 and a dock in 1883.[^2] The Light-House Board's 1891–1892 report records a fireproof oil house, 9 by 11 feet (2.7 by 3.4 m), with shelves for 450 five-gallon oil cans.[^4]

## The keepers

Patrick Comer was appointed the first caretaker of the lights on 29 August 1873. The archive lists him as principal keeper until 1891.[^2][^7] LighthouseFriends says he died in an upstairs bedroom of the house that year, after eighteen years at the lighthouse.[^2]

His wife, Bridget Comer, served as his assistant. LighthouseFriends spells her name Bridgett.[^2] The archive lists her as first assistant from 1873 to 1885.[^7] The Light-House Board's rules printed in 1864 said that women and servants were not to be employed in managing lights "except by the special authority of the Department."[^8] Those are general rules, not a record of this station. The sources here do not show whether they applied in 1873, or whether she had that authority.

Richard Stonebridge followed. The archive lists him as principal keeper from 1891 to 1922. LighthouseFriends says he kept the light for thirty-two years and retired on 1 July 1923.[^2][^7] The two sources disagree on when his service ended, and this story gives both dates.

Charles L. Sisson succeeded him, and the archive lists him for 1922 to 1924.[^2][^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Light-House Board's Sixth District report describes a storm that culminated on 27 and 28 August 1893. It says the storm's center "crossed the coast line between Savannah and Charleston leaving desolation in its course," and estimated a loss of "more than 2,000 lives on the sea islands" of South Carolina.[^5] The Daufuskie Island front beacon and the wharf at its rear were among the structures the storm damaged.[^5] The report says boat landings and plank walks needed such extensive repairs that rebuilding them was the economical course, and that the same was true of "the long wharves at Tybee Knoll Gut and Daufuskie Island light-stations."[^5]

In its annual report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1922, the Commissioner of Lighthouses named Keeper Richard Stonebridge. It says he "rendered assistance to two occupants of a disabled launch, and furnished them dry clothing, food, and shelter for the night."[^3] The report does not date the rescue more closely than that fiscal year.

On 31 August 1886 an earthquake struck. LighthouseFriends reports that Patrick Comer, who was in the lantern room when the first shock came, described the quake as one that "came like the roaring of a prairie fire." The quake lasted, the site says, fully fifty seconds.[^2] Plaster rained down from the interior walls, and the station clock stopped at 9:25 p.m.[^2]

None of the sources here ties a shipwreck to these lights.

## After the keepers

In 1924 the Lighthouse Service's bulletin recorded the Daufuskie Island Range as changed. It lists "Daufuskie Island Range, Calibogue Sound, S. C., two lights," as fixed to flashing and converted from oil to acetylene.[^6]

The government sold the lighthouse and its five acres in 1925, through sealed bids, to a Savannah buyer for $1,499.99.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the house then passed through several owners and was used as a hunting lodge in the 1930s. After several wild parties there, it says, an intoxicated member of the group made his way to the top of the tower one evening. The structure gave way as he leaned against the railing, and he fell onto the roof and died. Afterwards the house stood uninhabited and slowly deteriorated.[^2]

A private owner bought the lighthouse in 1965 and set out to repair it.[^2] The Beaufort County newspaper's 1967 account, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, described a house repainted and re-roofed, with refinished floors and rewiring.[^2] In 1984 International Paper Realty Corporation bought Haig Point, planning a low-key resort community.[^2] Using the original plans for the dwelling, the company had the lighthouse restored, and the light was lit again on 18 October 1986.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse belongs to the Haig Point community, a private residential enclave on Daufuskie Island that is reached by boat.[^2] The community uses the lighthouse to house its guests, and since 2019 Haig Point has offered a discovery experience that can include a stay there.[^2] The grounds, dwelling and tower are closed to the public, so the lighthouse is best seen from the water, as LighthouseFriends notes.[^2]

The lighthouse is a contributing property of the Daufuskie Island Historic District. The district covers the whole island and was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 2 June 1982, under reference 82003831.[^1] The archive's keeper roster for the station ends with Sisson's stint in 1924.[^7]
