Point San Luis light stands on a hillside ridge above the entrance to San Luis Obispo Bay, California, a wooden Victorian lighthouse whose square tower is attached to the keepers' house. It was first lit in 1890 to mark the approach to Port Harford, and the National Register nomination calls it the only survivor in California of the plain, simplified "stick" style.[^1]

## The place

By the mid-1880s Port Harford, in San Luis Obispo Bay, had become one of the most active ports between San Francisco and San Diego, according to the nomination. The National Register nomination notes that the coast had no lighthouse between Point Arguello and Piedras Blancas, a gap of 94 miles.[^1] Lighthousefriends quotes the Lighthouse Board's 1885 plea to Congress, which called San Luis Obispo Bay the most important roadstead on the Pacific coast south of San Francisco and pointed to the railway that carried the district's farm produce to the port.[^2] The station's volunteer keepers add that ship captains had long complained of rock reefs at the harbour entrance, and that in fog steamers sometimes had to lie off in open water for days waiting to approach.[^11]

The sinking of the steamer *Queen of the Pacific* strengthened the case. The National Register nomination dates it 30 April 1888; Lighthousefriends, which describes the ship taking on water off the port and settling on the harbour bottom about 500 feet from the pier, dates it 1 May.[^1][^2]

## Building the light

The sources disagree about the money. The nomination says Congress appropriated $50,000 for the 30-acre site in 1888; the Point San Luis Lighthouse Keepers give the date as 3 March 1887, for land, materials, a fourth-order Fresnel lens and a steam fog signal; Lighthousefriends gives 1886.[^1][^11][^2] All agree the figure was $50,000. Getting a deed to the headland proved difficult and the first bids were too high, according to Lighthousefriends. A second round of bids opened on 15 July 1889, and the contract went to Mr. Kenney of Santa Barbara at $18,993. The work was only half done by the 15 December 1889 deadline, and the contractor's bondsmen had to finish it.[^2]

The nomination names the Lighthouse Board's 12th District as architect and builder.[^1] The result was a wood-frame keepers' house, two storeys in the nomination and one and a half in the 1890 Notice to Mariners,[^2] with a 40-foot (12 m) square tower attached to its southwest corner, board-and-batten on the tower and clapboard on the house, the lantern room an eight-panel glass-and-iron one under a segmented cast-iron roof.[^1] Lighthousefriends quotes the Notice to Mariners: the light was to show "a light of the fourth order, showing red and white flashes alternately, with intervals of thirty seconds between flashes" from a focal plane 133 feet above low water, seen 17.5 nautical miles in clear weather.[^2] The station was handed over to the government on 14 May 1890 and the light was due to be shown about 30 June 1890; the nomination gives 30 June 1890 as completion.[^2][^1]

The station also had a steam fog signal, but the cement catchment and cisterns built with the station had no water in them yet because the rainy season was over. A pipeline was laid to Pecho Creek, about three and a half miles away, and Lighthousefriends dates the first sounding to 10 August 1890.[^2] The Light-House Board's report for 1891 records that the pipe line was "completed in August" and gave "an ample supply of good water", but that winter rains clogged the dam and pipes with earth and debris, so the reservoir was walled with stone and two intercepting dams were built upstream.[^4] A wooden landing wharf in the bay was strengthened and extended 8 feet that same year.[^4] The nomination dates the separate fog-signal building at both 1892 (its description) and 1890 (its statement of significance), and says its raised wooden platform once carried the fog horns.[^1]

Around it were a brick oil house, a privy, a barn, a Victorian duplex for the assistants and a carpenter's shop.[^1]

## The keepers

The USLHS archive, working from the National Archives registers, lists Henry W. Young as principal keeper from 1890 to 1905, followed by William J. Smith (1905-1920) and George F. Watters (1920-1929).[^3] Antonio J. Silva is listed as first assistant from 1907 to 1933, and John R. Moorefield worked his way up from second assistant in 1926 to head keeper in 1936, a post the archive gives him until 1947.[^3] A few men moved up within the station, among them Stephen D. Ballou, listed as second assistant in 1890 and first assistant to 1894.[^3] A laborer, John Bushnell, appears in 1900.[^3] Lighthousefriends reports that the keepers were also expected to watch the water for anyone in trouble.[^2]

From 1940 the archive lists a long run of Coast Guard crew under a separate heading, mostly with stints of a year or less, and its principal-keeper line continues to Fred Sandoval, 1974 to 1975.[^3] The full list is in the roster below.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The fog signal gives the station's weather in numbers. The Board reported the steam whistles running about 1,166 hours in the year covered by its 1893 report, 1,364 hours in 1894 and 1,785 hours in the year covered by the 1895 report, with 96 tons of coal burned in that last year.[^5][^6][^7] Lighthousefriends works that out as over seventy-four days.[^2] The same 1895 report says the landing wharf and the bridge to it had been "severely damaged by the winter storms" and were in a dangerous condition; a new wharf was built on piles fitted with dowels that seated in the rock, with a 45-foot truss bridge across the channel.[^7]

On the evening of 10 May 1916 Lighthousefriends says Keeper W. M. Smith saw a boat adrift near the breakwater and telephoned the news to the clerk at the Hotel Marie. A launch captain named John Neilson went out and found eight men in a lifeboat from the steamer *Roanoke*, which Lighthousefriends says was carrying 600 tons of dynamite. The rescuers first took all eight to be dead; three were found to be alive.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended June 1916 lists, among the year's acts by light-station employees, an entry about sighting and helping bring in a boat from the S.S. Roanoke, with five dead and three nearly unconscious; the entry credits W. M. Greene, second assistant keeper at San Luis Obispo Light Station, which the archive roster also lists (Wheeler M. Greene, 1914-1917).[^9][^3] The two accounts name different men.

## After the keepers

In 1915 the steam whistles, which the Lighthouse Service Bulletin said had been in use 25 years, were replaced with a compressed-air plant and sirens removed from Point Reyes.[^8] Lighthousefriends adds that the station was electrified in 1935 and that a second duplex went up during the Second World War when a radio listening station was built nearby.[^2] The Coast Guard replaced the Victorian assistants' house with two duplexes in 1961.[^1] According to Lighthousefriends the Fresnel lens was replaced by a modern beacon in 1969 and the station was automated in 1974; other records give the deactivation as 1975.[^2][^3] The nomination, written in 1989, says the original fourth-order lens had been moved to the San Luis Obispo County Historical Museum, that the keepers' house and tower were unoccupied and that vandals had taken some historical features.[^1]

The light station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1991. In 1992 the Port San Luis Harbor District received the 30-acre site from the federal government, on the understanding that it would be a historical, educational and recreational site.[^2][^11] The Harbor District helped form the non-profit Point San Luis Lighthouse Keepers in 1995 to restore the station and open it to visitors, and in 2005 the National Park Service approved a Historic Structures Report and Treatment Plan to guide the work.[^10] Lighthousefriends says the lens was brought back to the station in 2010 and is shown in the old fog-signal building.[^2]

## Today

The station belongs to the Port San Luis Harbor District. The Harbor District says access is either a docent-led hike on the Pecho Coast Trail, arranged through PG&E, or tours run by the Point San Luis Lighthouse Keepers.[^10] The keepers' website advertises seasonal tours, concerts and events at the lighthouse.[^12] Check the current tour schedule before going, and do not walk to the station on your own.
