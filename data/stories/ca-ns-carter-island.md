Carter Island Light stands on the south end of Carter Island, at the inner entrance to Lockeport Harbour on Nova Scotia's South Shore.[^1][^2] A light has been shown from the island since 1872, when a square wooden tower was built there. The tower standing now is a fibreglass one, put up in 1982 or 1989; the sources differ on the year.[^1][^2]

## The place

Lighthouse Friends says Carter Island is one of five small islands within Lockeport Harbour, and that its light guides vessels into the inner harbour.[^1] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society places the island about 640 m (0.4 mile) north of another island, which it also calls Cranberry Island, in the approach to the inner harbour.[^2] That is not the Cranberry Island light near Canso, which lies far to the east.[^3]

The Department of Marine's report for the year to June 1905 records "uncharted rock reported in entrance to Lockeport, N.S., by Capt. P. C. Johnson, D.G.S. Lady Laurier."[^4] 

## Building the light

Lighthouse Friends says the first light was built in 1872 by James A. Hayden at a cost of $460. It was a square wooden tower with a wooden octagonal lantern and an iron floor.[^1] A circular-burner lamp in a 53 cm (21 in) reflector showed a fixed red light from the top.[^1] NSLPS gives the same start year and describes the first tower as a square wood tower, white.[^2]

The station was hard to reach in rough weather. In 1874 the tower was stayed with iron wire rope and the keeper was given a boat 4 m (13 ft) long. Lighthouse Friends says the keeper was then "authorized to spend up to $200 on materials for a landing," and that his salary rose to $250 a year in 1878 because of the duties at the offshore station.[^1]

On 7 December 1896 a seventh-order lens made by Chance Brothers of Birmingham replaced the catoptric apparatus, Lighthouse Friends says.[^1] NSLPS instead dates a seventh-order dioptric lens to 1922, the year it also records a hand fog horn.[^2] The sources do not agree on the year, and both are given here.

A second tower was built in 1930. Lighthouse Friends describes a two-storey dwelling with a square wooden lantern on its roof, plus a fog-signal building, a boathouse and an oil shed.[^1] NSLPS also gives 1930 and says the second tower replaced the first.[^2]

The present fibreglass tower is dated to 1982 by Lighthouse Friends, which says the outbuildings were then demolished except the fog-signal building.[^1] NSLPS dates the fibreglass light to 1989.[^2] The Canadian Coast Guard list describes the present tower as white and cylindrical with a red horizontal band, and gives its height as 11.5 m.[^3] Lighthouse Friends and NSLPS give 9.1 m (30 ft).[^1][^2]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends gives the head keepers in order: James Lloyd (1872 to 1883), Albert Locke (1883), Charles M. Collupy (1883 to 1885), Robert McDonald (1885 to 1925), C. L. Hardy (1926 to 1936) and V. M. Mathews (1936 to at least 1937).[^1] Lloyd, the first keeper, was hired at an annual salary of $160 and first exhibited the light on 10 September 1872.[^1]

The Department's own reports also name Robert McDonald at Carter Island. Its keepers' table for the year to June 1909 gives "Robert McDonald Jan. 4, 1886" against Carter Island, a year later than Lighthouse Friends gives.[^5] His name also appears in the Department's list of lightkeepers for 1906-07, as "McDonald, Robert Carter Island."[^6]

In the year to June 1901 the Department supplied hand fog horns to a group of stations, Carter Island among them, "to answer the fog signals of vessels, whenever heard in proximity."[^7]

## After the keepers

NSLPS says the light was still manned in 1973, and that it was automated by 1993.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says that in 1982 caretakers for the island's historic buildings could not be found, and the fibreglass tower was put up in their place.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light.[^1] Its list gives the light as a white flash every six seconds.[^3] NSLPS's record of the 1989 light says it is no longer operating; the Coast Guard list does not agree.[^2][^3] NSLPS gives site access as by boat.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower closed, and that the closest view is from the wharf area in Lockeport.[^1] Because the island is Coast Guard property that can be reached only by water, this history does not suggest landing there.
