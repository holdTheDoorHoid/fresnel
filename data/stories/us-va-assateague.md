Assateague Light is a red-and-white banded brick tower on the southern end of Assateague Island, on Virginia's Eastern Shore, about 142 feet (43 m) tall. It is the second light on the island, built in 1867 to replace a short tower from 1833, and it still shows a navigation light today.[^1][^2]

## The place

Before 1833 there was no lighthouse on the long stretch of coast between Cape Henlopen at the mouth of Delaware Bay and Cape Charles at the mouth of Chesapeake Bay. Lighthouse historian Kraig Anderson records that Congress appropriated $7,500 on 3 March 1831 for a light on one of the Chincoteague Islands, and that in 1832 the customs collector at Norfolk, Conway Whittle, settled on Assateague Island and bought a fifty-acre site for $440.[^1]

The shoals were the hazard. By 1858 the Light-House Board judged the old tower ineffective because its light could not be seen beyond the Black Fish and Winter Quarter shoals, which Anderson says reach fourteen miles out from the island.[^1] The island itself has since grown: a visitor-information site for the island says the land south of the lighthouse is new ground that has built out about two miles since the first tower went up.[^3]

## Building the light

The contract for the first tower went to Noah Porter, who came from Massachusetts and had built Ocracoke Lighthouse in 1823.[^1] The job did not go smoothly. On 19 January 1833 the overseer, Richard C. Grant, wrote to Whittle that the tower stood only 39 feet above ground and that the brick Porter had shipped from Boston had run out at about 20 feet. He added that "Mr. Porter has tried to cheat and defraud in every possible manner."[^1] (The letter is reproduced on LighthouseFriends; Anderson gives the planned tower as 45 feet.) David Watson lit eleven oil lamps in fourteen-inch reflectors for the first time in the spring of 1833.[^1][^2]

An 1850 inspection, as Anderson summarises it, found many bricks had fallen out of the tower and the reflectors spotted where the silver had worn off, though the inspector called the light very brilliant and strong at night.[^1] A third-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps in 1856.[^1] The Board then requested funds for a new, first-order tower of 150 feet. Congress voted $50,000 on 20 June 1860, but the Civil War stopped the work. Anderson says the old light stayed lit for most of the war, and that the people of Chincoteague and Assateague voted 130 to 2 to stay with the Union.[^1] When work resumed in 1865 costs had risen, and Congress added $25,000 on 28 July 1866. By 13 December that year the tower stood 95 feet, and work finished in 1867.[^1] It was first lit on 1 October 1867 with a fixed white light from a first-order lens made by Barbier and Fenestre.[^1] Sources give the finished height as 140 feet (LighthouseFriends) or 142 feet (a visitor-information site for the island).[^1][^3] The Lighthouse Society's archive also lists 142 feet, but as the height of the light above mean high water, which is a different measure.[^2] The distinctive red and white bands came only in 1968.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive of the U.S. Lighthouse Society lists fourteen head-keeper appointments from 1833 to 1933, with John Hope serving twice.[^4] David Watson was the first (1833 to 1840). The long tenure belongs to John L. Anderton, listed as head keeper from 1874 to 1920, which is forty-six years.[^4] Samuel E. Quillin worked at the station from 1881 to 1920 as first assistant, apart from 1886, when William M. Parker is listed, and then served as head keeper until 1922. Walter J. Westcott was second assistant from 1905, first assistant from 1920 and head keeper from 1922 until the keepers left in 1933.[^4] The archive also lists a woman, Mary M. Smith, as first assistant from 1868 to 1872, under head keeper Charles H. Smith; no source read here says how they were related.[^4]

The new light came with a large duplex dwelling. Anderson says it housed the two keepers comfortably until 1872, when a second assistant who was not married to the head keeper was assigned to the station.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for the year ended June 1893 records that "the interior of the keeper's dweUing was torn out and it was remodeled" (the OCR's spelling), with additions to give the keepers proper living rooms.[^5] Anderson calls the enlarged house the "Keeper's Mansion," with three apartments.[^1] In 1900, he says, the grounds were graded and about 108,900 square feet of marsh sod laid to hold the sandy soil.[^1] A concrete bungalow followed in 1910 for Keeper William Collins, who was responsible for the beacon at Fishing Point.[^1] That beacon was a post with an elevated walk on the sand spit at the south end of the island, first exhibited on 18 November 1896; the Board reported that the sea washed it partly down once that winter and that its location was "almost impracticable" to fix because the shoal kept shifting.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 10 October 1891 the U.S. steamer *Despatch*, a government vessel, went ashore off Assateague on her way from New York to Washington. A newspaper dispatch of the time said the shipwrecked crew of 74 were landed at the Assateague life-saving station.[^7][^8] Anderson adds that the second lieutenant piloting her took the lighthouse for the light of the Winter Quarter lightship and ordered her closer to land.[^1] In its report covering 1891 and 1892 the Light-House Board lists a small brick oil house, 14 by 18 feet, erected at the station, and the frame barn rebuilt.[^9]

In the blizzard of 7 February 1895 the schooner *Sunbeam* was driven ashore on Assateague. The master wrote afterwards that the life-saving crew, trying to reach them in their boat, were "caught in the ice" and stayed there over seven hours, "until rescued by a volunteer crew headed by Captain John L. Anderton, keeper of Assateague Light-House." The *Sunbeam*'s crew then spent fourteen days at the station, shut off from the mainland by ice in the bay.[^10]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1927, reporting a storm (the same item prints a lightship master's account of a northeast gale from 19 to 20 February), says the wharf at Assateague was carried away and a considerable part of the oyster-shell road to the dwellings and tower washed away.[^11]

## After the keepers

In April 1933 the station was electrified. Three bulbs went into the first-order lens, generators and batteries were installed, and a clock switched the light on at night and off in the morning. The Bulletin described the light's strength as doubled and said the station became "one of a group of lights under the charge of a single keeper."[^12] Anderson reports that Westcott went to Hog Island, Collins to Cape Charles, and care of the light was given to the keeper of Killick Shoal; the big dwelling was sold, used as a hunting lodge and moved to Chincoteague. The 1910 bungalow and the oil house survive.[^1]

Power lines reached the island in 1961, and a rotating directional beacon replaced the Fresnel lens; Anderson says this let the Coast Guard drop the Winter Quarter lightship. The lens went to the Virginia Historical Society and, in 1975, to the Museum of Chincoteague Island.[^1] Ownership passed from the Coast Guard to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 2004, while the Coast Guard still maintains the light.[^3] After part of the lantern room deck fell in 2008, a restoration of about $1.5 million replaced the gallery deck and glass and repointed and painted the tower, which reopened to visitors in September 2013.[^1]

## Today

The tower stands in Chincoteague National Wildlife Refuge and is still an active aid to navigation.[^3] The Chincoteague Natural History Association runs climbs with volunteers. For autumn 2026 it announces Saturdays only, through 21 November, 10 am to 4 pm, with the last climb at 3:30 pm. It may close in bad weather, and visitors should check its page first.[^13] The association says parking is on the right side of Beach Road just after entering the refuge, with an access road for visitors who need to get closer to the base of the tower.[^13] Anderson lists the first-order lens as on display at the Museum of Chincoteague Island, not at the station.[^1]
