Fenwick Island Light stands about a third of a mile (about 540 m) back from the Atlantic, just north of the Delaware-Maryland line.[^2] It is a white-painted brick tower of 1858, the only brick, conical lighthouse ever built in Delaware.[^1][^2] It was first lit on 1 August 1859 and ran until the Coast Guard turned it off on 13 December 1978.[^1][^14] It was re-lit in 1982 as an unofficial private aid to navigation.[^14] The State of Delaware owns the tower, and volunteers open the base to visitors on a seasonal schedule.[^13][^12]

## The place

The Lighthouse Board asked for a light on Fenwick Island in 1855, because the shoal off the coast was dangerous to ships.[^1] The nomination quotes the Board's report: "Fenwick's Island Shoal is a very dangerous one for those," and "in thick weather a light on Fenwick's Island would serve to ascertain their position when the Henlopen light was invisible."[^1] The report noted that the nearest light to the south, on the southern tip of Assateague Island, was sixty miles south of Cape Henlopen. Vessels sailing north were in danger of piling up on the Fenwick Shoal before they could sight the Henlopen light.[^1]

The distance to the shoal differs by source. LighthouseFriends puts it roughly eight miles east of the lighthouse, and the Delaware history office gives six miles off the coast of Fenwick Island.[^4][^14]

Congress authorized the lighthouse on 18 August 1856, at a cost not to exceed $25,000.[^1] The site was a ten-acre tract that LighthouseFriends says was obtained from Mary C. Hall on 11 January 1858 for $50.[^4] The nomination places the first boundary stone of the 1751 Transpeninsular Line on the station's southern property line. The stone "served as the basis for the later Mason-Dixon Line."[^1][^2]

In 1892 a wooden pile bridge was built to connect the island with the mainland.[^6] The Board's report for that year describes it as about 225 feet (69 m) long and 12 feet (3.7 m) wide, with a draw span of 34 feet (10 m).[^6] The nomination says the bridge also opened the area to early recreational development.[^1]

## Building the light

Captain William F. Raynolds of the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers directed the construction, LighthouseFriends says.[^4] The tower's walls are brick, about 27 inches (69 cm) thick at the base and 18 inches (46 cm) at the top, with an inner cylinder of brick nine inches (23 cm) thick.[^2] The nomination gives the cost of the work as $23,748; LighthouseFriends gives $23,749.[^1][^4]

The lantern held a third-order Fresnel lens.[^1] The nomination says it was "of French manufacture," and LighthouseFriends names the maker as L. Sautter & Cie.[^1][^4] LighthouseFriends, citing Notice to Mariners No. 284 of 29 December 1858, says the work was complete, but "for some reason not given, the light would not be exhibited until August 1 of the following year."[^4] It was first lit on 1 August 1859.[^1]

The first head keeper was John Smith, with H. Hickman as his assistant.[^4][^5]

The first keepers' dwelling was built in 1857 and 1858, a two-story frame house on a brick base.[^2] It served both keepers' families, one on each floor, until "overcrowded conditions brought about construction of the second and larger house in 1882," according to the nomination.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the new house to 1881: bids were opened on 8 June, and the house was ready for formal acceptance on 12 November.[^4] The first house had a cistern, and the sources disagree on its size: 2,500 gallons according to LighthouseFriends, 2,800 according to the nomination.[^4][^2]

The nomination describes the light as "a fixed white light with a white flash of about 20 seconds duration every two minutes," turned by a clock "manufactured in 1878 by George M. Stevens Company of Boston."[^2] The lamps were fourth-order oil lamps, which the nomination says were replaced in 1899 with fourth-order electric lamps.[^2] The Board's 1900 report says a "new model fourth-order lamp" replaced the third-order burner on 13 July. Its year reads 1890 in the OCR text, while the nomination dates the change to 1899.[^8][^2]

The Board's report for 1896 to 1897 records that "Preparations were made for the thorough repair of this station."[^7]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster gives the keepers by stint.[^5] John Smith was head keeper from 1859 to 1861. William R. Hall followed, from 1861 to 1869, and David M. Warrington kept the light in two stints, 1869 to 1874 and 1874 to 1876. John A. Gunn was keeper from 1876 to 1880.[^5]

The New Friends of the Fenwick Island Lighthouse give Smith's dates as 28 February 1859 to 17 July 1861, and they note "Deceased" against James H. Bell's short stint of July and August 1876.[^11] The roster lists Bell for 1876 without that note, so the Friends' list is the only source for it.[^5][^11]

Jehu D. Bennett kept the light from 1880 to 1907, the longest principal's stint on the roster.[^5] He was followed by Samuel Soper, Willis B. Pardee, Alva F. Stites and Charles L. Gray. Gray was head keeper from 1926 to 1940.[^5] The Friends' list gives a "William D. Pardee" as keeper from July 1910, and a "W. B. Paradee" as assistant from 1909 to 1910. The roster gives Willis B. Pardee for both posts. The sources do not say whether these are the same man.[^11][^5]

Assistants lived on the station too.[^5] William E. Pepper was an assistant from 1869 to 1885, and George Walker from 1926 to 1936.[^5] LighthouseFriends says Paul Pepper, who later led the campaign to restore the light, had a great-grandfather who was the third keeper, David M. Warrington, and a grandfather, Edward G. Pepper, who served as an assistant keeper. The roster does not list Edward G. Pepper.[^4][^5] When the light was electrified in 1936, LighthouseFriends says, the assistant's post was abolished, and Walker was transferred to Fourteen Foot Bank.[^4]

The roster's last keepers are Arthur Aydelotte, 1940 to 1945; Neal Gray, 1945 to 1948; and John C. Gray, who appears for 1948.[^5] The Friends' list gives Neal Gray's name as "Neil" and adds two names the roster does not have: B. W. Hitchens, an assistant from 1927 to 1934, and Levin Day, an assistant whose years are not known.[^11]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A lightship marked the shoal from 1888 to 1933.[^4] LighthouseFriends says a lightship was anchored just east of Fenwick Island Shoal from 1888, and that a lightship was assigned to the station until 1933, when a lighted whistle buoy took its place.[^4]

On Christmas Day 1931 the keepers picked up a man whose small boat had stranded on a sandbar.[^9] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1932 printed the story under the heading "Lighthouse Keeper Rescues Eskimo off Delaware Coast."[^9] The bulletin says his boat "cast on a sand bar, about one-half mile south of the lighthouse," and that a man from Greenland was found "in an exhausted condition on Christmas day by the keepers of Fenwick Island Lighthouse."[^9] The unusual story he told the keepers was that he was on his way from Greenland to the Panama Canal, having stopped along the Massachusetts coast. The bulletin says that after two days of rest, during which he was provided with food, dry clothing, and shelter by the keepers, his sailboat was gotten off into deep water.[^9]

On 16 January 1932 the Washington Daily News reported that the story had come "to Commissioner Putnam, head of the Lighthouse bureau, from Charles L. Gray, head keeper at Fenwick Island."[^10] The column joked that the report was thin: "That was the story — that and nothing more."[^10] Its account of the voyage matches the bulletin's. Its only mention of Alaska is a joke about the man perhaps visiting "some cousins in Alaska."[^10] LighthouseFriends tells it differently, as a solo voyage from Greenland to Alaska by way of the canal, with the man unconscious when found.[^4] This account follows the bulletin and the newspaper.

## After the keepers

The light was electrified in 1936, LighthouseFriends says, and the assistant's post was abolished.[^4] The nomination gives 1940 as the year the lighthouse was automated, when much of the ten-acre property was sold.[^1] The Coast Guard had taken control of the station in 1939. It sold three-quarters of the land, including the old dwelling, to Charles L. Gray, who had just retired as keeper, for $1,610.79.[^4]

The light stayed in service until 13 December 1978, when the Coast Guard turned it off and removed the lens and the lighting mechanism. The pedestal and shaft were left in place.[^2] People protested the decommissioning, and LighthouseFriends credits Paul Pepper with the campaign to restore the light.[^4] The nomination was prepared in January 1979, and the tower was listed on the National Register that year.[^3][^14]

In February 1979 Governor Pete DuPont said, as the Friends' website quotes him, that the tower "should be maintained as an important link to Delaware's maritime history."[^13] On 21 September 1981 the Coast Guard transferred the tower to the State of Delaware, which leased it to the Friends.[^4] The light was lit again in 1982, equipped with "a less powerful light source and its returned Fresnel lens," according to LighthouseFriends.[^4][^14]

State senators helped obtain $400,000 to restore the tower.[^4] The restoration work was done in 1997, and a rededication ceremony was held in July 1998.[^4] Oliver H. Cropper led the Friends from 1992 until 2007, LighthouseFriends says.[^4] In 2007 the state asked Winnie Lewis to organize a new volunteer group.[^4][^13] In 2017 the state acquired the keeper's dwelling west of the tower.[^4] The state's history office says that restoration of the neighboring keeper's house has created a publicly accessible space, and that the volunteer group now offers a gift shop.[^14]

## Today

The State of Delaware owns the tower.[^13] The "New" Friends of the Fenwick Island Lighthouse, a volunteer group, cares for it and welcomes visitors.[^13][^14] The Friends' visiting page says: "Sorry, the State of Delaware, owner of the Lighthouse, does NOT allow climbing."[^12] Do not try to climb the tower.

The 2026 visiting schedule on the Friends' page is seasonal.[^12] The base is open on Saturdays and Sundays in April and May, Fridays to Sundays in June and September, Thursdays to Mondays in July and August, and Saturdays and Sundays in October, from 9 am to noon (to 1 pm in July and August). Easter Sunday is closed. The site is closed from November to 1 April, and the Friends close on rainy days.[^12]
