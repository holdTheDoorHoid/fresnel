Eldred Rock is a small island at the northern end of Lynn Canal in Southeast Alaska, and its white octagonal lighthouse, lit in 1906, was called the last octagonal lighthouse of its kind remaining in Alaska when it was nominated to the National Register in 1975.[^1] The building was put up for the steamers that ran to Skagway in the years after the Klondike gold rush, and it was staffed until 1973, when the station was unmanned.[^1][^2]

## The place

Lynn Canal is the long channel that leads north to Skagway and Haines. The National Register nomination explains why it mattered: after the Klondike gold rush of 1897-98, the main summer route to interior Alaska ran by steamer through the Inside Passage to Skagway, and then by railway into Canada.[^1] Four manned lighthouses were established in Lynn Canal between 1902 and 1906, and Eldred Rock was the last of them.[^1]

The rock had already claimed ships before there was a light. The Lighthouse Friends history says the passenger steamer *Clara Nevada* ran aground there and exploded in flames on 5 February 1898, and credits that loss with persuading Congress that a light was needed.[^2] Canada's Department of Marine reported another casualty the following year: the Victoria steamer *Tees*, bound from Skagway to Victoria, struck Eldred Rock on 5 October 1899 and went to Victoria for repairs to broken plates and frames.[^5] The Light-House Board's report for 1901 repeats an earlier statement of the Board's that listed Eldred Rock first among eleven localities selected for new aids to navigation in Alaskan waters; the report does not date that statement, though the Treasury letter printed after it is of December 1899.[^4]

## Building the light

The Light-House Board adopted plans for a light and fog-signal station in May 1905 and ordered the work done by hired labor, with operation planned for November 1905.[^1] Weather slowed the work. The nomination says the station was not completed and lighted until 1 June 1906.[^1] Some records give 1905 for the first lighting, but that is the year the Lighthouse Society's archive gives as the station's establishment, and the same archive dates the lens and the quarters to 1906.[^3] The 1906 lighting is the better supported date.

The building was one structure for everything: a two-story octagon on concrete foundations, 56 feet (17 m) high and 52 feet (16 m) across, with a concrete lower story and a wooden upper one, painted white under a black roof.[^1] A wooden octagonal tower and lantern rose from the apex. The light was shown 91 feet (28 m) above high water, was a fourth-order lens, and was visible about 15 miles (the nomination does not say statute or nautical).[^1] The fog signal was a first-class compressed-air siren.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the fog signal, furnace and fuel and water storage were on the lower floor and the three keepers had eight rooms above.[^2] The same source describes a Paris-made lens that turned a larger and a smaller bull's-eye panel, with ruby glass in front of one so that the light flashed alternately red and white. The keepers had to wind a 135-pound weight every five hours to keep it turning.[^2] A boathouse stood to the north, linked to the lighthouse by a tramway.[^1]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society's archive lists Nils P. Adamson as the first head keeper, serving 1906 to 1911, with Gust J. Hall and John H. Anderson as his first and second assistants.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says Adamson reached the station in time for the opening and settled on the island with those two.[^2]

On 26 March 1910 the two assistants, John Currie and John Silander, set out in the station launch for Point Sherman lighthouse. When they did not come back, Adamson put out word to passing vessels, and a boat later found the launch about two and a half miles northeast of the rock.[^2] Adamson later wrote, in a letter Lighthouse Friends quotes, that he was "unable to account for any accident" because there was "no wind to speak of and a smooth sea."[^2] The source says he searched Lynn Canal for a month, and resigned on 5 January 1911 and moved to Astoria, Oregon.[^2] Lighthouse Friends adds that he called out the men's names in his sleep for the rest of his life.[^2]

The station logbook of 23 November 1921, as quoted by Lighthouse Friends, records another night on the water. After a couple of days of gale-force wind, the mailboat appeared off the rock at 3 a.m., and head keeper George Stinson sent second assistant John Erickson out in the station boat to fetch the mail.[^2] Stinson lit two lanterns on the platform to mark the landing, but Erickson drifted south and west of the rock, and his lantern went out about 300 yards away. Stinson wrote that he was "alone on station" and that a passing steamer ignored his half-mast flag and four blasts of the siren.[^2] Four days later a neighbour from a nearby fox farm found Erickson ashore about two miles to the southwest. He had saved the mail, but the station boat was smashed.[^2]

Not every boat call ended that way. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that on 22 July 1919 keeper N. C. Monsen towed the disabled cannery tender *Chilkoot*, with nine men on board, a distance of 8 miles.[^6] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for 1922 notes that Stinson gave food, lodging and fuel to the two men aboard a launch.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Lighthouse Friends says a gale on the evening of 12 March 1908 left a ship stranded on the north end of the island the next morning. Assistant keeper Currie believed it was the *Clara Nevada*, brought back up ten years after her loss, before the storm returned that evening and took it back down.[^2] The source does not say what the vessel really was.

The Bulletin's index for 1930 to 1935 has an entry for Eldred Rock, Alaska, about the death of a keeper, with a damaged word after it; the article itself is too badly scanned to read, so the story of that death is not told here.[^8]

## After the keepers

The nomination gives 1973 as the year the station was unmanned and downgraded to a minor light, and Lighthouse Friends says personnel left that year and the fog signal and radiobeacon were discontinued.[^1][^2] The light itself remains, now with a 250 mm solar-powered optic.[^3] The original Fresnel lens went to the Alaska State Museum in 1976, and has been on display at the Sheldon Museum in Haines since 1981.[^2] Eldred Rock was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1975.[^2]

Efforts to save the building began with a Sheldon Museum committee seeking the station under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act. In 2004 the Forest Service, which controls part of the island, refused to let its property be conveyed into private hands, after Eldred Rock had appeared on the list of available lighthouses that summer.[^2] The Eldred Rock Lighthouse Preservation Association was formed in 2014, and in April 2020 the Coast Guard gave it a five-year lease.[^2]

## Today

Lighthouse Friends says the lighthouse is owned by the Coast Guard, with the grounds, dwelling and tower closed, and the Lighthouse Society's archive lists it as not open to the public.[^2][^3] The five-year lease to the preservation association began in April 2020; the sources read do not say what happened when it ran out. The lighthouse can be seen from ferries and cruise ships passing through Lynn Canal.[^2] Lighthouse Friends reports that volunteers marked the restoration with a grand opening around 1 June 2026, the 120th anniversary of the light, and that Skagway Excursion offers a lighthouse tour from Haines and Skagway.[^2] Check with the preservation association or the tour operator before planning a visit; do not land on the island on your own.
