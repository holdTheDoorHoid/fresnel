New Dungeness Light stands near the end of Dungeness Spit, a five-and-a-half-mile finger of sand, drift logs and beach grass that reaches into the Strait of Juan de Fuca in Clallam County, Washington.[^1] Lit on 14 December 1857, it was the first federal light built north of the Columbia River, and in 1992 it was still the only man-made feature on the spit.[^1]

## The place

The spit is low, barren and long. The National Register nomination describes it as sand, rocks, logs and beach grasses with no trees, though a Coast Survey party in 1852 recommended planting some to warn navigators.[^1] Its western shore faces the open Strait; on the east, New Dungeness Bay lies sheltered behind it.[^1] Captain George Vancouver named the place "New Dungeness" in 1792, after Dungeness Point on the English Channel, and the nomination says Spanish explorers had earlier called the point Santa Cruz.[^1] Its deceptively low profile and its length made it the scene of many wrecks, and mariners called it "Shipwreck Spit."[^1][^2]

The ground itself moves. A gale on 2 December 1871 opened a breach in the spit west of the station, and HistoryLink's account says the light was briefly on an island.[^2] The same essay reports that the spit has been breached again in 1971, 1975, 1993, 1996 and 1997, and that the station, built about 800 feet (240 m) from the end, now sits over half a mile (0.8 km) from the tip as sand and silt build the east end outward.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress passed an enabling act for a New Dungeness lighthouse in September 1850, and in August 1854 it reserved sites and appropriated $39,000 for this light and the one on Tatoosh Island.[^1] Construction began in 1856 under Isaac Smith.[^1] The nomination says federal officials worried about the remote site, the weather and possible Indian hostilities, and that Smith set his two crews to race each other. The New Dungeness crew, which included John Tibbals and the mason Konrad Schneider, won by two weeks.[^1]

The design followed the pattern of the Treasury architect Ammi B. Young: a tapering brick tower rising from the middle of a gable-roofed dwelling, with large sandstone blocks from the Bellingham area used for the house and brick for the tower.[^1] The tower was painted white on its lower half and a dark colour above, and a fixed third-order Fresnel lens sat behind an oil lamp.[^1] Sources do not agree on the tower's original height. The nomination says 89 feet (27 m), HistoryLink says 91 feet (28 m) and 100 feet (30 m) above sea level, and the Lighthouse Society archive lists 89 feet from base to the centre of the lantern.[^1][^2][^4]

A fog bell stood on the platform near the lighthouse. The nomination gives its weight as 1,100 pounds and says the keeper had to wind its striking weights every few hours, day and night, and ring it by hand when the striker failed; HistoryLink gives 1,200 pounds and a winding every 45 minutes.[^1][^2] In 1873 Congress appropriated money for a 12-inch steam whistle with two large boilers.[^1] HistoryLink dates the whistle building to 1874 and says the keepers fed the boiler about 200 pounds of coal for every hour it ran.[^2] The Light-House Board's own figure for the 1895 report year is that the whistle ran "some 488 hours" and burned about 35 tons of coal.[^5] Fresh water was a problem in the early years, and HistoryLink says supplies had to be barged in from the town of New Dungeness, two and a half miles away.[^2]

By 1895 the station had outgrown its houses. The Light-House Board wrote that the dwellings were "arranged for the accommodation of two families and one single man," that four keepers were employed, "sometimes three of them with families," and that a new dwelling was "urgently needed."[^5] It renewed the request in report after report; in 1899 it called the recommendation one "made in the Board's last four annual reports."[^6] The new keepers' house was finished by about 1905. The nomination says it was built in 1904 "at the cost of $45,000", while HistoryLink says Congress appropriated funds in April 1904, the house was finished in January 1905 and it cost about $5,000; the sources do not settle which is right.[^1][^2] HistoryLink adds that the original dwelling was altered into a duplex in 1906 and a new fog-signal building with sirens went up in 1907.[^2]

In 1927 the tower was cut down. The nomination says the district's chief lighthouse engineer, Clarence Sherman, noted structural instabilities and supervised the removal of the upper courses of brick, leaving a tower 63 feet (19 m) high; it gives the amount removed as 26 feet in one passage and 27 feet in another.[^1] HistoryLink adds a cause the nomination does not: cracking made worse, it says, by the concussion of Canadian artillery practice at Esquimalt.[^2] The lantern from the abandoned Admiralty Head light was set on the shortened tower.[^1][^2] The two sources then diverge on the lens. The nomination says a Barbier and Benard lens built in 1897 and earlier used at Admiralty Head went in; HistoryLink says the origin of the fourth-order lens is not known.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

Franklin Tucker and John Tibbals of Port Townsend kept the new light until February 1858, when Captain Thomas Boyling and Henry H. Blake arrived.[^1] HistoryLink says Boyling apparently preferred the sea, and that by 1 March 1858 Blake was listed as principal keeper; the Lighthouse Society archive lists Boyling as head keeper until 1860 and Blake from then until 1868.[^2][^4] The nomination and the archive therefore disagree about who held the post in 1858 to 1860.

Blake was twenty-three in the 1860 census, with his brother Walter J. Blake, eighteen, as his assistant.[^2] On 20 August 1862 he married Mary Anne McDonnell, the daughter of a farmer who lived at the foot of the spit; she became an assistant keeper, and the archive lists her as first assistant from 1863 to 1868.[^2][^4] Her mother and three brothers moved into the station, and the couple had three children while serving there.[^2] HistoryLink says Blake was officially relieved on 18 September 1868 by Jacob J. Rodgers (or Rogers), though the family apparently did not leave at once, and that the 1870 census shows Ester E. Rodgers and a Mary L. Smith as assistant keepers.[^2] Henry Blake died in 1871 at Port Townsend; in 2000 the Coast Guard commissioned a 175-foot buoy tender, the *Henry Blake*, named in honour of notable keepers.[^2]

Tucker came back as head keeper in April 1873 and stayed until December 1882, when he was transferred to Ediz Hook.[^1] The nomination names his successor as "Amos Morgan," serving until March 1896; the archive lists Osmore H. Morgan as acting keeper from 1882 and head keeper from 1883 to 1896, and the sources give different first names.[^1][^4] In the 1870s the archive shows the assistants' posts changing hands almost yearly, with names such as Alfred F. Turner and John Martin staying a year or less.[^4]

Edward A. Brooks held the station from 1902 to 1925, the longest head-keeper term in the archive's list, and his first assistant Anders G. Berner served from 1917 to 1928.[^4] Vivian R. Corrie came as second assistant in 1928, moved up to first assistant in 1931, and returned as head keeper in 1937.[^4] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin noted his help in refloating a grounded gas boat on 2 August 1929.[^9] By the 1940s the civilian keepers were giving way. HistoryLink says the men kept on until retirement while Coast Guard crews, usually posted for a year at a time, replaced them.[^2] In 1980 Seaman Jeni Burr was posted here, one of only two Coast Guard women to serve as a lighthouse keeper, according to HistoryLink.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The nomination lists the wrecks it considered prominent: the schooner *John Stevens* in 1858, just after the light was commissioned; the schooner *Kosuth* in 1862; the barks *Fremont* (1866), *George Washington* and *Iconium* (1867) and *Ocean* (1868); the bark *Christopher Mitchell* and the ship *Washington Libby* (1874); the Chilean barks *Savona* (1890) and *Eritrea* (1893); and the ship *R. K. Ham* (1894).[^1] HistoryLink counts more than twenty ships wrecked on the spit since records began in the 1850s, and says many more ran aground and floated off unrecorded.[^2]

One of the best-documented nights at the station involved no wreck. Just before dawn on 21 September 1868, HistoryLink's account says, a band of S'Klallam men attacked a party of eighteen Tsimshian people camped on the spit on their way home to British Columbia from the Puyallup hop fields. Seventeen were killed.[^3] The one survivor, a wounded pregnant woman, made her way to the lighthouse, where Henry Blake sheltered her; when some of the raiders tracked her there and demanded her, Blake refused, and later that day he took her to a neighbour's house in Dungeness.[^3] The dead were buried on the branch of the spit now called Graveyard Spit.[^3] The essay presents the raid as retaliation for a Tsimshian abduction of one of the leader's wives, and it records that Chief Chetzemoka helped identify the men, who were arrested and sentenced to hard labour before most were released.[^3] Local history, as HistoryLink reports it, also holds that about 1920 a Tsimshian man paddled to the spit and told keeper Brooks that he was the child born to the woman the Blakes had sheltered.[^3]

On the night of 6 October 1914, the 160-foot steamer *Sioux* ran aground on the spit in fog near the fog-signal building.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin credits Brooks with helping take off her passengers, and HistoryLink says he brought them to the lighthouse until they could be moved to the mainland; tugs pulled the steamer off on 12 October.[^2][^7] On the night of 14 to 15 October 1923 Brooks and Berner helped fight the fire that destroyed the power house at the Navy's radio compass station on the spit, and the Service commended them.[^8] In 1929 the keepers telephoned the Coast Guard when a boat overturned near the station and took two survivors in, giving them food and dry clothing.[^9] Lighthouse Friends adds that the third man in the party was lost.[^11]

The sea also ate at the station itself. In July 1924 the Service built a pile revetment 700 feet (210 m) long along the beach to hold the sand, at a cost of about $900.[^10]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard automated the light and fog signal in November 1975, according to HistoryLink; the nomination says 1976.[^2][^1] The rotating lens was taken out and replaced with a modern beacon, and the 1927 Fresnel lens is on display at the Coast Guard Museum Northwest in Seattle, HistoryLink reports.[^2] One keeper and his family stayed on in the 1905 house, and the original brick dwelling was vacated.[^2] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 11 November 1993.[^2]

In March 1994 the Coast Guard decided to close the station, with Seaman Seth Jackson and his wife as its last government keepers, and meant to board up the buildings.[^2] The New Dungeness chapter of the U.S. Lighthouse Society offered to take on the 32.5-acre reservation, and from 3 September 1994 it staffed the station around the clock with volunteer keepers, while the Coast Guard kept control of the light and fog signal.[^2] The chapter reincorporated in 2001 as the New Dungeness Light Station Association.[^2] On 11 July 1999 volunteers on the spit saw a beach fire racing toward the station, switched on the lawn sprinklers near the helicopter pad, and, the essay says, caused the fire to burn around the grounds; every building had smoke damage and none was lost.[^2] In early 2006 the Coast Guard announced plans to deactivate the tower and move the light to a structure at the end of the spit; preservationists protested, and the Association reached an agreement to keep the light in the tower.[^11]

## Today

The tower and the 1905 keepers' house stand on the refuge, and the Association's volunteer keepers run daily tours, from 9:00 am to 5:00 pm, with winter hours that vary.[^12] It is not a short visit: the walk is a rugged 11 miles round trip, and by boat the refuge allows landing only between two yellow posts due south of the station.[^12] HistoryLink describes the hike from the refuge parking area as about five and a half miles each way, and says access is limited to hikers at low tide and boaters who can land on the beach.[^2] The Association also lets members pay to serve as volunteer keepers for a week, living at the station.[^2] The nomination listed the Coast Guard as owner in 1992, and the Association has run the station under a Coast Guard licence since 1994; as of HistoryLink's 2005 account the Coast Guard kept the light, which flashes white every five seconds, and the fog signal.[^1][^2] The Association's page gives directions to the Dungeness Recreation Area and refuge parking.[^13]
