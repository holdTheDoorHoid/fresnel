Holland Rock Light stands on a bare rock in Chatham Sound, south of Prince Rupert, British Columbia.[^4][^5] A beacon was moved to the rock in 1908, a lighthouse was first lit there in 1913, and a fire destroyed that lighthouse in 1946.[^1][^3][^4] The light now shows from a steel skeleton tower on a fog-signal building, and the rock is reached only by boat.[^4][^6]

## The place

Holland Rock is barren and only about 3 m (10 ft) high. LighthouseFriends says sailors have long regarded it as a hazard.[^3] The rock lies on the western side of the southern approach to Prince Rupert harbour.[^3] The distance from Prince Rupert is given two ways: Rowlett puts the rock about 15 km (10 mi) south of the town, and a 2020 federal record says 16 km.[^4][^5]

LighthouseFriends says Captain Pender named the rock in 1867, after Lieutenant Swinton Colhurst Holland, who later became an admiral in the Royal Navy.[^3]

In 1908 the rock was marked by a beacon brought from elsewhere. The Department's annual report for the fiscal year 1908–09 says that the Quadra, then in the buoy service, "moved the automatic beacon from Green Top island to Holland Rock" in May.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the same month and year, and says the beacon had been placed on Green Top Island in 1906.[^3] Rowlett's directory also dates the move to 1908.[^4] A later passage of the same report says the beacon formerly kept at Green Top island had been removed and installed at this point.[^1]

## Building the light

Work on a lighthouse for the rock began in 1912. LighthouseFriends says a contract for $13,500 went to J.H. Hilditch to erect it, and that a diaphone fog signal was supplied for $1,515.12 by a company in Toronto.[^3]

The lighthouse stood on a concrete pier. Its two-storey dwelling carried a square tower, with an octagonal lantern room built into one corner.[^3] Rowlett says the square tower rose from a two-storey keepers' house and was built in 1913. Rowlett also calls the pier stone, where LighthouseFriends says concrete.[^4]

The Department's statement of lightstations and names of lightkeepers for British Columbia, in its report for 1913–14, lists "Holland rock light and fog alarm."[^2] LighthouseFriends says that Keeper James E. Gibson first showed the fixed white light in 1913.[^3] Rowlett dates the station to 1908, the year the beacon came to the rock. The lighthouse followed in 1913.[^4]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends lists the head keepers. The first is James E. Gibson, from 1913 to 1915.[^3] Between 1917 and 1921 nine different men begin stints as head keeper, several of them for a single year, such as Edward Biddle in 1917 and Joseph Sexton in 1920.[^3]

D. Rodman was head keeper from 1922 to 1933, the longest stint on the list.[^3] The list ends with G.V. Hanley, given from 1934 and at least until 1937.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In September 1946 an overheated cook stove set fire to the lighthouse.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the blaze spread quickly through the building, and that the keeper "had no choice but to flee the rock" in the station's skiff, taking his pet cat.[^3] Rowlett records that the lighthouse was destroyed by fire that year.[^4] The 2020 federal assessment says the site still holds foundations from the lightstation that burned in 1946.[^5] Afterwards, LighthouseFriends says, only rubble and the brick chimney were left on the pier.[^3]

## After the keepers

After the fire a white skeleton tower stood on the concrete pier.[^3] In 1949 a fog-alarm building went up on the pad. It housed two air-cooled, gasoline-engine compressors that fed air to a diaphone horn.[^3][^4] Rowlett describes the present tower as a steel skeleton, square and about 6 m (20 ft) high, on the fog-signal building.[^4][^6]

In June 1952 an experimental microwave system, developed by the National Research Council in Ottawa, let the keeper at Barrett Rock, about 8 km (4.9 miles) away, select, start and stop the compressors at Holland Rock.[^3] A 1955 article in *Electronics*, as LighthouseFriends reprints it, judged the system probably the first of its kind for remote control of a fog station by microwave.[^3]

In 2020 the Coast Guard proposed a new concrete helicopter pad at the rock, after the wooden pad collapsed and left no safe place to land a helicopter.[^5]

## Today

Holland Rock is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard and is reached only by boat.[^3][^4] The federal record describes shared Coast Guard and Environment and Climate Change Canada infrastructure on the site: a concrete building for electrical gear and batteries, a marine navigation aid and weather tower, and the collapsed helipad.[^5]

Both sources say the tower is closed.[^3][^4] They differ on the grounds: Rowlett says the site is closed too, while LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open.[^3][^4] Neither source invites visitors to land.

The light shows three white flashes every twelve seconds.[^4][^6] The Coast Guard list gives its focal height as 9.1 m and its range as 5 nautical miles.[^6] Rowlett gives a focal plane of 7 m (23 ft).[^4]
