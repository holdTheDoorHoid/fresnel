Mona Island Light is an iron tower on the east cape of an uninhabited island in the middle of the Mona Passage, about 37 miles from both Puerto Rico and Santo Domingo.[^1] Spain left the materials for it stored near the beach, where they rusted, and American authorities completed the tower and lit it in 1900;[^5] the station was reached by a boat through one opening in the reef.[^4]

## The place

Mona Island lies midway between Puerto Rico and Santo Domingo in the Mona Passage, between the Atlantic and the Caribbean.[^1] The Light-House Board described it in 1901 as uninhabited, about 6½ miles long and 5 miles wide, with no harbor.[^2] Ships that came for phosphate and guano anchored in the open roadstead; the Board wrote that when storms rose from the east these vessels, "it is said," "drop both their anchors and veer out all their chain cables," their crews took to the boats, and "in too many cases the vessels follow them." Eleven such vessels, it said, had been wrecked in the previous two years.[^2]

## Building the light

Two quite different plans were drawn up for the east cape in 1885 and 1886. The first was a second-class light with a masonry tower and a 25-room dwelling built around a courtyard; the second was an iron tower. The National Register nomination says the island's porous, brittle sandstone was thought better suited to iron than to a massive masonry building.[^3] The nomination says a lighthouse was reported under construction in 1888, yet the first reference to actual building, in 1894, speaks of "a metallic lighthouse"; it suggests the long delay could have come from technical problems, poor communication with Puerto Rico or the difficult ground, and says the light was finished between 1898 and 1900 according to secondary evidence.[^3] LighthouseFriends, a modern history site, says the iron parts were made in France, that work began in the early 1890s, and that it stopped around 1895 as the Spanish-American War approached.[^4]

The Light-House Board's account of what happened next is plainer. It says the Spanish government had left near the beach "a large quantity of material" for two large steel buildings for keepers' dwellings and a steel tower. The material had rusted during the years it was stored, and many parts had disappeared. The American military government decided to erect the light and chose a site, and little had been done by 1 October 1899, when Puerto Rico's board of public works took charge.[^5][^2] The work went on "under great difficulties," because there was no harbor and landing was sometimes impossible for days. The parts had to be moved from the shore up some 231 feet and more than a mile over rock so rough that it needed ballasting and blasting.[^2] The light was inaugurated on 30 April 1900, with about three months' work still to do on the station.[^2][^5] Money came from the 1900 appropriation for lighthouse repairs and from $60,000 voted by Congress on 6 June 1900 for the Puerto Rican lighthouse service.[^2]

The tower is a skeletal iron structure with a central cylinder, a spiral iron stair and a twelve-sided lantern.[^3] It held a second-order Fresnel lens by Sautter, Lemonnier & Cie, dated 1889 in the nomination, with six panels, three of them flashing, turned by clockwork driven by a 180-pound weight that ran down the stair column.[^3] The Board's 1901 report described a fixed white light varied by a flash every two minutes, 231 feet above high water and visible 20 nautical miles, shown from a black skeleton-steel tower joined to a gray dwelling with a red gable (the Board and the 1900 report call the structure steel; the nomination and LighthouseFriends call it iron). Material for a second house was put in store.[^2] The nomination notes that the plan called for the tower to stand between two identical dwellings but only one was built, and that it survives, built of steel plates and wooden framing, joined to the tower by a covered steel passage.[^3] Sources differ on the height of the tower itself: the 1901 report's OCR text reads 62 feet, a 1925 inspection account says 56 feet, and the Lighthouse Society's archive gives 52, measured to the center of the lantern.[^2][^6][^7]

## The keepers

The archive of the U.S. Lighthouse Society lists principal keepers from 1902 onward, beginning with Simeon Martin (1902-1914) and Manuel Perez Castillo (1914-1924).[^7] It also names Domingo Suarez-Rosa as principal keeper in about 1901-1902.[^7] The roster is the archive's, drawn from keeper registers, and has gaps, and no principal keeper is listed between 1945 and 1959.[^7] As general context, the Lighthouse Service kept on in Puerto Rico the keepers who had served under Spain, as citizens of Puerto Rico.[^8]

Commander G. W. Metz, assigned in November 1901 to oversee the fifteen lighthouses of Puerto Rico, reported, as quoted by LighthouseFriends, that Mona was "the most isolated and unattractive" station in his subdistrict and "farther out to sea than any other lighthouse" in the Service.[^4] He reported that there was only one landing, "through an opening blasted in the reef," that the keeper's boat sometimes stood off for days and had once been blown nearly to Santo Domingo, and that no keeper would stay "more than two years."[^4] The archive's dates give Simeon Martin twelve years and Manuel Perez Castillo ten as principal keepers.[^7]

A 1925 inspection account, from F. P. Dillon, superintendent of the Ninth Lighthouse District, gives a picture of the station in the keepers' time. Supplies came in through a gap in the reef to a strip of beach called Playa Pájaro (Bird Beach), then up an inclined tramway through a cave, hauled by a hand winch, and across a mile and a quarter of cactus-covered rock to the station by a small canopied car pulled by a mule named Macario.[^6] Dillon wrote that the keepers "speak only Spanish" but were not much different "from keepers in the United States," that they were devoted to their families, and that those with school-age children left them in Puerto Rico during the school term.[^6] There was no soil for a garden; the chickens that escaped the wild hogs gave eggs.[^6] The keeper had built a 4,000-foot road "last year" (that is, before the 1925 account) "at small cost," with labor from Puerto Rico.[^6]

Castillo was listed among those commended in 1919 for helping, with the station launch, a party whose schooner struck the rocks (the Bulletin gives April; the day is garbled in the Bulletin's OCR, and LighthouseFriends gives 28 April and places the wreck near Mona Island).[^9][^4] In July 1913 his first assistant, José P. Castillo, took in seven people from a disabled gasoline launch and cared for them until a vessel carried them to Mayagüez.[^10] The Bulletin's index lists an efficiency flag for Manuel Perez Castillo, which LighthouseFriends dates to 1922 and 1923, when it says the station was judged the model station of the district.[^11][^4] A Domingo Suárez Rosa, whose Spanish and American service the Bulletin says included Mona Island, and whom the archive lists there in about 1901-1902, retired in 1925 for physical disability after 26 years in the American service and 12 in the Spanish, and died that July.[^12]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A hurricane on 9 and 10 September 1921 damaged lights across Puerto Rico's Ninth District, Mona among them.[^13] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for fiscal 1922 lists $4,375 for repairing hurricane damage at Mona Island Light Station.[^14] A hurricane crossed southwestern Puerto Rico on the evening of 23 July 1926, and the Bulletin reported its principal damage at Mona Island, where the dwelling was partly unroofed and several outbuildings were destroyed.[^15]

## After the keepers

The nomination says the light was electrified in 1938 and automated in 1973, when its characteristics changed again. In 1976 the light was moved to another position, the lens was taken down and stored at the Coast Guard base in San Juan, and the old structures were abandoned.[^3] The Lighthouse Society's archive lists Coast Guard personnel at the station through about 1971.[^7] The tower and keeper's house were listed on the National Register of Historic Places, with a nominated area of 269 acres that follows a 1903 presidential proclamation giving the Coast Guard title to the site; the register's record carries a publication date of 22 October 1981.[^3][^16]

## Today

The island is a nature reserve run by Puerto Rico's Department of Natural and Environmental Resources, with no permanent inhabitants.[^17] The department issues camping permits, which must be obtained at least five working days ahead, and allows tents only at two beaches, Playa Pájaros and Playa Sardinera.[^18] The Lighthouse Society's archive records the station as not open to the public.[^7] The tower's present condition is not documented in the sources cited here. Treat it as a closed structure on a protected island where a DRNA permit is required for access.[^7][^18]
