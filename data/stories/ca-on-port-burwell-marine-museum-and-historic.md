The Historic 1840 Lighthouse at Port Burwell stands on a hill above the harbour on Lake Erie's north shore, at 17 Robinson Street in the Municipality of Bayham, Ontario. Built of wood in 1840, it is the oldest wooden lighthouse on the Canadian shore of Lake Erie. It has been closed to visitors since 2023, when its structure was declared unsafe, and in July 2026 it passed from the municipality to the Port Burwell Historical Society.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5]

## The place

Port Burwell sits at the mouth of Big Otter Creek. Mahlon Burwell, a surveyor for Thomas Talbot, laid out the village in 1830 and gave land to a company that was to make the creek mouth a harbour for exporting lumber and farm produce.[^2] The register says the lighthouse was built to guide shipping into that harbour after the harbour works passed from the company to the province in 1840, when the port played an important role in shipping local timber.[^1]

In 1837 a committee of the Assembly of Upper Canada recommended lights for Lake Erie, Port Burwell among them, and money was granted for the Port Burwell light that year.[^2] Alexander Saxon completed the wooden tower in 1840.[^2]

The Historical Society prints an extract from *Memories: A History of Port Burwell* by Frank and Nancy Prothero. The extract says that in a storm the light did little for a captain beyond showing him how much trouble he was in, and that in fair weather it was a dependable guide into port.[^3]

## Building the light

The tower was framed in large pine timbers from the Big Otter Valley, clad in lapstrake siding, and topped by a glassed cupola whose lenses were imported from France.[^3] In 1876 a superintendent of lights inspected it and described three base-burner fountain lamps and three 15-inch reflectors on an iron frame, in a wooden lantern. He gave the tower as 13.7 m (45 ft) high, and noted a focal plane of 27 m (90 ft) above Lake Erie, thanks to the bluff it stood on.[^2] The Historical Society gives a height of more than 20 m (65 ft) above the ground, so the two accounts disagree.[^3]

In 1910 a fourth-order lens, burning petroleum vapour under an incandescent mantle, replaced the lamps and reflectors.[^2][^7] The register dates a Fresnel dioptric lens to "around the turn of the century," about a decade before that change.[^1]

The harbour changed around the old tower. The breakwater piers at the harbour mouth were being extended by the Department of Public Works, and temporary lights were kept on the outer ends of both piers while the work went on.[^6] LighthouseFriends dates the extension to 1899.[^2] The car-ferry company that ran between Ashtabula, Ohio, and Port Burwell set up two range lights on the east pier in 1906, and the Department took them over in 1910.[^2][^7] An unwatched dioptric light followed on the west breakwater in 1914, and a diaphone fog alarm on the east pier in November 1917. The diaphone was discontinued in 1930.[^2]

## The keepers

The first keeper was James P. Bellairs, who served from 1840 to 1852.[^2] The lighthouse then passed to the Sutherland family. LighthouseFriends gives the keepers as Alexander Sutherland, 1852 to 1874; Alexander Sutherland, Jr., appointed on 7 May 1874 in place of his father; William Sutherland, 1885 to 1895; John Sutherland, 1895 to 1939; John Sutherland, Jr., 1939 to 1947; John Sutherland III, 1947 to 1952; Jack Hayward, 1952 to 1955, who was not a Sutherland; and John Sutherland III again, 1955 to 1962.[^2] The register says the family, with the exception of a three-year period, tended the light for over a century, from 1852 to 1962.[^1]

The 1877 inspection report, as LighthouseFriends gives it, noted that Keeper Sutherland had a family of sixteen.[^2] Department lists of lightkeepers name a Sutherland at Port Burwell in the year to 30 June 1906, and a John Sutherland at Port Burwell, Inner Range, in the year to 31 March 1911.[^8][^9] Both entries were checked on the page images, which confirm that a Sutherland kept the station in those years.

At a 1987 ceremony for the restored tower, a granddaughter of the family recalled that John Sutherland had been the longest-serving keeper, 45 years by her account, and had received a King George medal for long service.[^2] LighthouseFriends' roster dates his service from 1895 to 1939, which is 44 years.

## After the keepers

The light was taken out of service in 1962.[^1][^2] The village of Port Burwell acquired the tower in April 1965. The Historical Society says the village made a partial restoration in 1977 with a Local Initiatives Program grant, painted it in 1983, and then allowed public tours.[^3] The village made the tower a heritage property by bylaw in 1985.[^1][^3]

In July 1986 a larger restoration began, led by Leroy Eicher and a team of local Mennonite craftsmen who worked with the same hand tools used to build the tower. Virtually all of the long beams had to be replaced with Douglas fir, and the exterior siding had to be replaced too.[^3] The restored tower was the subject of a ceremonial relighting on 20 June 1987.[^2]

On 1 January 1998 the village was amalgamated into the Municipality of Bayham, which took over the lighthouse.[^3] In 2022 the municipality noticed problems and began a process to replace some exterior components. After objections from the community and the Ontario Historical Society, it dropped that approach and followed the process for historic buildings.[^3] In early 2023 the foundation and support beams were found to be unsound, and the structure was declared unsafe. A temporary collar and guy wires were put on against wind shear.[^3] The tower was closed to the public in 2023.[^4][^5] A consulting firm estimated in August 2023 that restoration would cost about $1.5 million.[^3]

The Historical Society wrote to the municipality in July 2024 to ask to acquire the lighthouse.[^3] On 25 June 2026 the municipality declared it surplus. On 30 July 2026 council approved transferring the property to the Historical Society, and the mayor handed the symbolic key to the Society's president the next day.[^3] An engineering assessment began on 1 September 2026, funded by a local donation.[^4] The Society has raised just under $55,000 toward the work, and the National Trust for Canada listed the lighthouse as endangered in 2026.[^4]

## Today

The Port Burwell Historical Society is now the owner and sole steward of the lighthouse.[^3][^4] The tower is closed to visitors, and engineered stabilization measures are in place.[^5] The Port Burwell Marine Museum sits across the street at 20 Pitt Street and opens daily from Victoria Day weekend to Labour Day. Its page reads "closed for the season" at the time of checking.[^5] LighthouseFriends reports that a Fresnel lens is still in place in the lantern room, and that the harbour's Outer West Breakwater Light and a privately maintained tower on the east pier remain active.[^2] Do not try to climb or enter the closed tower.
