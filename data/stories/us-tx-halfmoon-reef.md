Halfmoon Reef Light was a wooden screw-pile lighthouse on a shoal in Matagorda Bay, on the Texas coast. It was first lit in 1858, went dark during the Civil War and came back in 1868. In 1942 the Coast Guard decided to sell it rather than repair a storm-damaged walkway, and it now stands on land at Port Lavaca, beside Highway 35.[^1][^2]

## The place

The reef lies on the eastern side of Matagorda Bay,[^2] about two miles (3.2 km) from the mainland, according to a 1934 Associated Press report.[^3] The Texas historical marker beside the lighthouse in Port Lavaca says the light was an aid to ships trading in Port Lavaca and the nearby town of Indianola.[^4]

After the Matagorda Island light began guiding ships into the bay in 1852, requests came for lights to mark the channels and hazards inside it, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] In 1854 Congress gave the Lighthouse Board money, on the Board's petition, to build one on the southern tip of the reef.[^2]

## Building the light

The design took about two years to settle, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The plan was a hexagonal wooden house, sixteen feet (4.9 m) on each side, topped by a lantern room and standing on seven iron piles, each 25 feet (7.6 m) long. The piles had threaded ends, so they could be screwed nine feet (2.7 m) into the shoal.[^2]

General context, not this station's own record: Arnold Burges Johnson's 1889 history of the Light-House Service describes the screw pile's broad flange as helicoidal, like an auger, so that turning the pile bores it down into sand or mud.[^5]

The piles arrived at Matagorda Bay early in 1858, shipped from Baltimore to Galveston on the vessel that also carried the cast-iron extensions for the Matagorda and Bolivar Point lights.[^2] The light was finished by 1 July 1858, and its sixth-order Fresnel lens showed a fixed white light.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1858 for the original lens. It lists the optics in order: a lens lantern, then a sixth-order Fresnel, then a fourth-order Fresnel.[^1]

The dates are not settled. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1937 historical sketch says a lighthouse on Halfmoon Reef was completed by 1850, four years before the 1858 date in the Society's record and in LighthouseFriends.[^6][^1][^2] No other source here gives the earlier date.

Mariners said the dunes along the Matagorda peninsula sometimes hid the light from ships sailing the gulf, so that it seemed to flash like the Matagorda Island light.[^2] To end that confusion, a ruby-red glass chimney was put on the lamp, and the light turned red.[^2]

The war put the light out.[^2] The marker says Confederate troops disabled it in an attempt to hinder Federal efforts to capture Southern blockade runners.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the light was extinguished during the war but not dismantled or damaged, and that it did not return to service until 20 February 1868.[^2] The two accounts describe the wartime action differently.

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists 45 stints at the station, from 1859 to 1942.[^1] Its first head keepers, W. B. Bridges and Richard O. Hanlon, served from 1859 to 1861. The roster has no names between 1862 and 1867, when the light was still out of service.[^1][^2]

After the light came back, the keepers changed quickly. Three head keepers served in 1868 and 1869 alone: Joseph R. Sheldon, Benjamin C. Porter and John Y. Lawless. The roster lists Harriet J. Lawless as first assistant in 1869. Horace W. Crockett was head keeper from 1869 to 1882, and Jane Crockett was first assistant in 1869 to 1871 and again in 1873 to 1875.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the station twice had a keeper and assistant who were a married couple. A few children were raised there, but several wives kept a house ashore for fear that a child might fall into the water.[^2]

Stephen D. Hill was head keeper from 1902 to 1906. LighthouseFriends tells how his daughter Sadie walked in her sleep, and how her parents tied a string between her big toe and her sister's every night until the walks stopped.[^2]

Albert Broussard was head keeper from 1914 to 1917, and LighthouseFriends records that a son was born at the station in November 1914.[^2] Charles W. Heartt, head keeper from 1910 to 1913, and George W. Anderson, head keeper from 1918 to 1923, both appear in the rescue record below. Charles M. Teller was head keeper from 1923 to 1935.[^1]

Horace O. Welch was first assistant from 1930 to 1934 on the roster, though the newspapers of December 1934 call him the keeper.[^1][^3] Boatmen took him out to the reef on 13 December 1934.[^3] The Associated Press reported that his last log entry read "Lamp lighted at 6 p. m. All is well."[^3] Three days later the beacon was found dark, its oil used up and its wick burned out.[^3] Welch's money and personal effects were still in the house, but there was no trace of him.[^3] The AP described him as marooned on the reef without a boat, separated from the mainland by two miles of water swept by treacherous currents.[^3]

An Associated Press dispatch from Port Lavaca, printed on 29 December, said his body had been found that Friday at Pass Cavallo, three miles from the lighthouse.[^7] LighthouseFriends gives another place: the body washed ashore near Port O'Connor, twelve miles from the reef. It says the authorities ruled the death an accident, believing Welch had fallen from the lighthouse and drowned.[^2]

Dallas M. "Mike" Nelson, head keeper from 1935 to 1942, was the last keeper.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the assistant's post was abolished in 1935 and that an eight-day lantern was installed not long afterwards, so the remaining keeper could live ashore. The keeper called at the reef about once a week and was also given charge of several other lights on the bay.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends says the 1875 hurricane devastated Indianola and destroyed two screw-pile lights at the entrance to Pass Cavallo, killing all four of their keepers. The Halfmoon Reef house, it says, suffered little damage.[^2] A hurricane in 1886 caused moderate damage to the lighthouse.[^2] Traffic in the bay had declined, so the Lighthouse Board decided to discontinue the light rather than repair it. The house was kept as a daymark until the light was relit in 1902.[^2]

On 16 September 1913 Charles W. Heartt, the keeper, towed the disabled launch Nettie to port and gave her master and crew food, the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported.[^8] On 18 August 1916 a hurricane damaged Lighthouse Service property at Aransas Pass, Matagorda, Half Moon Reef, Galveston Jetty and Oliver Point.[^9]

On 6 January 1919 keeper George W. Anderson helped the captain of the launch Oma B., which had stranded near the station in a gale.[^10] Then came the hurricane of 13 to 14 September 1919. The Lighthouse Service commended assistant keeper Archie E. Fox for his efforts in maintaining the light after the hurricane severely damaged it.[^11] LighthouseFriends says Fox was trapped on the station during the storm.[^2]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported that in fiscal 1922 keeper G. W. Anderson went to help fishermen adrift in an open boat two miles from shore.[^12] On 1 February 1924 keeper Charles M. Teller, on his way from the station to shore, towed the sailboat Herbert D to Palacios. She had been aground for several days with her engines disabled and her crew exhausted.[^13] On 17 April 1925 assistant keeper W. A. Madden helped the motor boat Marine, which had grounded on the reef, and piloted her to Palacios.[^14]

## After the keepers

The light was lit again in 1902. LighthouseFriends says a fourth-order red lens went into service on 15 September 1902.[^2] In 1911 a schooner brought materials, and a work crew lived aboard it while they put a steeper roof on the house to stop persistent leaks.[^2]

The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1920 lists work in progress at the station: materials had been delivered, and framing of the joists was under way.[^15] The report for fiscal 1921 lists one side of the dwelling renewed, with gutters and the cistern.[^16]

In 1942 a hurricane tore the walkway from the lighthouse and left it sagging on its pilings.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1942 as the year the light was discontinued.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard decided to sell the structure rather than repair it. Bill Bauer and Henry Smith bought the lighthouse, which they planned to use as quarters for a night watchman at their Point Comfort dredging business.[^2] The house was moved to Point Comfort, about seven miles away, in 1943.[^4] LighthouseFriends says military personnel ordered the movers to leave the area before a planned bombing practice. The movers refused to leave until the house was salvaged, and the bombing was delayed.[^2]

By 1978 Bauer had owned the house for 36 years. That year he gave it to the Calhoun County Historical Commission to be used as a museum.[^17] LighthouseFriends adds that he supplied a sizable trust fund for its upkeep.[^2] The house was transported across the Matagorda Causeway and restored the following year, in 1979, as an Eagle Scout service project.[^2]

In 1985 the lighthouse was set on its present piers, a porch was added and three flagpoles were put up.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the unveiling of a Texas historical marker there to 1985. The marker's own inscription says it was "Erected 1980" and that the house was "relocated here in 1979."[^2][^4]

## Today

The lighthouse stands alongside Highway 35 in Port Lavaca, next to the Bauer Community Center.[^2] It is no longer an active aid to navigation, and its optic has been removed.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record lists the station as open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse still welcomes visitors to Port Lavaca, that the grounds and tower are open, and that entry is arranged at the Bauer Community Center next door.[^2]

The sources differ on who owns it. LighthouseFriends says the Calhoun County Historical Society owns the lighthouse. The 1978 gift went to the Calhoun County Historical Commission, and these sources do not reconcile the two names.[^2][^17]
