Squirrel Point Light stands at the southwest tip of Arrowsic Island, on the east bank of the Kennebec River in Sagadahoc County, Maine.[^1] Its shingled octagonal tower went up in 1898, with a keeper's house and a barn beside it; a boathouse followed in 1901.[^1] It is one of four river light stations the Lighthouse Board built on the Kennebec that year.[^1]

## The place

The Kennebec was a working river. In its 1892 report the Lighthouse Board counted "There were 3,137 arrivals" of vessels in the river during the year, and noted that two passenger steamers made "ninety-six round trips each" between Gardiner and Boston.[^4]

The river was hard to read at night and in fog. The Board wrote that it "not only has the sea fogs, which extend to Bath," but also its own river fog, and that on dark nights it was sometimes impossible to tell where the water ended and the shore began.[^4]

The Board said the Light-House Establishment "maintains no lights or fog signals in the Kennebec." The Kennebec Steamboat Company and the towboat companies had for many years maintained lanterns hung on the buoys at turning points.[^4]

In the same report the Board recommended a group of Kennebec lights, Squirrel Point among them. The estimate for the Squirrel Point light was $4,650, for "a fixed red light from a lens lantern, with a white sector."[^4]

The point's name is older than the light. The Citizens for Squirrel Point history says that Governor Shute's frigate *The Squirrel* ran aground on the point in 1717, on the way back down river from a treaty conference with local tribes.[^11] LighthouseFriends names Governor Samuel Shute and tells the same story.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress acted in 1895. The Board's report for that year records an appropriation of $17,000 "made by the act approved on March 2," for a set of Kennebec lights that included "a light at or near Squirrel Point."[^5]

The Board's report for 1896 says the title to the site was nearly ready, that plans were in progress, and that the station was expected to be completed that season.[^6] By the next year the title was in hand, a contract had been made for erecting the buildings, and the Board expected completion by February 1898.[^7]

A *Daily Kennebec Journal* article of 18 December 1897 described the work as nearly done. Crews had been busy "during the past several months" on the lights, bell towers and keepers' homes, and the lights would be "ready for occupancy when the contract for construction closes in January."[^3] The article placed the Squirrel Point light "opposite Phippsburg Centre," and said the government would hire four keepers at about $900 a year, to live and work at the lights through the year. The homes were "neat and comfortable dwellings."[^3]

The National Register nomination describes the complex as built in stages. The tower is an octagonal wooden frame structure of 1898, and the two-story keeper's house and barn date from the same year. The bell house was added in 1902, the boathouse in 1901, and the oil house in 1906.[^1]

The Board's 1899 report says that "a gallery with railing was built around the lantern," along with a boat slip and plank walks.[^8] The Board's 1901 report says that $1,620, appropriated on 6 June 1900, was applied in part to building a boathouse and boat slip, and that the barn and fuel house were moved nearer the dwelling.[^9]

The Citizens' history says a fifth-order Fresnel lens was added in 1902.[^11] The USLHS record gives 1902 as the year the original lens was installed.[^10]

## The keepers

George Matthews was the first keeper. LighthouseFriends says he came from Whitehead Lighthouse and kept Squirrel Point from 1898 to 1916.[^2][^10]

In 1916 Charles L. Knight, a clerk in the Lighthouse Inspector's office, asked for the station. LighthouseFriends says the move cut his salary from $900 to $552. After about a decade at Squirrel Point, Knight took a transfer to Goose Rocks Lighthouse, so his family could live in a town with a school.[^2]

The roster lists the keepers who followed: Asa Smith (1926 to 1937), Harold E. Seavey (1937 to 1953), Stanley C. Reynolds (1953 to 1955) and Clarence A. Skolfield (1955 to 1968).[^10] LighthouseFriends calls Skolfield the last civilian keeper of Squirrel Point, and says he retired in 1968 after thirteen years here.[^2]

Joseph Robicheau was the station's last keeper, leaving in 1981. After that, the keeper of the Kennebec River Range Light tended Squirrel Point for a time, until the light was automated.[^2][^10]

## After the keepers

The sources date the automation three ways. The USLHS record gives 1981.[^10] The Citizens' history says the light was "fully automated in 1979," and that its Fresnel lens then went to the museum at Portland Head Light.[^11] The National Register nomination, written in 1987, says the station "was automated in 1982."[^1]

Ownership has been contested. LighthouseFriends says that after Mike Trenholm first saw the lighthouse in 1993, he formed Squirrel Point Light Associates, and that Congress awarded the lighthouse to that group in 1996, with the deed following in 1998.[^2] The Citizens' history names a different holder: in 1996 the property went to a private entity, Squirrel Point Associates, under a deed requiring public use. It says the group that calls itself Citizens for Squirrel Point sued in 2002 to enforce the deed's reversion clause, and that a federal court ruled in its favor in 2005.[^11] LighthouseFriends dates the group's formation and its suit to April 2003, and says a federal judge sided with the group in early 2005.[^2]

The Citizens' history also says that in 2008 the Coast Guard changed the light's power from electric to solar, and disconnected the fog horn, which GPS had made obsolete.[^11]

## Today

The USLHS record lists Citizens for Squirrel Point as the owner, and says the station is not open to the public.[^10] LighthouseFriends gives the Coast Guard as owner, and says the grounds are open while the dwelling and tower are closed.[^2] The group says the Coast Guard has licensed it to give access "for the educational benefit of the public," and that restoration of the keeper's house and shed is under way.[^11]

The light is still an active aid to navigation. The Citizens' history says its "alternating three seconds of light and darkness" is now supplied by a modern optic.[^11][^10] The National Register describes the complex as "a relatively intact complex of five detached buildings."[^1] Check with Citizens for Squirrel Point before going.[^10][^11]
