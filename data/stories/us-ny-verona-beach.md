Verona Beach Light is a conical concrete tower at the eastern end of Oneida Lake in New York, where it marks the entrance to Wood Creek Canal.[^1][^2] It was first lit in the late summer of 1917.[^1] Lighthouse Digest also calls it the Sylvan Beach Light and places it very close to the town of Sylvan Beach.[^4]

## The place

Lighthouse Digest calls Oneida Lake the largest lake lying wholly within New York State.[^4] It says the three lights on the lake were built to guard travel on the 22-mile lake, which is part of the Barge Canal route.[^4]

The Barge Canal was a new canal that followed much of the original Erie route. The Erie Canal Foundation dates its completion to 1918,[^6] and Lighthouse Friends gives 1918 as the year it opened.[^1] Tug 44 instead links the tower's build year to the canal's opening, and gives 1917.[^3]

## Building the light

Lighthouse Friends dates the work to a state contract. Under contract 132, awarded to the Buffalo firm of Lupfer & Remick, work began in 1916 on three concrete towers for Lake Oneida. One of the three was at Verona Beach.[^1]

The other sources date the design or the build earlier. Tug 44 reports a sign on the tower's door that gives a design date of 1915 and names Lupfer & Remick.[^3] Lighthouse Digest says all three towers were built in 1915.[^4] The association's website says the three lights have been there since 1915.[^5] This story takes 1916 as the start of work, following Lighthouse Friends, and records the 1915 dates as disagreements. Tug 44 says construction was completed in 1917.[^3]

The tower was built of prefabricated concrete sections, eighteen feet each, barged in from Buffalo.[^1][^3] By the close of 1916 the Brewerton tower was complete, and the Verona Beach tower was about half done.[^1] Each tower carried a gas beacon behind an exposed glass lens.[^1]

The sources give different heights. The Society's record puts the center of the lantern 84.5 feet (26 m) above the base.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the Verona Beach tower is "curiously a half-foot shorter" than its two sister towers, which stand 85 feet (26 m) tall.[^1] Tug 44 gives 85 feet,[^3] and Lighthouse Digest gives about 80 feet.[^4]

Lighthouse Friends says the tower was soon connected to commercial power.[^1] Lighthouse Digest dates the change from a gas-cylinder fuel system to commercial electric service to 1927.[^4]

## The keepers

The sources name no keeper of this tower. Lighthouse Friends describes how the state ran its keepers for all of its aids. That is general context, not a record of this light.[^1] At first the state's Department of Canals and Waterways tended its more than 2,000 lighted aids with existing state employees and with people it hired from among friends and relatives.[^1] In 1920 the state set up a formal program for hiring and evaluating keepers, with examinations and regular inspections.[^1] Each keeper was given a motorboat and was responsible for inspecting all the aids in a sector of roughly ten miles.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by New York State Canals and managed by the Verona Beach Lighthouse Association.[^1] Grounds are open, and the tower is closed.[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives these directions: from Highway 13 (Main Street) in Verona Beach, go west on Oneida Street to its end, turn left on Forest Avenue, and after a short distance turn right on Fourth Avenue. The avenue ends at a small parking lot for the lighthouse.[^1]

The Verona Beach Lighthouse Association formed in 2002. Its website describes its aim as restoring, enhancing and maintaining the tower.[^5]

In 2003 the tower was still unpainted concrete. Lighthouse Digest reports that the association's treasurer said it had been white at one time, and that the group would like to see it white again.[^4] Tug 44 reports a new coat of white paint in 2014.[^3]

The sources disagree on whether the light is still an active aid to navigation. Lighthouse Friends says all of its towers are still active.[^1] Lighthouse Digest says all three are active,[^4] and Tug 44 says the state canal corporation maintains it as an active aid to navigation.[^3] The association's website calls the tower fully operational.[^5] The Society's record gives "Active Aid to Navigation?: No."[^2]
