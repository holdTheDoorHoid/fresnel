Peninsula Point Light stands at the southern tip of the Stonington Peninsula, where the land juts into Lake Michigan from Michigan's Upper Peninsula. A brick tower and keeper's house went up there in 1865 to warn ships off a shoal. The light was automated in 1922 and put out in 1936. The tower still stands in a Hiawatha National Forest picnic area, and visitors can climb it.[^1][^2]

## The place

The point is a dangerous corner. Terry Pepper's history says vessel masters passing it in the dark "were forced take extreme care as they rounded Point Peninsula to avoid the shoals which extended from the point some two miles into the lake."[^3] The Forest Service describes the traffic that made a light necessary: "During the 1860's, the ports of Escanaba, Gladstone, Fayette and Nahma were teeming with ships hauling fish, lumber, iron ore and other products."[^2]

The first formal request for a light in the sources read came in 1856. Terry Pepper says Michigan representative George Washington Peck "presented a petition on behalf of the citizens of Milwaukee" for a light on Point Peninsula, before the House of Representatives on April 21, 1856.[^3] Congress appropriated $5,000 for the station on 18 August 1856.[^3] LighthouseFriends says all but $727.94 of it "reverted to the treasury in 1859."[^4] Terry Pepper says "problems in obtaining title to the site were experienced," and that "construction of the new station was never undertaken."[^3]

Congress voted money again in 1864. The sources disagree on the date. Terry Pepper's history gives 2 July 1864 in one place and "July 20, 1864" in another.[^3][^1] LighthouseFriends also gives 2 July, and says $15,000 was allocated then.[^4]

A second shoal, Eleven-Foot Shoal, lay about two and a half miles southwest of the light.[^4] Congress came through with $60,000 in 1890 for a light there, and by 1892 that money had been redirected to build lightships.[^3] The Lighthouse Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1894 says light-vessel No. 60 was established on 6 October 1893 "in the northern end of Green Bay," to the southward and westward of Corona and Eleven-Foot shoals, to "mark the turning point for vessels bound into Little Bay de Noquette, Michigan."[^8]

## Building the light

The Forest Service says construction in 1865 included "a 1½ story brick home for the keeper and his family."[^2] Terry Pepper says the 40-foot (12 m) tower and the attached story-and-a-half keeper's dwelling "were both built of Cream City brick."[^1] LighthouseFriends describes a square tower "attached church style" to the dwelling.[^4]

A fourth-order Fresnel lens went into the decagonal lantern.[^3][^4] Terry Pepper says the lens sat at "a focal plane of 41 feet above lake level" (about 12.5 m), with a single white flash every thirty seconds.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the lens was "installed inside the tower's decagonal lantern room."[^4]

The first keeper, Ira D. Buck, "first appears on payroll records for the station on October 17, 1865." Terry Pepper thinks he showed the light soon after.[^3]

## The keepers

Buck "resigned his commission after only one month," and Terry Pepper says he was "not enamored with the isolated and arduous life of a light keeper."[^3] Thomas Bearse "was appointed to replace Buck, reporting for duty at the station on November 30." He "resigned from service after two years at Point Peninsula."[^3][^5]

Charles Beggs was dispatched to the station on 26 October 1867 and kept the light for twenty years. He "passed away at the station on February 14, 1887."[^3][^5] LighthouseFriends says he had a "profuse hemorrhage from the lungs" in late 1885, and that an attempt to make his widow, Mary, keeper after him failed.[^4]

Henry Corgan came in 1887 from Gull Rock, where he had been an assistant keeper.[^3] "After a short year at Point Peninsula," Terry Pepper says, he went back to the Keweenaw as keeper at Copper Harbor.[^3] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1924 notice on his retirement lists his stations as Gull Rock, Point Peninsula, Copper Harbor and St. Clair Flats, and says he had "more than 50 years' service" in all.[^6]

Peter Knudsen "was transferred in from Pilot Island, where he had served as Principal Keeper for the past five years." He left in August 1889.[^3][^5]

James D. Armstrong's appointment was reported in a Michigan weekly that September. The paper said the secretary of the treasury had "appointed James D. Armstrong keeper of tho lighthouse at Point Peninsula."[^7] Terry Pepper says he was "Acting Keeper until he was promoted to full Head Keeper status effective July 5, 1890."[^3] LighthouseFriends says there were no roads when he arrived, that he had to use a wheelbarrow to bring his belongings in, and that his "wife and six children grew to like the area."[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Ice did real damage. Terry Pepper says that after a particularly bad winter, in 1887-1888, "the landing dock and boat ways at Point Peninsula had been completely obliterated by ice."[^3] LighthouseFriends dates the destruction of the landing dock to the winter of 1885-86.[^4] The two sources disagree on the winter. Congress approved money for repairs to the dock in the sundry civil act of 2 March 1889, but Terry Pepper says "the repairs remained unmade" while the Board pressed for a new offshore light.[^3][^9]

LighthouseFriends prints entries from the station's logbook. A keeper's daughter shared them at a 1949 "pioneer day" on the point.[^4] On 21 August 1872 the schooner Pathfinder went aground on a reef off the light and was towed off. On 31 October 1896 the entry says "Two of the men was drowned from the 11 foot light ship" about two miles west of the shoal. The entry dated 18 December 1913 says the schooner Lucie Graham went onto the point the night before, and "The next morning the keeper went aboard but found no one there."[^4]

## After the keepers

The station changed in small steps. Terry Pepper says a work crew arrived in 1902, and "By the following year, a pair of dormers had been added on each side of the roof."[^3] On 1 July 1913 the kerosene lamp was upgraded to "an incandescent mantle fueled by coal gas."[^3]

In 1922 the light was automated. The Forest Service says "an automated acetylene light replaced" the oil lamp.[^2] Terry Pepper says the Fourth Order lens was removed from the lantern and replaced by a 300mm lens, and that the light was changed to "repeated 1-second flash followed by a nine-second eclipse."[^3] LighthouseFriends gives the new character as "a one-second white flash every ten seconds," and says the lightship at Eleven-Foot Shoal marked the shoal through 1925 and was then sold.[^4] Terry Pepper says that ship was removed in 1925 and "sold into private ownership the following year."[^3]

Terry Pepper says Armstrong stayed at the station until August 1925, when he moved to Escanaba. LighthouseFriends says he was made an extra keeper at Escanaba in May 1922.[^3][^4] The USLHS roster ends his stint at Point Peninsula in 1922.[^5]

In 1936, when the Minneapolis Shoal light was put into operation, Peninsula Point was put out. The Forest Service says "the property declared surplus."[^2] Terry Pepper says "the Point Peninsula light was decommissioned and its light permanently extinguished."[^3] LighthouseFriends says the property went to the Forest Service on 1 November 1936. Terry Pepper says ownership of the abandoned building passed to the Forest Service in 1937, and the Forest Service says it was granted custodianship that year.[^4][^3][^2]

The Civilian Conservation Corps "repaired the buildings and constructed picnic grounds" on the point.[^2] In 1948, the Forest Service could no longer finance repairs, and demolition was considered.[^2] LighthouseFriends quotes a Forest Service officer: "Because we have no money to maintain it."[^4] The Stonington Grange took over maintenance in 1949, and its restoration won a state prize that year.[^2][^3]

The keeper's dwelling burned in 1959. The Forest Service says "the brick tower survived."[^2] Terry Pepper says the dwelling "burned beyond repair in 1959," and that the damage to the tower's north side was repaired in 1962.[^3]

## Today

The Forest Service describes Peninsula Point as a picnic area with restrooms and tables. It says visitors "can climb the 40 foot circular staircase" (about 12 m) to the lantern.[^2] The Forest Service says the last 0.8 mile of road is "single-lane, narrow and winding," and does not recommend RVs or trailers "over 16-feet long or 8-feet high" (about 4.9 m long or 2.4 m high) on it.[^2] The Forest Service also calls the point "a popular spot to enjoy the Monarch Butterfly and bird migration."[^2]

The tower is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. NPGallery's asset record gives 28 April 1975 as the publication date.[^10][^2]
