Stoddart Island Light, known locally as the Emerald Isle Light, stands on the northwest point of a privately owned island at the entrance to Shag Harbour, on Nova Scotia's South Shore.[^1][^2][^5] It is a 6 m (21 ft) square wooden tower, and the light was discontinued in early 2004.[^2][^5][^1]

## The place

In 1831 Michael Wrayton came from Dublin to Nova Scotia aboard the brig *Mary*.[^1] The brig struck a shoal entering the harbour at Barrington, and the passengers spent the night pumping and bailing to keep her afloat.[^1] At Doctor's Cove he became a wharf owner and shipowner, with vessels in the West Indies trade.[^1]

Around 1860 he bought Stoddart Island and moved his family there.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the island was once called Wrayton Isle, but that Wrayton preferred the name "Emerald Isle", after his homeland.[^1] The island is privately owned.[^5]

The red light was meant to guide fishing boats and other craft into Stoddart Harbour.[^1] NSLPS places it on the northwest point, at the approaches to Shag Harbour.[^2]

## Building the light

In 1875 Wrayton had planned to show a light for mariners from a window of his house.[^1] Instead the government supplied an old eight-sided iron lantern on a cheap wooden tower, at a cost of $150.[^1] Wrayton tended the light for two years without pay, and from 7 May 1877 he received $100 a year.[^1]

The sources give different start dates. LighthouseFriends' keeper list begins Wrayton's keeping in 1875. NSLPS gives 1877 as the year the light began, and Rowlett gives 1877 as the year the station was established.[^1][^2][^5]

LighthouseFriends and Rowlett both date the present squat tower to 1886. LighthouseFriends says material from the old tower went into an oil house.[^1][^5] NSLPS gives the tower's height as 21 feet and calls it a square wooden tower, but gives no construction date.[^2]

The Department of Marine and Fisheries' report for the twelve months ended 31 March 1910 records that at Stoddart Island "a 5th order 270° dioptric illuminating apparatus has been substituted for the catoptric apparatus heretofore used."[^3] NSLPS's history gives the fifth-order dioptric lens from 1922.[^2] The two dates are twelve years apart, and the sources do not explain the gap.

## The keepers

Michael Wrayton kept the light from 1875 until 1879, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] His wife Maria died in September 1879. About three months later he drowned, with his two youngest children, when their boat, the *Ocean Belle*, went down.[^1] The sources give no place for the sinking.

His son William took charge of the light, assisted by his sister Agnes, known as "Aggie".[^1] In 1881 William was moved to the Bon Portage light, and his older brother Arthur became keeper here.[^1] Arthur drowned in Argyle Bay in 1892, leaving a wife, Alice, and nine children.[^1] Alice Wrayton looked after the light for a couple of years.[^1] LighthouseFriends says she was charged with manslaughter over the death of a hired hand, Billy Thurston, and was later exonerated.[^1]

J. A. Larkin kept the light from 1894 to 1896.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates Ephraim Larkin's appointment to 18 March 1896, says he served more than twenty-four years, and gives his keeper-list span as ending at least in 1931.[^1] The Department's keeper table for 1910 names Ephraim Larkin at Stoddart Island, but prints his appointment date as 18 March 1806. That does not fit the other sources.[^4]

Evelyn Fox, Ephraim Larkin's granddaughter through her mother, was born on the island on 16 May 1902.[^6][^1] She married Morrill Richardson on Emerald Isle, and the couple later tended the light on Bon Portage Island for thirty-five years.[^6][^1] Her book about their life on Bon Portage, *We Keep a Light*, was published in 1945.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources name no storm, shipwreck or rescue at this light. LighthouseFriends records two drowning events in the Wrayton family: Michael Wrayton's in 1879, with two of his children, and Arthur Wrayton's in Argyle Bay in 1892.[^1]

## After the keepers

NSLPS lists a hand fog horn "in answer to vessels" at the station between 1941 and 1960, and says the fog horn was discontinued between 1962 and 1965.[^2] NSLPS's history gives the fifth-order dioptric lens as flashing green, with a four-second period, in 1990.[^2] NSLPS says the light was automated by 1993.[^2] LighthouseFriends says it was discontinued in early 2004.[^1] Rowlett's directory says it has been inactive since 2004.[^5]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds and tower are closed.[^1] NSLPS says the tower still stands.[^2] Rowlett says the island is privately owned and reachable only by boat, with the site and tower closed.[^5] Sea kayaking tours pass the light.[^1] Because the island is private and the tower is closed, visitors should not land there or climb the tower.[^1][^5]

NSLPS's own entry contradicts itself on whether the light still operates. It calls the light operational, then answers "No" to whether it is still operating.[^2] The other sources date the end of the light to 2004, so this story follows them.[^1][^5]
