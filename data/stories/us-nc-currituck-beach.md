Currituck Beach Lighthouse is the brick tower at Corolla, on the northern Outer Banks of North Carolina, built to fill a forty-mile dark gap in the coastal lights between Cape Henry and Bodie Island. First lit on 1 December 1875, it keeps the natural brick that sets it apart from the other tall Outer Banks towers.[^2][^3]

## The place

By the early 1870s the Light-House Board was describing the coast here as a gap in an otherwise lit seaboard. Its report said that once Bodie Island was finished, the "dark space" between Cape Henry and Bodie Island would be "a distance of eighty miles and an unlighted space of forty miles", and that a first-order light in the middle would let ships bound round Cape Hatteras see a warning before coming within eighteen or twenty miles of the shore.[^3] The Board gave the reason ships kept close in: vessels from northern and eastern ports ran inside the Gulf Stream to find a favourable current and a smoother sea, and many laden ships had been lost between the two lights.[^3] The Board also noted that an earlier appropriation for this light, made about ten years before, had "reverted to the Treasury".[^3]

The Board first aimed for Poyner's Hill, but found the best point to be near Whale's Head Hill, which it placed about two miles to the north.[^3]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $50,000 on 3 March 1873, a further $70,000 on 23 June 1874 and $20,000 on 3 March 1875.[^3] The site was purchased and North Carolina ceded jurisdiction to the United States in November 1873.[^3]

Materials were the first problem. Currituck Sound was shallow, so the Board's report said freight vessels had to unload at a pier near Church's Island, and the material was then carried in shallow-draught scows about ten miles to the light-house wharf.[^3] Borings in January 1874 found fine sand over a layer of soft mud, roughly four feet thick, with sand again beneath it, so the engineers planned a pile-and-grillage foundation: piles driven 22 to 24 feet and capped with crossed 12-by-12-inch timbers.[^3] Pile driving began on 19 June 1874.[^3] Workers' quarters, shops, a roughly 300-yard wharf into the sound and a railway to the tower site went up first.[^3] The Board planned a brick tower, 150 feet high in its first description, with an octagonal pyramid base under a conical shaft.[^3] Modern sources give the finished tower as 162 feet (49 m), while George Putnam's 1917 survey of the service gave 163 feet, so the sources do not agree on the figure.[^2][^4][^5]

The tower was completed and the light shown on 1 December 1875. It was a first-order light, fixed white, varied by red flashes at intervals the Board gave as about a minute and a half.[^3] The keepers' dwelling was begun in November 1875 and the Board counted the station complete at the end of that fiscal year.[^3] The US Lighthouse Society archive lists the station as established in 1873 and the tower as dating from 1875; 1873 is the year of the first appropriation and the land purchase, while the Board's report gives 1 December 1875 for the first lighting.[^1][^3]

The light itself changed its fuel. The Outer Banks Conservationists' timeline says the lamp changed from lard oil to mineral oil in April 1884.[^4] In 1913 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin listed Currituck Beach among lights whose illuminant had been changed to incandescent oil vapor.[^14] The same timeline records electrification on 4 July 1933.[^4]

## The keepers

The first principal keeper was Nathaniel G. Burris, who served from 1875 to 1879, with Lewis N. Simmons as first assistant and Thomas T. Everton as second assistant.[^1] In all the archive lists eleven principal keepers up to 1939. Among them were William J. Simmons (1895 to 1905), Nathan H. Swain (1905 to 1921), William Riley Austin (1921 to 1928) and Homer T. Austin (1928 to 1937).[^1]

Some careers ran long. William Riley Austin is listed as second assistant from 1891 to 1912, first assistant from 1912 to 1921, then principal keeper until 1928, a span of 1891 to 1928 on this one light. Wesley L. Austin was first assistant from 1893 to 1912.[^1] The Austin surname recurs again with Edward B. Austin, second assistant from 1930 to 1933.[^1]

Two keepers' voices survive in the printed record. In 1928, according to the Outer Banks Conservationists' timeline, First Assistant George G. Johnson set down his duties: on at midnight, checking the light and machinery, and keeping the watch until sunrise, with the machinery that turned the lens wound every three hours.[^4] Keeper William Riley Austin, the same timeline says, described a working day that averaged fifteen hours.[^4] The timeline also records that Johnson's daughter Sadie drowned at Virginia Beach in August 1927 and was buried at Corolla.[^4] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1923 recorded that on 7 October Johnson, as first assistant keeper, helped a yacht that had gone aground near the light station.[^6]

The last principal keeper in the archive's list, William J. Tate, had a second claim to be remembered. In 1929 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that Tate, then keeper of the Currituck Sound lights, had helped the Wright brothers at Kitty Hawk from 1900 to 1903: his wife was postmistress, he answered the Wrights' first inquiries about weather and the lie of the land, and both brothers boarded with the Tates during their first stay. The Bulletin dates Wilbur Wright's arrival at Kitty Hawk to 12 September 1900; Lighthousefriends says he was welcomed into the Tate home on 13 September.[^7][^2] The Bulletin printed Tate's own recollection that local people thought the brothers "harmless cranks" attempting something impossible.[^7]

The keepers' quarters themselves were judged inadequate. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin said they had been so "for a number of years", and in December 1919 the district's construction force began moving an unused two-storey frame dwelling, about 33 by 34 feet and about forty tons, from the Long Point station across Currituck Sound by scow. The workmen lived in the house throughout, and the move, with repairs to the wharf, finished on 23 March 1920 at a cost of about $4,000.[^13] The archive lists the moved building as now the museum shop.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The station's best-documented emergency came on 31 January 1878. The steamer *Metropolis*, bound from Philadelphia for Brazil with railroad iron and a large party of labourers, sprang a leak and was run ashore on the beach at 6:45 in the morning in a high easterly sea and fog.[^8] The Life-Saving Service reported 245 aboard and 85 lost; Lighthousefriends gives 214 workmen aboard and nearly 100 lives lost.[^8][^2] At about 10 that morning, Burris said, a messenger from the Light-House Club told him a vessel was ashore, and he set out down the beach with both assistant light-keepers. On the way they found a man and a woman washed up and tried without success to revive them.[^9] The inspector's report puts the club "nearly half a mile below" the light.[^10] The Service's inspector noted that the time of day for events was hard to fix, because "the clocks in the dwellings are seldom correct"; perhaps the only accurate time-piece on Currituck, he wrote, was at the light-house.[^10]

When Burris went home that afternoon he took a survivor with him. He later swore that he fed and sheltered sixty-one people that night, and about seventy-six at breakfast and dinner the next day, before they left for the steamer to Norfolk.[^9] The Life-Saving Service's own report blamed the "undue distances" between stations on the North Carolina coast, noting twelve miles between Stations 4 and 5, and said new intermediate stations had been authorised that June.[^8] Lighthousefriends says this loss, with a similar disaster two months earlier, prompted the Poyner's Hill life-saving station, six miles south of the light.[^2]

Most of the Board's later entries in the compilation are about upkeep. They record a system of magneto-electric call-bells, extensive repairs to dwellings, outhouses, fences and oil-house, and a renewed landing wharf and pier 1,585 feet long, though the compilation does not print the year for each.[^3] A report of the later 1890s records an iron oil house erected and about 280 feet of board fence built, and in March, according to the 1901 report, a telephone replaced the burned-out call-bell apparatus.[^11][^12]

## After the keepers

The date the resident keepers left is disputed. The Outer Banks Conservationists' timeline says the light was "made automatic, and personnel is discontinued" in September 1937, and that Tate then looked after it as a visiting keeper from the Coinjock reservation.[^4] The US Lighthouse Society roster lists Tate as principal keeper to 1939, and Lighthousefriends gives 1939, when the Coast Guard took over the Lighthouse Service.[^1][^2]

Lighthousefriends reports that coastguardsmen were stationed here in the Second World War to patrol the beach, after which the property was abandoned and the keepers' house was stripped by vandals.[^2] In January 1952 the 31-acre tract was sold to North Carolina for $3,000 for "muskrat experimentation and research".[^4] The tower was entered on the National Register of Historic Places in October 1973.[^4] The Outer Banks Conservationists were incorporated on 30 January 1980 and began restoring the keepers' dwelling under a lease from the state.[^4] The tower opened to climbers on 21 July 1990.[^2][^4] Under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act the deed to the tower passed to the Conservationists on 17 October 2003, after a dispute with Currituck County over ownership.[^2][^4][^15] The federal government kept the first-order Fresnel lens, and the Coast Guard is responsible for operating the light.[^15]

## Today

The tower and the restored keepers' complex are owned and run by Outer Banks Conservationists, Inc.[^4] The light still works as a navigation aid, showing a white flash every twenty seconds.[^15] The tower is open to climbers in a season running from mid-March to December, and the Conservationists give the climb as 220 steps, where Lighthousefriends gives 214.[^2][^4] Check the opening times with the Conservationists before visiting.
