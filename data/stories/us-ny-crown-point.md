Crown Point Light stands at Crown Point, on the New York shore of Lake Champlain, where the lake narrows between Crown Point and Chimney Point.[^1][^2] It was built as a working light in 1858. In 1912 its tower was rebuilt as the Champlain Memorial, a monument to the explorer Samuel de Champlain, and the navigation light moved to a steel tower beside the water in 1926.[^6][^1]

## The place

The point had been a fort site since about 1737, more than a century before the light.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the French built a windmill there around 1737 as part of Fort St. Frederic. The British later built Fort Crown Point on the same ground, and the outer works, the Grenadier Redoubt, can still be seen just south of the lighthouse.[^1]

The need for a light was argued for some twenty years.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that Naval Lieutenant Charles T. Platt recommended lights at Windmill Point and Crown Point in 1838, and that in 1853 Henry B. Smith, the customs collector at Plattsburgh, said a light was needed more at Crown Point than anywhere else on the lake, to guide ships through the narrow channel between Crown Point and Chimney Point.[^1] Putnam's history of the lighthouses says the light was set to "mark a turn in the channel."[^6]

## Building the light

Congress provided $8,000 for the light on 3 August 1854.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the title to the site took a couple of years to settle, so work began only in 1858.[^1] The builders were O'Neal and Ellis of Malone and Champlain, New York.[^1] The tower was a fifty-five-foot (17 m) octagonal limestone tower joined to a wooden cottage.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's place record gives the tower's material as granite and its shape as octagonal, and it lists a fifth-order lens from 1858.[^2]

The sources disagree on when the light first shone. Putnam's history says a light "was placed here in 1858".[^6] LighthouseFriends says the fifth-order Fresnel lens first showed at the opening of navigation in 1859, with its focal plane at eighty-three feet (25 m).[^1] In 1872 Congress appropriated $1,500 for repairs, which led to a new barn and general repairs to the dwelling and grounds.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1893 notes a survey of the reservation and some minor repairs.[^8]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster names eleven principal keepers at Crown Point, from J. W. Norton in 1859 to Louis P. Brown in 1926.[^2] The third was James Raine, who had enlisted in a New York cavalry company in 1861 at the age of fifty-four and returned to Crown Point after a year's service.[^1] Raine kept the light until his death in 1886.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says that Raine's successor, Lafayette Spaulding, was endorsed by a local paper, which expected that he would have his lamps "trimmed and burning" at the proper hours.[^1]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1 May 1920 announced the efficiency flags for 1919, awarded in each district to the station with the highest general efficiency that year.[^4] The Third District's flag went to Crown Point, and the Bulletin names the keeper as Jesse Orton.[^4] Orton had taken over in 1916, and the Bulletin's index also lists him at Crown Point.[^2][^3]

After the light became a memorial in 1912, four keepers served there.[^1] LighthouseFriends says all of them had trouble with visitors, who felt the reservation was public property.[^1] Louis P. Brown, the last keeper, had "No Trespassing" signs placed around the property, along with a "Rules for Visitors" sign.[^1] Visitors could climb the tower only in visiting hours, and never on Sunday.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that in the summer of 1925 Brown gave a trespasser a glancing blow on the jaw during an argument on a Sunday.[^1] Brown fell ill that autumn, and his wife Catherine looked after the light and the grounds until the next spring, when he decided to resign.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 18 March 1868, Raine tried to take his two daughters across the frozen lake to Chimney Point in a sleigh, with his son.[^7][^1] A telegram from Crown Point, printed by the *Worcester Daily Spy*, reported: "Capt. Raine, the lighthouse keeper, assisted by his son, in attempting to draw his two daughters across Lake Champlain to Chimney Point in a sleigh, broke through the ice."[^7] The son reached firm ice and succeeded in saving his father, but the two women fell under the sleigh. The newspaper says their bodies were not recovered until two hours later.[^7] LighthouseFriends names them as Mary, eighteen, and Anna, thirty.[^1]

## After the keepers

Brown retired on 31 July 1926, and the reservation passed to New York State.[^1] The light's job passed to a steel skeleton tower built near the water, with a flashing acetylene light sixty-seven feet (20 m) above the lake. LighthouseFriends gives the estimated cost as $3,358.[^1] The Lighthouse Service's index for 1924–1929 lists "Crown Point lighthouse reservation donated to NY State."[^5]

The bridge at Crown Point was completed in 1929, and in 1931 the steel tower was moved to Windmill Point, where it was used against liquor smuggling.[^1] The keeper's dwelling later came down.[^1]

In 2004 Governor George Pataki announced $730,000 from the state's Environmental Protection Fund for the covered pier and for restoring the lighthouse.[^1] The restoration was completed in time for the 2009 Champlain quadricentennial.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse belongs to the New York State Department of Environmental Conservation.[^1] It stands in the Crown Point Public Campground, reached from the New York side of the Lake Champlain bridge.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds are open and the tower open in season. The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists the station as not open to the public, so check before a visit.[^2][^1]
