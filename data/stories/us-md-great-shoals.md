Great Shoals Light was a square wooden house on a screw-pile foundation at the mouth of the Wicomico River in Maryland. It was first lit on 15 August 1884. The house was taken down in 1966, and a steel tower was built on its foundation.[^1][^2]

## The place

The light stood on the southern end of the Great Shoals, in Monie Bay, on the north side of the entrance to the Swash Channel that leads into the Wicomico River.[^2] The Chesapeake Chapter of the U.S. Lighthouse Society dates the first step to 1882, when the Lighthouse Board recommended a light there at an estimated price of $15,000.[^5] Congress appropriated $15,000 on 3 March 1883 "for a lighthouse on the Great Shoals".[^2] The plan was for a square screw-pile lighthouse at the river's entrance.[^5]

## Building the light

The Lighthouse Board's steamer Jessamine made the borings needed to fix the exact site.[^2] The lumber was bought in January 1884, and the metal-work contract was let on 16 February. Framing of the superstructure began that February at the Lazaretto depot in Baltimore.[^2][^5]

The Oakland, Maryland, *Republican* reported in July 1884 that "the government is now engaged in building a lighthouse at the Great Shoals, at the mouth of the Wicomico river."[^3]

On 17 July 1884 the Jessamine reached the site with a working party on board and scows in tow carrying the metal-work.[^2] The piles were driven from the 19th to the 21st, and the iron foundation was in place on the 22nd. The frame went up on the 26th, and by the 31st the tin roof was on.[^2] On 5 August the work platform came down. By 9 August all the work except the painting was finished. Two men stayed behind to paint the interior and to wait until the keepers came.[^2]

On 15 August 1884 the light was shown from the new house.[^2][^5] Its fifth-order Fresnel lens dates from that year.[^1] The light stood 37 feet (11.3 m) above mean high water.[^1]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster lists 39 keeper stints at Great Shoals, from 1884 to 1951.[^1] Fourteen of them are principal keepers. Two principal keepers appear for the first year. Zebedee Harper is listed for 1884 to 1885. John E. Graves is listed from 21 to 30 August 1884.[^1]

Isaac J. Hearn is listed as a first assistant from June to November 1885, and as principal keeper from 1885 to 1889. The two entries overlap.[^1] Somerfield Smith was first assistant from 1889 to 1890, then principal keeper for nineteen years, from 1890 to 1909. That is the longest principal's stint on the roster.[^1]

Gary E. Powell was an assistant in 1904 to 1906 and again from 1910 to 1918. He was principal keeper in 1918 and 1919.[^1] Charles C. Tyler was an assistant in 1906 and 1907, then principal from 1912 to 1917 and again from 1919 to 1924.[^1]

The roster gives William F. McDorman as first assistant from 1918 to 1925. It gives Walter F. McDorman as first assistant from 1925 to 1937 and as principal from 1937 to 1948.[^1] The archive lists them under different first names. Its listing does not say whether they were one man or two.

Gordy Z. Parks was a first assistant from 1937 to 1943 and principal keeper in 1950 and 1951.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives his first-assistant stint as running to 1945, so the two sources disagree about when it ended.[^2] The archive's last line for the station is a Coast Guard entry dated "at least 1951", with no end date.[^1]

Daniel A. White is on the roster as first assistant from 1895 to 1900.[^1] A 1922 Annapolis paper names a Daniel Asbury White as a former "lighthouse keeper at Shark's Fin", and as having also kept the lights at Great Shoal and Thomas' Point. It places those posts before his seven years as a watchman at the Naval Academy, and says he was born on the Eastern Shore and came to Annapolis in 1901.[^13] The paper, dated 10 March 1922, reports that he died in Brooklyn at the age of 62.[^13] The archive records him as a first assistant at both Great Shoal and Sharkfin Shoal. The paper calls him a keeper.

The Board's report for the fiscal year 1899 records that the fog-bell machine was repaired, new model fifth-order lamps were installed, and the tin roof was repaired.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

According to the Chesapeake Chapter's timeline, the Wicomico was frozen solid in 1905, and the keeper walked ashore to send his mail.[^5] The timeline's source list includes the *Baltimore Sun* of 7 February 1905, which the timeline does not quote.[^5]

In March 1914 the keeper, Charles C. Tyler, and his assistant, Gary E. Powell, helped a party of four in a disabled gasoline launch.[^2][^5] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1912 to 1917 lists Great Shoals rescues at pages 111 and 247 of that volume.[^6]

On the night of 30 October 1916, the Bulletin reports, C. C. Tyler, keeper, brought three people from a disabled motor boat to the light and gave them food and shelter for the night.[^7]

On 13 December 1918 William McDorman, an assistant keeper, fed and lodged three men who had been oystering near the light and had become lost in fog.[^2][^8]

On 25 June 1919 the keepers saw a skiff, the *Robert L. Webster*, drifting south toward Nanticoke Point. They brought it in, moored it near the light and told its owner where it was.[^2][^9]

On 6 October 1920 W. F. McDorman, whom the Bulletin calls an assistant keeper, rescued a motor boat that had drifted away from the schooner *Eddie*, anchored near the light.[^10] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year 1921 calls him the keeper of the station, though the same entry wrongly places the station in Virginia.[^11]

In January 1940 the Norfolk Coast Guard headquarters told the Associated Press that ice had led to orders removing keepers from lights on Chesapeake Bay.[^12] The cutter *McLane* was sent to the Great Shoals light to take off the people stationed there.[^12]

The same timeline lists a second ice removal, on 22 February 1943, with the keepers back on 6 March. It also says that in 1942 the light was blacked out and the fog bell silenced as a wartime measure.[^5]

## After the keepers

The Society's place record gives 1965 as the year the light was discontinued.[^1] LighthouseFriends and the Chesapeake timeline date its deactivation to 1966.[^2][^5] In 1966 the house was taken down. A steel skeletal tower went up on the old screw-pile foundation, and LighthouseFriends says an automatic light was mounted on that foundation.[^1][^2]

## Today

The light is not open to the public.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says a modern light is exhibited from the old screw-pile foundation, and that distant views of the foundation are available from the shore nearby.[^2] The Society's record, by contrast, lists the station as not existing and not an active aid to navigation.[^1] The two sources disagree about the present light, and this story does not settle which is right.
