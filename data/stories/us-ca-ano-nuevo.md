Año Nuevo Light Station was a steam fog signal from 1872 and a light from 1890, on Año Nuevo Island, a rocky island of roughly nine acres (about 4 hectares) five miles (8 km) south of Pigeon Point on the San Mateo County coast. The station was deactivated in 1948, and the island is closed to visitors today.[^1][^2]

## The place

The point and the island keep a Spanish name. The chaplain of the expedition that sailed from Acapulco in January 1602 named a landmark "Punta de Año Nuevo," New Year's Point, for the day on which it was sighted.[^2]

The coast had been surveyed before any light stood there. An 1855 Coast Survey report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, said the point "possesses all the requisites as a site for a guide to Santa Cruz harbor."[^2] LighthouseFriends also names three vessels lost in the years before the government acted: the Carrier Pigeon in 1853, the Sir John Franklin in 1865 and the Coya in 1866. The page does not say where they were lost, so they are not placed at the island here.[^2]

## Building the light

An appropriation for a first-order light was made in 1868, and the government bought the island, and land on Pigeon Point, for $10,000 in 1870.[^2] The Lighthouse Board decided on a first-order light on Pigeon Point and only a fog signal station on the island.[^2]

The fog station was built in 1872. The island's twelve-inch steam whistle first sounded on 29 May 1872, with John Kenney as the first head keeper.[^2] A San Francisco newspaper of 1897 also says that in 1872 the government "built the fog station on the island."[^18]

The keepers' dwelling stood at the south end of the island. A wooden walkway ran north to the signal, about halfway up the western side, and a catchment basin and cistern were built to supply the whistle's water.[^2] Boats reached the mainland from a landing and, later, a boathouse on the east shore.[^2]

A duplicate whistle was added in 1880. LighthouseFriends says that on 1 January 1881 the signal changed from a fifteen-second blast each minute to a ten-second blast and fifty-five seconds of silence.[^2] The 1897 newspaper still describes "a whistle that gives a blast of fifteen seconds every minute."[^18] The two accounts do not agree, and the sources do not settle which is right.

The first light came in 1890. LighthouseFriends says a lens lantern on a post, on the seaward side of the fog signal, was placed in operation on 12 February 1890.[^2]

The whistles' work can be counted year by year. In the Light-House Board's volume for the fiscal years 1891 and 1892, the 12-inch steam whistles, in duplicate, "were in operation 640 hours."[^3] In fiscal 1893 they ran "some 602 hours,"[^4] in fiscal 1894 "some 847 hours,"[^6] and in 1895 "some 783 hours."[^7] In 1896 they ran "some 752 hours,"[^9] in 1899 "some 1,041 hours,"[^19] and in 1901 "some 672 hours."[^13]

The Board also reported the island's upkeep. In fiscal 1894 the tramway "from the landing to the signal was overhauled," 675 feet (206 m) of rail was renewed, and a turntable was put in "to dispense with a long curve."[^5] In 1896 a 35,000-gallon tank went up on a masonry foundation.[^8] In 1897 the keeper built a landing wharf "for small boats," mostly from lumber collected on the beach.[^10]

The fog-signal building was rebuilt in 1899, when "the old fog-signal building was torn down" and a new frame building "was erected in front of the old site."[^11] In 1900 a small frame structure with plate-glass panes was built around the lens lantern "to protect it from the weather," and the tramway was rebuilt.[^12]

LighthouseFriends adds that a nine-room addition was built onto the dwelling in 1905, and that a 15,000-gallon redwood tank was erected in 1907. In 1911 a lantern room on top of the tank displayed a fourth-order light, and in 1914 a square skeleton tower was built beside the tank, with the Fresnel lens moved into it.[^2]

The sources disagree on the tower's date and material. The USLHS record dates its tower to 1890 and calls it wood.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes a steel skeleton tower built in 1914.[^2] This story gives both accounts and does not choose between them.

In 1912 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin, under "important changes in aids to navigation," listed "Ano Nuevo Island, Cal." among the lights changed from fixed to flashing or occulting.[^14]

## The keepers

The USLHS roster lists 26 principal keepers between 1872 and 1948, with acting keepers, first and second assistants and Coast Guard names.[^1] The first head keeper, John W. Kenney, is listed for 1872 alone.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he was removed after about six months, and that most of his immediate successors lasted only a year or two before leaving or being removed.[^2]

In 1902 two keepers and their families shared one dwelling.[^2] LighthouseFriends quotes the Light-House Board's 1902 report as calling it "barely sufficient for one."[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for fiscal 1915 still listed dwellings as "yet needed" at a number of stations, and Año Nuevo Island was among them.[^17]

Thomas H. Butwell was head keeper from 1894 to 1901.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he had worked as a boatman in San Francisco before he came here, and that he brought his setter, Jip, to the island. The dog's death in 1897 was noted in a San Francisco paper.[^2] In July 1897 the San Francisco Call wrote that Butwell was "in charge of the station," that "He has only one assistant," and that the two of them "on many occasions keep the fog whistle going day and night."[^18]

Frederick A. Kreth was head keeper from 1938 to 1948.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that in 1939 he reported a pod of killer whales attacking a sperm whale; the dead whale floated into the shallows near the station.[^2]

In 1916 a keeper's complaints about sea lions, which the keepers were forbidden to kill, were picked up by newspapers across the country.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On Sunday, 8 April 1883, Keeper Henry W. Colburn and his assistant, Bernard A. Ashley, tried to cross to the mainland with two visitors, brothers Clayton and Frank Pratt, who had come over from a nearby dairy farm.[^2][^1] Their boat was swamped in rough seas and drifted out to sea, and LighthouseFriends says all four drowned.[^2]

Stranded on the island, the keepers' wives started the fog signal and raised the station's flag at half-mast to catch the attention of passing vessels, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The steamer Los Angeles dispatched a small vessel to the island and carried word of the tragedy to maritime officials.[^2] LighthouseFriends also says that John Ryan, then first assistant at Pigeon Point, was sent to take charge. The roster lists a John C. Ryan as head keeper here from 1883 to 1886. The sources do not say whether they are one man, so the two are not joined here.[^2][^1]

The crossing was dangerous.[^2] The Call wrote in 1897 that in rough water "the passage can only be made at great risk of life," and that at low tide the shallowest place "is about sixteen feet deep" (about 5 m).[^18] The same article says eight fog-signal keepers "have been drowned while crossing this bit of treacherous water." No other source here gives that number, and LighthouseFriends describes only the 1883 drowning, so the story reports the claim without confirming it.[^18][^2] The Call also reported that the keepers "have had to remain on the island for weeks at a time."[^18]

On 22 October 1926, at 4:40 a.m., an earthquake knocked the oil-vapor lamp off its base onto the lens.[^2] LighthouseFriends says about a third of the lens prisms came out of their brass frame and fell to the lantern-room floor.[^2] The keeper's letter, printed in the Lighthouse Service Bulletin in 1927, says he put the light out at once. It reports a second shock at 5.45 a.m., after which the lens was torn loose from its table.[^15] A replacement fourth-order lens was shipped, and the light was soon back in service.[^2]

A 1932 Lighthouse Service Bulletin item on storm damage says that during the unusually heavy storms that swept the greater part of the Pacific coast "during December and January," at Año Nuevo "heavy seas broke well up onto the reservation."[^16] The sea partly destroyed the landing wharf, damaged the retaining bulkheads and the foundations at the storehouse, and seriously undermined foundations next to the fog signal, the circulating water tank and the oil house.[^16]

## After the keepers

The station was deactivated in 1948, and a whistle buoy about 1,600 yards (1.5 km) south of the island took over its warning work.[^2] The USLHS record gives 1948 as the year it was discontinued.[^1]

In 1958 the island went to auction. A private bidder offered $100,000, but the State of California had first claim, and it bought the island in April 1958 for $51,094, adding it to the new state reserve on the mainland.[^2]

In 1976 the state cut down the steel tower, which was likely to collapse. The toppled tower remains on the island and can be seen from the mainland.[^2]

## Today

Año Nuevo Island is owned by California State Parks, and LighthouseFriends reports its grounds and dwelling closed.[^2] The island and the point lie within the Año Nuevo State Reserve. From December to March, the point can be reached only on scheduled guided walks. The walk from the state park's parking area is about 1.7 miles (2.7 km).[^2]

The historic fog-signal buildings on the island are used as a laboratory and dormitory by the Institute of Marine Sciences at the University of California, Santa Cruz.[^2] Do not land on the island; it is closed.
