Indian Head Lighthouse is a wooden octagonal house with a tower rising from its middle, set at the end of a long breakwater on the southern side of Summerside harbour, Prince Edward Island. It was built at the end of a shoal that runs out from the shore, and it was put in operation for the opening of navigation in 1881.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The historical marker at the site says the light was placed at the end of a long shoal running out from MacCallum's Point as a guide for vessels entering Bedeque Bay.[^3] Lighthouse Friends adds that the breakwater from the point forms the southern side of Summerside harbour, with the lighthouse at its outer end, and that the structure is exposed to the waves of Northumberland Strait.[^2] The spit continued beyond the lighthouse itself. In its report for fiscal 1900 the Department of Marine and Fisheries recorded a red-painted cask moored as a buoy, 300 feet from the lighthouse, to mark where the spit ended.[^4]

## Building the light

Parliament voted $4,000 in 1878 for a new lighthouse and pier at Indian Point, Summerside Harbour, and in 1879 a contract for $6,880 went to Richard Keeffe of Alberton. The light was ready for the opening of navigation in 1881.[^2] The marker also names Richard Keefe as contractor and dates construction to 1880.[^3] The Prince Edward Island register entry instead names Thomas Beattie, an Island builder, as the man under whose direction the Department of Marine built it in 1881; the sources read do not reconcile the two names.[^1]

The design was unusual: an octagonal keeper's dwelling at the base with a two-storey tower above it, topped by an iron lantern. The register says this form was once common where a separate house could not be built, such as on rocks. The building first stood on a circular pier, and an engraving of it appeared in *Picturesque Canada* the following year.[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives the tower's height as 12.8 m (42 ft) and says it showed white to all points seaward and red between two stated bearings; in 1882 the white sector was redefined to run between Seacow Head and the southernmost point of Miscouche Shoals.[^2] The ground floor held a store room, a kitchen and a living room.[^2]

## The keepers

The first keeper was Captain Charles Peters, who had come to Summerside in 1855. Lighthouse Friends says he sailed aboard many vessels owned by James C. Pope; the Island register says ships built by J. C. Pope. He died in 1881, in the light's first season, and James Walsh replaced him.[^1][^2][^3] Walsh stayed until 1898, and Joel S. Allen was appointed on 18 May 1898; a departmental salary table from 1910 shows him still in the post at $400.[^2][^8] Lighthouse Friends lists later keepers through to at least 1961.[^2]

The accounts differ on how the keepers lived here. The register entry says the keepers rowed out in the evening, stayed the night to tend the light and returned to shore in the morning, and that none lived at the lighthouse permanently.[^1] The marker says only that Walsh spent many years in the lighthouse's "summer residence", and Lighthouse Friends describes a kitchen and living room in the dwelling.[^3][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sea was hard on the structure. A breakwater was built to protect it; Lighthouse Friends dates it to 1884, under a $3,950 contract with Ronald Campbell, the marker places it by 1883, and the register says 1892.[^2][^3][^1] Lighthouse Friends counts at least seven repairs to it between 1887 and 1897. In the spring thaw of 1897 a strong southwest wind drove ice over the breakwater and carried away the boathouse and smashed the keeper's rowboat; a replacement cost $30.[^2]

The siding of the lower part of the house had shrunk and split by 1894, so it was stripped, covered with tarred felt and shingled, and the lantern was scraped and painted under Keeper Walsh's supervision.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1907 the original circular iron foundation was replaced with an octagonal cement one, supervised from the Charlottetown agency at a cost of $2,541.74; the Marine branch reports of 1906-07 and 1907-08 describe first extensive repairs and then the new foundation.[^5][^6] The marker adds that a new breakwater, 3,140 feet long, was begun the same year.[^3] The 1907-08 report lists the station with a fourth-order, 270-degree apparatus, which fits Lighthouse Friends' account of a 270-degree, fourth-order Fresnel lens in 1908.[^6][^2] The report for 1909 records a 35 mm Diamond vapour installation replacing the duplex lamp at Indian Point.[^7] The marker dates a new incandescent vapour light to 1907, so the two sources differ by a year or two.[^3]

When the station was later automated, the interior partitions in the dwelling were taken out so roof leaks could be found.[^2] Lighthouse Friends does not give the date.

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard List of Lights appears to carry the station as Summerside Harbour Wharf Light, "on outer end of breakwater"; the match rests on name and place, not on a stated link to Indian Head.[^9] Its signal is five seconds of white light and five of darkness.[^2] Lighthouse Friends reports that the Coast Guard owns it and that the grounds and tower are closed. The breakwater is washed over at high tide.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says it was recognized as a heritage place under the Prince Edward Island Heritage Places Protection Act on 3 October 2012.[^2] The City of Summerside has petitioned for ownership under Parks Canada's Heritage Lighthouse Program.[^2] The register calls it a local landmark visible from Summerside.[^1]
