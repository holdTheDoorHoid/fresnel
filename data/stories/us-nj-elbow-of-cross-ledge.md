Elbow of Cross Ledge Light stood on a shoal about five miles offshore from Fortescue, New Jersey, in Delaware Bay.[^1][^5] It was a brick keepers' house on a cast-iron and concrete caisson, first lit in 1910 on an "elbow" of the ledge that jutted toward the main shipping channel.[^1][^5][^7] A freighter struck it in fog in 1953, and a red steel skeleton tower now stands on the old foundation.[^5][^6]

## The place

The first light on Cross Ledge was built in the 1870s near the southern end of the ledge.[^5] Later the Lighthouse Board chose a point on the ledge that stuck out toward the main channel, an "elbow," for a new light a little over two miles to the northwest.[^5][^7] The Friends' newsletter says the elbow made ships alter course slightly and counted as a hazard to navigation.[^7]

In its report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1900, the Light-House Board said that Congress had authorized a national harbor of refuge near the mouth of Delaware Bay. A stone breakwater a mile and a half long was being built, to shelter an anchorage of over 700 acres. The Board said the harbor would be of little use at night unless it was lit, and it recommended a gas-lighted beacon on an iron caisson at the Elbow, at a cost of not more than $60,000.[^2] Congress appropriated $75,000 in April 1904 for a lighthouse and fog signal there.[^5]

## Building the light

Work went slowly. Bids for the cast-iron foundation had to be re-advertised, and the metalwork reached the depot on the Christiana River by January 1907.[^5] The caisson was sunk into the ledge through the pneumatic process, against strong currents and rough seas.[^5]

In September 1907 a severe storm swept away much of the working platform. At least one workman fell into the bay and drowned, and a government inspector in a scow was adrift until a lighthouse tender found the scow two days later.[^5] Work stopped that year with the caisson filled with concrete. The Board asked Congress for $21,500 more, which was granted on May 27, 1908.[^5]

The superstructure was a two-story octagonal brick dwelling for the keepers, with an iron watchroom and a lantern room above.[^5] The lens was a fourth-order Barbier, Benard and Turenne Fresnel with four panels.[^5] Three keepers moved across from the old Cross Ledge light: Ethan A. Duffield, Julian Bacon and Harry W. Sheppard. Their light was first exhibited on February 1, 1910.[^5] A 2,000-pound fog bell was mounted on the deck beneath the veranda.[^5] That summer the Bridgeton Evening News reported that twenty people had visited the light on the previous day, and LighthouseFriends quotes the account.[^5]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists 64 stints at the station from 1910 to 1950, covering principal keepers, first and second assistants, and Coast Guard personnel.[^1] Julian Bacon held the principal post twice, from 1910 to 1918 and again from 1920 to 1930.[^1] Oscar A. Conard served as principal keeper from 1918 to 1920, and Clyde S. Harrison from 1930 to 1944.[^1] Harry T. Wilson (1944 to 1947) and John C. Gray (1947 to 1948) are the last principal keepers on the list.[^1] Coast Guard personnel appear from 1939 to 1950.[^1]

Bacon's name appears in the Bulletin as well. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1918 to 1923 lists "Bacon, Julian, keeper Elbow of Cross Ledge light station, efficiency flag awarded" at page 22.[^3] In July 1916 he set out in his motorboat for his monthly leave with his family in Laurel, Delaware.[^5] The motor stalled about four miles from the station, and the boat drifted.[^5] He swam ashore late on a Wednesday afternoon.[^5] A New Castle News account of the ordeal, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says he survived three days and nights with little water and food for one meal.[^5] Bacon went on to serve in other lights and retired from Cherry Island Rear Range Light in 1933.[^5]

The crews lived with the danger of ships hitting the house.[^5] A Philadelphia Evening Bulletin article, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, said that the four-man crew slept in their life jackets, ready to jump into the bay if the house came down.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A severe storm along the Middle and North Atlantic coasts in early February 1920 caused slight damage at the Elbow of Cross Ledge, the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported.[^4] The Friends' web page describes a 1951 storm as a hurricane that damaged the light and led to the crew being removed.[^6] LighthouseFriends says a powerful storm that year seriously damaged the lighthouse.[^5]

On 20 October 1953 the ore ship *Steel Apprentice*, inbound to Philadelphia, was moving through thick fog with its radar out of commission.[^5][^7] The light suddenly appeared directly in its path.[^5] The ship struck the Elbow head-on.[^7] The upper two-thirds of the lighthouse toppled into the bay, and the caisson was heavily damaged.[^5][^6] The Friends' newsletter says the collision knocked most of the lighthouse into the bay.[^7]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard automated the light in 1951 and removed the crew.[^5][^6] Power came from a submarine cable laid from Fortescue. A second cable ran to Miah Maull Shoal light so its keepers could control the Elbow.[^5]

After the collision the Coast Guard paid Mack Construction of Cape May $100,000 to finish the demolition and put a skeleton tower on the caisson.[^5] The sources do not agree on when the new tower went up. The USLHS record gives 1953 as the year the tower was established.[^1] The Friends' web page says an automated light was installed on it in 1954.[^6] The Friends' newsletter says it was "erected and relit in 1955."[^7] The newsletter adds that the Coast Guard burned what was left of the wreck down to its granite base in 1962, and that the station was solarized in 1998. No other source read mentions the 1962 burning.[^7]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the station, and the tower is closed.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the light is best seen from the water.[^5] Summer boat cruises pass it.[^5] A sign near the base prohibits anchoring because of the submarine cable that feeds Miah Maull Shoal.[^5]

The light is still in service, operated and maintained by the Coast Guard's Aids to Navigation Team from Cape May.[^7] The USLHS record, however, says the light is not an active aid to navigation.[^1] Wreckage at the base has become a fishing spot, because it shelters fish.[^5][^7]
