The Pointe-Ouest light stands on the western tip of Anticosti Island, in the Gulf of St. Lawrence, Quebec. Its first tower, a stone tower lined with fire brick, was first lit on 15 March 1858.[^1][^2] That tower was demolished in 1961 or 1967, depending on the source, and a steel skeleton tower took its place.[^1][^2][^4] The Coast Guard still lists a light at the point, and the two keepers' houses are now an inn.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

## The place

The Coast Guard places the light on the west extremity of Anticosti.[^3] A survey found the local limestone unsuitable for construction, but a quarry was opened about 2.4 km (a mile and a half) from the site to produce stone for the tower's interior walls.[^2] The sand, it was decided, would have to come from Mingan, about 58 km (36 mi) away.[^2] Two other lights had already been built on the island, at Southwest Point (1831) and Heath Point (1835).[^2]

The island had a private proprietor, Mr. Menier, whom LighthouseFriends names as Henri Menier.[^2][^5] The Department's report for the year ended June 30, 1900 says Mr. Menier had a surveyor mark off the government property at this station, which the Public Works Department had bought as one acre. The explosives magazine, the house of refuge and one storehouse lay outside the marked limit.[^5]

## Building the light

In 1853 the government of the Province of Canada signed a contract with the builder Charles-François-Xavier Baby for four new lighthouses, one of them at the western point of Anticosti.[^2] The Dictionary of Canadian Biography says Baby held government and Trinity House lighthouse contracts from 1851 and completed one on Anticosti Island in 1856.[^6]

LighthouseFriends says the foundations were dug down to solid rock by the end of 1854.[^2] Twenty men were to winter on the island so that work could continue, and in the winter of 1855 to 1856 the men dressed stone to line the tower.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the tower had two layers of white fire brick as its outer covering.[^2] The register places the fire brick inside the tower, so the two accounts disagree.[^1] The tower was finished in 1856 apart from the lantern and the lighting apparatus.[^2] Mechanics who came from France fitted the lantern and lens in 1857 and trained the keepers.[^2]

To allow time to advertise the four new major lighthouses, their lighting was delayed until navigation opened in 1858, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The fixed white light was first shown from a second-order Fresnel lens on 15 March 1858.[^2] The register describes the lens as a rotating second-order Fresnel lens, where LighthouseFriends says the light was fixed white. This story follows LighthouseFriends on that point, and the disagreement is unresolved.[^1][^2] The register gives the tower's height as 33 m (108 ft) and says it was lined inside with fire brick.[^1]

The first keeper, David Ballantyne, was given a nine-pounder cannon to fire once an hour in limited visibility.[^2] In a typical season the station used about 220 gallons of porpoise oil for the light and 1,200 pounds of powder for the fog cannon.[^2]

## The keepers

David Ballantyne kept the light from 1858 to 1863, then came Louis Malouin (1863 to 1877) and A. Alfred Malouin (1877 to 1912).[^2] The Department's keeper table for the fiscal year ending March 31, 1908 lists "Alf. Malouin" on the West point row.[^7]

In 1907 Alfred Malouin wrote to J. U. Gregory, the Marine Agent at Quebec, that he could not live on his salary.[^2] He had been paid the same salary since succeeding his father some thirty years earlier, and out of it he paid an assistant whose cost kept rising.[^2] To supplement his income, he worked in the winters as an adviser to Henri Menier, the owner of the island.[^2] That same day Gregory testified before the Royal Commission on the Civil Service that keepers at the most remote stations should be able to retire on a pension.[^2] LighthouseFriends quotes his evidence.[^2]

The station's depot of provisions for shipwrecked men was broken into in the winter of 1868 and 1869, and flour and pork were taken.[^2] In 1875 the lighthouse inspector reported that settlers had taken the depot's provisions by force, and would keep doing so each winter until severely punished.[^2]

LighthouseFriends lists the later keepers as Lorenzo Malouin (1912 to 1928), Horatio Malouin (1928 to 1938), J. Marcel Duguay (1941 to 1964), Louis Henley (1965 to 1972), Luc Caron (1972 to 1973) and Jean-Eudes Rioux (1974 to 1979).[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In November 1877 the bark *Northumbria* was wrecked on Anticosti.[^2] LighthouseFriends says her crew were given clothing, blankets, and enough pork, peas, tea, flour and sugar to last the winter, from the depot.[^2]

In April 1900 a severe storm was blowing when the keeper's residence caught fire.[^5] The Chief Engineer reported that a defective chimney had caused the fire, which was put out with little damage.[^5] The keeper was allowed $10 for the repairs and for refastening shingles. The report gives total expenditure at West Point as $165.[^5]

From 1 July 1901 the cotton-powder fog signal at West Point was fired every fifteen minutes instead of every twenty.[^8] When a ship's fog signal was heard close to the shore, the signal was fired at five-minute intervals until she had passed the station.[^8]

The register says a fire in 1950 damaged the tower from an adjacent building.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes a fire about fifty years after 1900 that destroyed the living quarters attached to the tower, after which new residences were built.[^2] In April 1982 the trawler *Calou*, of Gaspé, ran aground near West Point, and LighthouseFriends notes that the wreck is a favourite with photographers.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1953 the government ship *Chesterfield* supplied the lightstations of the North Shore, and W. A. Calladine sailed on her.[^2] Calladine's note on West Point, which LighthouseFriends reprints, describes supplies carried ashore by cart and horse through the shallows and moved from the surf boat. More than 150 barrels of oil had to be delivered.[^2]

The tower's fate is a disagreement. The register says the 1950 fire damaged the tower and that it was blasted down in 1961 because its structure had become unstable.[^1] LighthouseFriends and the Route des phares give 1967, the year the steel tower replaced it.[^2][^4] Either way, the 1858 tower is gone. The register lists three buildings on the site, a skeletal tower, and the remains of the old lighthouse and of the keeper's residence.[^1]

The steel skeleton tower is 29.4 m (96 ft) high.[^3] It shows a white flash every five seconds from a focal height of 27.6 m (91 ft), with a range of 11 nautical miles, and it operates all year.[^3] The register says the new light was fully automated in 1979, and LighthouseFriends says the same.[^1][^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^2] The two keepers' dwellings, which LighthouseFriends says date from the 1950s, belong to the Municipality of Anticosti Island and are run as the Auberge de la Pointe-Ouest, which offers overnight stays.[^2][^4] From Port-Menier, the way is signposted to the inn, and LighthouseFriends describes it as a dirt road of 15.9 km (9.9 mi).[^4][^2]
