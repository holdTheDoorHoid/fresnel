Chatham Point Light stands where Discovery Passage turns to meet Johnstone Strait, on the coast of British Columbia.[^1] It began around 1908 as an unwatched acetylene beacon, and it had no keeper of its own until a proper station was built in 1957.[^3][^1] Today it is a staffed lightstation of the Canadian Coast Guard, and the water is the best place to see it.[^1]

## The place

Captain George Vancouver named Point Chatham in July 1792, after his consort ship, the *Chatham*, which sailed with him on the survey.[^1] Vancouver found the point conspicuous: three channels meet there, and three rocky islets lie close to it.[^1] That description comes from his log, as LighthouseFriends quotes it. The light was put up to mark the spot where Discovery Passage turns sharply toward Johnstone Strait.[^1]

## Building the light

The Department of Marine and Fisheries' report published in 1908 lists "Chatham point" among its gas beacons, described as masonry, steel framework, gas-light and bell.[^2] The report published in 1909 lists Chatham Point among the gas beacons placed in service since the previous report.[^9] The two reports do not agree on when the beacon was placed in service, and LighthouseFriends gives 1908.[^1] The report published in 1910 gives the working details: steel cylindrical tanks on steel framework, and acetylene that was "generated automatically", so that the lights were "unwatched lights."[^3] Its station table names Chatham Point as a "Gas and explosive fog bell beacon", so the beacon also carried a fog signal.[^4] The report published in 1911 lists the explosive fog bell at Chatham Point among its discontinuances.[^5]

The sources disagree about what the beacon stood on. LighthouseFriends describes a white steel tank on a concrete base, while the 1908 report says masonry.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

An unwatched beacon needed no keeper. The first keeper came with the new station of 1957: Oscar Edwards, who was transferred from Chrome Island on 26 February 1957.[^1] That year a diaphone fog horn was set on a bluff at the point, run on air that oil engines compressed. The station also got a fog-signal building, two one-story keepers' dwellings and a boathouse.[^1]

LighthouseFriends names thirteen keepers, from Edwards to James Scott, who served from 2023 to at least 2024.[^1] The roster table on this page shows them in order. The 2010 appendix to the Senate's report lists two permanent residents at the station.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The rescue that LighthouseFriends records comes from a keeper's own account, printed on its page. At about three in the morning on 30 April 2012, a distress call came in within the station's response area. The keeper and his assistant set out in the station's 18-foot aluminum boat, across Johnstone Strait into Burgess Passage, in the dark and the rain. They found a man and a woman bailing with ice-cream pails; their bilge pump was inoperative. The keepers pumped out the boat with a portable pump. A Coast Guard search-and-rescue vessel reached the scene about forty minutes later.[^1]

## Today

LighthouseFriends describes Chatham Point as still staffed, lists keepers up to 2024, and says the Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse.[^1] The station is the keepers' home and workplace.[^1][^7] The Senate committee's report of 2011 counted it among the staffed lightstations of the Pacific Region, and said three of them could be reached by road. Chatham Point was one of the three.[^7] LighthouseFriends notes that the land route is a private logging road, and that the grounds and tower are closed. It says the station is best seen from the water.[^1]

The Coast Guard's list describes the light as a green flash every five seconds, 6.4 m (21 ft) above the water and visible 8 nautical miles, with a fog horn that sounds a two-second blast in every twenty seconds.[^6] The grounds and tower are closed, so visitors should not land at the station.[^1]
