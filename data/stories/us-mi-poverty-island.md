Poverty Island Light Station stands on the southern side of Poverty Island, in northern Lake Michigan, about seven miles from Fairport in Delta County, Michigan.[^1] Its brick tower, completed in 1875, marks the Poverty Island Passage into Green Bay.[^1] The light was automated in the 1950s, its lantern room was taken off the tower, and it has not shown a light for many years.[^1][^2]

## The place

Four passages lead from Lake Michigan into Green Bay.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that after a railroad reached the iron ore mines from Escanaba in 1865, Little Bay de Noc at the northern end of the bay became a major port.[^1] In its annual report for 1867 the Lighthouse Board argued that ships using the northern passage would need a light at night: "a light-house on Poverty island is necessary."[^1] The General Land Office reserved the island for lighthouse purposes that year, but the Lighthouse Board had to repeat its funding request each year until Congress appropriated $18,000 on 3 March 1873.[^1]

## Building the light

Construction began on 28 August 1873.[^1] On 25 October a fire broke out in the workers' sleeping shanties, destroying the temporary quarters and some building materials.[^1] A work crew returned on 13 May 1874.[^1] On 1 August 1874 a sixth-order lens showed a fixed white light from a temporary lantern on the roof of the finished dwelling. The top of the tower walls then stood 31 feet (9.4 m) above the water table, and more money was needed to finish the work.[^1]

Congress provided another $3,000 on 3 March 1875.[^1] The tower's lantern room was first lit on 10 August 1875, with a fourth-order Fresnel lens that gave a flashing red light at a focal plane of 78 feet (24 m) above the lake.[^1] The tower was brick, joined by a covered passage to a brick dwelling.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's place record gives the light's height above mean high water as 80 feet (24 m).[^2] The tower was badly cracked by faulty construction, and in 1883 a retaining wall was built around its footing to stop it settling.[^1]

Oil was stored in a circular iron oil house erected in September 1894, and a boat landing was built in the shape of an L to make a harbor for the keeper's boat.[^4]

LighthouseFriends says an incandescent oil-vapor lamp increased the light to 1,900 candlepower in April 1916, and the 1916 Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists the change from oil to incandescent oil vapor.[^1][^9]

Under an act of 19 July 1919, an allotment of $6,500 was made for a dwelling at Poverty Island.[^6] The completed house was a one-story bungalow with a basement, 24½ by 42½ feet (7.5 by 13 m), with a porch added.[^7] In 1921 the station's steam whistles were changed to air diaphones.[^10] The 1922 report lists $13,424 for replacing the steam fog signal with a diaphone and repairing the dwelling.[^8]

## The keepers

The station had two keepers, a head keeper and an assistant, until a second assistant was added in 1887.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster begins its head keepers with George Larson (1874-1882).[^2]

James McCormick was head keeper from 1911 to 1915.[^2] In July 1912 the station's launch caught fire while he was returning from Escanaba. LighthouseFriends quotes a newspaper's account in which he jumped into the water, drifted for some time and was picked up by a passing steamer.[^1]

Niels P. Jensen was head keeper from 1915 to 1936.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he had served 21 years when he was struck and killed by a car in October 1936, while walking to the school board meeting on Washington Island.[^1]

Benjamin F. Johnson arrived in 1921 and was assistant keeper until 1923, when he became first assistant. He was head keeper from 1936 to 1943.[^2][^1] The roster shows William H. Lee as an assistant from 1922 to 1923.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that in June 1923 Lee went to Washington Island to pick up his wife and two of his children and bring them to Poverty Island for the summer. After he returned to the station, Lee, aged 57, was found dead of a heart attack. Head keeper Jensen decided that Johnson should take the body to Washington Island.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The steamer *Oscar Townsend* ran aground on Gull Island in June 1884, and LighthouseFriends quotes a newspaper's view that a fog signal at Poverty Island light would have saved the steamer from disaster.[^1] A fog-signal building began on 15 August 1885, and its 10-inch steam whistle was first used on 15 October 1885.[^1][^2] The Light-House Board's reports record how long the whistles ran each year: some 295 hours in the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894, 489 hours in the year ended 30 June 1895, and 244 hours in the year ended 30 June 1896.[^3][^4][^5]

Johnson's trip to Washington Island with Lee's body did not go smoothly. LighthouseFriends says that he stopped at St. Martin Island Lighthouse for directions, and then darkness and fog caused him to lose his way and become stranded on a reef. He freed himself the next morning, reached Washington Island, arranged the funeral, and returned to Poverty Island to bring Lee's wife and children back.[^1]

## After the keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's place record gives 1957 as the year the light was automated.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the main light, fog signal and dwelling were electrified in 1952, the foghorn was discontinued in 1954, and the station was automated in 1958.[^1] By 1965 the lantern room had been removed from the tower, leaving an automated light on its top.[^1]

According to LighthouseFriends, the Delta County Historical Society was given permission to restore the discarded lantern room and place it atop the renovated Sand Point Lighthouse at Escanaba.[^1] The Society's own page describes "a duplicate cast iron lantern room from Poverty Island" on that tower.[^11] The two accounts do not agree on whether the lantern now at Sand Point is the original.

The light was deactivated in 1995, LighthouseFriends says, and the station has deteriorated since.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's place record gives the discontinuation date as about 1990.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the lighthouse. LighthouseFriends says its grounds and tower are closed, and that it is best seen by boat.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's place record lists it as not an active aid to navigation and not open to the public.[^2] LighthouseFriends notes that excursions passing the island may be offered, and that private cruises include a view of the lighthouse.[^1]
