Great Duck Island Light stands on the southern tip of a 220-acre island in the Gulf of Maine, a brick tower and its fog-signal house built in 1890 to guard the approach to Blue Hill Bay and Mount Desert Island.[^1][^2] The Light-House Board placed it about 11 miles south of the entrance to Frenchman Bay.[^3] Its keepers were for decades a small community of families, and its fog signal ran more hours in some years than almost any other in the country. Today the station belongs to a college, and the island belongs mostly to seabirds.

## The place

The Light-House Board first asked Congress for a light and fog signal on Great Duck Island in its report for 1885. It argued that one would help vessels "caught in easterly gales" reach the harbors of refuge at Bass Harbor or Southwest Harbor, and it pointed out that the nearest fog signals on either side, at Matinicus Rock and Petit Manan, were 60 miles apart.[^4] The estimate then was $10,000. By 1888 the Board had raised it to $30,000 and added a new argument: the growing "importance of Mount Desert and the vicinity as a summer resort", which had increased the traffic of steamers and yachts bound for Frenchman Bay.[^4][^1]

Congress appropriated the $30,000 by an act of 2 March 1889.[^4] The island itself had a history before the government came. According to Jeremy D'Entremont, it took its name in the 1700s from a pond that drew flocks of ducks each spring; William Gilley bought it in 1837, and Charles Harding became owner in 1867. The Gilley-Harding homestead on the northern side burned in 1882.[^5] The government secured title to about 11 acres at the southern end of the island, with about an acre on the east side for a landing and a right-of-way between the two.[^4]

## Building the light

The work was done in 1890. The Light-House Board's report says the station "was commenced in May and finished in December, 1890."[^3] Before the tower could rise, the builders put in a landing: a 2,251-foot road ran from the east-side landing to the station, with a double boat slip 200 feet long, a boathouse and a steam winch for hauling up materials. A barn, 20 by 30 feet, housed the workmen while the fog-signal house and dwellings were begun.[^4] D'Entremont adds that a lumber schooner from Lubec was wrecked on the island in a snowstorm in January 1890, with the loss of three crewmen who were buried on the island; this comes from his account alone, and the Board's reports in our corpus do not mention it.[^5]

The finished station had a brick tower 35 feet (11 m) high to its focal plane with a small brick service room, a brick fog-signal house 32 feet square, a rain shed with coal bunkers for 60 tons, three six-room dwellings, a barn, a boathouse at the landing and an engine house. The steam fog signal was installed in duplicate, and the light was first shown on 31 December 1890. A 1,200-pound fog bell was provided to be rung by hand in an emergency.[^3] The National Register nomination gives the tower's height from base to the centre of the lantern as 35.5 feet, while the Lighthouse Society archive lists 42 feet; the sources measure from different points or disagree.[^1][^6] The original lens was a fifth-order Fresnel.[^6] The Register's data lists the US Army Corps of Engineers as designer.[^7]

The steam whistle went into service in January 1891. In the report year that followed, it ran 473 hours and burned about 18 tons of coal.[^8]

The station was kept up and altered over the next decade. In 1893 the boundary fence was extended to the sea and the boat slip lengthened by 30 feet.[^9] The Board recorded a change in the colour of the tower on 20 May 1900; D'Entremont says the tower had been red and was made white.[^10][^5] In 1901 a stone oil house was built, the west boat slip was extended and a branch slip cut.[^11] According to D'Entremont the lens was changed from fifth to fourth order in 1902, and a tramway was added from the landing to the fog-signal house to save hauling coal by wheelbarrow.[^5] LighthouseFriends reports that a diaphone replaced the steam whistle around 1930.[^12]

## The keepers

William F. Stanley was the first head keeper. The Lighthouse Society's compilation of National Archives records shows him appointed on 19 December 1890 and leaving on 15 April 1909 by transfer to Cape Elizabeth.[^4] Lighthouse Digest says he had served earlier at Mount Desert Rock and Matinicus Rock, and that one of his first orders from the Board was to post "No Trespassing" signs and protect the herring gull colony on the reservation, at a time when sea birds were being hunted for the millinery trade and gull eggs gathered for food. Local people who had long hunted and gathered eggs on the island were eventually persuaded to leave, according to that account.[^13] Joseph M. Gray took over as head keeper on 16 April 1909.[^4] D'Entremont's keeper list dates his promotion to 1905, but LighthouseFriends quotes Gray himself as becoming head keeper in 1909, as the clippings file and the Society roster also have it.[^5][^12][^6]

The Lighthouse Society roster lists a head keeper and two assistants for most of the years to 1943, and a long run of Coast Guard names after that.[^6] The station's families lived in the three dwellings and the sources give glimpses of what that meant. Nathan A. "Ad" Reed arrived as an assistant keeper in 1902 with his wife Emma and, by the accounts of D'Entremont and LighthouseFriends, sixteen children, quite possibly the largest family in the Lighthouse Service. A disused barn was fitted out as a schoolroom, and one of the Reed daughters, who had earned a teaching certificate at Castine, became the teacher.[^5][^12] Dalton Reed, who was seven when the family came and stayed ten years, recalled in a high-school magazine interview that the lighthouse inspector arrived by steamer once or twice a year, and that everything brass in the whistle house had to be polished for the visit.[^12] He also remembered two fishermen whose boat had broken down and who rowed ashore through freezing spray: "They were nothing but a solid bed of ice."[^12]

Leverett S. Stanley served as an assistant keeper from 1924 to 1940 by the roster's reckoning, with his family on the island. His son Lyford, two weeks old when they arrived, later told a newspaper that supplies were landed on the opposite side of the island and carried to the keepers' house by wheelbarrow.[^5][^6] A fog signal that sounded for thirteen days straight is attributed to Lyford Stanley by D'Entremont and to the first keeper by LighthouseFriends, so the story has been told of more than one era.[^5][^12]

D'Entremont also reports that James Freeman, who had been at Petit Manan, became keeper around 1940 (the Society's roster says 1939), and that his daughter later wrote of a stormy night when her mother Iva became seriously ill and the Coast Guard brought a doctor and nurse to the island, saving her life.[^5][^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Fog was the station's business. In its report for the 1895 year the Light-House Board recorded that the 10-inch steam whistle sounded about 1,362 hours and burned about 59 tons of coal.[^14] For 1932 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin listed Great Duck Island as the second foggiest station in the country, at 1,617 hours, 157 hours short of Point Reyes, California.[^15]

On 15 September 1931 the fishing schooner *Rita A. Viator* of Portland grounded near the station, during the night and in heavy seas by D'Entremont's account.[^17][^5] According to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin, the vessel was "pounded to pieces" on the rocks, and keeper Andrew H. Kennedy and assistant keepers Earle E. Benson and Leverett S. Stanley rescued the captain and crew.[^16][^17] Secretary of Commerce Robert P. Lamont sent each a special letter of commendation.[^16]

D'Entremont also records a February 1955 night when a Coast Guard couple stationed on the island, expecting a baby earlier than planned, had to walk an hour and a half through deep snow to the north end, where a tug could dock in the rough seas.[^5]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1986 and the Fresnel lens was replaced by a modern optic.[^6][^5] D'Entremont names BM1 Donald Parker as the last Coast Guard officer in charge.[^5] Most of the station's secondary buildings, including the barn and rain shed, had been removed before automation.[^1] The Nature Conservancy bought most of the rest of the island in 1984, and in 1998 the light station, with Mount Desert Rock, passed to the College of the Atlantic under the Maine Lights Program.[^5] The college says it shares the island with the Conservancy, the State of Maine and a private summer resident.[^2]

## Today

The light is still an active aid to navigation, now powered by solar panels.[^6] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 14 March 1988.[^7] The tower, the head keeper's house (the survivor of the three built in 1890), the fog-signal building, the 1901 oil house and a shed make up the listed group.[^1] The College of the Atlantic owns about 12 acres: the old light-station property, with the head keeper's house, two boathouses and the tower. The station was renamed the Alice Eno Field Research Station in 2000, and the college's students research the island's large breeding populations of Leach's storm petrels and black guillemots.[^2]

The station is private and not open to the public, and the island is closed from April through October to protect nesting seabirds. Do not land.[^6][^2] D'Entremont notes that the light is hard to see except from a private boat or plane.[^5]
