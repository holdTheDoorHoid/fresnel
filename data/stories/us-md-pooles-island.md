Pooles Island Light stands on a low, wooded island in the upper Chesapeake Bay, at the mouth of the Gunpowder and Bush rivers.[^1] Its stone tower was built in 1825, and the National Register form calls it the earliest extant lighthouse in Maryland.[^1] The light was decommissioned in 1939. The island has been part of Aberdeen Proving Ground since 1917, and the tower is closed to visitors because of that.[^1][^3] It was relit with solar power in 2011.[^3][^11]

## The place

Most of the island is wooded wetland, and its highest ground "does not exceed 15 feet above sea level."[^1] Captain John Smith visited the island in 1608, and the maps that followed spelled its name many ways.[^1] During the War of 1812, "the British fleet plundered the settlement" in April 1813.[^1]

The island mattered to ships because it divided the traffic into two channels. The National Register form says that it "separated Chesapeake Bay into two shipping channels," and that the light on its northwest side "marked the deeper shipping channel and was the only beacon for ships passing the dangerous shoals at Gunpowder Neck."[^1]

## Building the light

Congress passed an act on 26 May 1824 that authorized lighthouses on Thomas Point and Pooles Island.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that year Congress "appropriated $5,000 for a beacon on the northwest tip of the island."[^3] The government bought the land from Peregrine Wethered, of Kent County, for $500 on 25 March 1825.[^1]

On 16 April 1825 a contract was signed with John Donohoo of Havre de Grace and Simon Frieze of Port Deposit. The price was $4,754, and the work was to be finished by 1 November 1825.[^1] Donohoo was "the most prolific builder of lighthouses in the Chesapeake Bay during the nineteenth century," according to the National Register form.[^1]

The form gives the tower a total height of 40 feet 6 inches (12.3 m), with a base 18 feet (5.5 m) across that narrows to 9 feet 6 inches (2.9 m) at the top.[^1] The directory gives 44 feet (13.5 m).[^11] The granite blocks came from Port Deposit.[^1] The archive's record gives the height from base to the centre of the lantern as 30 feet (9 m), which does not settle the question.[^2] The keeper's house was stone, "constructed of stone measuring 34 by 20 feet."[^1]

The first lamps are where the sources part ways. The National Register form says the light was "lit by nine oil lamps."[^1] LighthouseFriends says it had "Thirteen lamps and an equal number of spherical reflectors."[^3]

A fog bell and tower went up in 1828, the form says.[^1] LighthouseFriends adds that Congress earmarked $2,800 that year for "erecting a tower and placing a bell thereon, with machinery."[^3] An inspector in 1838 reported that the fog bell could not be heard more than one mile, because its hammers were "too small, and the peals not sufficiently quick."[^3]

The lens came later. LighthouseFriends says that in 1855, on the Lighthouse Board's initiative, a fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps, and the archive gives 1855 as the year the original lens was installed.[^3][^2] The National Register form instead says the station "was fitted with a fourth-order Fresnel lamp in 1899."[^1] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 records that "New model fourth-order lamps were supplied" to the station.[^7] The 1899 entry speaks of lamps, not a lens, so the sources disagree on when the lens came.

Other work followed. In 1867 the lamps were upgraded with "new and improved lanterns."[^1] The fog-bell tower "was rebuilt in 1879 to replace the original bell," according to the form, while LighthouseFriends dates the new tower to September 1878.[^1][^3] A 2,000-gallon brick cistern followed in 1880, and a brick second floor went onto the keeper's house in 1883.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the second floor to 1882.[^3] A front porch was added in 1887.[^1] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 says that "In August a boathouse was built" and that a windlass was provided.[^4]

On 10 April 1894 "two red sectors were placed in this light," marking turning points in the channel east of the island.[^5] The coal house was remodeled into an oil house in the late 1890s.[^6] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 also says that "The fall of the fog-bell hammer was increased" to make the signal carry further.[^7] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 says a porch was built and brick pavement laid.[^8]

## The keepers

The archive's roster begins with Sylvester Nugent, who kept the light from 1825 to 1839.[^2] Keepers changed often in the first forty years. Isaac J. Allen kept the light from 1865 to 1870 and again from 1870 to 1893.[^2] LighthouseFriends calls him and Stephen A. Cohee "the station's longest-serving keepers."[^3]

Cohee kept the light from 1893 to 1915.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends describes him as the father of eight, and says that thirty-two of his descendants "were on hand to witness the relighting of the lighthouse" in 2011.[^3] The same account calls him the last keeper, but the archive's roster lists Amasa J. Simpson as keeper from 1915 to 1918.[^2][^3]

## After the keepers

In 1917 the federal government took the island for Aberdeen Proving Ground. The National Register form says it "acquired the 280 acres of Pooles Island from the estate of C.C. Homer," and that the Coast Guard kept "6.94 acres of the island for the lighthouse complex."[^1]

The form says "The light at Pooles Island was automated in 1917." LighthouseFriends says the War Department had the last keeper removed in 1918, "when the light was changed from oil to acetylene gas" and the fog signal was stopped.[^1][^3] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1918 to 1923 lists a "Pooles Island, change in illuminant."[^9]

A separate light went up in November 1927 on a caisson near the south end of the island, to mark the channel on its east side. LighthouseFriends describes an automatic acetylene light and a fog bell there.[^3] The Bulletin, which calls it an unwatched light and fog-signal station marking Pooles Island Bar, dates its commissioning to 29 November 1927.[^10]

The light was decommissioned in 1939. The National Register form says the light "remained in unattended operation until 1939, when it was decommissioned by the United States Coast Guard and the lens removed." LighthouseFriends says the keeper's house was later torn down.[^3] The form says "All above-ground remnants of the keeper's house and associated outbuildings have been removed."[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the Army proposed National Register listing in 1994, and that the restoration effort began in 1997, the year the lighthouse was listed.[^3] The directory says the Army carried out a fuller restoration in 2010.[^11]

The Aberdeen Proving Ground commander, who took over in 2009, insisted on relighting the abandoned lighthouse.[^3] The light was outfitted with solar power in 2011 and shows "seven white flashes, in a 4+3 pattern, every 16 s."[^11]

## Today

Pooles Island is a military reservation. The National Register form says the lighthouse lies "within the restricted boundaries of the Aberdeen Proving Ground Military Reservation."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is currently inaccessible to the public, and that live artillery shells from Army training litter the grounds.[^3] The directory says the island "is littered with unexploded ordnance."[^11]

LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is best seen from the water, and that boat trips pass it.[^3] The form describes the tower as vacant and in good condition, apart from some mortar joint failure, and says it has "been the target of vandalism."[^1]

Do not land on the island. Its grounds hold live shells, and the tower is closed.
