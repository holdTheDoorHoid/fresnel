Anacapa Island Light stands on the cliff-top at the eastern end of the Channel Islands' easternmost island, marking the southern entrance to the Santa Barbara Channel off Ventura County, California. Its reinforced-concrete tower, Spanish-style houses and landing hoists were finished in 1932, and the National Register nomination calls it the last light station complex completed in California.[^1][^3][^7]

## The place

In 1915 the Commissioner of Lighthouses described nearly all coastwise vessels, and many bound for Panama, as using the Santa Barbara Channel, and by 1932 the Lighthouse Service called Anacapa a valuable aid to coastwise traffic and to vessels approaching Los Angeles from the open sea.[^5][^7] The National Park Service ties the first calls for a light here to the wreck of the passenger steamer *Winfield Scott* in 1853; Congress, it says, chose instead to pay for a cheaper mainland station at Port Hueneme, which began operating in 1874.[^2] The National Register nomination agrees that the Lighthouse Board preferred an "economy lighthouse at Port Hueneme in 1874" to an island beacon.[^3]

## Building the light

The first aid on the island was small. The Park Service says a 50-foot (15 m) metal tower carrying an acetylene light was built on the tip of East Anacapa in 1911, and the National Register nomination says "Not until 1912 was a navigation light placed on Anacapa, that being an unmanned skeleton tower."[^2][^3] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1912 lists "Acetylene lenslantern light established" at Anacapa Island.[^4] The sources therefore differ on whether the beacon dates from 1911 or 1912; the Bulletin and, twenty years later, the Lighthouse Service's own description of the new station both give 1912 for the first beacon.[^4][^7]

The case for something better built up over the next decade. In its 1915 report the Commissioner of Lighthouses asked for $103,000 for a light and fog signal, noting that the desirable course "leads close to the eastern end of Anacapa Island, which is now marked by a small beacon light, not sufficiently powerful to be of service in hazy weather." The American Shipmasters' Association had petitioned for the work.[^5] By the 1917 report the estimate had risen to $115,000.[^6] The Park Service records that the tank steamer *Liebre* grounded on the east end of the island on 28 February 1921, directly under the light tower, and that local inspectors blamed the inadequate station.[^2]

Construction of the present station began in May 1930 with a contract for landings, hoists and roadwork, and the Lighthouse Service's own account says "The first contractor failed early in the undertaking."[^7] There was only one place on the island that could serve as a landing, and the cliffs there were "over 100 feet high and almost vertical." Rock was blasted away to make an upper and a lower landing, joined by a stairway, with a hoist at each.[^7] The Park Service describes a 39-foot (12 m) concrete cylindrical tower topped by a third-order Fresnel lens; the Bulletin gives the same height but calls the tower conical.[^2][^13] In the Bulletin's description the beam made one revolution in 60 seconds and the light had a "geographical range of 23 miles."[^13] The station was placed in commission on 25 March 1932, replacing the 1912 beacon.[^7] The Park Service names Frederick Cobb as the keeper who lit the first light that day.[^2]

## The keepers

The Bulletin said the station had separate quarters "of a modified Spanish type" for four keepers, and a 26-foot (8 m) cabin launch that the keepers used to reach a mainland point 18 miles distant for mail and supplies. The launch was stocked with food and water, because on return it was often impossible to land and it had to wait at sea in the island's lee "for one or more days and nights."[^7]

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the National Archives keeper registers, lists Frederick S. Cobb as principal keeper in 1932–1933, followed by Mars P. Kinyon (1933–1935), Charles R. Coursey (1935–1938), Joseph May (1938–1940) and Thayer J. Allen (1940–1941), with a run of first, second and third assistants beneath them.[^1] The Park Service adds that the Coast Guard took over from the Lighthouse Service in 1939, and later keeper lists in the archive include Coast Guardsmen.[^2][^1] The Park Service says island residents "occupied their time working, fishing, hunting rabbits", and that television had reached Anacapa by 1951.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In December 1932 an easterly gale cut the station off from the mainland. Keeper Cobb's report, printed in the Bulletin, put the wind at "gale, estimated 75 to 80 miles," said both radio antennae were blown down and the telephone house had to be shored with timbers, and noted that "Green seas broke over sea wall on lower landing but no damage done."[^8] With the landing still unusable he added that there would be "no launch to Ventura until a landing is possible."[^8]

The fog signal drew praise in print too. The 1933 volume of the Bulletin carries a steamship captain's report, written after a passage past the island on 8 March, that called it "one of the best fog signals I have ever had experience with", and the district superintendent pointed out that it stood 265 feet up, suggesting that height helped its carry.[^9]

In 1934 the Bulletin recorded that second assistant keeper "C. H. Coursey", with the keeper, "rescued three men from the cruiser Harmony who were marooned on the south coast of Santa Cruz Island." Before a Coast Guard vessel could arrive, he "swam through the breakers carrying a line", along which the men were pulled about 400 feet (120 m) to the station launch.[^10] The same year, when an assistant keeper's wife was critically injured in a fall, the station radioed the battleship *California*, which took her off the island on 22 November; the Bulletin names her as Mrs. Coursey.[^11] The Park Service tells the same story but calls the assistant keeper Rex Coursey, and the archive's roster gives Charles R. Coursey, so the family's given names are not settled here.[^2][^1]

The light was extinguished during the Pacific Coast blackout of 1942, and the Navy operated the station during the war.[^2] In 1943 the Navy plugged a sea-eroded tunnel under the lower landing.[^3]

## After the keepers

The Park Service says the Coast Guard modernised the station in 1961, replacing the fog signal system, and in 1962 a plan was drawn up to automate Anacapa and set up a rescue facility at Point Hueneme.[^2] The station was automated in 1968, and the nomination records that the automation work included burning the hoist house on the lower landing.[^3][^12] By 1970 the Park Service and Coast Guard had agreed on joint custody of the island's land, wharf and hoist, and park rangers now live in the residences.[^2] In 1989 the Coast Guard replaced the historic Fresnel lens with a solar-powered acrylic lens.[^2]

## Today

The light is an active aid to navigation operated by the Coast Guard, and the Park Service manages the island as part of Channel Islands National Park. The Park Service says visitors "are not permitted to tour the lighthouse", but the surrounding area can be explored, and the original Fresnel lens is displayed at the Anacapa Island visitor center.[^2] The nomination notes that the island's landing is reached by water only, so any visit is by boat.[^3] The station is on the National Register of Historic Places (reference 91001101).[^3]
