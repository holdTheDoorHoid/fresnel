Isle Haute light stands on the summit of Isle Haute, a high island in the upper Bay of Fundy off Cape Chignecto, Nova Scotia.[^1] The first light was a wooden tower attached to the keeper's house, lit in 1879 by LighthouseFriends' account and destroyed by fire in 1956.[^1][^4] A steel skeletal tower has shown a light there since, and the island is now a National Wildlife Area where entry needs a permit.[^1][^4][^6]

## The place

Samuel de Champlain described the island in 1604.[^1] LighthouseFriends prints his account of an island "some 40 or 50 fathoms (300 feet) in elevation," surrounded by great rocks except at one place, where a slope runs down to a pond of salt water.[^1]

In 1840 Commander John Robb, of the sloop *Satellite*, selected the island's western point as the site for a light.[^1] He thought a light there "would be generally useful, not only as a guide into the Basin of Mines and Cumberland Basin."[^1] Calls for a light on Isle Haute continued.[^1] In 1847 tenders were received, but the site Robb had chosen proved inaccessible and the work stopped.[^1] The following year, in 1848, the government divided the grant between a light at Black Rock and one at Apple River.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the objections were the island's inaccessibility and its height, which it was felt would often have meant the light was obscured by fog or haze.[^1]

A later danger was a hazardous ledge west of the island.[^2] Dan Conlin's 1997 curatorial report for the Nova Scotia Museum, as Ed Coleman summarises it, says a light was first suggested in 1855, and that the Royal Navy strongly recommended one in 1857 because of that ledge.[^2]

## Building the light

In 1875 the government of Canada appropriated money for a light on the island.[^1] Two years later Calvin Cogswell of Oromocto, New Brunswick, won the contract, at $3,700.[^1]

The tower went up on the island's highest point, just west of the centre.[^1] It was a square wooden tower, 16 m (53 ft) high, attached to a dwelling.[^1] With the lantern and apparatus, the whole job cost $5,920.36.[^1]

The light had a focal plane 111 m (365 ft) above the bay, and LighthouseFriends says that in clear conditions it could be seen from about twenty miles away.[^1]

Captain Nelson Card was appointed keeper on 20 November 1877, at $500 a year.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the light was not placed in operation until 15 January 1879.[^1] Conlin's report, as Coleman summarises it, says the lighthouse was established in 1878 and that its first keeper was Leon Card.[^2] The Lighthouse Directory gives 1878 for the station.[^4] The sources agree on the surname. They disagree on the first name, and on whether 1878 or 1879 is the year the light began.

Card built a mile-long road from the beach up to the station, graded so that supplies could be hauled up it.[^1][^2] He also cleared ground around the light and planted crops on about an acre and a half.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

Card kept the light until 1889.[^1] His wife and daughter joined him on the island, and Conlin says the family ran a productive farm. The island sent sheep, cattle and hay off by boat and barge.[^2] Conlin also says visitors came to the island. At the turn of the century up to 300 people could arrive in a single day, with July 12 gatherings organised by the Orange Lodge.[^2]

George J. Reid followed Card, from 1889 to 1904.[^1] Percy E. Morris kept the light from 1904 until 1941, the longest term of any keeper on the island.[^1] The keeper table in the Department's 1909 report, printed in 1910, gives Percy E. Morris's appointment as 2 August 1904, at $530 a year.[^8]

In 1931 a writer for the *Berwick Register* interviewed Morris on a visit ashore at Harbourville.[^3] SaltWire later reprinted the article.[^3] It says he had kept the light for 28 years, as the sole resident of the island apart from one helper. He kept between 50 and 100 registered black foxes, which had the run of the island, along with 12 cattle, 70 sheep and lambs, 14 pigs, six goats and about 20 hens and chickens.[^3] The island yielded hay, oats and potatoes, and a radio let him keep in touch with the outside world.[^3] A later editor's note gives his service as 37 years, from 1904.[^3]

Donald Morris kept the light from 1941 to 1946, and John M. Fullerton from 1946 to 1956.[^1] The sources do not say how the two Morrises were related.

Treasure seekers came to the island as well.[^3] The 1931 article reports that a man from Vancouver had, for the past two years, spent August and September searching for treasure, apparently without encouraging results.[^3] In 1947 the historian and author Edward Snow bought a map.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that about five years later he concluded the map showed pirate treasure on the island, and he set out for Isle Haute.[^1] There he stayed with Fullerton, his wife and their teenage son.[^1] The next morning Snow returned to the site with Keeper Fullerton and his son and continued his search.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In winter, Conlin says, ice, tides and storms cut the Cards off from the mainland for weeks at a time.[^2] The keeper set up a system of fires that could be seen from Advocate, the nearest mainland community. One fire meant all was well, two fires meant someone was sick, three meant a doctor was needed, and four meant a death.[^2]

## After the keepers

Human occupation of the island ended in 1956, and an automated light took over, Conlin says.[^2] LighthouseFriends says a fire destroyed the station in October 1956, and the long-focus reflector came down through the charred tower. An automated light was then set up on a skeletal tower, and that tower is still there.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory gives the replacement as a 12 m (40 ft) square steel skeletal tower.[^4]

## Today

Isle Haute is a National Wildlife Area.[^6] The federal government designated it in 2025, to protect migratory birds and their habitat.[^6][^7] The island is 89 hectares, three kilometres long, with cliffs about 100 m high.[^6][^7] Entry is prohibited year-round unless a permit is issued, to protect migratory birds, their nests and eggs, and sensitive Indigenous archaeological records.[^6]

The Lighthouse Directory describes the site and tower as closed.[^4] Visits to the island need permission from the Wildlife Service, and the Coast Guard owns the light while the Canadian Wildlife Service manages the site.[^4] LighthouseFriends notes that Advocate Boat Tours offers trips to the island.[^1] The entry rules still apply, so ask the Wildlife Service before any landing.[^6]

The light today flashes white every four seconds, at a focal height of 112.1 m, with a range of seven nautical miles.[^5] It shows from a square skeleton tower.[^5] The Coast Guard list gives the structure as 13.0 m high, where the Lighthouse Directory gives 12 m.[^4][^5]
