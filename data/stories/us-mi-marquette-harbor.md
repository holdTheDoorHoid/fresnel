Marquette Harbor Light stands on Lighthouse Point, a rocky peninsula on the western shore of the bay at Marquette, Michigan, where a lighthouse has marked the way into one of Lake Superior's early iron-ore ports since 1853. The brick house-and-tower visitors see today dates from 1866 and has since been raised a storey and painted red.[^1][^4]

## The place

Marquette grew up around the Jackson mining company's iron operations a few miles inland, and was incorporated in 1849, with docks and wharves soon built to ship the ore.[^1] Congress appropriated $5,000 for a lighthouse to guide vessels into the harbour on 28 September 1850.[^1] The site chosen was the rocky point jutting from the western shore of the bay, between Marquette and Presque Isle.[^1]

## Building the light

Work began early in 1852 and was finished on 1 October, but with the navigation season nearly over the station was not put into use until June 1853, when its first keeper, Harvey Moore, arrived.[^1] No drawings of that first building have come to light; the Marquette Maritime Museum thinks it probably resembled the old Copper Harbor lighthouse, a story-and-a-half stone house with a separate rubble tower.[^1][^10] A sixth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps in 1856, according to Terry Pepper's history; the US Lighthouse Society's database records the original optic as fourth order, installed the same year.[^1][^2]

The stone buildings wore out quickly. The Lighthouse Board asked for a replacement in its 1865 annual report, and $13,000 was appropriated; LighthouseFriends dates the appropriation to 7 April 1866.[^1][^3] The new station was a brick dwelling with a square tower built into its lakeward gable, put up on the highest ground on the point.[^1] Sources disagree on the new lens's arc of visibility: Pepper says 190 degrees, while the museum and LighthouseFriends give 180, widened to 270 in 1870.[^1][^10][^3]

A fog signal followed. Congress allocated $4,000 in 1873, and a wooden building with a 10-inch steam whistle was put on the very end of the point, on a ledge blasted flat about ten feet (3 m) above the water.[^1] Pepper reports that two separate fog-signal plants were installed in 1880-81 so that one could stand ready if the other failed.[^1]

## The keepers

Nelson Truckey was keeper from 1861, but he helped form Company B of the 27th Michigan Infantry and went to war; his wife Anastasia succeeded him and kept the light until 1865.[^1][^2] In the log for 31 May 1877 Keeper Philip Morgan's entry began "Strong wind from the South. Smoky." and went on to the fog-signal chores and the line "The woods all on fire," the first of many smoke mentions in the station logs.[^1]

Patrick McGuire arrived with his family in May 1882, and his first log entry records that he "found it in a bad and dirty condition."[^1] His wife Catharine is listed as first assistant from 1882 to 1891.[^2] LighthouseFriends, citing an 1891 newspaper, says women assistants were not supposed to serve at fog-signal stations and that her service ended after she went to visit her sisters in Chicago without giving proper notice.[^3] Unlike on many Great Lakes stations, Pepper writes, the Marquette keepers lived at the light all year, and McGuire's log mentions his children skating on the frozen harbour.[^1]

In July 1903 Keeper Robert Carlson put out a bonfire lit by picnickers on the point; the next morning, according to Pepper, a surfman from the neighbouring Life-Saving station who had been among them attacked him on the way to the breakwater. Carlson spent two weeks in bed, and it was uncertain whether he would regain his sight.[^4] That October Carlson was exchanged with Charles Kimball of Whitefish Point.[^4] Kimball stayed until 1925; LighthouseFriends calls his twenty-two years by far the longest term at the station.[^2][^3][^4] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded his retirement on 30 April 1925 at age 71, after nearly 53 years in the service, the last twelve wearing the efficiency star; Pepper gives 20 April and a different age and length of service.[^4][^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The fog signal ran for a record 697 hours in 1887, and on the night of 29 November 1889 a violent sea tore the breakwater beacon from its mounting.[^1] Townspeople complained about the whistles: the Mining Gazette, as quoted by Pepper, called the noise a "dismal tooting", and in April 1897 the Lighthouse Board had a sound deflector of pine framing, iron and sawdust built around them.[^1][^5]

On 30 April 1898 Keeper William Wheatley and a friend were sailing off Little Presque Isle when a squall capsized the boat. A newspaper reported that the other man swam ashore, but Wheatley "was afraid to trust his endurance to the icy waters of Lake Superior."[^6] The Life-Saving crew searched through the night.[^1] The date his body was found differs by source: 21 May in Pepper, 21 June in LighthouseFriends.[^1][^3]

On the night of 31 October 1911, by one newspaper's account, the steamer D. Leuty stranded off Lighthouse Point.[^8] LighthouseFriends dates it 1 November, in a snowstorm, and says the captain blamed a silent fog whistle; Kimball answered that steam had not had time to rise after the steamer's signal.[^3] On 23 November 1912 a northeast gale with snow broke over the sea wall, and Kimball found the new fog-signal machinery filled with sand.[^4]

## After the keepers

In 1908 a new fourth-order lens gave a red flash every five seconds, the second storey was added in 1910, and the brick was painted red in 1911, though the museum's history dates the red paint to 1965.[^3][^4][^10] A new brick fog-signal building was commissioned on 22 May 1912.[^4] Radiobeacon and radiotelephone transmitters began service on 20 April 1928, and by 1932 Marquette was the shore contact for the offshore stations in Lake Superior, with mail for them read to their keepers by the Marquette keeper.[^4][^9] After 1939 the Coast Guard took over; in the Second World War the station was a training base for up to 300 recruits, and Pepper says the fog-signal building was demolished in the 1980s.[^4] The Coast Guard last housed personnel in the lighthouse in 1998.[^3]

## Today

The Marquette Maritime Museum leased the building in 2002 and has re-roofed and repaired it.[^3][^10] On 30 July 2016 the property was transferred to the City of Marquette as a park, dedicated in 2019, and the museum still runs the tours.[^10] The US Lighthouse Society lists it as an active aid to navigation, and it is on the National Register of Historic Places.[^2][^11] The museum says tours are escorted only, from the museum entrance, Tuesday to Sunday at 11:30, 1:00 and 2:30, and involve climbing 45 steps.[^10]
