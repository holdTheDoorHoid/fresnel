Belle Isle South End Upper Light stands at the southern tip of Belle Isle, a barren island at the eastern entrance to the Strait of Belle Isle, in Newfoundland and Labrador. Its round limestone tower was first lit on 15 March 1858 with a first-order Fresnel lens. The old tower has been inactive since 2017, and the light the Canadian Coast Guard lists for the station now shows from a shorter skeleton tower.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

## The place

Lighthouse Friends describes the island as a barren one at the eastern end of the strait, which is the northern gateway between the Atlantic and the Gulf of St. Lawrence.[^1] The heritage statement says the construction of the light "marked a major step in the development of the route around Newfoundland."[^2]

In 1853 John Page, the Province of Canada's chief engineer of public works, marked a site about 244 m (800 ft) from the island's southern point and 128 m (420 ft) above the sea. The height would let the light be seen far off, Page noted, but low fog banks, common in some seasons, came with it.[^1]

In 1906 the Department of Marine's report was still correcting a belief that the current ran constantly inward through the strait. The report says that error "is very misleading to vessels", especially for ships reckoning their course round Anticosti.[^8]

## Building the light

In 1853 François Baby, the proprietor of a tug line below Quebec, was contracted to build four major lights in the Gulf of St. Lawrence. Belle Isle was one of them.[^1]

The 1854 season was lost. The men sent out searched for eight days for the spot Page had marked the year before, could not find it, and did no work that year.[^1] The contractor then had to build a road of nearly 2.4 km (1.5 mi), cut through granite as it climbed a ravine.[^1] In 1855 arrangements were made to winter twenty-two men on the island. By the end of 1856 the keepers' double dwelling was finished, and only the upper storey of the tower remained.[^1]

That same year the steamer *Doris*, carrying fodder for the horses that worked on the construction, was wrecked. Lighthouse Friends says the island grew nothing the animals could eat.[^1]

The lantern and the first-order Fresnel lens were made in Paris, and French mechanics came to fit them and train the keepers.[^1] The tower's outer coating was to be two layers of "the best English fire-brick".[^1] The heritage statement describes pine shingles now covering the white fire brick.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the tower was covered with clapboards by 1869 to protect it from frost and easterly winds.[^1] The sources do not say when the shingles replaced the clapboards.

In 1879 a second light went up at the southern end of the island, about 91 m (300 ft) lower than the first, in a wooden pyramidal tower. Its fixed catoptric light was 49 m (160 ft) above the sea.[^1] In 1908 the wooden tower was taken down, and a new metal lantern with a second-order lens was set on the raised foundation to show an occulting white light.[^1] The Department's report published in 1908 says the new occulting lights at "Belle Isle, high light" and "Belle Isle, low light" could not be put into operation, although the apparatus was available. The report links the problem to "the difficulty of transporting men and materials for construction work in this agency."[^11]

The fog signal was a nine-pounder gun, fired every hour in fog or snow, which used about 2,000 pounds of powder a season. A 32-pounder replaced it in 1882, and rockets were in service from 1891.[^1] In 1897 $20,000 was allocated for a first-class signal. Its first-order double sirens began operating on 9 September 1899, at a cost of $20,112.64, with compressed air from a water wheel at the landing carried up to the sirens.[^1] A new wooden fog alarm building was put up in 1909, and in 1912 the fog alarm moved to the upper light.[^1]

In 1924 a radio direction-finding station was built near the light. By 1958 the light station was using electricity from the radio station, and the lighthouse itself was electrified in 1960.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists the head keepers from 1856, when D. Vaughan is first named. Martin Colton follows (at least 1867 to 1882), then Michael Colton (1882 to 1900) and P. J. Colton (1901 to 1906).[^1] The Department's list for the year ended 30 June 1907 still names him at Belle Isle, so his service there ran at least into 1907.[^14] The list has three keepers of that name. The sources read here do not say whether they were related.

Michael Colton's name is at the foot of the Belle Isle ice report for the spring of 1900, where he signs as lightkeeper.[^5] The keeper tables in the Department's reports for 1902 and 1906 list a "Colton, P. J." at Belle Isle, and in 1906 the entry reads "Light and Fog Alarm."[^10][^9]

The reports put the station in the Quebec lighthouse division, which, as the 1900 report describes it, ran "from Montreal to the end of the strait of Belle isle."[^7]

After P. J. Colton came Jean L. Thibaudeau (1907 to 1910), Octave Dubois (1910 to 1915) and Paul Thomas (1915 to 1918). Michael Thomas kept the light from 1918 to 1944, Fred A. Davis from 1944 to 1958, and Fred Roberts from 1958 to at least 1964.[^1]

In 1966 assistant keeper Clifford Flynn asked for a switch at the upper light that would let the keepers light the lower one from it. Flynn wrote that in October, November and December the trip down the steep incline was very dangerous "with storms of wind and icy hills," and that "there is not a secure life-line."[^1] In March 1969 a fire broke out in the fog alarm building while keepers were using gasoline to clear old paint from its floor. Lighthouse Friends names the keeper, Clifford Flynn, and his two assistants, Fred Osbourne and Abner Budgell, and says all three were flown to hospital in St. Anthony.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

At half past nine on the night of 9 August 1876, lightning struck the tower and the dwelling. It tore clapboards from the tower, smashed its door, and seriously injured Keeper Martin Coltin and a member of his family.[^1]

The Belle Isle agents' ice report for the 1899–1900 winter begins with ice that came out on 17 December 1899: "First slab ice came out from the north-east and west of the island."[^5] In May 1900, "From the 1st to the 12th the Straits were blocked with heavy ice." By 4 June the straits were clear.[^5]

Late in September 1899 the Dominion Line steamer *Scotsman*, from Liverpool, went ashore in the Strait of Belle Isle. A wire datelined Heath Point, Quebec, on 28 September reported that "the overdue steamer Scotsman is ashore".[^12] A report printed in the *Dakota Chief* on 5 October said the steamer *Montfort* had arrived at Rimouski with 249 of the *Scotsman*'s passengers, and that fifteen of them had drowned.[^13] Other items on the same page gave the number of passengers as 250.[^13] A Montreal item on the same page put the accident down to a mistake "about the new light in the Straits of Belle Isle", and did not say which light it meant.[^13] The sirens had begun operating on 9 September 1899, before the wreck was reported.[^1]

The Department's report published in 1901 mentions the wreck without a death toll. Under its entry for Cape Norman, the report says that signal flags supplied to that keeper "proved quite useful in answering inquiries from passing steamers, especially in the case of the steamer Scotsman, wrecked on Belle Isle, some of the cargo of which floated around the vicinity."[^6] On 6 June 1900 the tug *Ingram* of St. John's went to the wreck.[^5]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the station was de-staffed in 2001, but its upper and lower lights stayed in service.[^1] In 1990 the lower and upper lights and the keepers' duplex were declared Recognized Federal Heritage Buildings.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory says the station was granted heritage status in 2015.[^4]

By 2017 the historic tower was inactive. The active light had moved to what the Directory calls "a short skeletal tower," and the Coast Guard still lists a white flash every twenty seconds for the station.[^4][^3]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the station. Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1] The Coast Guard's list gives the active light a white flash every 20 seconds, from 137.1 m (450 ft) above the water.[^3] The historic 1858 tower is inactive.[^4]

Lighthouse Friends suggests seeing the station by boat or from the air.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory says the site is open and the tower closed, and that the station is accessible only by boat in heavy seas or by helicopter.[^4] The Lighthouse Directory describes a separate Belle Isle North End light, built in 1905 at the island's northeastern end. That is a different station.[^4]
