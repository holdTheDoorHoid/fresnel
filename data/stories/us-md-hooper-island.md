Hooper Island Light is a round iron "spark plug" tower standing in open water in the Chesapeake Bay, roughly three miles west of Hooperville on Upper Hooper Island in Dorchester County, Maryland (the nomination says about three miles, Chesapeake Bay Magazine three to four). It was lit in 1902 to mark the shoals along the bay's low eastern shore, and it is still an active aid to navigation, though it has had no keepers since 1961.[^1][^2][^11]

## The place

The shoals along the Eastern Shore of the Chesapeake off Hooper Island were the reason for the light. In its report for 1897 the Light-House Board wrote of "a stretch of about 30 miles" between Cove Point and Smith Point that needed a better light. Along part of it, the Board said, navigators were "without a guide", and a vessel of heavy draft that strayed from the sailing course could run onto dangerous shoals. Many such vessels traded to Baltimore, and their number was increasing.[^3] The Board noted that the western shore of the bay was bluff and easy to see at night, while the eastern shore was low, and that the shoals lay along the eastern side.[^3] It estimated that a light there could be established for not more than $60,000.[^3]

A lightship had marked the place before. The National Register nomination says a lightship was on the station by 1855, was "thoroughly repaired and refitted" in 1856, and was either "removed and sunk or destroyed by the insurgents" during the Civil War. One was back on duty by 1866. The nomination says it is not known when that lightship station was given up.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress authorized up to $60,000 for a light and fog signal near Hooper Island on 1 July 1898 and appropriated $30,000. A second $30,000 followed on 3 March 1899.[^2] By the Board's account for the year to mid-1899, a boring had been made at the site to find how deep a safe foundation lay, designs had been adopted, and contracts had been let: $18,955 for the metalwork and $29,000 for erecting the lighthouse.[^4]

The work did not go smoothly. The ironwork was due by 13 September 1899, but the contractor for the metal did not finish delivering the caisson, the foundation cylinder and the iron for the foundation pier until 21 October 1899, and the rest reached the Lazaretto depot at Baltimore on 21 December.[^5] The erection contractor could not begin, and in May 1900 the Board annulled his contract and advertised again. New bids were opened on 14 June 1900.[^5] The National Register nomination names the builder as Toomey Brothers of Guilford, Connecticut, whose winning bid was $31,300, and says work began on 4 February 1901.[^2] (Lighthouse Friends gives the contractor's first price as $29,000 and describes the same re-bid.[^9])

The tower rests on a foundation sunk by the pneumatic process. A wooden caisson with an airtight working chamber was towed to the site and sunk while workers inside dug away the bottom and weight was added above.[^2] The caisson with two tiers of iron plate was launched on 21 May 1901, and the assembly was towed to the site by tug on 6 July.[^2] The Board's tender *Thistle* spent part of the year to mid-1901 relocating the buoy on the site of the new lighthouse.[^6] Concrete was poured into the cylinder, which had to go down 13 1/2 feet (4.1 m) below the mud line. It reached its depth on 31 August 1901, about 300 tons of riprap were placed around it against scour, and the last iron plates were bolted on by 17 September.[^2] The tower was "practically" finished by 10 February 1902.[^2]

The light was first lit on 1 June 1902. Its lens was a fourth-order Fresnel made in Paris in 1888.[^2] The nomination spells the maker "F. Babier & Company"; Lighthouse Friends gives F. Barbier & Company.[^2][^9] The fog bell was made by McShane of Baltimore in 1901.[^2] The cast-iron cylinder is 33 feet (10 m) across and about 36 feet (11 m) from the mud line to the gallery deck, with about 18 feet (5.5 m) of it above high water. The tower on top is four storeys with a watch room and lantern above, which the nomination says makes it the only cast-iron caisson light in Maryland with a watch room.[^2] The station is "integral": the keepers lived in the tower itself, with the galley on the first level and living and working rooms above.[^2]

## The keepers

The USLHS roster, compiled from the registers of lighthouse keepers, lists Albert Olsen as the first principal keeper (1902 to 1904), with William R. Schoenfelder as first assistant.[^7] Over the station's years it lists fourteen principal-keeper stints and thirty-six first-assistant stints. Charles H. Applegarth served 1904 to 1914, and Edwin C. Tyler from 1921 to 1936, the longest stretches the roster shows.[^7] Otho Bounds appears twice as head keeper (1914 to 1915 and 1916 to 1919) and later as first assistant (1919 to 1922).[^7] Between March and November 1912 it lists four first assistants in succession, the first for about two months.[^7] These dates come from the archive's listing, whose own cautions apply: it marks some spans "at least", and it does not say who held the post between the last principal keeper it names, Harold M. Messick (1951 to 1958), and automation in 1961.[^7]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1926 records that E. C. Tyler, keeper, and J. W. Simpson, assistant keeper, rendered assistance to the occupants of a disabled motor boat on 5 July.[^8] The Bulletin that year also printed letters received by the Baltimore superintendent of lighthouses from the keepers of three Fifth District lights, Hooper Island among them, about radio sets donated to keepers.[^10]

## After the keepers

A 110-volt generator replaced a battery pack on 28 August 1937, and the nomination says the fog bell was then replaced by an air-diaphragm horn.[^2] Lighthouse Friends dates the electrification to 1936.[^9] The Coast Guard fully automated the light on 21 November 1961.[^2] On 15 September 1976 a Coast Guard party found that the original fourth-order Fresnel lens had been stolen; it was replaced by a solar-powered optic.[^2]

Lighthouse Friends says the Coast Guard deemed the lighthouse excess and offered it free to eligible public and nonprofit bodies in 2006, and that the deed went to the U.S. Lighthouse Society in May 2009.[^9] The tower was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 2002.[^12] In 2022 the General Services Administration auctioned it. Bidding opened at $15,000 on 8 August, drew fifteen bids from five bidders and closed on 23 September at $192,000.[^11][^12] The sale included only the structure; the submerged land under it stays with the federal government.[^11] The buyer had to honor historic-preservation covenants, let the Coast Guard keep operating the light, and sign an agreement with the U.S. Navy, because the lighthouse lies within a Navy-controlled surface danger area.[^11]

## Today

The buyer was Richard Cucé, a Pennsylvania industrial painter,[^9] who told SpinSheet he hoped to turn the tower into an environmental center and to open it for tours by 2024.[^13] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists the owner as Richard Cucé and The Lighthouse Centers, the group working on the restoration.[^7][^14] The light is still an active aid to navigation, a 300 mm solar-powered optic.[^7]

The tower is private property in open water, reached only by boat,[^2] and it lies in a Navy danger area with access governed by an agreement.[^11] The Archive lists it as not open to the public.[^7]
