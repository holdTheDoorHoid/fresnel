Keweenaw Waterway Lower Entrance Light stands about 0.4 mile (0.6 km) out in Lake Superior's Keweenaw Bay, at the far end of the Portage River breakwater near Jacobsville, Houghton County, Michigan.[^1] Its octagonal steel tower on a timber crib was built in 1919 and 1920, and it has been an aid to navigation since 1 August 1920.[^1]

## The place

The Portage River mouth is the way into the Keweenaw Waterway, a dredged channel that cuts across the Keweenaw Peninsula to Portage Lake. Shipping on Lake Superior turned the waterway into a corridor for copper and fuel stops after the mid-1800s.[^1] The federal government bought the waterway in August 1891, and the Army Corps of Engineers later deepened the entrance and lengthened the breakwater on its eastern side.[^1]

The lights began at the river mouth. In 1868 a pair of range lights went up onshore to mark the channel, and the Portage Entry Light was set up that year at the offshore end of the breakwater.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1902 the Lighthouse Establishment built a wooden pierhead light, 27 feet (8 m) tall, at the breakwater's end. It was replaced in 1911 by a two-story iron skeletal tower that carried a fourth-order Fresnel lens showing a fixed red light.[^1]

The Bureau of Lighthouses planned a new, taller light in 1917, with electric lights along the waterway.[^1] The Register form dates the work on the light to 1919 and 1920. The fourth-order lens from the 1911 light went into the new tower, and the light was established as the Keweenaw Waterway Lower Entrance Light on 1 August 1920.[^1] The Bulletin says the wider electric system along the river was commenced in January 1918 and completed in October 1920.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1 November 1920 gives the new light a focal plane of 68 feet (21 m) above the water, and a siren fog signal that gives "a blast every 30 seconds".[^2]

The main light and fog-signal station stood on a reinforced-concrete base on a stone-filled timber crib. It had a steel tower and lantern, and the fog signal was a six-inch siren run by compressed air from a power house on shore.[^1][^3] A power house and quarters for three keepers went up near the shore end of the breakwater.[^3][^1] Final completion was set for 15 October 1920.[^4]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists 53 entries for the station, from 1868 to 1971.[^5] The first principal keeper in the archive, (Guy) Earl Edgerton, served from 1868 to 1872.[^5]

Oliver A. St. Andre was principal keeper from 1919 to 1941 in the archive's list.[^5] The nomination says Lighthouse Service keepers ran the light from 1920 to 1939 and that Coast Guard personnel took over after that.[^1] The two dates differ, and the sources here do not settle which is right.

Franklin Witz was first assistant from 1920 to 1930, and Louis I. Wilks and Charles I. Mattila served as second and third assistants during the 1920s.[^5] The archive's Coast Guard entries run from the late 1940s into the 1970s.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The one dated incident in the sources read for this story is a mishap in 1924. On 8 May about 1,500 feet of the pipe that carried air to the fog signal was carried into the lake. Keeper St. Andre, his first assistant Witz, third assistant Wilks and a keeper at large named Mattila raised the pipe and reconnected it without assistance, and the fog signal was back in service on 10 May. The Lighthouse Service commended them for it.[^6]

## After the keepers

The fog signal was later replaced with a duophone system.[^1] The original fourth-order lens was removed around 2010 and replaced with a modern LED optic. The power cable along the breakwater was disconnected, and the light now runs on batteries charged by a solar array on the lantern gallery.[^1]

The light was automated in the 1950s.[^1] The old Portage River Light at Jacobsville was discontinued in 1920 and sold to a private owner in 1958. It is now the Jacobsville Lighthouse, which operates as a bed and breakfast. It is a different structure and should not be confused with this one.[^1]

## Today

The 2014 nomination records that the Coast Guard owns the light and runs it as an automated aid, with a modern optic showing a white flash every six seconds.[^1] The Register form says the light is reached from shore along the breakwater, but it is not open to public visitation.[^1] Visitors should not walk out to it.

In May 2014 the Coast Guard deemed the light excess and made it available to eligible groups under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act.[^7] LighthouseFriends reports that the Keweenaw Waterway Lighthouse Conservancy was awarded the light on 20 September 2016, and that the group later seemed to have gone inactive.[^7] The sources read here do not confirm who owns the light now.
