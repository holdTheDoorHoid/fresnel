Charleston Light is a triangular tower of steel and aluminium on Sullivan's Island, on the north side of the entrance to Charleston Harbor. It was dedicated in 1962, and the Park Service calls it the last major lighthouse built in the United States.[^1][^2]

## The place

For nearly two centuries the main light for Charleston's entrance stood across the harbor mouth on Morris Island. The National Park Service dates the first permanent lighthouse there to 1767. Confederate forces destroyed it in 1861, and a new tower went up on Morris Island after the Civil War, in 1876.[^1] The jetties built to redirect the ship channel then caused serious erosion on Morris Island, and the Park Service says that when a new light was built on Sullivan's Island, closer to the new main channel, the Morris Island light was decommissioned.[^1] LighthouseFriends puts it in terms of the threat: erosion endangered the Morris Island tower, and a replacement was therefore planned for Sullivan's Island.[^2]

Sullivan's Island had carried navigation lights before. LighthouseFriends records a pair of beacons near Fort Moultrie in 1848 to guide mariners over Charleston Bar.[^2] The 1962 tower stands in a Coast Guard historic district that grew from an older life-saving site. The Park Service says the Life-Saving Station on the island was established in 1894 and completed on 1 August 1895, while LighthouseFriends says a lifesaving station was built there in 1898.[^3][^2] The National Register nomination puts the lighthouse about 100 feet (30 m) southeast of the district's 1898 bunker.[^4]

## Building the light

Construction began in 1960 and the tower was finished in 1962.[^5] The Park Service gives 15 June 1962 as the date it was dedicated, and says it was designed by Jack Graham while he was serving in the Coast Guard.[^1] LighthouseFriends adds that Graham finished his architectural training in 1957 under Louis Kahn, signed up with the Coast Guard a year later after learning he would be drafted, and in 1959 was asked to come up with a lighthouse design; he based it on a triangle, and persuaded the Coast Guard to put an elevator in because of reported accidents on lighthouse stairs.[^2]

The triangular plan was chosen so the tower could withstand winds of up to 125 miles per hour.[^1] The frame is steel girders with aluminium-alloy siding.[^1] Sources disagree on how tall it is. The Park Service says 162.5 feet (49.5 m), one local guide says a 140-foot (43 m) tower, and the National Register nomination describes it as approximately 150 feet (46 m).[^1][^5][^4] Inside, LighthouseFriends describes air conditioning and an elevator that took 74 seconds to rise, after which a vertical ladder still had to be climbed to the lantern room. It gives that ladder as 25 feet (7.6 m); the Park Service says 35 (10.7 m).[^2][^1]

The first light was enormous. LighthouseFriends reports 28 million candlepower from carbon-arc lamps costing $900 apiece.[^2] While the lamps were lit, keepers were required to wear an asbestos welding suit to enter the lantern room, and plate steel was fitted to the landward side of the lantern room to pacify the neighbours.[^2] The light was later cut back. The Park Service says five years after the opening, to 1.2 million candlepower; another website says 1967 and 1,170,000; LighthouseFriends says "a decade later" and "just over a million."[^1][^6][^2]

The tower's paint was also changed. It first wore red-orange and white, and was repainted black and white after complaints from island residents.[^1] The National Register paperwork describes the present scheme as the upper seven stories black and the remaining eight white.[^4]

## The keepers

Charleston Light was a Coast Guard station, not a family light. In the 1970s, LighthouseFriends says, it was staffed by a first-class petty officer in command, a second-class petty officer and two non-rated crew members.[^2] One of them, Mike Sejman, spoke to The Charleston Observer in 1976. As LighthouseFriends reports it, he said it was a little sad to think the light would soon be automated, and that the romance of lighthouse keeping was about gone.[^2] The station was open to the public for a few hours on weekends and holidays, and visitors had to sign a release before climbing the ladder to the lantern.[^2]

## After the keepers

The date the light was automated is not settled. LighthouseFriends says the tower was "now-automated" by November 1976, when servicing passed to the Coast Guard station in Charleston; another lighthouse website says 1982.[^2][^6]

The land around it changed hands in stages. The old Life-Saving Station grounds were listed in the National Register as the U.S. Coast Guard Historic District on 19 June 1973, the year Coast Guard activity on the island fell off when the new Charleston station opened.[^4][^3] The Park Service says most of the property was transferred to it after Hurricane Hugo in 1989; LighthouseFriends dates the Park Service takeover of the life-saving buildings to 1986 and says the lighthouse was excluded.[^3][^2] The 1989 hurricane is also remembered at the tower itself: the Park Service says the triangular design's strength "was demonstrated when it withstood Hurricane Hugo in 1989."[^1]

The lighthouse itself passed to the Park Service on 29 May 2008, according to LighthouseFriends; the Coast Guard kept responsibility for the light, and the Park Service took on the tower.[^2][^7] By 2012 the tower was closed to visitors for health and safety, and a Park Service volunteer told a local television station the cause was chipped, lead-based paint inside.[^8][^9]

## Today

The tower is part of Fort Sumter and Fort Moultrie National Historical Park, and the Coast Guard still cares for the light and lanterns.[^1][^7] The lighthouse is fenced off and closed to the public, but the grounds can be visited by day, and the Park Service has held occasional open houses on the old Coast Guard grounds.[^5][^7] The interior is closed because of hazards.[^7] Nobody should try to enter the tower.
