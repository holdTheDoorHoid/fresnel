Round Island Passage Light stands in northwestern Lake Huron, a short way off the southern tip of Mackinac Island, on the north side of the channel between the island and Round Island.[^1] It was commissioned on 15 April 1948 and has never had a resident keeper: the light was run from a control house on the island.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The nomination calls the passage the most direct water route linking Lake Superior and Lake Michigan.[^1] Shoal water lay along the channel's northern edge, a hazard to shipping, and buoys were the first marks used along its boundaries.[^1]

An earlier light had marked the other side. The nomination says Round Island Light was established in 1894 to mark the south side of the passage.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that light began operating in 1896.[^1][^3] The two sources disagree on the date.

## Building the light

By the early 1940s the Coast Guard had concluded that the passage's northern side needed a permanent light, the nomination says.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the plan to 1936, and says construction was delayed until after the Second World War. As an interim measure, a radiobeacon bell buoy was placed 630 feet (190 m) off Mackinac Island's West Breakwater.[^3]

The new light was designed to be operated remotely from Mackinac Island.[^1] Construction began in 1947 and included a control house at the island's southern tip. Control and power cables ran underwater to the structure, so no one had to be stationed on the offshore light itself.[^1] The control house was about 0.25 mile (0.4 km) northwest of the light and held the equipment for the beacon, the fog signal and the radiobeacon.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes a Coast Guard Bulletin description of the substructure: a timber crib 56 feet (17 m) square, under a concrete superstructure.[^3]

The light was an array of sealed-beam lamps set in windows near the top of the tower.[^1] The nomination says it flashed green and could be seen for 16 miles in clear weather.[^1] The Coast Guard Bulletin, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, describes an occulting green light every 10 seconds, also visible for 16 miles.[^3] The two sources disagree on the characteristic. The fog signal was a compressed-air diaphragm fog signal, with its machinery in the basement of the concrete pier.[^1] The nomination describes one horn on a pole at the pier deck's corner; the Bulletin, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, describes two horns.[^1][^3]

## After the keepers

The beacon was changed from green to flashing red in 1959, so that it would follow the red-right-return rule for ships coming down from the upper lakes.[^1] The radiobeacon's distance-finding service ended in 1962, although the radiobeacon kept transmitting as a marker. Before then, the radiobeacon and the fog signal had worked together as a distance finder: a ship's crew timed the seconds between a radio signal and the sound of the fog horn, then divided by five to get statute miles, within about ten percent.[^1]

The day mark was changed in 1968 to a white tower above a red base on a white pier.[^1] The Round Island Passage Light was automated in 1973, which meant its equipment ran without anyone present.[^1] The control house on Mackinac Island was shut down and later disposed of.[^1] A RACON radar beacon went on the tower roof in 1984, and the radiobeacon was discontinued in 1993.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard owned the light when it was listed on the National Register on 7 August 2013.[^1][^3] The nomination says it is accessible by water and is not open to the public.[^1][^3] In 2014 the General Services Administration opened an online auction for the light on 18 August. It closed on 30 September with a high bid of $65,500.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the light is now privately owned, and that its tower is closed.[^3] None of the sources names the buyer.

Visitors can see it from the ferries to Mackinac Island from St. Ignace or Mackinaw City, and from a Shepler's lighthouse cruise, LighthouseFriends says.[^3]
