Holland Harbor Light, known as Big Red, stands on the south side of the entrance to Lake Macatawa, the Black Lake of the early settlers, at Holland, Michigan.[^1][^2] The tower was painted red in 1956, and local people began calling it Big Red.[^1][^7]

## The place

In 1847 the area was, LighthouseFriends says, "a swamp and an insect-infested forest."[^1] The settlers needed a way from their town to the lake.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that after vain attempts to get government aid, the settlers dug the link themselves with picks and shovels, and that on 1 July 1859 the small steamboat Huron was able to call at Holland.[^1] In its 1900 report the Lighthouse Board called Holland "a port of entry for Grand Rapids," and said its lake trade was "both passenger and freight, during the season of navigation."[^4]

The light stood on the south pier as a pierhead beacon.[^1]

## Building the light

The sources do not agree on when the first light was shown. LighthouseFriends says Congress provided $6,000 for a beacon at Holland on 15 July 1870, and that a light was established on the south pier later that year.[^1] The commission's history gives $4,000 for construction, begun in 1870, and says the light first shone in December of that year.[^7] The Lighthouse Society's record gives 1873 as the year the station was established.[^2] These dates are not reconciled in the sources.

The first tower was a wooden beacon on the pier, painted white, with a fifth-order Fresnel lens showing a fixed red light.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the focal plane as thirty-two feet (9.8 m), and says mariners could see the light for up to eleven and a half miles.[^1] The commission's history gives the focal height as 34 feet.[^7] Melgert Van Regenmorter was hired as the first keeper, at a salary of $540 a year. LighthouseFriends says he had to find his own housing for several years, because difficulties in getting title to the parcel delayed the keeper's dwelling until at least 1873.[^1]

The pier grew with the port. LighthouseFriends says a 550-foot (168 m) elevated walk was built along the south pier by 1875, and that the lighthouse was moved 264 feet (80 m) lakeward in 1880, with the walk extended to match.[^1] On 26 May 1890 a post light was set at the outer end of the extended pier to form a range with the main light.[^1] Later that year the scow Breakaway carried the post light away while trying to enter the harbor, and the Board's report says the post was rebuilt.[^3][^1] In 1901 the Board reported that "A lantern carriage and wire rope were provided for running out the lantern light to the post on the pierhead."[^5]

On 4 January 1902 a square, pyramidal steel tower replaced the post light, taller than the wooden beacon.[^1] The commission's history gives the same year for a new 42-foot (13 m) steel beacon in place of the wooden structure.[^1][^7] In 1907 a fog signal building was added next to the pierhead tower, with a steam whistle and quarters for two assistant keepers, and a fourth-order lens was installed in the tower. The Board had asked for a fog signal starting in 1900.[^1][^7][^4] The two pier lights were electrified in 1932.[^1] In 1936 a square tower was erected on the fog signal building, combining the light and the fog signal in one building.[^1][^7]

## The keepers

Melgert Van Regenmorter kept the light for thirty-seven years, from 1870 to 1907, according to the roster and LighthouseFriends.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends describes him as one of the original settlers who came with Albertus C. Van Raalte, and as a Civil War veteran.[^1] The commission's history puts his tenure as keeper at 1874 to 1907, so the sources differ on when his service began.[^7] LighthouseFriends says that when the fog signal was being built in 1907, the sixty-nine-year-old keeper apparently decided to end his service rather than learn to run the new equipment.[^1]

Charles Bavry was transferred to Holland from Pilot Island, Wisconsin, to replace him in 1907.[^1] Joseph Boshka followed in 1911, transferred from the head keeper's post at Plum Island, Wisconsin. He had begun as a second assistant at Seul Choix Point in 1898.[^1] The roster has him from 1911 to 1940, and the commission's history from 1912 to 1940.[^2][^7] The Bulletin's index lists Boshka's name under the heading "Holland range light station wins efficiency flag."[^6] LighthouseFriends says the station was the district's model station in 1916, and that Boshka was allowed to fly the efficiency pennant the following year.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Boshka kept the station for twenty-nine years, retired in 1940 at sixty-seven, and lived in Macatawa near the light until his death in 1965.[^1]

Henry Rocheleau served as second assistant from 1917 to 1922 and returned as first assistant in 1925, according to the roster.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that in 1919 he was recognized for rescuing two people from a capsized canoe and taking them ashore.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The commission's history says a storm in 1880 damaged the pier and shifted the beacon.[^7] The Board's report records the loss of the post light to the Breakaway, and LighthouseFriends says the loss came in a northwest gale.[^3][^1]

On 22 December 1904 a gale damaged the crib under the steel tower.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the light was discontinued, and the tower was dismantled and stored, then set up again on a temporary wooden foundation the next spring.[^1] The commission's history records a collision in the Holland channel in 1905, when the Argo struck a sand bar and collided with the north pier, causing $1,000 in damage.[^7]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard announced in 1970 that it planned to abandon the light, and Holland residents signed petitions and wrote letters.[^1][^7] LighthouseFriends quotes the Coast Guard's reply, which called the light "an aesthetic landmark" and said its maintenance would continue.[^1] The Holland Harbor Lighthouse Historical Commission was formed in 1974 to save and restore the light.[^1][^7]

The National Register of Historic Places listed the light in 1978.[^8] In the same year, according to the commission, the Fresnel lens went to the Holland Museum and an acrylic lens took its place.[^7] In 2005 the Coast Guard offered the light as excess property under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000. The National Park Service transferred it to the commission in February 2007.[^1][^7]

The 2025 Light List gives the light's characteristic as alternating white and red, every ten seconds, from a square tower on the building.[^9]

## Today

The light is owned by the Holland Harbor Lighthouse Historical Commission.[^1][^7] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] From Holland State Park, on the opposite side of the entrance, the light can be seen well.[^1] The Lighthouse Society's record lists the station as open to the public.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the south side of the lake entrance is mainly private property, where limited public access is allowed, but the walk to the light is long. Its most specific access rules describe summer 2014, so check current conditions before going.[^1]

The Fresnel lens is on display at the Holland Museum.[^1]
