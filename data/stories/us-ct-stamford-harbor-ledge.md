Stamford Harbor Ledge Light stands on a ledge at the entrance to Stamford harbor, about two-thirds of a mile from the nearest mainland, in Connecticut. It is a conical cast-iron tower on a cast-iron caisson, first lit in 1882. The government discontinued it in 1953. It is now privately owned, closed to visitors, and reached only by boat.[^1][^2][^7]

## The place

The nomination describes the harbor at Stamford as "an area busy with schooners, packet steamers, oyster boats, and pleasure craft" in the late nineteenth century. It says the light recalls an era when "Long Island Sound was thronged with commercial shipping."[^3]

The tower sits on a base of rock and cement, on the ledge the nomination calls Chatham Rock.[^1] Heap calls the reef Harbor Ledge, and places the light on "the south-west extremity of Harbor Ledge in Stamford Harbor."[^8] LighthouseFriends calls it Chatham Rocks.[^4] The tower stands about 750 feet south of the breakwater at the harbor entrance, and the nearest point of mainland is about two-thirds of a mile away.[^1]

Board officials came out in 1871 and proposed a day beacon on the ledge, plus a lit beacon on the far side of the channel. It was not until 1881 that Congress appropriated $30,000 for a lighthouse, and the site chosen was Chatham Rocks, about two-thirds of a mile offshore.[^4]

## Building the light

The Stamford light belongs to a family of caisson lights. The nomination says the prefabricated iron caisson came into use in 1876, and that by 1880 the Board's engineers had moved away from the elaborate early superstructures to a simpler truncated-cone tower. It adds that Stamford Harbor is "in design and dimensions identical to two other area lights, Latimer Reef (1884)" and Saybrook Breakwater Light.[^3]

LighthouseFriends says the sections came from a Boston foundry and were bolted together on site.[^4] The caisson is 30 feet across and 28 feet high, made of cast-iron plates bolted together. Its base is a footing of rock and cement set on Chatham Rock. Inside, a second brick-lined iron cylinder held an oil tank and a cistern for fresh water. The space between the cylinders was ballasted with concrete and rock.[^1] The superstructure is about 49 feet high, with four tiers of cast-iron plates, and a twelve-sided lantern at the top.[^1]

From 10 February 1882, the Board announced, a fourth-order light showing red would be exhibited from the new tower. The focal plane was 60 feet above mean low water. LighthouseFriends quotes the notice, and adds that the tower had a fog bell struck by machinery.[^4] The nomination gives the same 60-foot figure, from a 19th-century chart, and says the light could be seen for 11 1/4 nautical miles.[^1]

Neil Martin, the first keeper, requested a transfer after ten months. LighthouseFriends says the tower was considered cramped and uncomfortable, even by the reduced standards of lighthouse towers, and was not a favorite of the keepers posted there.[^4] The second keeper, Nahor Jones, tried to make a home on the ledge for his family. He carried their furniture out, and built a dock and a chicken coop.[^4] A storm soon carried off the chicken coop, the dock and the boat. The family moved back ashore, and Jones rowed out to work for the rest of his service.[^4]

The Lighthouse Board's annual report for fiscal 1894 lists "Stamford Harbor" among its station entries. An oil house was built there that year, and minor repairs were made.[^9]

## The keepers

The archive lists 30 stints for the station, from 1882 to 1953, under the headings principal keeper, first and second assistant, and Coast Guard.[^10] Samuel A. Keeney served from 1887 to 1903, the longest stint in the archive's list, and Martin L. Sowle served from 1938 until the light was discontinued in 1953. LighthouseFriends says Sowle was in charge for the last sixteen years of the light's operation.[^5][^10]

John J. Cook was keeper from 1907 to 1909. In 1908 his launch broke down on the way back to the tower with his wife aboard, and they spent a stormy night adrift. That story is told below.[^5][^10]

Edgar M. Whitford, Robert M. Fitton, Raymond F. Bliven, Louis E. Colbert and Albert C. Brennecke followed in the 1920s and 1930s.[^10]

A fog signal arrived in 1937, and assistant keepers were posted to the station. The head keeper could no longer have his family living at the light, because the signal and the assistants needed room in the tower.[^5] The nomination and LighthouseFriends both describe a fog bell struck by machinery from the start, so the sources do not make clear what the 1937 fog signal was.[^1][^4] Andrew A. McLintock served as first assistant from 1937 to 1941, and Maxwell A. Deshon and Matthew C. Gallego as second assistants in 1937 and 1938. Coast Guard men Abraham Halpern (1939 to 1940) and Harold A. Weisbein (to 1953) appear in the roster under the Coast Guard heading.[^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1908 Keeper Cook was trying to bring his wife back to the tower after she had recuperated ashore for two weeks. The launch's engine broke down as they approached, and the boat drifted onto rocks. Cook lost his only oar and could not stop the boat being tossed about the Sound through the night. When he lost the oar, he shouted to his mother-in-law, Laura Weickman, who was on the balcony, to keep the light burning. A crew from the Eatons Neck lifesaving station found the boat at daybreak and brought the couple off, both nearly dead from exposure.[^5] Laura kept the light burning for two nights, alone in the tower. Cook tried to return after the first night but could not make a landing. The storm eased on the third day, and he got out to the lighthouse to take over from her.[^5]

LighthouseFriends quotes a 1908 newspaper interview in which Cook described Christmas at the light. "And that light up aloft is a sermon in itself," he is quoted as saying.[^5]

On 27 August 1913 Keeper Joseph Meyer, ashore for provisions, could not get back to the light because of high seas. The light was out. Three Stamford yachtsmen put out in a small powerboat, forced open a window, got into the tower, and had the light working by 1 a.m. Meyer was so exhausted by his efforts that he fell asleep ashore and did not wake until daylight.[^5]

Raymond F. Bliven was appointed keeper in 1930. On the evening of 13 August 1931 he set the light going and rowed ashore to meet a friend for dinner. He started back to the tower about 10:30 that night. The light burned steadily the next day and went out the following night, and the harbormaster found the tower empty. The *Spruce*'s crew found an upturned small boat about a quarter mile from the tower. A local yachtsman found Bliven's body on 15 August, roughly half a mile from the boat. The death was ruled an accidental drowning. LighthouseFriends says he is believed to have slipped and fallen back into the station's boat while climbing the ladder up the tower's side.[^5]

On 2 October 1939 two civilians were taking up can buoys for the winter in a privately marked channel near the light. Their 18-foot motor workboat swamped in the heavy sea and sank. Keeper Martin L. Sowle, on duty, saw an oil slick where the men had last been seen, and put out in a 16-foot outboard dory. He found one of the men hanging to a 20-foot spar and lifted him into the boat. The second man's body was recovered a week later.[^5] Sowle received a silver lifesaving medal in 1940 for the rescue. The Coast Guard noted that it was one of many occasions on which he had helped people in distress near his station.[^5]

## After the keepers

In 1949 the Coast Guard held public hearings on closing the station. Local maritime groups objected, but the light was decommissioned in 1953 and the property was offered for sale.[^6] The nomination says the government discontinued the light in 1953, after which it passed through a number of owners.[^2]

LighthouseFriends describes what followed. The city's mayor got the government to hand the lighthouse to the city, on condition that it be kept as a historic site for at least twenty years. The city did not maintain it and gave it back to the government eight years later. In 1967 three Stamford men bought the derelict tower at auction for $10,100. After several more owners, the tower went to auction again in 1984 for $230,000. The buyer, a private owner, put about $300,000 into a new kitchen, heating and electrical work, paint and paneling, and a small breakwater and dock. The owner never spent a night there.[^6]

The Rowlett directory records the light as inactive from 1953 to 1985 and then "Reactivated."[^7] The light was for sale again in 1996, at $1.1 million, and the owner died in 1998.[^6][^7] The tower was offered for sale again in 2008.[^6] Rowlett says it was on sale again in 2020, at $1.595 million.[^7] In August 2023 two men, Gary Kalan and Brendan McGee, bought it for $500,000. They set up a nonprofit, the Stamford Harbor Lighthouse Project, to fund its restoration.[^6][^7] The Rowlett directory says they expect the restoration to cost about $4.5 million.[^7]

## Today

The light is privately owned and closed. Rowlett's directory lists the site and tower as closed, the lighthouse as accessible only by boat, and the owner or site manager as the Stamford Harbor Lighthouse Project.[^7] The directory says the lighthouse was secured and its debris removed in 2024, with structural restoration beginning in 2025 and continuing in 2026.[^7] The nomination describes it as boarded up but in relatively good condition, and says that a small privately maintained navigational aid is carried on a metal orb ventilator atop the lantern roof.[^1][^2]

Whether the light shows a beacon today is not settled by these sources. Rowlett's directory gives a white flash every four seconds from a focal plane of 80 feet, and calls the light reactivated. The nomination gives the focal plane as 60 feet, from a 19th-century chart, and describes only the small beacon on the roof.[^1][^7]
