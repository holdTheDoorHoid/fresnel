Cape Vincent Breakwater Light was a pair of lights that marked the ends of a timber breakwater off the village of Cape Vincent, in Jefferson County, New York, where Lake Ontario becomes the St. Lawrence River.[^1] Its first lights were shown in 1901 from lens lanterns on masts.[^1] Its wooden towers gave way to steel structures in 1951, and one of the old towers still stands beside Route 12E on the village's southern edge.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1854 the historian Franklin Hough wrote that a breakwater in front of the piers was "demanded by the commercial wants of the place," and LighthouseFriends quotes him to that effect.[^1] The government did not pass an act for the work until March 3, 1899.[^1] That act provided for a timber crib 1,400 feet (427 m) long, built parallel to the railroad wharf and 500 feet (152 m) out from it.[^1] The first section was finished in 1900.[^1] The whole breakwater cost nearly $200,000 before it reached its full length.[^1]

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1901 lists light-house structures for the Cape Vincent breakwater among the engineering work in its tenth district.[^2] On August 31, 1901, two temporary fixed red lights were established on the finished part of the breakwater.[^1] Each was shown from a lens lantern on a mast with a small house at its base, and the lights stood about twenty feet (6 m) from the two ends.[^1]

In 1904 twin wooden towers replaced the temporary lights.[^1] Each was eleven feet (3.4 m) square and 25 feet (7.6 m) high, with an octagonal lantern room under a copper roof. LighthouseFriends gives that height; the USLHS record gives 23 feet (7 m) from base to lantern centre.[^1][^3] They showed fixed red light from five-day lens lanterns.[^1] In November 1906 the eastern tower was moved 500 feet (152 m) farther east, onto concrete piers at the end of a newly completed section.[^1] A 500-foot (152 m) lifeline of cable on iron pipe posts ran along the breakwater so that the keeper could reach the eastern light in bad weather.[^1] In 1915 that light moved another 250 feet (76 m), by which time the breakwater measured 1,381 feet (421 m).[^1]

## The keepers

John H. Larock came from Crossover Island to become the first keeper of the breakwater lights in 1901.[^1][^3] He had to find his own lodging in town, but a rented boathouse was provided for the small boat he rowed out to the lights.[^1] The Light-House Board asked for $5,000 in 1902 to buy a site and build a dwelling and boathouse, and it repeated the request in the two years after.[^1] LighthouseFriends found no evidence in the Board's reports that the buildings were ever built.[^1]

Larock served until 1910.[^3] William Wybrands followed him, from 1910 to 1912, and Ralph H.B. Scobie acted as keeper for part of 1910.[^3]

Halsey W. Crapo had entered the Lighthouse Service in 1909, serving first at Cleveland and Lorain, Ohio, before he was transferred to Cape Vincent in 1912.[^4] The Bulletin's 1918-1923 index lists his name against an efficiency flag for the Cape Vincent breakwater station at page 22, and page 22 of the 1918 volume names him in its table of district keepers.[^5][^6] LighthouseFriends says he received the flag in 1917 and again in 1927, and the 1927 award does not appear in the Bulletin sources read for this story.[^1]

Crapo died on June 5, 1929, according to the Bulletin's death notice.[^4] LighthouseFriends adds that he was also the harbor master, that he had done his morning work at the lights, and that he took ill with chest pain shortly after lunch at home. He died less than an hour after being stricken, at forty-one.[^1] Frank L. Sellman, who had been head keeper at the Ashtabula lighthouse, succeeded him.[^1][^3]

## After the keepers

On June 28, 1939, the breakwater lights were changed from incandescent oil vapor to electricity, which increased their intensity.[^1] In 1951 the old wooden towers were taken off the pier and replaced by white steel skeletal structures with tank houses.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Fifth-order Fresnel lenses were still in the wooden towers when they were removed.[^1] The USLHS record gives the original optic as a lens lantern, installed in 1901.[^3] It also gives 1951 as the year the station was discontinued, and its disposition as "moved to park display."[^3]

A local man acquired the second wooden tower, and his children used it as a playhouse.[^1] It later fell into disrepair and was demolished.[^1]

## Today

The surviving tower stands alongside Route 12E (Market Street) on the southern outskirts of Cape Vincent, near the Town of Cape Vincent highway department building.[^1] The grounds are open and the tower is closed, and LighthouseFriends names the Village of Cape Vincent as its owner.[^1] The USLHS record says the station is not open to the public and is no longer an active aid to navigation.[^3]
