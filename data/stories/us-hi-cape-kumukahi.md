Cape Kumukahi Light stands on bare black lava at the easternmost point of Hawaii island,[^5][^6] near the village of Kapoho, which the 1960 eruption destroyed.[^10] It is a steel tower that replaced a small acetylene light in 1934.[^5] In 1960 a lava flow came up to the tower and divided around it.[^7]

## The place

In 1915 the Commissioner of Lighthouses called Cape Kumukahi "the easternmost cape of Hawaii." Ships bound to Hilo from the Panama Canal or the southeast, he wrote, had "no landfall light." The ground was "barren, undulating lava rock," and landing from the sea was "impossible at most times."[^1]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of April 1934 described the point as a low mass of bare, black lava with a jagged top, and said ships rounding it had to give the cape a berth of about 3 miles.[^5] Traffic to Hilo and Honolulu from the Panama Canal and South American ports passed the point, the Bulletin said, and the need for a primary light "has been evident for many years."[^5]

## Building the light

Lighthouse Friends says the Lighthouse Board made its "first request for a lighthouse at Cape Kumukahi" in 1907.[^7] Each of the Commissioner's annual reports from the one for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1915 carried a detailed estimate for a light there: $22,000 in that report and the same sum in each report to 1919, $26,500 in the 1920 report, $18,500 in 1921 and $28,000 in 1922.[^1][^2][^3] The 1915 estimate recommended "an acetylene light." Its only practical way to supply the station was "by railroad from Hilo to Kapoho and then by wagon road 3 miles to the cape."[^1] The 1922 estimate proposed "a temporary gaslight on this cape" as soon as a site could be acquired.[^3] A 1933 Bulletin note still called the new light a "proposed new light."[^4]

Lighthouse Friends says the government bought fifty-eight acres on the cape at the end of 1928.[^7] The 1934 Bulletin says the site "was acquired in 1929."[^5] The two dates disagree.

In 1929 a small acetylene light went up for local use, the Bulletin says.[^5] Lighthouse Friends describes that first structure as "a thirty-two-foot wooden tower."[^7] In 1932 two miles of road were built to the site, the Bulletin reports.[^5]

The steel tower was finished in 1934.[^5] Lighthouse Friends says the concrete foundation was two blocks with "a thick layer of sand in between," because of the frequent earthquakes in that volcanic area, so that the lower block could give with the ground rather than pass its jolts up to the tower.[^7] The Bulletin gave the light 1,700,000 candlepower and a flash "every 6 seconds."[^5] The Bulletin gives the new steel tower a height of 110 feet (34 m),[^5] while the archive gives 125 feet (38 m) from the base to the center of the lantern,[^6] and Lighthouse Friends says "roughly 125 feet above the ground."[^7] The sources disagree on the height.

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends calls Charles K. Akana a "veteran keeper" who "served at nearly every major light in the islands." He took charge of the new station in 1934, with William J. Watkins as first assistant.[^6][^7]

Joseph Pestrella came to the cape in 1938, "transferred from the lighthouse tender Kukui," the Lighthouse Friends history says. He "brought in soil and trees" at his own expense. His orchard of lemon, mango and tangerine trees was later burned by the lava.[^7] The archive has him as first assistant from 1938 to 1942 and as principal keeper from 1942 to 1960.[^6]

Lighthouse Friends also names two keepers the archive does not list: Frederick E. Nihoa, head keeper from 1936 to 1942, and Joseph Brown, an assistant from 1946 to 1948.[^7]

In 1955 "a lava flow threatened the station," and Pestrella remained on duty. In 1956 he was named "Civil Servant of the Year" for the Hawaii area.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 13 January 1960 "a fiery fountain of lava" opened in a sugar cane field about two miles east of the light, just north of Kapoho.[^7] The station's logbook for that day, as Lighthouse Friends describes it, "records that the keepers felt earthquakes at 8:30 a.m." and that the station was not damaged.[^7]

A newspaper report of 20 January said that a Coast Guard lighthouse and a housing development on the cape could be hit if the flow kept coming.[^8] In an Associated Press report datelined Kapoho Village, 30 January, a Civil Defense spokesman said the light and a beach community at Kumukahi Point "still were not out of danger."[^9]

On 2 February, Lighthouse Friends says, the lava "swallowed the keeper's dwellings" and the orchard, and the heat "caused the generator's fuel tanks at the tower to explode," putting the light out. The flow then "remarkably divided into two streams" and passed both sides of the tower.[^7] A Coast Guard inspection on 11 February "found that the tower was in good condition."[^7]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says a ten-ton lighted buoy stood in for the light until the Hilo Electric Company strung power lines from the Kapoho Beach lots. The light was then "fully automated," and Pestrella was transferred to Makapu'u Lighthouse on Oahu.[^7]

The archive lists the station's other structures as "DESTROYED BY VOLCANIC ERUPTION" and records no keeper's quarters.[^6] Lighthouse Friends, though, describes keepers' dwellings that the lava swallowed.[^7]

## Today

A U.S. Geological Survey photograph, published in its chronology of 31 August 2019, "shows the Cape Kumukahi lighthouse and Kapoho area," with the 2018 lava flow in the top half of the picture.[^10]

Lighthouse Friends says the light is "owned by the Coast Guard," with the grounds open and the tower closed. It says the road was affected by the 2018 eruption, so "access to the lighthouse may not be possible."[^7] The U.S. Lighthouse Society record gives the station as not open to the public.[^6]

Lighthouse Friends also notes "a monitoring station for ALOHA" beside the cape.[^7] The archive lists the light as an active aid to navigation.[^6] The sources here do not say whether it is shown now.
