Spring Point Ledge Light stands on a ledge near the entrance to Portland Harbor, off Fort Preble in South Portland, Maine.[^2] It is a tower on a cast-iron caisson, first lit on 24 May 1897, and the National Register nomination calls it the most intact of Maine's three surviving "spark plug" lights.[^1] A granite breakwater now joins it to the shore.[^10]

## The place

Spring Point Ledge runs out from the shore at Fort Preble into the main channel to Portland Harbor.[^2][^10] The Trust's history calls it "the scene of many shipwrecks and groundings," and says the buoys set on it were of "limited usefulness in bad weather."[^10] The National Register form says the ledge "was witness to a number of wrecks and accidental groundings."[^1]

Portland Harbor was busy. The Trust says it was "one of the busiest on the east coast" by the late 1800s, and that seven steamship companies were carrying more than 500,000 passengers a year past the ledge.[^10] LighthouseFriends says those companies petitioned the Lighthouse Board for a warning.[^2]

The Board's First District report for 1894 asked for "a fog bell and a light of the fifth order" on the ledge, and described the ledge as lying "in bold water at the edge of the channel."[^4] That report said the same request had been made in the Board's last three annual reports. The Trust says the Board was "finally persuaded in April 1891"; the National Register form dates the repeated requests to "beginning in 1892."[^10][^1] Congress did not act until 1895.[^4][^10]

## Building the light

Congress acted in March 1895. Its act appropriated $20,000 "for establishing a light and fog-signal station at this point," and allowed a contract of up to $45,000.[^5] The rest came in June 1896: an appropriation of $25,000 "for finishing" the station, which the Board's report for that year describes.[^6]

The Board advertised for bids in April 1896. On 8 May it accepted the bid of Thomas Dwyer of New York, at $27,490.[^10] The USLHS roster lists Dwyer as the contractor.[^9] Construction began in August 1896 with the leveling of the rock that would carry the foundation.[^2] The work was set back in September, when a storm damaged the cylinder as it was being placed in position (see below).[^10][^2] The Board expected the station to be ready for lighting by 1 April 1897.[^6]

The Trust says Dwyer became embroiled in a dispute with the Board over the cement in the caisson. There was, the Trust says, "no indication that the cement used was actually flawed," but Dwyer had apparently filled the caisson without first submitting the cement for testing, and he was "penalized $300 for the infraction."[^10]

In January 1897 the Board authorized the district engineer to reserve $3,200 for the lens and illuminating apparatus.[^10] The Trust says the fog bell came from a Boston foundry that spring for $180.[^10] LighthouseFriends dates the lantern room's hoisting to early February 1897.[^2] The Board authorized acceptance of the lighthouse on 8 April, and the Board's report for the year lists "A fifth-order light" and "A bell struck by machinery," both dated 24 May 1897, at the station.[^10][^7]

The first keeper was William A. Lane. On 24 May 1897 he and First Assistant Harry Phillips lit the lamp for the first time, according to the Trust.[^10] LighthouseFriends says Lane had been transferred from the lighthouse tender *Myrtle*.[^2]

The sources do not agree on the upper tower. The National Register form describes "a poured concrete base encased in cast iron plates" and a "cast iron structure," with the keepers' quarters in the second stage and a ten-sided iron lantern above.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes a "cylindrical white-brick tower" on the caisson.[^2]

By October 1897 the Corps of Engineers recommended that the tower be painted black and white, probably so that the light would stand out from the nearby shoreline.[^10] In mid-November the Corps told the district engineer that the keepers had, in fact, painted it themselves.[^10]

## The keepers

The USLHS roster names thirteen head keepers between 1897 and 1954, when the entry for Joseph Bakken begins.[^9] Lane was head keeper from 1897 to 1901, and Phillips first assistant in 1897 and 1898.[^9] Otto A. Wilson's term as head keeper, from 1906 to 1931, is the longest closed term on the roster.[^9]

LighthouseFriends says the keepers lived inside the tower. The lowest level was a cellar for coal and equipment, and two cisterns below it caught rainwater from the gallery. A hand pump in the kitchen brought the water up.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says that, unlike some offshore lights, it was normally quite easy for keepers here to go ashore by boat when they wanted.[^2]

Electricity came in 1934. The Trust says an underwater cable from Fort Preble lit the station on 1 May 1934, and that a second cable let the keepers here monitor the Portland Breakwater light.[^10] LighthouseFriends gives the same year for the electrification.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A Portland dispatch dated 21 March 1876, printed in the *Daily Kennebec Journal* of 22 March, reported that the barkentine *Harriet S. Jackson*, from New York for Wiscasset, had gone ashore on Spring Point ledge at 12 o'clock "last night." The paper said she "lies in a dangerous position" and was "probably a total loss."[^3] LighthouseFriends calls her a 393-ton bark. It says her crew stayed aboard until dawn and then walked ashore along a plank, and it calls the wreck "the final straw" in the campaign for a light on the ledge.[^2]

A southeast storm struck Portland on 6 September 1896, while the cylinder was being placed in position. LighthouseFriends says it dumped five inches of rain on the city, and the Trust says it shattered two sections of the cylinder, causing more than $5,000 in damage. The Trust says the work was then significantly delayed while Dwyer awaited replacement plates from a mill in Pennsylvania.[^10][^2]

In 1898, when the Spanish-American War brought submarine mines to the harbor entrance, mariners were told the light would be discontinued until further notice. The Trust says Spring Point Ledge and Portland Head lights were dark for about three months, and both resumed on 26 July.[^2][^10]

On 20 August 1908 the schooner *Electric Flash* ran into the lighthouse in broad daylight and demolished one of its lifeboats. The owners offered a 14½-foot (4.4 m) peapod as a replacement, and the Board accepted it.[^10]

In the storm of 27 and 28 January 1933, the keepers were cut off. LighthouseFriends quotes a local newspaper, which quoted a keeper: they had been "marooned off here with a small supply of food," and "we were on the rocks for eatables."[^2]

## After the keepers

The Board's report for 1899 records a storm porch and minor repairs at the station.[^8]

The breakwater that joins the light to the shore was built by the Army Corps of Engineers, in granite of about 50,000 tons. The Trust gives 1951 for its completion.[^10] The National Register form says the breakwater, 900 feet (274 m) long, was completed in 1952.[^1] The USLHS record also gives 1951.[^9]

The Coast Guard automated the light in May 1960 and removed the fifth-order Fresnel lens.[^10][^9] The Trust says the lens's whereabouts were unknown for thirty years after its removal, until Trust members found it in a private museum in Michigan in 2009.[^10] LighthouseFriends also says the lens's location was learned in 2009, and dates a Justice Department lawsuit, filed in Michigan on the Coast Guard's behalf to recover it, to 2016.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard now has the lens, without giving a date.[^2]

Under the Maine Lights Program, the Coast Guard transferred the station.[^10] The Spring Point Ledge Light Trust was formed by the Portland Harbor Museum in early 1998. On 20 June 1998 the Trust accepted a ceremonial deed from the Coast Guard's commandant.[^10] LighthouseFriends says the Trust opened the tower for tours in 1999.[^2]

The Trust's first major restoration came in 2004, when corroded roof plates on the main gallery were replaced.[^10]

## Today

The Trust owns the lighthouse tower and some surrounding land, which is all underwater.[^10] The USLHS record lists the station as open to the public.[^9] The Coast Guard keeps the aid-to-navigation function under a memorandum of understanding with the Trust.[^10] The Trust describes the current light as a 36-watt incandescent lamp behind a 300 mm plastic Fresnel lens, while the USLHS record lists an LED optic.[^10][^9]

The National Register lists the light tower alone, not the later breakwater.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is open to the public during the summer, with the grounds open and the tower open only occasionally.[^2]
