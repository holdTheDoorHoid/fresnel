Cap-des-Rosiers Lighthouse stands on low shale cliffs at the head of the Gaspé Peninsula, where the Gulf of St. Lawrence gives way to the river.[^2][^5] Its tower, about 34 m (112 ft) with the lantern, is counted as the tallest lighthouse in Canada.[^2][^3][^5]

## The place

Heritage statements describe the light as a major coastal mark at the head of the Gaspé Peninsula, serving the shipping that entered the estuary of the St. Lawrence from the Gulf.[^2] Quebec's heritage register calls it the lighthouse that has known the most shipwrecks and names the *Chippewa*, the *Arabian*, the *Indian Chief*, the *Pékin*, the *Douchfour* and the *Carricks* among the ships lost off it.[^3] One of these was the *Carricks* of Whitehaven, an Irish emigrant ship that went down off the cape in 1847. A Gaspé tourism text puts the number aboard at 187, of whom 87 died, and says families in the village took in the 100 survivors.[^4]

The federal heritage review ties the tower to a wider push by steamship operators on the colonial government for better aids to navigation along the St. Lawrence.[^2]

## Building the light

The tower was designed by John Page, Chief Engineer of the Department of Public Works of the United Canadas, and built by the contractor Charles François-Xavier Baby in 1853-1858, according to the federal statement;[^1] Quebec's register dates the work 1854 to 1858 and credits the Canadian Board of Public Works.[^3] Lighthouse Friends adds that the contract was let in October 1853, that the stone could not be found nearby and had to come from Gaspé Basin, and that by the end of 1854 only the foundations had been dug to rock.[^5] By the end of 1856 the keepers' duplex, joined to the tower by a covered passage, was finished along with the tower masonry, and mechanics from the lens maker came in 1857 to fit the optic.[^5]

The sources do not agree on the money or on the lens. Lighthouse Friends gives the cost as nearly $115,000 and the lens makers as the Paris firm of L. Sautter.[^5] The Quebec register gives $75,000 for the construction, with a further $38,000 for an optic made by Barbier, Bénard et Turenne from Saint-Gobain glass.[^3] Both say the light went into service on 15 March 1858, and the register calls it the first first-order catadioptric light on the St. Lawrence.[^3][^5] The Historic Sites and Monuments Board says the original optical apparatus has been in use ever since, though the light source changed: a fixed white light until 1903, then incandescent petroleum vapour, a Canadian burner lamp from 1921, a hand-switched electric lamp from 1950, and partial automation from 1972 and full automation from 1981.[^1]

The tower's outer face is also described differently. The federal heritage statement speaks of limestone walls faced with white marble;[^2] the Quebec register describes stone facing blocks with a firebrick lining;[^3] Lighthouse Friends says the builders used two tiers of white fired brick to save on cutting stone, that damp got in anyway, that the tower was clapboarded by 1870, and that it was reclad in brick in 1954 and in marble in 1984.[^5] The Board's minutes record stucco coats in 1861, 1881 and 1897, new windows in 1884, and repairs in 1929-30, 1954, 1984 and 1993.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists the keepers from Eugène Trudeau (1856-67) and Auguste Trudeau (1867-86) through Jean B. Vien (1886-90) and Eugène Costin (1890-1915), then P. E. Theriault, J. Napoléon Côté, J. Ferguson (1927-31), Côté again (1931-35), Joseph Ferguson (1935-51) and Joseph-Narcisse Rioux (1951-70). Whether the two Fergusons were the same man the list does not say.[^5] Costin, spelled "Eugene" in the Department of Marine's reports, appears there as keeper of the light and fog alarm at Cape Rosier in the staff lists for the fiscal years ended June 1901 and June 1904.[^6][^7] In the report for the fiscal year ended June 1900, the keeper (not named in the passage) stated that, by actual testing, the whistle was much stronger than the horn, and the department chose the whistle for regular use, keeping the horn in reserve.[^8]

A nine-pounder gun reached the station in 1857, and Lighthouse Friends says keepers were required to fire it every hour in fog and snowstorms until 1881, when the interval became thirty minutes.[^5]

The gun gave way to a steam whistle that Lighthouse Friends says was moved here from Cap Gaspé in 1883.[^5] The Department of Marine's 1901 inspector found the whistle's sound blocked toward the sea by the smoke stack and had the boiler turned end for end.[^9] In the fiscal year ended June 1905 the department made large improvements to the fog-alarm building and replaced the whistle with a diaphone plant, to start the next season.[^10] The dwelling was thoroughly repaired in the fiscal year ended June 1909 for $2,403.17.[^11]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The cape's worst-remembered wreck, the *Carricks*, belongs to the years before the light: it sank in 1847, eleven years before the lamp was first lit.[^4][^5] The sources read for this story do not tie any later rescue to a named keeper. Lighthouse Friends does report that a submarine was seen from the lighthouse on 15 September 1942 and that Keeper Joseph Ferguson sent word to the authorities.[^5]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the original dwelling was demolished in 1956 and two new houses built;[^5] the Quebec register says the keeper's house came down in 1981 and that the fog-signal shed and a hangar date from 1958.[^3] These may refer to different houses; the sources do not say. The light was fully automated in 1981.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is a national historic site (designated 1974) and a classified federal heritage building (1994).[^5] The light is still in service and automated.[^3] A non-profit committee runs the site as a summer attraction, and Forillon National Park is close by.[^3][^5] For the 2026 season its website lists opening from 29 June to 28 August, with a site-access fee and guided tours that climb the tower, limited to five people.[^13] Radio-Canada reported, in an article undated in the copy read, that the limit came from Fisheries and Oceans Canada, the department responsible for the lighthouse, because the floors and stairs were designed for one or two people, the keeper and an assistant.[^12]
