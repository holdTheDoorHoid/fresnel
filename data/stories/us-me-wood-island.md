Wood Island Light stands on a 35-acre island two miles off the mouth of the Saco River, where the vessels of Saco and Biddeford turned in from the open Atlantic. Lit in 1808 as a shingled wooden tower, it has been rebuilt more than once; most accounts date the granite tower seen today to 1858, though they disagree on how much of it was new. It is remembered for a fog-bell-ringing dog named Sailor, for a night-time rescue in 1865, and for one grim day in 1896.[^1][^2][^4]

## The place

The Saco River was a working waterway long before the light. Roger Spencer was granted the right to build a sawmill there in 1653, and later grain and textile mills along the river helped support Biddeford and Saco; D'Entremont adds that fish and lumber were shipped out as well.[^2][^4] The National Register nomination singles out the "heavy maritime traffic in and around the commercial centers of Saco and Biddeford" as the reason for a light on this small, shallow island at the river's mouth.[^1] Until Whaleback Light went up in 1829, Wood Island was the southernmost of the principal lights in Maine.[^1]

Congress authorized a lighthouse "on Wood Island, or on Fletcher's Neck" by an act of 8 March 1806, and the government bought eight acres of the island.[^2] The price paid to Pendleton Fletcher and others is given as $140 in one account and $160 in another.[^2][^4] D'Entremont notes that Fletcher's Neck, the point beside Biddeford Pool, was itself regarded as a hazard to navigation.[^4]

## Building the light

The first station was finished by 1 September 1807 at a cost of $4,750, according to D'Entremont; the builders were Benjamin Beal and Duncan Thaxter, whom he describes as subcontractors to Winslow Lewis.[^4] It was a 45-foot (14 m) octagonal wooden tower sheathed in shingles, with a one-story dwelling beside it.[^2][^4] For reasons no source here explains, it was not lit until 1808.[^4] It used hanging lamps and a rotating "eclipser" that made the light appear to flash.[^5]

The wooden tower did not last. In 1835 the collector of customs John Chandler called it rotten and said it "rocks", so that it could not be shored up.[^2] The sources then disagree about what followed. Lighthouse Friends and D'Entremont say $5,000 was appropriated in 1838 and a new conical rubblestone tower was built in 1839 with a new dwelling (rubblestone in Lighthouse Friends, granite in D'Entremont), and that another $5,000 was appropriated in 1854 for a further rebuilding.[^2][^4] The Friends of Wood Island Lighthouse describe plans from 1835 for a granite tower and say the tower was "reconstructed" in 1857-58 to take a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^5] D'Entremont says the 1858 Light-House Board report announced the work complete, but adds that the records are unclear and that the tower was probably extensively renovated rather than wholly rebuilt.[^4] The US Lighthouse Society archive lists the present tower as established in 1858, while the National Register nomination dates the tower to 1808 and calls it the original, a claim the other accounts contradict.[^3][^1] The surviving tower is 47 feet (14 m) from base to the focal plane of the lens.[^1]

Quality was a recurring complaint. John Adams, who became keeper in 1841 at $350 a year, gave the engineer I. W. P. Lewis a statement that his stone dwelling leaked at the windows and chimneys, had no floor in the cellar, and that the plaster had fallen in places; he wrote that "My dwelling-house is built of rough stone, with brick gables."[^2] An 1850 inspection under Keeper Stephen Bachelder found the tower and dwelling still leaky, according to D'Entremont.[^4] In 1856 the light was changed from white to red so that it could not be mistaken for the lights to the north at Cape Elizabeth.[^4] The Light-House Board's 1899 report still lists Wood Island as a fourth-order light, fixed red varied by a red flash every minute.[^8]

The present wood-frame keeper's house dates from the 1850s.[^4] The National Register nomination judges from photographs that it began as a one-and-a-half-story dwelling and was raised, given a porch and had its ell heightened, probably in the 1890s; the Friends of Wood Island Lighthouse and Lighthouse Friends date the remodelling to 1906, when the roof was raised and dormers added to make the Dutch Colonial house seen today.[^1][^5][^2] The stone oil house was built in 1903.[^1]

## The keepers

Benjamin Cole of Saco was the first keeper, at $225 a year, and D'Entremont reports that he had commanded a privateer in the Revolution; he stayed about a year.[^2][^4] Philip Goldthwaite then lived on the island with his family for twenty-three years until his death in 1832, and his son Tristram kept the light until the family could move their cattle and belongings off the island in 1833.[^2] The early keepers were farmers as much as lamp-tenders: Abraham Norwood built a barn, fence and walls, and when John Adams arrived in 1841 Norwood demanded $200 for the barn and charged him $14 a ton for the hay he had cut after Adams's appointment.[^2][^5]

Ebenezer ("Eben") Emerson, keeper from 1861 to 1865, was a former sailor and, in later life, known as "Uncle Eben" and a deputy marshal in Biddeford.[^3][^4] His rescue of 17 March 1865 is described below.

Thomas H. Orcutt, a former sea captain from Sedgwick, Maine, who had earlier kept Saddleback Ledge Light, was keeper from 1886 until his death in 1905.[^3][^4][^5] His dog Sailor, a mostly black mongrel taken to the island as a two-month-old puppy, learned to answer passing steamers' salutes by taking the bell cord in his teeth. In 1894 the Lewiston Journal reported, as quoted by D'Entremont: "It is customary for passing steamers to salute the light and the keeper returns it by ringing the bell."[^4] Sailor died in Orcutt's arms a few months before the keeper did, and Orcutt's son-in-law, Levi Jeffers, filled in until a successor came.[^4][^5] Charles A. Burke followed in 1905 and told mainland residents that the island rocked during the October 1906 earthquake shocks, with no damage done.[^3][^4]

Earle E. Benson, a First World War veteran, became keeper in 1934, the last of the Lighthouse Service keepers. He joined the Coast Guard when it took over in 1939 and, by D'Entremont's account, stayed until 1952 (the USLHS archive ends his term in 1951); with his wife Alice he had television installed after the station was electrified around 1950 and followed the World Series on it.[^4][^2][^3] Coast Guard crews followed him until the light was automated in 1986. D'Entremont records that a keeper in the 1970s was required to switch on the fog signal when visibility fell below 2 1/2 miles and to watch 20 navigational buoys near the island.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

At about one in the morning of 17 March 1865, Keeper Emerson got up to trim his lamp and, the newspaper says, thought he heard a human voice amid the roar of the breakers. The Union and Journal of Biddeford, 31 March 1865, reported that he and a neighbour he had roused pulled through the fog to the brig Edith Ann (the Friends call it the Edyth Ann) of Nova Scotia, aground on Old Washburn Ledge; after many vain attempts Emerson got aboard through breakers it put at nearly forty feet high, and saw the crew placed in the one boat still hanging from its davits, with orders to cut loose when the order came; as a high sea rose the order was given.[^7][^5] The crew landed safe and the brig went to pieces. Just before leaving the cabin he pocketed two guinea pigs.[^7] The paper judged that Emerson was "entitled to great credit" for his bravery and presence of mind.[^7] Later accounts say he was given a pair of binoculars by the British (the Friends) or Canadian (D'Entremont) government; they also give the date as 16 March, while the newspaper says the 17th.[^4][^5][^7]

On 1 June 1896 a killing took place on the island. A Portland paper's Biddeford dispatch dated 1 June reported that Howard Hobbs, a young fisherman who had been drinking, shot and killed Frederick W. Milliken, a fisherman, game warden and special policeman, and then took his own life.[^6] D'Entremont dates the shooting 2 June and says Hobbs went to the keeper's house, where Orcutt advised him to give himself up.[^4] Lighthouse Friends gives 1 June and says Hobbs was behind on his rent.[^2] The episode is the main source of the island's later reputation as haunted, a belief D'Entremont and Lighthouse Friends both report.[^2][^4]

## After the keepers

The fog bell had been installed in 1873 in a pyramidal tower and struck by weight-driven machinery; D'Entremont gives the bell as 1,315 pounds, alternating single and double blows every 25 seconds, and a 1,200-pound replacement in 1890.[^4][^2][^5] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1912 list of changes includes Wood Island among the lights whose illuminant was changed to incandescent oil vapor.[^9] The station was electrified around 1950, and in the late 1960s or 1972 the lantern room was replaced by a rotating airport beacon, which locals called the "headless" tower.[^2][^5][^4][^1] The dates differ: the Friends and Lighthouse Friends say the late 1960s, while D'Entremont and the National Register say 1972. In 1986 a new aluminium lantern, fabricated at the Coast Guard's Industrial Support Detachment in South Portland, was installed by helicopter and the light was automated; the last keepers left that year.[^2][^5] In 1970, 28 of the island's 35 acres were deeded to the Maine Audubon Society.[^4]

The empty buildings decayed until Lighthouse Digest placed the station on its "doomsday list".[^2] The Friends of Wood Island Lighthouse, a chapter of the American Lighthouse Foundation, formed in 2003 and raised the flag again on 19 September 2004.[^2][^4][^5] New handrails went into the tower in 2008, masonry restoration of the tower followed in 2009-10, and the keeper's house exterior was restored in 2011, with its porch returned to the 1906 design.[^4] A private gift of $218,000 in 2015 paid for a full restoration of the dwelling.[^2] The station was nominated to the National Register of Historic Places in 1987.[^1]

## Today

Wood Island Light is an active aid to navigation owned by the Coast Guard and licensed to the Friends of Wood Island Lighthouse.[^3][^10] The island lies offshore from Biddeford Pool and can be reached only by boat; the Friends run tours in July and August.[^10] The USLHS archive lists the station as not open to the public, while Lighthouse Friends says the grounds, dwelling and tower are open in season, so visitors should go with the Friends' tours.[^3][^2] Lighthouse Friends reports that resident keepers occupy the dwelling from May to December.[^2] Most of the rest of the island is managed by Maine Audubon and part of it is private, and the light can be seen from a shoreline trail at Biddeford Pool.[^4]
