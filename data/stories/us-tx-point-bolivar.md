Bolivar Point Light stands on the north side of the entrance to Galveston Bay, at the western end of the Bolivar Peninsula in Galveston County, Texas.[^1][^2] Its black-and-white cast-iron tower, first lit on 19 November 1872, is the second iron tower built on the point. The light went out in 1933. The tower is privately owned and closed to visitors.[^2][^3][^4]

## The place

The Handbook of Texas says the federal government built a lighthouse on the point in 1852 to prevent shipwrecks.[^3] The idea was older. On 3 February 1845 the Republic of Texas set aside $7,000 for a light on the eastern end of Galveston Island.[^2] After Texas joined the Union, the federal government took over navigational aids in Texas, and Congress set aside $15,000 in 1847 for lights at Galveston and at Matagorda Bay.[^2] The site moved from Fort Point, on Galveston Island, to Bolivar Point on the far side of the entrance. Protracted negotiations with the landowner, and delays by the Texas government in giving up jurisdiction over the site, postponed work on the light until the fall of 1851.[^2] A lightship marked the Galveston bar in the meantime.[^2]

## Building the light

The iron sections for the 65-foot (20 m) tower were cast in Baltimore by Murray and Hazlehurst.[^2] The keeper's dwelling was finished in April 1852. Work on the tower, which was painted red, went on until October.[^2] By Christmas Day eighteen lamps with 21-inch reflectors were in the tower.[^2] Aaron Burns, the first keeper, earned $600 a year.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says the district engineer asked for a brighter light and more iron sections in the light's first year, and that harbor pilots also complained. The Lighthouse Board added a third-order Fresnel lens and another 24 feet (7 m) of tower sections, and finished the changes by the summer of 1858.[^2]

Sources disagree about what happened to this first tower in the Civil War. The Handbook of Texas says the Confederates destroyed the original lighthouse so that it would not help the enemy.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the tower was dismantled completely. No piece of it has ever been found, so it assumes the iron went into armor plating or was melted into munitions.[^2] The Service's own 1937 history says that during the war many of the Gulf lights were extinguished and their property badly damaged, and that it was not until about 1870 that all the lights had been restored.[^13]

A temporary wooden tower, 34 feet (10 m) high, went up near the old site.[^2] A fourth-order Fresnel lens was activated on 5 August 1865.[^2] On 15 July 1870 Congress appropriated $40,000 for a second iron tower. The Board patterned the new station on the Pass a l'Outre light in Louisiana.[^2] Bailey and Debevoise of New York City supplied the iron tower for $25,850, and William F. Crow and Company of Galveston built the dwelling.[^2]

The tower's third-order lens was first lit on 19 November 1872.[^2] The tower was painted with black and white horizontal bands.[^2] Mariners complained that the light was less bright than the 1852 light, and the district engineer decided the lens was faulty. A second-order lens was installed in 1882.[^2]

## The keepers

The keeper lists, drawn from the National Archives keeper registers and printed by the US Lighthouse Society, run from 1852 to 1933.[^5] They have 69 entries, for principal keepers, first and second assistants and one laborer.[^5] Aaron Burns, the first principal keeper, is listed for 1852 to 1853 and again for 1853 to 1856, with Alexander Moore between the two stints.[^5] George Bryan, C. Atkins and Frederick Schrier followed before the war.[^5] Lewis C. Dubree was principal keeper from 1865 to 1888.[^5]

The roster lists Hannah Dubree as first assistant from 1874 to 1883, the same years Lewis Dubree was principal keeper.[^5] It does not say how the two were related, and this story does not assume that they were.[^5]

Horace W. Crockett was principal keeper from 1888 to 1894. In 1894 his assistant, Edson C. Brace, accused him of drunkenness and of threatening violence.[^2] Crockett charged Brace with failing to keep a proper light and with exposing himself to a passing train.[^2] The district inspector came, Brace resigned, and Crockett retired to run a store he owned in Port Bolivar.[^2]

Harry C. Claiborne, whom LighthouseFriends calls the best keeper in the district, began at Bolivar Point on 15 December 1894, transferring from the twin light at Pass a l'Outre. He kept the light until 1918.[^2][^5] In 1895 the Board sent keepers to staff a light-house exhibit at the Atlanta exposition, detailed on the recommendation of the district inspectors.[^6] The Board's report lists Claiborne on that detail from 14 October to 9 November 1895.[^6] LighthouseFriends names the exposition as the Cotton States and International Exposition.[^2]

LighthouseFriends quotes his wife, Virginia Claiborne, on life at the station: "Life at the lighthouse was very lonely and friendless."[^2] The roster also lists a Mrs. J.B. Caliborne as first assistant in 1906, spelled that way by the archive, and a J. B. Claiborne as laborer in 1901 and as first assistant in 1905. The sources do not say how they were related to Harry Claiborne.[^5]

James P. Brooks was an assistant from 1914. He became principal keeper in 1918 and kept the light until 1930.[^2][^5] LighthouseFriends says he received the Service's efficiency flag for the best-kept station in the district in 1918, 1920 and 1922.[^2] Louis Buras, Sr., was the last principal keeper, from 1931 to 1933.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Galveston hurricane of 8 September 1900 flooded the island, and the tower became a refuge.[^2] Putnam quotes the inspector's report that, through the keeper's efforts, "the lives of one hundred and twenty-five people were saved."[^11] Putnam's account says the keeper harbored and fed a large number of the refugees for a considerable time, and used up his family's own provisions in doing so.[^11] LighthouseFriends says that over 120 people sought shelter in the iron tower, sitting in pairs on the spiral stair, and that the water caught in a bucket outside was salty.[^2] The Foundation counts 125 refugees.[^4]

The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1901 says the bids for repairing the station by contract were rejected as excessive.[^7]

On 16 and 17 August 1915 another hurricane struck.[^8][^9] The Service's 1915 bulletin reprints the Galveston Daily News account of the night. The paper said that "Sixty people, a large part of whom" were women and children, and that they spent the night on the tower's stairs.[^9] Putnam gives the number as fifty,[^11] LighthouseFriends as sixty,[^2] and the Foundation as 61.[^4]

According to the newspaper, the mechanism that turned the lantern was put out of commission at 9.15 p.m. by the swaying of the tower. Assistant keeper James P. Brooks turned the big lamp by hand until about ten o'clock.[^9] By then the water was neck-deep over the tower floor, and the wind had dashed open the big iron door at the base. Brooks, armed with a rope, tried to make the door fast. He was badly battered by the wind and waves before it was done.[^9][^2]

The Service's bulletin says the keeper's dwelling was washed off its foundation, and that everything about the station was completely destroyed except the tower and that house.[^8] The newspaper reported that all structures save the keeper's residence were swept away.[^9] According to LighthouseFriends, the oil ran out and the light went dark for two nights before a new shipment reached the station.[^2][^9] The newspaper called it the first time the light had failed to burn since it was built.[^9]

Hurricane Ike in 2008 tore the finial off the top of the tower, wiped out the brick kerosene house and other buildings on the grounds, and left the water over eight feet (2.4 m) deep, according to the Foundation.[^4]

The Bulletin's index lists commendations for Harry C. Claiborne (spelled "Clairborne" in the index), James P. Brooks and Christopher T. Morris on page 182, the page where the index also places the storm account (pages 182-183).[^10]

On 15 November 1917 the Fort San Jacinto guns, firing practice shells in a dense fog, hit the lighthouse during two hours of target practice.[^2] LighthouseFriends says one projectile tore a hole through the tower about 25 feet (8 m) above the base, and another landed just outside the assistant keeper's gate.[^2] The Handbook of Texas says the shelling shook up the keeper and his family.[^3] The Foundation says nobody was injured.[^4]

In 1919 the Service's awards list named James P. Brooks, Philip E. Brooks and William H. Smith for caring for residents who had taken refuge in the station dwellings. The entry does not name the storm.[^12]

## After the keepers

In 1894 the Board proposed changing the light from a second-order fixed white light to a five-second flash, so that it would form a range with a new light at the entrance to the harbor.[^1] The Board said the light was "already some distance inland," and that its value "as an offshore light is, in consequence, greatly impaired."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the signature was changed in 1907 to a flashing third-order light.[^2]

The Commissioner's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1918 lists two frame dwellings on high iron columns, set on pile foundations, with an oil house, outhouses and fences, as completed work.[^14]

In 1930 the Lighthouse Commissioner recommended discontinuing the light after the Galveston jetty light's candlepower was increased.[^2] Public protest delayed the decision, but the light was put out on 29 May 1933.[^2][^4] The Handbook of Texas says the South Jetty Light replaced it, and that plans to make the tower and grounds into a public park failed.[^3] The War Department received the property in 1935.[^2]

In 1947 the government sold the property as surplus.[^2][^3] The sources do not agree on the buyer. LighthouseFriends says rancher Elmer V. Boyt bought it for $5,500.[^2] The Handbook of Texas says the sale went to the E. W. Boyt interests, which closed the lighthouse to the public.[^3] The Foundation says the property was auctioned and has since been owned by descendants of E. V. Boyt and his sister, Ila Maxwell.[^4] This story does not choose between them.

The lighthouse appeared in the 1970 television drama *My Sweet Charlie*.[^2][^3] In 1977 the Texas Historical Commission registered it as a historic landmark.[^3] Two descendants of the first private owners formed the Bolivar Point Lighthouse Foundation in 2015. A 2018 condition assessment put the cost of restoration at $2.5 million.[^2]

## Today

The tower is privately owned, and its grounds and tower are closed to the public.[^2] The Foundation says the tower now needs restoration.[^4] On 15 May 2023 a crane lifted the lantern room off the tower, and a team from Architexas cataloged the removed material for reuse and replication.[^2] A ribbon cutting in late 2024 marked a new entry door and a repainted black-and-white daymark.[^2]

The lighthouse stands on the north side of Highway 87, about a mile from the Galveston ferry landing at Port Bolivar.[^2] The ferry crosses the mouth of Galveston Bay.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the tower's third-order Fresnel lens can be seen at the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History in Washington.[^2]
