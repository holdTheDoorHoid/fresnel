Quatsino Light stands on the south-east end of Kains Island, just inside the northern entrance to Quatsino Sound, on the west coast of northern Vancouver Island.[^1][^7] A light has shone from the island since 1907, and keepers have lived there since 1910.[^1][^2]

## The place

In 1904 the British Columbia Board of Trade asked the Department of Marine for a light at the entrance to the sound. The mills and mines on its shores needed one.[^1] The government set the island aside as a lighthouse site in 1905.[^1] The Department's report published in 1908 describes the new light as a guide "to vessels entering Quatsino sound."[^2]

The island has had two names. LighthouseFriends says it was called Entrance Island until about 1898, when it was renamed to avoid confusion with the Entrance Island off Nanaimo.[^1] The government's reports went on calling it Entrance Island in 1907 and again in the report for 1923-24, long after LighthouseFriends dates the change.[^2][^6]

## Building the light

The first light was a temporary one, set on the south-east end of the island in 1907. The report published in 1908 calls it a "31-day Wigham light" and describes it as "fixed white dioptric of the seventh order", elevated 27 m (90 ft) above high water and visible fifteen miles to the southward, from "a small inclosed wooden tower."[^2] LighthouseFriends uses the same name.[^1] The report published in 1911 uses it for the lamp that replaced it: "The thirty-one day Wigham lamp has been discontinued, and replaced by a dioptric 5th order fixed white light, shown from a new lighthouse."[^4]

Work on the permanent light began in 1910, using day labour under H. C. Killeen. Local men were hired to carry building materials to the island. A square, two-storey wooden dwelling with an octagonal iron lantern on its roof was finished that December.[^1]

The fog alarm came later. The report for the fiscal year 1923-24 says the new fog alarm at Entrance Island "was put in operation on December 10, 1923."[^6] LighthouseFriends dates the fog alarm to 1924, when it was erected at the station and the keeper's pay was raised.[^1] The two sources give different years, and the story follows the Department's date.

## The keepers

The first keeper was G. H. Jackson, who was paid $300 a year to keep an eye on the temporary light.[^1] The Department's statement of lightkeepers, in the report published in 1909, gives "Quatsino Light" with Jackson as its keeper.[^3]

Nels C. Nelson became the island's first resident keeper in 1910, and he lived there for about five years. The 1913 report lists him at Quatsino.[^1][^5]

James Sadler followed, with a young family. In 1918 mail went for six months without reaching the island. Later that year Sadler resigned, after his wife's health broke down.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he told his story to the Victoria *Colonist*, which printed it.[^1] In 1921 the superintendent of lights, Gordon Halkett, reported on the next keeper, James Quinn, a seventy-year-old former sailor. Halkett reported, "I believe it is only a matter of time before he will be unable to look after himself."[^1]

Alfred Dickenson, who was keeper from 1922 to 1925, and his wife first reported that the island was almost a paradise. By the spring of 1925 he had asked for leave, because his wife had not seen another woman in nearly a year.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 19 December 1913 a visitor, Walter Peterson, drowned offshore when his skiff capsized. His grave is marked by a stone monument that replaced an earlier wooden cross.[^1]

On 23 October 1929 the keeper, Sydney Warren, was rowing out to meet the mail boat when a wave swamped his skiff and threw him into the water. The weight of his wet clothes pulled him down. His daughter swam out and brought him to the surface, and the mail boat took him up, but efforts to revive him ashore failed.[^1]

## Today

Quatsino is a staffed lightstation of the Canadian Coast Guard, the keepers' home and workplace.[^1][^8] The 2010 appendix to the Senate's report lists it as a remote staffed station with two permanent positions.[^8] Its present light flashes white every five seconds, 28.4 m (93 ft) above the water and visible 18 nautical miles, from a white cylindrical tower.[^7] A white circular tower replaced the wooden lighthouse in 1977.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the station can be reached by kayak or boat from Winter Harbour, and it says the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] The station is a Coast Guard lightstation and the keepers' home, so visitors should not land there uninvited.[^1]
