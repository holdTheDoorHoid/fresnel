Buck Island Light, on an island about two miles south-west of St. Thomas (one directory gives three miles south), marks the approach to Charlotte Amalie, the harbor of St. Thomas.[^2][^1][^6] A tower was put up there under the Danish government.[^1][^3] Sources disagree on whether that happened in 1913 or 1916, and the light now shows from a newer steel tower beside the old one, which the Coast Guard rebuilt in 2023.[^3][^2][^9]

## The place

The Danish West Indies were a Danish colony from 1754. The United States bought the islands from Denmark in 1917.[^1] Buck Island lies south of St. Thomas, and its high point carries the light.[^1] The 1927 Lighthouse Service inventory calls the light a "leading light to Charlotte Amalie" and gives the town as five miles away by boat.[^6]

The island has been a wildlife refuge since 1969, and the directory lists the Fish and Wildlife Service as owner of the historic light.[^1]

## Building the light

Three later records give 1913. Kebby Kelley of the Coast Guard's Environmental Management Division wrote in a 2003 note, printed on Russ Rowlett's photo page, that the light was built that year in Danish Colonial style, as a square steel tower about 25 feet high.[^2] Rowlett's directory also gives 1913, and says the Danish government built the light shortly before the islands became a U.S. territory in 1917.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record also gives 1913.[^7]

A government notice printed in the *St. Croix Avis* in June 1916 says the light was erected that year. The notice is dated St. Thomas, 8 June 1916, and the paper printed the Danish text with an English translation. It says the light would be lit on 1 August 1916.[^3] It describes a square white tower 30 feet (9 m) high, with three flashes every twenty seconds, a flame about 125 feet (38 m) above the sea, a lens of the sixth class, and a visibility of about 17.5 nautical miles.[^3] That tower height is taller than the 25 feet (about 8 m) that Kelley and Rowlett give for the 1913 tower, and the sources do not explain the difference.[^3][^2][^1]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses listed "Buck Island, Virgin Islands" among the lights established in the fiscal year 1918.[^4] By 1922 the light had been changed from oil to acetylene, at a cost of $1,000.[^5] The 1927 inventory describes an acetylene flame fed from gas cylinders, each serving the light for about 60 days, and a reinforced concrete dwelling 375 feet south-west of the tower. It gives "August 1, 1916" as the date the station was first built or established.[^6]

The sources do not reconcile the 1913 and 1916 dates.

## The keepers

When the 1916 notice was printed, the light was unattended: "The Lighthouse is unguarded."[^3] The 1927 inventory lists one keeper upstairs in the dwelling and one laborer in the basement.[^6] No keeper is named in the sources for this station, and the Society's roster lists none.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources name one storm. Hurricane Irma, a Category 4 hurricane, made landfall on St. Thomas in 2017. The Virgin Islands Free Press reports that the island's "old tower" was toppled on its side during the storm. The article does not say which tower that was.[^8] The sources name no wreck or rescue tied to the station.

## After the keepers

The Harbor Board of St. Thomas conveyed the lighthouse and its improvements to the United States on 29 July 1919, according to Kelley's note.[^2] The light stayed in use until the mid-1990s, when a modern steel tower went up about 50 feet (15 m) north-west of it. The old light was then deactivated.[^2] In 2003 the Coast Guard declared the station excess property, the General Services Administration was arranging its transfer to the Fish and Wildlife Service, and the Coast Guard was preparing a nomination to the National Register of Historic Places.[^2]

## Today

The historic light's tower is inactive, and the directory describes the site and tower as closed.[^1] The active light is a newer steel skeletal tower, about 40 feet (12 m) high, placed adjacent to the historic light. It has a focal plane of 139 feet (42 m) and a white flash every four seconds.[^1]

In April 2023 a Coast Guard crew righted the 20,000-pound concrete foundation that had held the old tower, according to the Free Press. The 3,500-pound replacement tower was delivered to the top of the island by helicopter sling load and assembled there.[^8] A Coast Guard release dated 5 May 2023 said the rebuilt light was fully operational.[^9]

The island is closed to the public as a bird sanctuary, and the Society's record gives "Open to Public: No".[^1][^7] Visitors should not try to land on the island.
