Annandale's range lights guided vessels from the Northumberland Strait into the river at Annandale, Prince Edward Island, which the Department's reports call the Grand River and Parks Canada the Boughton River. The rear tower, a slender wooden frame 19.8 m (65 ft) tall, is the tallest range light on the Island.[^1][^9]

## The place

The Department of Marine and Fisheries set up the Annandale Range Lights in 1898 to bring mariners into the river and to the public wharf at the village. According to LighthouseFriends, the land cost $80 and was bought from Alfred Robertson and from George and Edwin McFarlane; the lights began to burn on 26 August 1898, with Robertson as first keeper.[^2] Parks Canada's statement of significance puts the lights in a wider setting: at the turn of the twentieth century the Island lacked a land transport route, goods such as potatoes left by water, and the lighthouse supported local shipbuilding, fish processing and fishing.[^1]

## Building the light

The front light stood in the village, 67 m (220 ft) north of the shore and 95 m (312 ft) northeast of the public wharf.[^2][^4] The 1898 rear light stood on the north shore of the river, about 1,323 m (4,340 ft) behind it: an open-framed tower 17 m (56 ft) tall, its channel-facing side slatted, showing a fixed white light.[^2] A red light on a mast at the northwest corner of the wharf marked the turning point in the channel.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that the work was supervised by Milton Walsh of the Department of Marine and Fisheries and cost $2,705.55.[^2]

The first rear tower did not last. The Department's report printed in 1901 says it "was blown down by the gale of October 11, 1900," and that on 20 November the lights were all discontinued for the rest of the season "to prevent confusion."[^3] The report printed the next year describes the replacement: a new open-framed wooden tower with sloping sides and an enclosed lantern, 29 m (96 ft) closer to the front light on the same range line, on the east side of Juniper Point. It stood 19.8 m (65 ft) from base to vane, carried a fixed white catoptric light 23.8 m (78 ft) above high water, and was expected to be visible 14 miles on the range line. The side facing the channel was slatted.[^4] The wharf mast light was discontinued because buildings on the wharf would have blocked the new range.[^4]

LighthouseFriends adds that Walsh returned in 1905 to enclose the rear tower, giving it the shingled appearance it has today, and that the front light was moved to a new site in 1917.[^2] The departmental report printed in 1918 lists the move of the Annandale front light among the year's works, under a contract with J. M. Morrow for $70.[^5] LighthouseFriends does not say when the original front tower was replaced by the present pyramidal one.[^2]

## The keepers

Alfred Robertson, who had sold land to the Department, kept the lights from 1898; the Department's table of lightkeepers printed in 1911 shows him at Annandale on a salary of $110 a year, appointed in 1898.[^6] LighthouseFriends and the Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society both list James Norton as keeper from 1912 to 1922.[^2][^7] The departmental table printed in 1914 shows a J. Norton at the Annandale range with what appears, by column position in the OCR, to be an appointment date of 12 July 1912.[^8] LighthouseFriends then lists an Alfred Robertson again from 1922 to 1936 (the sources do not say whether he was the same man as the first keeper) and Andrew Banks from 1936 to 1964; the Society's list ends with Robertson's return and no end date.[^2][^7]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends reports that the range lights were electrified in June 1960, when Chester Banks was appointed caretaker at $100 a year, saving the cost of two keepers.[^2] The back range was sheathed and reshingled in 1988. In 2015 the rear light was discontinued and the front light became a sector light, showing white over an arc of one degree between a red and a green sector.[^2] Sources differ on whether the rear light still shows: LighthouseFriends says it was discontinued in 2015, while the Lighthouse Society's page lists it as active.[^2][^7]

In February 2020 the government moved the rear tower roughly 30 m inland, away from an eroding bank, and it was repaired, reshingled and repainted that year; the Norton family formed the non-profit Annandale Lights Inc. to preserve it.[^2] Annandale Lighthouse Inc. says the tower was moved 100 feet (30 m) from the shore in mid-winter.[^9] In October 2020 the Minister of Environment and Climate Change announced its designation as a heritage lighthouse under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act.[^2]

## Today

The rear tower is surrounded by private property. Annandale Lighthouse Inc. says it is often occupied by guests, that the tower now holds the Lighthouse Keeper's Inn, and asks visitors not to approach it except on public access days, which it announces each year.[^9] A rose garden planted for Queen Elizabeth II's Platinum Jubilee was unveiled there on 11 August 2022.[^9] Parks Canada notes that the tower is visible from all vantage points in the village.[^1]
