Sand Key Light is an iron skeleton tower on a reef about seven miles southwest of Key West, Florida (Putnam's figure; LighthouseFriends puts Key West eight miles away), built in 1853 to replace a brick tower that a hurricane erased seven years earlier. The island it was built on is a heap of sand that comes and goes with the storms, and the station's story is largely a record of that sand disappearing.[^2][^3]

## The place

Sand Key lies beside a channel into Key West, on a reef where sand piles up into a small island in calm years. After the United States took Florida in 1821, a wooden daymark was set there to warn ships of a hazard that was almost hidden.[^2] Congress allocated $16,000 on 18 May 1826 for a lighthouse on the key.[^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends dates the first light to 15 April 1827, on a brick tower of about 70 feet.[^2] Putnam's 1917 history gives the brick tower as 60 feet high and says it was built in 1826, and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1937 also puts the first tower in 1826.[^3][^4] The surviving record of the keepers' registers lists a station established in 1827.[^1]

A hurricane on 11 October 1846 swept the first tower away, along with the island. Putnam says the buildings were undermined and fell, "burying the inmates"; LighthouseFriends says waves were seen rolling over the reef the next morning with no trace of the tower.[^2][^3] The keeper, Joshua Appleby, and five visitors were on the key.[^2] A 140-ton vessel, the *Honey*, was bought and sent as a lightship to mark the reef meanwhile.[^2]

Congress appropriated $20,000 on 3 March 1847 for a replacement, and the new Lighthouse Board chose a screw-pile iron design by Isaiah W. P. Lewis.[^2] Work stopped when the money ran out. Congress voted a further $44,127.81 on 31 August 1852, and George Meade, who had finished Carysfort Reef Light that March, took over the assembly, which began again on 22 January 1853.[^2] Putnam calls him Lieutenant G. G. Meade, the engineer officer in charge.[^3] Putnam gives the tower as 121 feet high with a base 50 feet square, on 17 piles bored 14 feet into the reef; LighthouseFriends gives 132 feet and says the piles went ten feet down.[^2][^3] The Light-House Board's later description puts the lantern 109 feet above sea level, and gives the cost to establish the station as $126,335; Johnson's 1889 handbook says the structure cost $101,520.[^5][^6] Putnam says it was lighted in July 1853, and LighthouseFriends says 20 July, with a first-order Fresnel lens made in Paris.[^2][^3]

## The keepers

The first keeper named is John R. Flaherty (1827 to 1830). His widow Rebecca Flaherty then kept the light until about 1835.[^1][^2] A Key West lawyer, William Randolph Hackley, visited in June 1831 and wrote that the key was only 150 to 200 yards long, and that Mrs. Flaherty, her sister and a hired man were "the only inhabitants of the key", with sometimes only the two women.[^2] Rebecca married Captain Frederick Neill in November 1834, and he succeeded her as keeper.[^2]

Joshua Appleby (1837 to 1846) had been a wrecker, a salvager of wrecked ships, before he took the light.[^1][^2] His daughter, her husband and their young son, and a friend with her adopted daughter, were visiting him that October.[^2]

For the iron tower, the archive's register lists a head keeper and two assistants for the station from 1853.[^1] Later stints were long: Charles G. Johnson was head keeper from 1899 to 1922 and Hezekiah A. Pierce was an assistant (1913 to 1922) before returning as head keeper (1928 to 1932).[^1] In the Board's mid-1890s description, the three keepers were paid $800, $480 and $475 a year.[^5]

Terns nested on the key in the quiet years, and keepers took their eggs to friends in Key West. Charles G. Johnson reported to the ornithologist William Dutcher in 1902 that nine to twelve thousand birds had once nested there, but that so many eggs had been taken that only a few hundred young hatched; he then signed up as a bird warden and drove off anyone who disturbed the nests.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The iron tower survived storms that stripped the island. Putnam says a gale in 1856 washed the island away with everything on it while the water rose six feet around the tower, and that an October 1865 hurricane again took everything but the lighthouse.[^3] LighthouseFriends adds the hurricanes of October 1870 and one in 1875, and says the dwelling inside the tower was replaced after a $20,000 appropriation in 1874.[^2] During the hurricane of 20 October 1926, the Lighthouse Service reported that Sand Key and American Shoal had "but little damage".[^7]

The light itself was altered in step with its surroundings. The Board fitted red sectors in 1891, changing the characteristic on 30 September 1891, and added two more on 30 April 1893 to mark dangerous water.[^8][^9] By 1931 the light showed a group of two flashes every ten seconds, made by a fixed lens and an acetylene double flasher.[^10]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the light was automated and the dwelling vacated in 1941, not long after the Coast Guard took control of it.[^2] During a renovation on 12 November 1989 a fire broke out; the heat collapsed the spiral stair, and the Coast Guard decided to save the tower but strip out the dwelling and central column.[^2] The tower had no light from 1989 to 1998, when a solar-powered beacon was placed on top.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard deactivated the light in 2014, judging the structure unstable, and shows the same characteristic from a nearby structure; the tower still stands on the reef.[^2] Its National Register of Historic Places nomination form, dated 15 December 1972, calls it typical of the offshore lights built around 1850.[^11] Declared excess in 2017, it was offered at auction by the General Services Administration, which closed on 27 November 2020 with a high bid of $83,000.[^2] The Florida Lighthouse Association lists it as closed to the public, reachable only by boat from Key West.[^12]
