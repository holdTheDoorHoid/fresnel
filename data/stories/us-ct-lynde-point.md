Lynde Point Light is a white, eight-sided brownstone tower on the west side of the mouth of the Connecticut River, where the state's largest river meets Long Island Sound at Old Saybrook.[^1][^3] Its fifth-order Fresnel lens still shines, but the light was automated in the 1970s, and as of autumn 2026 its owner is about to change.[^1][^3][^9]

## The place

The Connecticut River is the largest in New England, and by the early nineteenth century ship traffic calling at Old Saybrook's harbor was considerable.[^3] Congress authorized "a sufficient light-house to be erected on Lynde's Point, at the mouth of the Connecticut River" on 6 April 1802, and deed records show the government bought the land from William Lynde.[^1] Lighthouse Friends puts the price at $225.[^3] That account adds that the crossing of the shallow bar at the river entrance was tricky and dangerous before breakwaters were built, and that evaporation from the nearby marsh made a local mist that could hide the light even on clear nights.[^3]

## Building the light

Abisha Woodward, a master carpenter of New London, won the contract at the end of November 1802 for a shingled, octagonal wooden tower 35 feet (10.7 m) high with an iron lantern and a stone foundation.[^1] Sources differ on when it was finished. The National Register nomination dates the station to 1802, from the deed and the contract, while the US Lighthouse Society archive and Lighthouse Friends give 1803.[^1][^2][^3] Lighthouse Friends gives the cost as $2,200 and describes seven lamps with nine-inch reflectors showing a fixed white light.[^3]

Within a generation the tower was failing. In 1838 Lt. George Bache wrote, as Lighthouse Friends quotes him, that "The tower is very much decayed, and is about to be taken down."[^3] The government had advertised in April 1837 for a granite or freestone tower 45 feet high, with the old lighthouse to be moved aside and kept burning while the new one went up.[^1] None of the offers received fell within the $5,000 appropriated, and on 7 July 1838 Congress added $2,500.[^1][^3] On 18 August 1838 the contract went to Jonathan Scranton, Volney Pierce and John Wilcox of Madison, Connecticut, for an octagonal masonry tower 65 feet (19.8 m) high, 25 feet (7.6 m) across at the base and 12 feet (3.7 m) at the top, with walls five feet (1.5 m) thick at the bottom and two feet (0.6 m) at the top.[^1] The builders chose brownstone over the granite or freestone the contract allowed.[^1] The date inscribed on the tower reads 1838 or 1839, the last numeral being partly hidden by repointing, and sources differ on whether the light was first shown in 1838 or 1839.[^1][^2]

The tower was fitted with ten patent lamps and reflectors, the reflectors being 14 inches across.[^1][^3] By 1855 the district inspector, A. Ludlow Case, reported that "The crib-work supporting the foundation of the tower is decayed and giving way," and recommended a fourth-order lens in place of the lamps.[^3] The nomination says the first Fresnel lens at Lynde Point was a fourth-order lens by Barbier and Fenestre; Lighthouse Friends dates its installation to 1857, with a fog-bell tower and the start of work on a new keeper's house and a seawall.[^1][^3] The fourth-order lens was replaced by a fifth-order lens in 1890, which Lighthouse Friends says still stands in the lantern.[^1][^3] The nomination describes that fifth-order lens as built in 1881 by Barbier and Fenestre of Paris, and also says the lighthouse burned whale oil until 1879, when kerosene replaced it.[^1]

A fog signal followed more slowly. The first record is an $800 appropriation in 1850; a fog bell was established in 1854 for $1,000, its striking machine was finished in 1856, and the apparatus was reworked in 1874 and again in 1883.[^1] After the Civil War a congressional appropriation paid for repairs, and the Light-House Board's reports for 1867 and 1868 mention rotten shutters and stairs and a plan to build a brick lining wall that was never carried out.[^1] The nomination judges that the surviving wooden spiral stair probably dates from that renovation.[^1] The keeper's dwelling had a frame kitchen added in 1833, and a gambrel-roofed Gothic Revival house replaced it in 1858.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates the start of that work to 1857.[^3]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society archive lists Philip Grumley as principal keeper from 1803 to 1818, followed by John Ingraham and then King Miller from 1819.[^2] Lighthouse Friends, citing an 1828 item in the New York Spectator, says Miller drowned in Saybrook Harbor on 11 June 1828 when his small skiff upset.[^3] Daniel Whittlesey followed him. The archive has Whittlesey in charge until 1841, and then his wife, Catharine F. Whittlesey, as principal keeper from 1841 to 1852.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says he died in June 1842 and that she looked after the light until 1852, so the two sources are a year apart on when the change came.[^3]

John Ninde Buckridge's service is the best told. According to Lighthouse Friends he enlisted in the US Navy in 1850 at seventeen, served in the Union Army in the Civil War, and had a leg amputated after an axe slipped on a wood-cutting detail in March 1864. He then kept a food market in West Farms, New York, until 1877, when he entered the Lighthouse Service, and was put in charge of Lynde Point in 1881.[^3] The US Lighthouse Society archive dates his term from 1883 to 1902, so the sources disagree on when he arrived.[^2] Fenwick's 2026 plans propose restoring the site to its look under Buckridge.[^9]

Elmer E. Gildersleeve kept the light from 1903 to 1925.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says his son Lawrence, born in the lighthouse in 1906, recalled in a 1975 New Haven Register interview that the family passed the time with cards and the phonograph and by watching ships go by, and that he was sometimes sent to the shore for eels or flounder for supper.[^3] Arthur J. Baldwin followed in 1925 and stayed to 1941.[^2] The archive's list of principal keepers runs on to 1977, with Laureat Leclerc shown from 1959 to 1969, and a separate list of Coast Guard names covers the same decades.[^2] Lighthouse Friends quotes a Coast Guardsman's wife in 1976 as finding the posting "so boring."[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Gildersleeve's name recurs in the Lighthouse Service's reports of help given to boaters. Lighthouse Friends says that in 1915 he helped two people whose boat was caught in ice reach the shore and sheltered them overnight.[^3] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that on 24 August 1922 he rescued two persons from drowning near the station, and the Secretary of Commerce officially commended him for it.[^5] Lighthouse Friends adds that they were swimmers carried out by the tide off Folly Point.[^3] The Bulletin also reported that on 26 May 1924, helped by his son, he aided the occupants of the motor boat Zip, which ran onto a stone pier north of Folly Point, and of the motor boat Yankgee, with three women aboard, which ran ashore the same day near the station.[^6]

A storm on 24 October 1917 did damage to Saybrook (Lynde Point) Light Station among several lights of the third lighthouse district. The Bulletin gives no detail for this station.[^4]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard history that the Hartford Courant cites says the light was electrified in 1955.[^10] The light was automated in 1975 by Lighthouse Friends' account and in 1978 by the nomination's account and the Coast Guard history the Hartford Courant quotes.[^1][^3][^10] Lighthouse Friends says it was used as Coast Guard housing for several years afterward.[^3] In 1966 the Coast Guard pulled down the 1858 dwelling and put up a masonry duplex, and the Old Saybrook Historical Society was unhappy about it.[^1][^3] The General Services Administration's 2023 notice instead says the duplex and a garage were built in 1970.[^7] The lighthouse went onto the National Register of Historic Places, with its record dated 29 May 1990, though the GSA notice says 1989.[^1][^7]

## Today

Lighthouse Friends lists the Coast Guard as the owner and reports that the grounds, dwelling and tower are closed.[^3] The property is reached by an easement over Sequassen Avenue, a private way, and is not open to the public.[^3][^7] Distant views are possible from Neponset Avenue, and River Quest cruises pass it.[^3]

On 31 May 2023 the GSA offered the lighthouse at no cost to eligible public and non-profit bodies under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act.[^7] In a notice published on 14 October 2026, Connecticut's environmental department said the General Services Administration had asked it to review the proposed transfer of ownership and stewardship to the Borough of Fenwick, which proposes to maintain the light in partnership with bodies that include the Old Saybrook Historical Society and the Lynde Point Land Trust.[^8] The Hartford Courant reported on 16 October 2026 that Fenwick plans to restore the site to its condition under Buckridge and to demolish the duplex.[^10] CT Insider reported on 22 October 2026 that the Department of the Interior awarded the property to Fenwick in May, that the transfer follows federal remediation and could come by spring 2027, that stabilizing the seawall is estimated at $415,000, and that the plan includes in-person and virtual guided tours.[^9]
