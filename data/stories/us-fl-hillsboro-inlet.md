An iron skeleton tower on a three-acre lot north of Hillsboro Inlet, Florida, the Hillsboro Inlet Light was lit in 1907 to complete the chain of lights along the Florida reefs. It still holds its second-order Fresnel lens, and the station itself is visited only on scheduled boat tours.[^2][^13]

## The place

The Light-House Board recommended a light at Hillsboro Point in each of its last ten annual reports, as the 1895 report notes. Steamers heading south after making Jupiter Inlet light, it wrote, "hug the reef very closely to avoid the current", while "the dangerous reef making out from Hillsboro Inlet compels them to give it a wide berth and to go out into the Gulf Stream."[^3] A light at Hillsboro Point would also let vessels coming across from the Bahama Banks check their position.[^3] LighthouseFriends quotes the Board as adding that the light would "complete the system of lights on the Florida Reefs".[^2] LighthouseFriends says the Board asked Congress for the money every year from 1885 to 1901.[^2] The Hillsboro Lighthouse Preservation Society, which now looks after the station, traces the idea back further, to a recommendation in 1855 that went unfunded.[^4]

## Building the light

On 12 February 1901 Congress authorized a first-order light at or near Hillsboro Point at a cost of up to $90,000, but made no appropriation.[^5] The money came in pieces: $45,000, $25,000 and $20,000.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates them 1902, 1903 and 1905; the Preservation Society dates the three acts 28 June 1902, 3 March 1903 and 3 March 1904.[^2][^4] A site south of the inlet was tested first and found unsuitable for a foundation, so three acres of swampland north of the inlet were bought.[^2] Sources disagree on when: the National Register nomination says the government bought the site on 9 November 1903, while the Preservation Society gives deeds signed in August 1904.[^6][^4]

The tower is an octagonal, pyramidal iron skeleton with a central stair cylinder.[^6] The Preservation Society and LighthouseFriends say the Russell Wheel & Foundry Co. of Detroit won the contract for it, that J. H. Gardner of New Orleans was paid $16,792 to clear the land, lay the foundation and erect it, and that G. W. Brown of West Palm Beach was paid $21,500 for the keepers' dwellings and outbuildings.[^2][^4] The Society says the tower was shipped in January 1906 by way of the Great Lakes, the Mississippi, the Gulf and Miami, then floated north by barge to the inlet, a journey the Society puts at 4,000 nautical miles.[^4] The National Register nomination gives a different origin for the iron: it says a Chicago steel firm assembled the light for the 1904 St. Louis exposition and that the government bought it afterwards. The same nomination also says Russell Wheel and Foundry of Detroit was contracted for the metal work on the tower, Brown for the buildings in 1905, and Gardner to erect the tower and foundation in January 1906.[^6] The nomination thus tells both stories, and the question is open.

The height is given several ways: 132 feet (40 m) in the nomination, 137 feet (42 m) in LighthouseFriends, and 136 feet (41 m) to the lantern room and 147 feet (45 m) overall in the Preservation Society's history.[^6][^2][^4]

The lens is a second-order Fresnel from Barbier, Bénard & Turenne of Paris, ordered for $7,250 according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The nomination says it was made in 1880.[^6] The light was first lit in March 1907. LighthouseFriends gives 7 March; the Preservation Society has the keepers arriving on 7 March and the light shining on 8 March.[^2][^4] The lens floated on a pool of mercury so it could turn.[^2]

## The keepers

The first head keeper was Alfred A. Berghell, with Henry A. Keyes and Robert H. Thompson as his assistants.[^1] The Preservation Society lists 1907 pay of $800 for the head keeper, $550 for the first assistant and $500 for the second.[^4] Berghell served until 1911. The Society's history, which draws on station correspondence, describes his last year as one of conflict: assistants who defied him, an inspector who did not back him, and a resignation letter in July 1911.[^4]

His successor, Thomas Knight, kept the light for most of the next twenty-five years.[^2] The one break came in 1919, when he moved to Jupiter Inlet hoping for better schooling for his children, thought better of it, and swapped back with Charles Seabrook.[^1][^4] In May 1920 he turned a storehouse into a one-room school, and a teacher lived with the family for the term; children from the lighthouse and from the fishing camp across the inlet attended.[^4] LighthouseFriends reports that the station won the district efficiency flag at least four times under Knight, and that his help to disabled boats and seaplanes was noted at least eighteen times.[^2]

Assistants came and went quickly, with more than a dozen second-assistant appointments between 1907 and 1920.[^1] Judge B. Isler served as second and then first assistant from 1920 to 1939.[^1] The Preservation Society says he retired in July 1939, just after the Lighthouse Service passed to the Coast Guard, as the last civilian keeper.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The tower stands close to the shore, and hurricanes reached it. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported what Knight told the bureau about the 1926 storm: from 3 to 8 a.m. on 18 September 1926, "solid green seas swept entirely across the reservation, carrying away the boathouse and the decking of the wharf." The bank around the tower was cut away, leaving the concrete foundation exposed to a depth of 8 feet (2.4 m). Fifteen people whose houses were destroyed were given refuge at the station, and Knight reported that the light was kept burning although the tower's vibration broke mantles and threw mercury out of the lens trough.[^7] LighthouseFriends gives the date as 19 September.[^2]

A keeper's report on the November gale of 1935 was also printed in the Bulletin: northeast wind at about 70 miles an hour at 11 a.m., and seas sweeping the reservation from 11 a.m. to 3 p.m. Some mercury slopped out of its tub, but the lens kept working.[^8] LighthouseFriends dates this storm to 1936, but the Bulletin issue reporting it was published in early 1936 and calls it the storm "of last November".[^2][^8]

The Service's reports credit the Knight household with several small rescues. In April 1913 Knight brought the crew of the disabled schooner *Alice Holbrook* to the station and kept them overnight.[^12] In May 1921 he and the second assistant cut a trail that stopped a forest fire reaching the station.[^10][^4] In a later report Knight, his first assistant and the keeper's sons rescued the three occupants of a small motor boat swamped on the bar, gave them food and dry clothing, and towed the boat to land; Knight and a son also went to the Norwegian steamship *Tancrede*, aground about ten miles north, and Knight, the assistant and two sons to the steamship *H. C. Folger*, aground about five miles south.[^9]

In 1931 Knight reported that 495 large vessels had passed southbound between 24 May and 24 June, which the Bulletin took as a sign of how busy the Straits of Florida were.[^11]

## After the keepers

The Preservation Society says the light was dimmed in May 1942 for the war and restored to full power in August 1944.[^4] The nomination and LighthouseFriends say the light was automated in 1974; the Society says 1973.[^6][^2][^4] After that the dwellings became beachfront vacation cottages for senior military personnel.[^2]

In 1992 the lens drive failed and a temporary beacon replaced the lens. A study then recommended removing the lens to a museum or historical society (the Society's history dates the study to 1993, LighthouseFriends to 1996). The Society's history says the uproar led to the Hillsboro Lighthouse Preservation Society, whose first aim was to keep the lens in place; LighthouseFriends dates the Society to 1997.[^2][^4] Hundreds of pounds of mercury were removed and a ball-bearing system installed. The light was relit on 28 January 1999, but the new bearing failed within weeks. A one-piece Torrington bearing was fitted, and a second relighting followed in August 2000.[^2][^4]

In 2012 the Coast Guard asked for public comment on shutting down or shading the light after complaints about sea turtle hatchlings. The light stayed on, obscured between 114 and 119 degrees.[^2] In January 2013 the Coast Guard handed maintenance of the lighthouse to the Society.[^4]

## Today

The light is active. The Preservation Society says a 1966 bulb change brought the light to about 5.5 million candlepower with a range of 28 nautical miles; LighthouseFriends places the rise to 5.5 million candlepower at the electrification of 1932.[^4][^2] The station is on the National Register of Historic Places; LighthouseFriends and the Society give a listing date of 16 February 1979.[^2][^4]

The Coast Guard owns the station and the Society manages it. The grounds are otherwise off limits to the public.[^2] Visits are by scheduled tour, most months on a Saturday, by boat from a dock in Pompano Beach, with a walk around the grounds and a climb of the tower.[^13] The Society also runs a museum, open on tour days.[^13] The tower can be seen from the public beach and park on the south side of the inlet.[^2]
