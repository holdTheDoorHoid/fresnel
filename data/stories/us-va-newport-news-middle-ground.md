Newport News Middle Ground Light is a cast-iron lighthouse on a concrete-filled caisson in the waters off Newport News, Virginia.[^1][^2] It is located just west of the Interstate 664 bridge over the James River.[^1][^2] It was built in 1890 and 1891 to guide ships out of the Newport News docks.[^1] Since its 2005 sale it has been privately owned.[^1]

## The place

Ships leaving the Newport News docks with a 24-foot (about 7 m) draft passed south of the Middle Ground shoal. The Lighthouse Board's 1887 annual report, as LighthouseFriends reproduces it, says such vessels always passed to the south of the Middle Ground, and that masters hesitated to sail in very dark or foggy weather.[^1]

The Board asked for a light and fog signal on the shoal.[^1] Its survey found sand and clay down to about 32 feet (about 10 m), below which the bottom was clean white sand.[^1] The report concluded that no less suitable structure than an iron caisson should be built there.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated the requested $50,000 for the lighthouse in 1888.[^1] Bids were sought the following year. A low bid of $17,830 came in for the ironwork, and the Board cut corners to close the gap.[^1] The riprap at the base of the caisson was reduced by a third.[^1]

The sources disagree about the caisson's material. The 1887 report asked for an iron structure 35 feet across, to be filled with concrete.[^1] The Archive's record describes the foundation as an iron-and-concrete caisson.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes a wooden caisson, sunk in 1890 and later filled with concrete.[^1] This story does not choose between them.

Work began in the spring of 1890. In July the caisson was towed to the site and sunk, and by the end of October it had reached its prescribed depth.[^1] The tower was completed in January 1891 and formally accepted on 6 March 1891.[^1] On 15 April 1891 the light was first shown from the lens, for the benefit of mariners.[^1]

The lens was a fourth-order Fresnel lens, showing a fixed white light with a white flash every twenty seconds, from a focal plane of fifty-four feet (about 16.5 m).[^1][^2] The Archive's record gives the light as 52 feet above mean high water, so the two figures differ.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the caisson was first painted black and the tower brown.[^1]

## The keepers

James B. Hurst was appointed the first head keeper, but he was transferred within a couple of months. His assistant, Daniel J. Clayton, took over and stayed a little over three years, resigning in 1894.[^1] The archive's roster lists 52 stints, from 1891 to 1954.[^2]

Malachi D. Swain was head keeper from 1917 to 1920, and Martin B. Tolson was an assistant.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the two were recognized in 1920 for helping a motorboat lost in fog.[^1][^5] Homer T. Austin followed Swain as head keeper.[^2] LighthouseFriends says Austin was head keeper when a motorboat that had become uncontrollable ran into the station's ladder during a storm, and that he helped its occupants.[^1]

On 19 December 1937 the assistant keeper James B. Cox was in charge of the light, and a temporary substitute keeper was also on the station.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that when the substitute went up on deck he found Cox unconscious. The coroner concluded that Cox had died of an acute coronary occlusion.[^1] The archive lists Cox as a first assistant in 1937.[^2]

The archive lists Harvey E. Mayor as head keeper to 1954, and the roster closes with two Coast Guard names for that year.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded several rescues by keepers at this station. In August 1917, "Carl G. Marsh, assistant keeper of Newport News Middle Ground Light Station, Va., brought to the light station a party of five persons in a motor boat which had become disabled and was adrift …"[^3]

In January 1919, "M. D. Swain, keeper of Newport News Middle Ground Light" towed a disabled supply boat "to the steps of the light station."[^4]

The 1920 issue of the Bulletin credits Swain and Tolson with helping three occupants of a motor boat lost in fog to reach a harbor. The entry is dated 1 October and prints no year.[^5]

The 1921 issue records that on 15 May the keeper, H. T. Austin, helped the occupants of a motor boat that had become uncontrollable during a storm and run into the station's ladder.[^6][^1]

## After the keepers

The 1926 Bulletin reported a plan for painting the roofs of lighthouses with marks for airmen, "along certain defined airways in Chesapeake Bay." The roofs of the selected lights were to carry serial numbers, and the list includes Newport News Middle Ground: "They are numbered serially, beginning at the north."[^7]

The light was automated in 1954, and LighthouseFriends says its characteristic was changed to a white flash every six seconds.[^1] Changing the batteries, which had to be done every nine days, was hard work, because the iron ladder from the first-level deck was awkward to climb.[^1] In 1979 an errant ship tore pieces of the first-level decking away, and a 1982 inspection found sections of the gallery balustrade missing.[^1] The light got solar power in 1987, and in 1988 the Coast Guard spent $14,400 on a long list of improvements.[^1] Inspectors in 1992 found the tower's iron plates corroding.[^1]

The lighthouse was offered under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act in 2002, and no eligible agency or non-profit group took it on. In 2005 it was sold at an online auction of surplus government property.[^1] It fetched $31,000, far below the normal value of a sturdy offshore lighthouse, because of its condition.[^1] A private family officially opened it as a residence on 4 July 2006.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is privately owned, and its tower is closed.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it remains an active aid to navigation, and that its owner must give the Coast Guard access to service the beacon.[^1] It can be seen from the Interstate 664 bridge and from King-Lincoln Park, but LighthouseFriends says the best views are from the water.[^1] LighthouseFriends counts it as the oldest caisson lighthouse in the state.[^1]
