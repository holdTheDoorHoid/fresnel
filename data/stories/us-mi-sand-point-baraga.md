Sand Point Lighthouse stands on a point on the west side of Keweenaw Bay, Lake Superior, a little north of Baraga, Michigan, across the water from the village of L'Anse. The Lighthouse Service built its red-brick tower and dwelling there in 1877 and 1878 to mark the harbor at L'Anse, where a railroad and ore docks were planned.[^1][^2][^6]

## The place

L'Anse, French for "The Cove," is a small, sheltered bay in the southwest corner of Keweenaw Bay.[^1] Peter Crebassa, whom LighthouseFriends calls the "town father of L'Anse," built a trading post on the bay in 1837, and a Catholic mission followed nearby.[^1] The 1883 county history gives a different sequence: it says Dubay had founded the American Fur Company's post, and that Crebassa moved it to the east shore in 1836.[^2] Railroad and dock plans for L'Anse in 1870 changed the bay's importance, and the Lighthouse Board asked Congress for a light to mark the harbor.[^1]

Congress provided $10,000 for a lighthouse at L'Anse on 3 March 1873, but the Service did not obtain a deed until May 1876, to a parcel on Sand Point, on the opposite side of the bay from the village.[^1] Work started in August 1877.[^1]

## Building the light

The lighthouse was built of red brick, with a one-and-a-half-story dwelling and a tower centred in its lakeward-facing gable.[^1] The tower measures 36 ft 6 in (11 m) from base to ventilator ball, LighthouseFriends says. It carried a fifth-order Fresnel lens by L. Sautter & Co. of France, with a lamp that showed a fixed red light at a focal plane of 39 feet (12 m) above the bay.[^1] The 1883 county history calls it a fifth-order light, and says it was "completed in 1878, and the first light made August 10 that year."[^2]

John B. Crebassa lit it for the first time on 10 August 1878.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

John B. Crebassa was keeper from the first night until his death on 1 March 1908.[^4][^1] The 1883 county history says he kept the light at Portage Entry from 1865 to 1878.[^2] The L'Anse Sentinel's obituary says he "applied for a position as light house keeper at Portage Entry" in 1865, and that after thirteen years there he was transferred to Sand Point.[^4] LighthouseFriends calls the earlier station Portage River Lighthouse, and gives his time at Sand Point as nearly thirty-nine years, which does not fit the 1878 start and 1908 death (about thirty years).[^1] The obituary says "For forty-three years Mr. Crebassa has climbed the stairs of the tower" to light the lamp, and that the inspectors' records showed a clean sheet for him.[^4]

The obituary gives his death as "Sunday afternoon last, March 1st, 1906."[^4] That date was a Thursday. The paper was printed on 7 March 1908, and 1 March 1908 was the Sunday before, so the year printed appears to be a misprint and the death was in 1908. LighthouseFriends' account, which has him die three days after a Sentinel article of 27 February 1908, fits that year.[^4][^1] The Coast Guard's account, as the Mining Gazette reports it, gives his retirement as 1 March 1908.[^6]

The obituary says he married Miss Hannah Wilbon of Detroit when he was 25. LighthouseFriends names his wife as Anna, and says that Anna kept the light for a few weeks at the opening of navigation in 1908.[^4][^1] The two accounts name different wives. The USLHS roster lists Anna Crebassa as principal keeper for 1908.[^7]

Thomas G. Thomson, transferred in from the Eagle Harbor Range Lights, followed in 1908 and kept the station until 1911.[^1][^7] LighthouseFriends spells his name Thompson in its text, and the USLHS spells it Thomson.[^1][^7] A 1917 item in the Sentinel prints a letter from Thomson written from Eureka, California. It says he "was keeper at the Sand Point light station near" Baraga a few years before, and that until recently he was stationed at Loleta, California.[^5]

Richard Thompson, whom LighthouseFriends calls "the fourth keeper of the light," served from 1911 until 1922.[^1][^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources used here tie no wreck or rescue to the light, and most of what they record is maintenance. The Light-House Board's report for 1894 lists "Sand Point, Lake Superior, Michigan," where "Some 86 running feet of sidewalk were laid connecting the dwelling with the outhouse and boathouse" (about 26 m).[^3] LighthouseFriends gives the figure as "some eighty feet."[^1]

Erosion was a recurring problem. LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse was jacked up in 1897 and moved back 200 feet (61 m) from the shore on account of erosion, and that a wall of hemlock timber was built around it with the space filled with sand.[^1] In 1910 the inspector, Oliver G. Brown, recommended a chimney on the privy, since "in warm weather stench becomes offensive." He also reported that "Beach is washing away steadily," and warned that the dwelling would be in danger again unless something was done, as LighthouseFriends quotes his report.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1922 the light moved off the old tower.[^1] An acetylene light was set atop a pipe mast, 35 feet (11 m) tall, rising above a small red-brick tank house 37 yards (34 m) east of the lighthouse. Its focal plane was 35 feet (11 m), and it showed a white flash every three seconds, LighthouseFriends says.[^1][^6] The Mining Gazette reports that the light was automated in 1922.[^6][^7]

The old lighthouse was sold off once it was no longer needed. A local dentist was among its private owners, and one of the owners added an enclosed porch on the south side.[^1] The Keweenaw Bay Indian Community bought the lighthouse and the surrounding property in 1994. LighthouseFriends reports that the community has used the building for offices and has not yet found money to restore it.[^1]

## Today

The current light is a white flash every four seconds, shown from a square skeletal tower on the point.[^1] The Keweenaw Bay Indian Community owns the lighthouse.[^6][^1] The sources disagree on access. LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open while the dwelling and tower are closed, and that the site is part of the Ojibwa Recreation Area.[^1] The Mining Gazette reported in 2024 that the lighthouse "is not open to the public."[^6] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists it as open to the public.[^7]
