The Reedy Island Range Rear Light stands beside Route 9 at Taylor's Bridge, in New Castle County, Delaware, about two miles from the west bank of the Delaware River.[^2][^1] It is the rear light of a pair that marks the centreline of part of the river's main channel. Its cast-iron tower was completed in 1910, and it still shows a light.[^1][^9]

## The place

Near the end of the nineteenth century, commercial interests in Philadelphia felt they were at a disadvantage. LighthouseFriends says the river was too shallow for large ocean carriers, which were forced to call at New York, Boston or Baltimore instead.[^2] Congress passed a river and harbor act on 3 March 1899 authorising a deeper channel.[^2] The new channel did not always follow the old one, so some existing range lights were moved and others were built.[^2][^8] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 gives the reason in one sentence: "The reestablishment of these ranges is made necessary by the construction of a new channel in the Delaware River, to take the place of the channel now marked by these ranges."[^4] On 3 March 1901 Congress appropriated the money, and the Board's next report recorded it: "By the act approved March 3, 1901, $60,000 was appropriated for the reestablishment of these ranges."[^5] The original lighthouse parcel, about 5.7 acres, was recorded in New Castle County on 11 September 1901.[^6] LighthouseFriends gives the rear range's parcel as 5.62 acres.[^2]

The name also belonged to an older light. A rear light at Port Penn Wharf was first exhibited on 14 March 1896, according to the Light-House Board's report for fiscal 1896.[^7] LighthouseFriends says that when the new lights were named in 1904, the older Reedy Island range, with its rear light at Port Penn, was renamed Old Reedy Island Range, and that the name changes were likely to cause confusion for some time.[^2] The 1896 date therefore belongs to that earlier tower, not to the one at Taylor's Bridge.

## Building the light

The new range began with temporary lights. A Notice to Mariners of 27 January 1904 set the date for the new lights as 16 February 1904, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The Delaware Bay Lighthouse Keepers and Friends Association says the rear light was first lit on that date, from atop a 100-foot temporary lantern post with a hoisting apparatus.[^9] LighthouseFriends describes the temporary rear light as a fixed white light shown at 120 feet from a pole, so the two accounts disagree on how high it stood.[^2]

The keeper's dwelling, oil house and barn came before the tower. Work on them began on 7 May 1906, after two rounds of bids had been rejected.[^2] The tower took longer. The Board first sought $19,500 in 1906 for a new tower, and Congress did not authorise the money until 27 May 1908.[^2] Earlier plans had been to move the tall 1876 tower of the Finns Point range here, but pressure from mariners kept that range in place, and the Board built a new tower instead.[^2] Contracts for fabricating and erecting it were awarded in June 1909.[^2]

The tower is made of cast-iron pipe segments, braced by diagonal tie rods that run between cast-iron junctions.[^1] The nomination describes the design as "easy to erect, and capable of being dismantled and moved if needed."[^8] The tower is 35 feet (11 m) square at its base and narrows to 10 feet (3 m) at the top. It rests on nine legs set on concrete pads.[^1] The nomination says the light is 110 feet (33.5 m) above the ground.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes the 1910 Lighthouse Service report as giving the total height of the tower as about 125 feet (38 m), and as saying the tower was to have a fifth-order range lens; it names the lens's supplier as the Macbeth-Evans Glass Co. of Pittsburgh.[^2] The sources do not reconcile the two heights.

The lamp inside the lantern was lit for the first time on 27 July 1910, and the temporary light on its pole was put out.[^2][^9] The nomination says the present light, a red beacon, is not the original apparatus.[^1]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists three principal keepers for this light, one after another: John D. Steller (1904-1911), Aaron S. Kimmey (1911-1927) and Robert B. Davidson (1927-1939).[^3] Steller's years took in the temporary light, the building of the tower and its first lighting in July 1910.[^3][^2] LighthouseFriends also lists him as head keeper of the front light in 1904.[^2]

Kimmey came to the rear light in 1911. LighthouseFriends says he had earlier served at Egg Island and at Brandywine Shoal, and that he spent February to April 1911 at the front light before taking the rear post.[^2] LighthouseFriends names the family as Aaron, Annie and their four daughters. They planted a large garden and several fruit trees on the arable land at the station, and the produce helped them live on what LighthouseFriends calls notoriously low keeper pay, $516 a year for this post.[^2]

Kimmey died at his home at Taylor's Bridge in January 1927. The Smyrna Times reported that the keeper of the Reedy Island range light had died unexpectedly of heart trouble, that he was survived by his wife and four daughters, and that the funeral and burial were held at Lewes.[^10] LighthouseFriends gives the date as 12 January 1927. It also reports the family doctor's opinion that the many flights of steps at the lighthouse had contributed to his death. Its references include a September 2002 Lighthouse Digest article about Kimmey.[^2]

Robert B. Davidson kept the light from 1927 until 1939, the last keeper on the roster.[^3]

## After the keepers

The roster ends in 1939. That was the year the Bureau of Lighthouses was absorbed into the Coast Guard, and the nomination closes its period of significance there: "In 1939, the Bureau was absorbed into the U.S. Coast Guard."[^8] No source here gives the year the light was automated. LighthouseFriends says that when it was, the station's buildings were sold into private hands.[^2]

After the sale, LighthouseFriends says, the dwelling was still inhabited, but it and the other buildings did not receive proper upkeep and slowly deteriorated. On the evening of 6 April 2002 a fire destroyed the dwelling and badly damaged the oil house.[^2] The old barn stood for a few more years, until a heavy load of snow collapsed it.[^2] In January 1989 the cracked foundation pads were replaced. The Coast Guard required the new pads to match the originals in material and size.[^1] The register nomination had been prepared in 1983 under contract to the Coast Guard.[^6]

## Today

The light still shows.[^9] LighthouseFriends lists the lighthouse as owned by the Coast Guard, with its grounds and tower closed, and says the light can be seen alongside the road.[^2] The Coast Guard owns and maintains the 1988 fibreglass emergency communications and lighting building at the foot of the tower.[^1] The original lighthouse parcel is held today by the Coast Guard and a private individual, and the outbuildings are privately owned.[^6][^1]

LighthouseFriends reports that the crumbling piers under the tower were repaired around 2019.[^2] It also reports that on 20 February 2025 the light was made available to eligible entities under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000. When no entity was found to take possession, an online auction for the property was initiated on 16 June 2026.[^2] The tower and grounds are closed to visitors, and the former keeper's buildings are private.[^2][^1]
