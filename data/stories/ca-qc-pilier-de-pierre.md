The Pilier-de-Pierre lighthouse is a round stone tower on a barren islet in the St. Lawrence, some 6.5 km off Saint-Jean-Port-Joli, Quebec. Lit in 1843, it is one of the oldest lighthouses in the province and still works as an aid to navigation. Its last keeper, Antonio Bourgault, left in 1960.[^1][^2][^4]

## The place

The Traverse is a tricky section of the south channel of the St. Lawrence below Quebec City. A history of the station says that the tides there are strong, rocks abound and good anchorages are hard to find. Two small islets known as the Pillars lie at its head, about three miles (5 km) from the south shore by that account. Stone Pillar (Pilier de Pierre) is a quarter of a mile long and bare, while Woody Pillar, a mile and a quarter to the west, has trees on its western side.[^1] The federal heritage text puts the tower instead some 6.5 km offshore, so the sources do not give one distance.[^4]

How to mark the Traverse was argued over for years. In 1828 a committee of the House of Assembly of Lower Canada heard two experts. Captain Lambly, harbour master at Quebec, proposed a stone lighthouse on Stone Pillar. Captain Bayfield of the Royal Navy insisted that a floating light at the foot of the Traverse would serve better.[^1] A schooner-rigged floating light, the *Brillant*, was moored there in 1831. In 1832 the Trinity House of Quebec, finding the floating light inadequate without an auxiliary light at the south-west entrance of the narrow channel, called for a lighthouse on Stone Pillar.[^1] The money came with an act of 1841 that taxed vessels leaving Quebec or Montreal for outside ports at two pence, between 1843 and 1845. The proceeds were to pay for lighthouses on Stone Pillar and on Bicquette Island.[^1]

## Building the light

Quebec's heritage register says the tower was designed in the summer of 1842 by Charles Atherton, a civil engineer of Montreal, and built by the Montreal contractor Joseph Andrew. It gives the cost as 4,042 pounds.[^3] Lighthouse Friends agrees on Atherton as designer but names the builder as Joseph Archer.[^1] The register says the limestone blocks were cut in Scotland, and its sector file adds that prisoners cut them.[^3] The Trinity House of Quebec, which Lower Canada created in 1805 to look after navigation on the river, chose the site.[^2][^3]

The tower was built to hold everything under one roof: the ground floor was a store and the two floors above were living rooms for the keepers, with brick lining inside and a wooden spiral stair serving the three levels.[^2][^3] The sources disagree on its height: the Quebec register gives 15.9 m (52 ft), while the federal heritage designation says 18.3 m (60 ft).[^3][^4]

The first light was a set of fifteen lamps and reflectors that turned once every minute and a half, showing a flash. It was first shown on 28 September 1843.[^1] The federal heritage text gives 1843 as the year of construction, though one passage in it prints 1834.[^4]

A second light was added nearby. On the Algernon Rock a timber pier with a reflecting tower was built in 1876, but the reflecting apparatus was never put in operation and was destroyed by fire in 1879. A sixth-order lens was then installed, and it showed a fixed white light from 20 April 1880. The Stone Pillar keeper was responsible for it and was expected to hire an assistant. Lighthouse Friends says the Algernon light was made unwatched in 1927.[^1]

The river was getting busier and faster. In 1893 an inspector reported that the stone dwelling was old and in bad order, and a new one was built in 1895 for 975 dollars. In 1894 the interval between flashes was cut from ninety seconds to thirty, because the increasing speed of vessels on the St. Lawrence made shorter rotations desirable on several lights.[^1]

## The keepers

Charles Julyan was the first keeper, paid 100 pounds a year, out of which he had to hire an assistant. He could leave when shipping closed for the season, but Trinity House held him answerable for any vandalism in his absence.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends says Julyan appears to have swapped posts in 1854 with Thomas Roche, keeper at Heath Point on Anticosti Island, and that Roche served at Stone Pillar until 1859. It reports that he petitioned the House of Assembly for a pension in 1860, saying he had lost his eyesight at Stone Pillar. It also quotes a vote of the Legislative Assembly, which it dates 1859, granting an indemnity to Roche, "late Light-house Keeper at L'Islet", for "having lost his sight while on duty" and because two of his sons had been "drowned in November last, while making the last trip from Lighthouse to shore". The two dates do not agree, and the vote as quoted names L'Islet and does not say which light the sons were leaving.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends lists Henry Harding (1859 to 1862), D. Vaughan (1862 to 1869), Damase Babin (1870 to 1874), Louis Damase Babin Jr. (1874 to 1901), Georges-Octave Leclere (1901 to 1911), E. Francouer (1912 to 1921) and Joseph-Francis Giasson (1921 to 1926). A Department of Marine table of keepers, in the volume catalogued as fiscal 1909, lists "Geo. Leclerc" with an appointment date of 30 July 1901, matching the start of Leclere's term as Lighthouse Friends gives it. The table prints him under Algernon Rock, the neighbouring light that the Stone Pillar keeper also served.[^1][^6] The Quebec register says that keeping a lighthouse was a trade practised by members of families from Saint-Jean-Port-Joli, notably the Bourgaults and the Leclercs.[^2]

Antonio Bourgault kept the light from 1926 to 1960, thirty-four years and longer than any predecessor.[^1] The register records that he carved inscriptions in the stone at the foot of the tower, still visible.[^2] The register also says that the isolation of the rock imposed a hard life on keepers and their families.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The light was built at a place where ships ran aground. The Quebec register says the shoals and strong currents here were associated with many wrecks.[^2] Lighthouse Friends and the Department of Marine's reports document several.

About 580 m (1,900 ft) south of the tower lies Algernon Rock, a ledge barely visible at high tide. Shortly after midnight on 1 June 1857 the 84 m (277 ft) steamer *Canadian*, of the Montreal Steamship Company, ran onto it. The ship was lost, but everyone aboard survived.[^1] A local newspaper reported in 2016 that the lighthouse's owners planned a 21 m (70 ft) scale model of the SS *Canadian* for the maritime interpretation trail. It dates that wreck to 1857 and places it off Saint-Jean-Port-Joli near the "roche à Veillon"; it does not mention Algernon Rock, so the article and Lighthouse Friends may be describing the same loss in different words.[^5]

The Department of Marine's reports for the twentieth century list later groundings and collisions near the light. In the report for the year to March 1916, the steam barge *Henry B. Hall* stranded a mile and a half southeast of the "Stone Pillars light" in smoky weather and came off undamaged; the report blamed the pilot.[^7] In the report for the year to March 1922, on 18 August the steamers *Mashinonge* and *Canadian Recruit* collided "off Stone pillars", half a mile east of buoy 64Q. The *Canadian Recruit* sank with no lives lost.[^8]

A lighthouse had its own hazards. In July 1913, Lighthouse Friends reports, a serious fire destroyed the dwelling built in 1895, together with the lantern and lighting apparatus.[^1] The Department's report for the year to 31 March 1915 lists, under Stone Pillar, a new dwelling to replace one destroyed by fire (J. E. Fortin of Saint-Jean-Port-Joli, 4,444.44 dollars) and a new lantern and single-flash reflector to replace those destroyed by fire (3,383.21 dollars, by day labour). The table is garbled in the scan, so the amounts are matched to the Stone Pillar entry by position; they agree with the figures Lighthouse Friends gives.[^1][^9] The Quebec register dates the new lantern and a two-storey house to 1914, within the period of that report, which runs from April 1914 to March 1915.[^3] The dwelling was linked to the tower by a wide passage.[^3]

## After the keepers

On 20 November 1955 the keeper's dwelling burned down, with an adjoining shed, and Bourgault lived in a small shed until the station lost its keeper in 1960.[^1][^3] The light was then automated and run by the Canadian Coast Guard.[^2][^3]

In 2005 Bourgault's grandson Pierre-Yves Bourgault and the member of Parliament Paul Crête began approaching Fisheries and Oceans Canada to save the tower.[^3] With federal money, the Friends of Port-Joli, a non-profit, restored it from 2006 to 2008: new door and windows, new lantern glass, repointed joints and a solar-powered LED light.[^1] The federal government named it a heritage lighthouse on 16 April 2015.[^4] The Friends became its owners on 17 November 2015 for a symbolic one dollar, with 30,000 dollars from Ottawa for repairs. The Friends must maintain it for fifteen years and open it to visits. Fisheries and Oceans stays as tenant and keeps running the light. The islet itself belongs to the Quebec government.[^5] Quebec classified the tower as a heritage immovable on 16 June 2022.[^2]

## Today

The tower still shows a white flash every six seconds, and the Coast Guard's list of lights marks it as a seasonal light.[^10] It is the only building on its islet, some 6.5 km out.[^3][^4] The 2015 agreement obliges the Friends of Port-Joli to make the tower accessible to the public for visits, while Lighthouse Friends, which still names the Coast Guard as owner, records the grounds and tower as closed.[^1][^5] Lighthouse Friends says it is best viewed by boat, with a distant view from a rest stop beside QC-132, where information panels stand.[^1] On the shore at Saint-Jean-Port-Joli a maritime interpretation trail carries a reproduction of the lantern.[^11] Anyone planning to go out to the islet should first ask the Friends of Port-Joli.
