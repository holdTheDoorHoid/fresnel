Faro de Puerto Ferro (Puerto Ferro Light) stands on a rocky point on the south coast of Vieques, the island east of Puerto Rico. Spain began it, the United States finished it, and it was abandoned in 1926.[^2][^3]

## The place

The light guided ships through the Vieques Passage, which separates Vieques from the main island.[^3] (Translation: the light "guiaba la navegación por el Pasaje de Vieques.")

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for 1915 describes the tower and dwelling as built "on top of a rocky promontory undermined for some time by the sea," and says the structure was in danger of collapsing.[^4]

## Building the light

Spain began the tower in 1896, using essentially the same plans as Punta Mulas on the north coast of Vieques.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends says the Spanish–American War interrupted the work.[^2] Just a few months after control of Puerto Rico passed to the United States, work resumed.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the light was first activated in December 1899.[^2] The U.S. Lighthouse Society record gives 1899 as the year the station was established and the year its lens was installed.[^1]

The sources do not agree on the first lighting. EnciclopediaPR says the lighthouse "fue iluminado por primera vez en 1896" (translation: "was first lit in 1896").[^3] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 says the completion of Puerto Ferro and Mona lights makes 15 the total number of lights in Puerto Rico. That report does not give a date for the first light.[^6]

The tower was octagonal and stood on a one-story dwelling.[^2] A spiral cast-iron stair led to the lantern room.[^2] The lens is also in dispute. LighthouseFriends and the U.S. record both describe a third-order Fresnel lens.[^2][^1] EnciclopediaPR says the light is classed as fifth order "porque su luz tenía un alcance de 12 millas" (translation: "because its light had a range of 12 miles").[^3]

## The keepers

The roster names Lorenza Castro as principal keeper from at least 1901 to at least 1903, and Esteban Parra Furniz from at least 1905 to at least 1911.[^1] Ramon Romero is listed from at least 1912 to 1916, and Simeon Martin from 1916 to 1918. Later principal keepers include Jose Escalera-Velez, Rufino Flores Graniel, Jose Francisco C. Correa and Jose M. Velez, who is listed from 1925 to 1930.[^1]

On 17 November 1912 the Bulletin reports that "a bottle paper was found at Port Ferro Light Station," and that it was forwarded by the keeper, Raimon Romero. The roster has Ramon Romero.[^7][^1] The Bulletin says the paper showed the bottle had been set adrift on 12 October 1907, about 240 miles east of Cape Henry, Virginia, and that it was "possible that it made a circuit of the North Atlantic" in five years.[^7] LighthouseFriends gives the same date and distance, and surmises that the bottle had made a circuit of the North Atlantic in just over five years.[^2]

Domingo Suárez Rosa served 26 years in the U.S. Lighthouse Service and 12 years in the Spanish service.[^9] The Spanish stations he named include Port Ferro.[^9] The Bulletin's obituary says "He was retired on July 1, 1925," at the age of 62, and that he died on 24 July.[^9] The Bulletin's 1924 to 1929 index also lists him as keeper of Port Ferro.[^11] The roster does not list him, and the years he kept Port Ferro itself are not stated.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 2 May 1922, LighthouseFriends says, two earthquake shocks were felt at the light. The second was stronger than the first and cracked the kitchen chimney in two places.[^2]

On 19 March 1925 at 4.50 in the morning, a severe earthquake shock was felt at the station.[^8] The Bulletin's report from the keeper says it "threw the lighting apparatus out of level," that part of the mercury was thrown out, and that the apparatus "stopped functioning until sunrise," when the keepers repaired it.[^8] The shock also "caused old cracks in the building to enlarge and new cracks to develop on the roof of the dwelling."[^8]

## After the keepers

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for 1915 gives an estimate of $34,000 for a light station at or near Port Real. It proposed to "dismantle the present Port Ferro Light Station" and to erect a new light at Port Real, "about 3 miles westward," using the same apparatus.[^4] The request was repeated in later estimates. A later estimate asks $40,000 for "Establishing a light station at or near Port Real, P. R., or East Point Vieques Island."[^5]

LighthouseFriends says the station continued to operate until it was boarded up and abandoned in 1926.[^2] A later automated skeletal tower beside the light eventually collapsed from corrosion, leaving no navigational aid on the point.[^2]

Between 1941 and 1942, LighthouseFriends says, the U.S. Navy took two-thirds of Vieques, including the area around the light. The military ended its control of most of the island in 2003.[^2][^3]

## Today

The lighthouse lies inside the Vieques National Wildlife Refuge. LighthouseFriends says the U.S. Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and that the peninsula it stands on and some nearby beaches are open to the public, while a good portion of the refuge remains off-limits.[^2]

The Fish and Wildlife Service's Puerto Ferro Trail (East) is open year round. It is about 0.29 miles (0.47 km) long and starts at the second parking lot on the peninsula, and it leads to the historic lighthouse.[^10] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is missing its lantern room and needs restoration.[^2]

LighthouseFriends also says the lighthouse has been sealed off to protect its interior, and that climbing to the roof is unsafe. Do not go inside the ruin or climb it.[^2]
