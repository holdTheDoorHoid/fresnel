Indian Harbour light is a square tower on the southeast point of Paddy's Head Island, at the entrance to Indian Harbour, in Halifax County, on the eastern side of St. Margaret's Bay, Nova Scotia.[^1] The Department of Marine and Fisheries put it in operation in 1901 to guide small vessels into the harbour, and the Canadian Coast Guard still lists it.[^1][^4]

## The place

Paddy's Head Island is joined to the mainland by a small wooden bridge, and the light stands on the island's southeast point.[^2] The 1901 report explains the purpose of the light: it was a guide "to small vessels seeking an entrance into Indian harbour through the channel between the southern extremity of Paddys head island and Wreck island."[^1]

## Building the light

The Department built the tower itself, by day labour, under a foreman named Amos McLennan.[^1] The report gives the cost as $728.24.[^1]

The tower was square and wooden, with sloping sides and a square lantern, all painted white.[^1] The report gives its height as 10 m (33 ft) to the top of the lantern vent.[^1] The light was 11 m (36 ft) above high water and, the report says, "should be visible 7 miles from all points of approach by water."[^1] The lens was pressed glass.[^1] The report dates the start of the light to 20 May 1901.[^1]

The Coast Guard's list now gives the structure as 9.4 m (about 31 ft) high, and the range as nine nautical miles.[^4] That range is a nominal figure in nautical miles, while the 1901 report gives a visibility of 7 miles without saying how it was measured. The sources do not say how the two heights were measured.[^1][^4]

## The keepers

The 1901 report's list of light-keepers names "Boutilier, Henry" for Paddy's Head, Indian Harbour.[^3] LighthouseFriends spells the name Boutillier.[^2] It says he was appointed the first keeper on 6 June 1901, at $100 a year, and that he looked after the light until it was electrified and de-staffed in 1945.[^2] NSLPS says the light was automated by 1945.[^5] LighthouseFriends names no other keeper.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Chief Engineer's report for 1903 says the barque Ashlow "formed an obstruction to navigation in Indian harbour." The Department invited tenders and made a contract for $100, and the wreck was removed for that sum.[^6] The report does not say where the wreck lay, or whether it was near the light.

In April 1904 the Department placed four wooden spar buoys in Indian harbour, on the east side of St. Margaret's Bay, to mark shoals in the channel leading from Wreck island to the coves in the harbour.[^7] Three were painted red, and the fourth was black.[^7] The Chief Engineer's report for 1903, printed in 1904, already describes four spar buoys in the harbour, without giving a date.[^6] The sources do not say whether these were the same buoys.

## After the keepers

Once the keeper was gone, the Department hired George Richardson as a caretaker in 1955, to help with maintenance and to report any problems with the light.[^2] Richardson held the post until 1967, when the Department of Transportation took over maintenance.[^2]

In 2013 local residents formed the Friends of Paddy's Head Lighthouse.[^2][^8] They asked the Department of Fisheries and Oceans for ownership of the tower, so that they could look after it.[^2] The tower was structurally sound then, though some siding on its seaward face was missing.[^2][^8]

In 2015 the lighthouse was one of the entries in *This Lighthouse Matters*, a crowdfunding contest run by the National Trust for Canada and the Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society.[^2] It did not reach the top three in its category.[^2]

In May 2017 the Coast Guard used a helicopter to land building supplies near the lighthouse, and workers clad the tower in vinyl siding.[^2][^8] The old wooden lantern room was beyond repair, so a new aluminum one was made.[^2] Because helicopter availability was short, the lantern was not set on the tower until 7 February 2018.[^2][^8]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse.[^2][^8] Its list gives a fixed white light, 11 m (about 36 ft) above the water, with a range of nine nautical miles.[^4] The Coast Guard list describes the tower as a "white square tower with red upper portion."[^4] Rowlett describes the tower as painted white, with the lantern painted red.[^8] The 1901 report says the whole building was painted white.[^1]

Getting close takes care. LighthouseFriends says access to the lighthouse itself is over private property, and that the grounds and the tower are closed.[^2] The Lighthouse Directory describes the site as open with the tower closed, reached by a strenuous walk.[^8] The two sources disagree about the grounds. Do not cross private land to reach the light, and do not climb the tower.
