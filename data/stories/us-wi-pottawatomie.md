Pottawatomie Light stands on a bluff at the northwest end of Rock Island, off the tip of Wisconsin's Door Peninsula, where ships coming up from Green Bay turned into Lake Michigan. It is a stone keepers' house with a light tower on its roof, begun in the 1830s and rebuilt in 1858. The light went out of service as a keepers' station in the 1940s and as a light in the 1980s, and the old house is now a museum run by volunteers.[^1][^2]

## The place

The nomination says the light was built on the northwest point of the island because the most used channel of navigation, before Chicago and Milwaukee had gained prominence, lay north of Washington Island.[^1] It marked the passage from Green Bay into Lake Michigan, and the nomination calls it one of the three earliest lighthouses in Door County.[^1] Its lantern stood 137 feet (42 m) above sea level and was visible, the nomination says, for twenty miles (32 km).[^1]

The keepers still hauled some of their water by pail from the lake until a well was drilled near the lighthouse in 1909. In 1879 steps were cut down the bluff to a new landing platform, so the keeper no longer had to walk more than a mile (1.6 km) to the landing at the island's southern end.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $5,000 for the lighthouse on 30 June 1834, and another $3,000 the following year.[^2] On 2 April 1836 Michael Dousman signed a contract to build a conical tower of stone or brick, thirty feet (9 m) high and topped by an eight-sided cast-iron lantern, with a keeper's dwelling beside it.[^2] John Scott of Detroit was paid $474 to supply and install the light, which had eleven lamps set in fourteen-inch (36 cm) reflectors.[^2] On 20 October 1837 Joseph Hobbs certified to the collector of customs at Mackinac that the work was sound, though it was more than a year after the lighthouse was to have been completed.[^2]

The dates disagree. LighthouseFriends says the light was completed in 1837, a year after Wisconsin became a territory.[^2] A 1937 Lighthouse Service Bulletin article calls the lighthouse "erected in 1837."[^7] The nomination says the original building was constructed in 1839, according to Coast Guard drawings.[^1]

The light was rebuilt in 1858. The nomination, quoting the Lighthouse Board's list of lights for 1866, says the earlier light "washed away" and was replaced.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes the 1858 work as a new two-story dwelling with a square wooden tower on its northern gable, topped by a nine-sided lantern and a fixed fourth-order lens with a focal plane of 159 feet (48 m).[^2] The nomination describes a two-story building of grey limestone with a square base for the tower, and the Lighthouse Board's report for 1894 records a new boathouse and landing crib at the station.[^1][^6] The sources disagree on whether the 1858 tower was stone or wood.

The light was converted from lard oil to kerosene in 1880, and a brick oil house was not built until 1904.[^2] A Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1915 lists Pottawatomie among the lights changed from oil to incandescent oil vapour.[^5] A telephone cable reached the station in 1903.[^2]

## The keepers

David E. Corbin, a veteran of the War of 1812 and a former fur trapper, was appointed the first keeper on 19 December 1837.[^2] An inspector in 1845 found him faithful but lonely, since he was a bachelor, and granted him leave on condition that he return with a wife. The next year he had not found one. By the 1850 census a woman, Catharine Storce, and her three children were living at the lighthouse with him, along with a laborer.[^2] Corbin died in December 1852 and was buried in a small cemetery just south of the light.[^2][^3]

The keepers who followed are listed in the U.S. Lighthouse Society's register. Willis W. Allen, Ira Cass and Joseph LeCuyer each kept the light for a few years in the 1850s.[^3] Among the 1860s keepers were Martin Trobee and Abraham Capers, and the register lists Paulina A. Capers as first assistant from 1868 to 1870.[^3]

William C. Betts was appointed keeper on 22 March 1870. Seventeen months later the thirty-eight-year-old bachelor married Emily Rohn, sixteen years old, the daughter of Victor Rohn, keeper of the nearby Pilot Island light. Emily became her husband's assistant, and their first child arrived in 1874.[^2] The register lists Emily J. Betts as first assistant from 1872 to 1882.[^3] The Betts kept a logbook that recorded storms and shipwrecks. On 20 January 1876 the keeper noted that two men attempting to row from Washington Island to the mainland had never arrived after a storm. LighthouseFriends quotes a later entry saying the men had been seen several times and, at the last account, were still adrift among the ice.[^2] In 1882 budget cuts removed the assistant keepers at Pottawatomie, and the couple's annual salary fell from $960 to $560.[^2] Betts resigned in 1886 to work his farm on Washington Island.[^2][^3]

The register lists Mollie M. Boshka as first assistant in 1907. Charles Boshka was principal keeper from 1902 to 1911.[^3] The register names John Fitzgerald as assistant from 1907 and principal keeper from 1929 to 1941, and Edward H. Cornell as principal keeper from 1911 to 1928; the Bulletin of 1914 shows the two working together.[^3][^4] The last head keeper was Ernest R. Lockhart, from 1946 to 1947. In 1947, LighthouseFriends says, wet-cell batteries were put in place to power the light and the dwelling was closed.[^3][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 16 June 1914 Edward H. Cornell, keeper, and John Fitzgerald, assistant keeper, rendered assistance to two men in a disabled motor boat, and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin printed it.[^4] On 6 September 1916 the steamer Peter Reiss, bound for Green Bay with coal, ran aground on St. Martin's Reef. The keepers notified the Coast Guard and delivered messages to the stranded steamer, and a tug and lighter removed some of its coal so that it could be freed from the reef. LighthouseFriends says the ship spent two weeks in dry dock.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Society's record gives 1966 as the year the light was automated, a date that does not match LighthouseFriends' 1947 battery account.[^3][^2] The nomination, prepared in 1977 and 1979, says the light "is still in use but is now automated," that its original enclosure had been removed, and that "the building is vacant and boarded up."[^1] The lighthouse was listed on the National Register on 20 April 1979.[^8]

The light's history after the house closed is complicated. LighthouseFriends says solar panels replaced the batteries in 1986, and that the Coast Guard put up a steel skeleton tower near the lighthouse in 1988 to show the light.[^2] The Society's record lists the optic as a battery-powered beacon, and records the light as discontinued in the 1980s.[^3]

Friends of Rock Island State Park began work to restore the house in 1994, and a new lantern room, built to replace one the Coast Guard had removed, went up in 1999.[^2] A headstone for David Corbin's grave followed in 2003. The house was dedicated as the Pottawatomie Lighthouse Museum on 17 May 2004, and volunteer docents began living there for week-long stints in summer that year. A replica fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed in May 2005.[^2][^9]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by the Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources and stands at the northwest end of Rock Island State Park, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The grounds are open, and the dwelling and tower are open in season. Tours run daily from 10 a.m. to 4 p.m. from Memorial Day weekend to Columbus Day.[^2] Visitors reach Rock Island by a passenger ferry from Washington Island and then walk a 1.2-mile (1.9 km) hike to the light.[^2] The Friends of Rock Island's docent program recruits volunteers,[^9] and LighthouseFriends says docents have lived in the house for week-long stints each summer since 2004.[^2]
