Deer Island Thorofare Light, also called Mark Island Light, stands on a small island at the western entrance to the Deer Island Thorofare, off the town of Stonington, Maine.[^1][^2] It is a square brick tower that was once joined to a keeper's dwelling, which burned in 1958.[^1][^2] The Island Heritage Trust has owned the island and the light since 1998.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The thorofare is a narrow passage between the southern end of Deer Isle and a scatter of islands and ledges. It links East Penobscot Bay with Jericho Bay, and Mark Island sits at its western entrance.[^2] In 1855 a lighthouse inspector, W. B. Franklin, recommended a light on the island to "enable vessels to cross Isle au Haute bay, and get into Deer Island thoroughfare," as LighthouseFriends quotes his recommendation.[^2]

The USLHS archive gives Mark Island as an alternate name.[^1] The Trust notes that the light is officially known on nautical charts as the Deer Island Thorofare Lighthouse.[^3]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated the requested money in August 1856. The six-acre island was bought from David and Mercy Thurlow that December for $175.[^2] Work on the square brick tower began in the summer of 1857.[^2] The light was first shown at sunset on 1 March 1858, from a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^1][^2] The USLHS archive gives 1857 as the year the station was established and 1858 as the year the tower was established.[^1]

In 1878 the keeper's dwelling, first painted brown, was clapboarded and painted white.[^2] In 1881 some 175 trees were cut on the island so the light could be seen from every approach.[^2] On 15 October 1884 a bell began to sound in a frame tower 75 feet (23 m) northwest of the light.[^2] It was struck by machinery, a double blow every fifteen seconds.[^2] A boathouse came in 1877, and in the fiscal year ended 30 June 1895 the station gained an oil house and a fuel house.[^2][^5]

The Light-House Board tested the fog signal here on 2 June 1894.[^4] The report gives the wind as south-southwest at two knots.[^4] An observer named Livermore, aboard the Myrtle, traced how far the bell could be heard.[^4] The limit of hearing ran from about a quarter mile dead to windward out to much farther on the lee side.[^4] The report says the bell's mounting threw its sound nearly in the direction the wind was blowing.[^4]

## The keepers

Thomas Colby Small, a second cousin of David Thurlow, one of the island's sellers, was the station's first keeper, from 1857 to 1861.[^1][^2] He had gone to sea before a fall from a ship's rigging ended that work.[^2]

Samuel E. Holden, a Civil War veteran, kept the light from 1868 until his death in 1874.[^1][^2] His wife, Melissa, then took over as keeper, with five children under thirteen to look after.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that was too much for one person, and quotes an 1875 inspector's report calling the station neglected and saying the keeper should be removed if matters did not improve.[^2] The roster lists Melissa Holden as principal keeper until 1876.[^1][^2]

John E. Purington was keeper until 1916.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he swapped stations that year with Allen C. Holt, who was keeper at Nash Island, so that Holt's children could go to school in Stonington.[^2] The children boarded in town in winter and came out in the station's motor dory in fall and spring.[^2] The roster gives Purington's start as 1914; LighthouseFriends says 1912.[^1][^2]

An account by Edwin Mitchell, who sailed with the Maine Sea Coast Mission's steamer Sunbeam, describes a stop at Mark Island.[^2] The keeper, whom Mitchell calls Alva Robinson (the USLHS roster spells it Alvah), was on the rocks at the landing, and his wife and daughter met the visitors at the kitchen door.[^1][^2] Mitchell found the kitchen spotless and wrote that the three Robinsons were the only people on the island.[^2] The account, as LighthouseFriends prints it, is undated.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 13 May 1917 keeper Allen C. Holt towed the disabled steamer Minnehaha to a place of safety, the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports.[^6] LighthouseFriends says that year he was recognized for helping float the schooner Sarah and Lucy, which had run ashore on rocks off Andrews Island, and for towing a stranded motorboat to Stonington.[^2] In 1921 he towed a disabled powerboat to Sand Beach, and on the way back a disabled lobster boat to Stonington.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard automated the station in 1958.[^1][^2] On 10 September 1958 a battery charger in the dwelling's basement exploded and started a fire.[^2] Firefighters from Stonington and the Coast Guard cutter Laurel saved the brick tower, but the dwelling was lost.[^2] The Coast Guard chose to automate rather than rebuild the dwelling.[^2] The outbuildings were torn down, a modern optic replaced the Fresnel lens, and a continuously sounded horn replaced the fog bell.[^2] The optic is now an LED unit, solar powered, according to the USLHS archive.[^1]

Residents complained about the horn's noise.[^2] A reduction in its power and a move in July 1959 eased the complaints.[^2]

In late 1997 the Maine Lighthouse Selection Committee announced that the Island Heritage Trust would become the owner.[^2] The Trust says the light passed into its hands in 1998, under legislation that transferred 36 Maine lighthouses to local groups.[^3] LighthouseFriends gives the number as 35.[^2] A celebration on 14 March 1998 drew local residents, keepers' descendants and former keeper Ralph Andrews.[^2]

## Today

The Island Heritage Trust owns the island and the light. The Trust is raising money for restoration, and it sells a history of the light by Marnie Reed Crowell.[^3] The USLHS archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower closed, and that the light is best viewed from the water.[^2]
