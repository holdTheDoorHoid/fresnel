Sambro Island is a granite island off the mouth of Halifax Harbour, and its lighthouse is considered the oldest operating one in North America, according to Canada's register of historic places.[^1][^2] Nova Scotia's first elected assembly voted the money to build it on its opening day, 2 October 1758.[^2] Since then the tower has been raised, reshingled, striped red and white and fitted with one light after another,[^3][^4][^5] and the register calls it "the most prominent navigational landmark" for ships using the port of Halifax.[^1]

## The place

Sambro Island lies off the entrance to Halifax Harbour, in what is now the Halifax Regional Municipality.[^2] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society describes a granite island about two nautical miles outside the entrance, with more than thirty shoals around it, while a map note on Lighthouse Friends gives about 8 km (5 miles) southwest of the entrance.[^2][^5] Halifax was founded in 1749 as the British Navy's chief base in North America, but the approach, the society says, was "often masked in fog", and for the town's first nine years nothing signalled the dangers.[^2] The register of historic places adds that Captain James Cook saw and recorded the light on entering the harbour, and that the tower stood there when some 12,000 Loyalists landed at Halifax in 1783.[^1]

Joshua Slocum, who left Boston in the *Spray* on 24 April 1895 on a voyage around the world, sailed from Yarmouth, Nova Scotia, on 2 July and passed Halifax Harbour the next evening. He wrote that at 6:45 p.m. on 3 July he was "in close under Chebucto Head light", and that he "watched light after light sink astern as I sailed into the unbounded sea, till Sambro, the last of them all, was below the horizon." He called the island "the Rock of Lamentations" and said it "carries a noble light".[^6]

## Building the light

In 1752 the Governor and Council tried to raise money for a light on "Cape Sambrough" by lottery, without success.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives the scheme as 1,000 tickets at £3 each, meant to raise £450, with ticket-holders refunded when too few sold.[^5] On 2 October 1758, the first day of the first session of Nova Scotia's General Assembly, an act was passed for a lighthouse on "Sambro Outer Island", with £1,000 taken from the duties on spirits and a levy on ships entering the harbour; by early November the work had begun.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says a Captain Rous deeded the island to the government and that his brother Joseph was hired as the first keeper;[^5] the *Dictionary of Canadian Biography* entry for the naval officer John Rous, who commanded the *Sutherland* at Louisbourg and Quebec and died at Portsmouth on 3 April 1760, lists among his survivors his brother "Joseph, who was the first keeper of the lighthouse at Sambro".[^7]

The sources do not agree on every date for the tower and its light. The society's light details list 1758 as the year work began and 1759 as the year lit.[^2] The earliest dated notice the society quotes is a letter from Halifax dated 28 April 1759, printed in the *Pennsylvania Gazette* on 14 May. It says the light "was lighted the 23rd Instant" and stands "one League S.W. of the Harbour, on Sambra Island".[^2] The society judges it probable that a temporary light had been shown earlier,[^2] and the federal heritage review dates the tower to "1758 or 1760".[^2]

The original tower was stone, the society says: 18 m (60 ft) from the base to the weather vane over the lantern, with the fixed white light 35 m (115 ft) above the sea.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives a base about 6 m (20 ft) across and walls 1.7 m (5.5 ft) thick; the heritage review puts the cut-granite walls at 1.5 m (5 ft) at the base, narrowing to 1.2 m (4 ft) where the old work meets the 1906 concrete.[^5][^2] Among the features the register values are the tower's slight taper and eight sides, its narrow slit openings, its granite-and-concrete construction and its interior hardwood column and stairs; the society's heritage statement adds that the column carries a winding wooden staircase.[^1][^2]

The lantern and lamps are recorded in stages. In 1834 the lantern held 128 small panes of glass, and a commission appointed that year reported that the light could not be improved until a new iron lantern was fitted. The society dates the new lantern to 1864 and Lighthouse Friends an improved lantern room to 1865.[^2][^5] In 1877 a second-order Fresnel lens by Chance Brothers of Birmingham, which cost $4,942, was fitted.[^5]

Fog signals changed several times. For many years the island answered ships' signals in fog with cannon.[^2] Arnold Burges Johnson's *The Modern Light-House Service* (1889) records, without a date, Admiral Sir A. Milne's remark that he "had often gone into Halifax Harbor, in a dense fog like a wall, by the sound of the Sambro fog-gun."[^8] A Daboll trumpet worked by a caloric engine was started on 20 September 1865. Lighthouse Friends says it served reasonably well for a few years and then became unreliable; on 16 June 1870 the machinery was in such poor repair that the keeper turned the fly wheel by hand, and by 25 July it had broken down altogether. Mariners were told that a pair of 24-pounder guns, fired one after the other, would answer ships' signals in the meantime.[^5] A steam whistle came next, with an evaporating house that made fresh water from salt, a wharf and a tramway to bring coal to the whistle house. The engineer James Skinner started it on 16 October 1876, and when needed it sounded a ten-second blast every minute.[^5] On 15 June 1891 the whistle was discontinued and an explosive signal replaced it, a "bomb rocket" fired every twenty minutes, and every ten minutes from 1899.[^5]

The tower was raised and the light replaced in 1906. The Department of Marine and Fisheries' report for the year ended 30 June 1906 says the tower "has been raised and a first order single flashing light erected, replacing a second order fixed light". The lens came from Barbier, Benard & Turenne of Paris. It had four panels of 90 degrees each, made one revolution in 20 seconds and gave a flash of 0.29 seconds in each 5-second period; it was to burn acetylene under a mantle.[^9] The next report, for the year ended 31 March 1907, says the old octagonal stone wall was topped with an octagonal concrete wall 6 m (20 ft) high and a new circular iron lantern, with white-painted shingles on the sides and a red lantern, so that the lighthouse stood 25 m (82 ft) from base to vane. The work was done by day labour under J. A. Legere, was completed in November 1906 and cost $2,934.[^3] The society's own history says 6.7 m (22 ft) was added where its heritage review and the report say 6 m (20 ft).[^2][^3] In the year ended 31 March 1908 the department had Sambro and six other lighthouses repainted with white and red stripes or bands "to make them more conspicuous when snow is on the ground".[^4]

## The keepers

Joseph Rous, the first keeper, had a brother who was a naval captain, John Rous, whose ship the *Sutherland* was among those that ran past the batteries of Quebec on the night of 18 to 19 July 1759.[^7][^5] Lighthouse Friends lists Joseph as keeper from 1759 until his death in May 1769, followed by Joseph Woodmass.[^5] Matthew Pennell was in charge by 1772 and, by Lighthouse Friends' account, stayed until he died in early 1801.[^2][^5] In 1799, aged 85, he asked the government for a pension, saying he had put nothing by as keeper while raising four children and three orphaned grandchildren; the government granted him £100. His son, Matthew Pennell Jr., was appointed after him, and James Goodwin took over in 1817.[^5]

In 1862 Captain Alfred Ryder of HMS *Hero* inspected the light at the request of Vice Admiral Alexander Milne, and Lighthouse Friends prints his description of the lamps, which shows how one keeper worked. Ryder counted thirteen lamps, nine of them fitted with reflectors, arranged round a flat, nearly circular table in an octagonal lantern, and remarked that "if the number of panes of glass was a merit in a Light House, Sambro would probably stand at the head of the list of known Light Houses." Every night the keeper deliberately tilted each lamp back, with small wooden wedges, to stop the oil running over when, "as the keeper describes it", it "swells with the heat". Ryder added that the reflectors, 47 years old, had so little silver left that the tilt made little difference. New lamps and reflectors were installed promptly after his report.[^5] Ryder does not name the man; both keepers' lists in the sources put William Gilkie in charge at that date.[^5][^2]

On Lighthouse Friends' list one family, the Gilkies, held the light from 1839 to 1929: William, his sons Joseph and Alfred, and Alfred's son Arthur.[^5] The society dates William Gilkie's term from 1856 rather than 1839.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says William died in July 1875 and his son Joseph, who had run the fog trumpet, took over; Joseph drowned on 1 March 1876 while trying to reach the island from the mainland.[^5] Another son, Alfred, then took the light. Lighthouse Friends gives his term as 1876 to 1915, the longest of any keeper there, though the same page also says "nearly thirty years".[^5] The Department of Marine's staff tables for 1901 and 1906 name the Sambro keeper as "Gilkie, Henry A", the 1901 table giving his appointment as 8 January 1877; the table for the year ended March 1911 has "Alfred Gilkie" in the same place.[^10][^11][^12] Alfred's son Arthur J. Gilkie appears in the 1915 table as "A. J. Gilkie", appointed 19 April 1914 at $880 a year, and Lighthouse Friends has him serving until 1929.[^13][^5]

A newspaper account of the 1884 wreck of the *Daniel Steinmann* (below) gives a snapshot of the household. The statement comes from the keeper's brother, whom the page prints as "Alex. Gilnie"; since he calls the keeper "my brother Alfred", and Lighthouse Friends lists an Alexander Gilkie as assistant engineer from 1876, he is taken here to be Alexander Gilkie.[^14][^5] He listed the island's permanent residents: the keeper, Alfred, with his wife and three children; himself and another brother, Stewart; a bombardier and four gunners of the Royal Artillery; and Alex. Houseman, the engineer in charge of the fog whistle. The keeper and the bombardier were away in Halifax that night, making the annual returns.[^14]

After the Gilkies came Warren Smith (1929-42) and William A. Smith (1942-61), then G. A. Soltesz (1961-65) and J. E. Gwynn (1965-68), and finally John G. Fairservice, assistant from 1964 and head keeper from 1968 to 1988.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 8 April 1771, during a storm, the Boston sloop *Granby* broke up on ledges near the island with all hands lost. Lighthouse Friends says she carried £3,000 to pay the naval staff at Halifax; the society says the money was for the dockyard staff.[^5][^2] Commodore Gambier, the naval commander-in-chief, reported that "the fatal accident happened for want of a light being properly kept in the lighthouse", adding that the Navy's own ships had at times had to fire on the lighthouse to make the keepers show a light.[^2][^5] Other vessels complained about paying for a light that, in words the society quotes, "serves no other purpose than the shameful one of putting money in the pockets of a nominee of the Governor's"; Lighthouse Friends says merchant masters resented the light duties.[^2][^5] An inquiry found that the governor had appointed the keeper, who was allowed to keep the duties paid by ships entering the port and bought only the cheapest supplies, and that the light burned fish oil and was left dark if it went out when no ships were in sight.[^2] Nothing came of Gambier's call for the government to take over the light. Instead, in 1772, with Pennell in charge, fountain lamps with flues to carry off smoke were fitted, and the blackening of the glass became far less of a problem.[^2]

On the night of 3 April 1884 the steamer *Daniel Steinmann*, bound from Antwerp for Halifax and New York, ran onto the ledges north-east of the island.[^15][^14] The New-York Tribune's dispatch of 4 April described heavy thunder and lightning between eight and nine o'clock, and at ten "a squall sprang up, which proved to be a small hurricane", with "a thick fog and heavy rain"; it also said that fog had kept Captain Schoonhoven from seeing the light.[^15] His own statement, printed in the *Indianapolis Journal* on 7 April, says otherwise: at about 9:15 he saw a faint light through the mist, took it for Chebucto, and ten or twenty minutes later saw it was "the fixed light of Sambro"; he put the helm hard over "but it was too late", and he "at no time heard the fog-whistle off Sambro."[^14]

Alexander Gilkie, who said he had relieved the engineer on the whistle at six, kept it sounding until 8:45, when the fog cleared enough for him to see Devil's Island and Chebucto Head, and then stopped it. Just before 9:30 the lookout sighted a steamer about 1.6 km (1 mile) south by east of the light, and the whistle was started again at 9:45 to warn her off the rock.[^14] By Gilkie's account the ship struck twice, her anchor did not hold, and she drifted across the shoals and sank between 275 and 365 m (300 and 400 yards) from the island's shore; the captain said a heavy sea washed the passengers on deck overboard.[^14] Gilkie and a brother put out in a boat to reach the steamer, but it was too small and they turned back. They went up instead, burned lamps on the rocks, lit bonfires along the shore and waved blue lights, and within the hour one of the steamer's boats with seven men came in sight and was guided to a landing. At daybreak, Gilkie said, two people were seen clinging in the forward rigging, and Stewart and three others took a boat out to the wreck and landed the captain and one passenger.[^14] Accounts of the rescue boat's crew differ: the captain's statement counts three men and another report in the same issue five.[^14] The Tribune reported nine people saved out of ninety passengers and thirty-four crew: the captain, five sailors and three passengers.[^15]

The Department of Marine and Fisheries' annual reports record later losses. On 17 March 1914, the department's salvage report says, the steamer *City of Sydney* struck the Sambro ledges in dense fog; she was found to be full of water fore and aft and became a total loss, though the steamer *Cabot* salved part of her cargo.[^16] The same volume's casualty return says she "struck off Shag rock, N.S." with no lives lost, and its table of wreck inquiries records that the master was censured for an error of judgment after a hearing at Halifax on 23 and 24 March.[^21] On 5 February 1917 a vessel whose name is unreadable in the scanned report stranded and was lost near Sambro Island. After an inquiry at Halifax on 12 and 13 February her master was severely censured for not sounding as he approached the coast, but because he had used his best judgment after the accident in saving the lives of everyone on board, his certificate was not dealt with.[^17] On 2 July 1918 the *City of Vienna* stranded near Sister Ledges, Sambro; the inquiry found the master not at fault, though it called it an "excusable error of judgment" that he had not stopped his ship when he heard the Sambro fog signal.[^18] And the *Bohemian* stranded near Sisters Rock on the Sambro ledges with the loss of six lives; at the formal investigation in Halifax on 5 and 6 March 1920 the master, Ernest C. Hiscoe, was found at fault for not having taken soundings, and his certificate was suspended for four months.[^19]

The tower itself came under strain in 1950. In January the keeper, W. A. Smith, noticed that the lighthouse was swaying.[^2] The society says it was found to be leaning by 20 cm; Lighthouse Friends says measurements later showed it more than 1.27 m (50 in) out of plumb.[^2][^5] Digging at the foot of the tower in the spring exposed cracked mortar and loose stones; that autumn the masonry was grouted and a concrete collar about 90 cm thick and 2 m high was built round the base.[^2]

## After the keepers

Much of the old equipment went while keepers were still on the island. A diaphone fog signal came in 1963.[^2][^5] In 1966 the first-order Fresnel lens was taken out and a DCB-36 put in its place, a rotating beacon of the kind used at airports; the lens was lent to the Maritime Museum of the Atlantic.[^5] The society's history says the iron lantern was swapped for an aluminium one in 1966 as well, but its own list of dates and its notes on the 1998 repairs give 1968, as does Lighthouse Friends (May 1968).[^2][^5] John G. Fairservice, the last keeper, left in 1988, and the light has been automated since.[^5][^2]

By 1998 the two keepers' houses stood abandoned and vandals had stripped the hardwood floors and stairs; before that year's repairs ended, volunteers from the Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society put plywood over the houses to slow the decay.[^5] In 1996 the tower was rated "Classified", the highest ranking for a federal heritage building, and the gas house on the island "Recognized".[^2] The society says the lighthouse had been designated a National Historic Site in 1937, with a plaque placed in the village of Sambro.[^2]

The 1998 repair began at the end of August, after great chunks of concrete had been falling from the platform that carries the lantern. When the platform was chipped back, the 1906 concrete turned out to have been mixed with beach sand and stones, and the salt in it had rotted the work. The restoration was finished in December 1998, and when the old shingles were stripped the tower proved to have been whitewashed originally.[^2][^5]

In April 2007 a storm surge tore out the submarine cable that fed the island with electricity. The island ran on diesel generators until solar panels went in during March 2008; at the same time the DCB-36 gave way to a Tideland TRB400 lens system and the diaphone to electronic horns.[^5][^2] The tower was painted that summer ahead of its 250th anniversary.[^2][^5] About two weeks after Lighthouse Friends' late-August 2008 photographs, the head keeper's house, a building from the 1960s, was lost to fire.[^5] In 2016 the federal government awarded Quinan Construction Company of Orillia, Ontario, a $1.3 million contract to restore the tower's stonework and concrete foundation.[^5]

## Today

The tower is owned by the Government of Canada and operated by the Canadian Coast Guard.[^2] The Coast Guard's *List of Lights* gives a white flash every 6 seconds (1 second of light, 5 of dark), a focal height of 42.7 m above the water and a nominal range of 23 nautical miles, from a white octagonal tower with red horizontal bands; it lists the light as operating 24 hours a day, year round.[^20]

The island is reached only by boat. Lighthouse Friends lists the grounds as open and the tower as closed, and says the Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society occasionally offers trips; the tower can be seen from Chebucto Head and from Crystal Crescent Beach Provincial Park. It is federal property run by the Coast Guard, so the way to see it up close is an arranged trip, not an unplanned landing.[^5][^2] The first-order lens that served from 1906 to 1966 is on display at the Maritime Museum of the Atlantic in Halifax, according to Lighthouse Friends and the society, and Lighthouse Friends adds that the DCB-36 that replaced it is displayed there too.[^5][^2]
