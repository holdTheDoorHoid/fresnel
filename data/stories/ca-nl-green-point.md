Green Point Lighthouse stands on the rugged tip of the Port de Grave Peninsula, on the south side of the entrance to Bay Roberts Harbour on Conception Bay, Newfoundland and Labrador. Its cast-iron tower was first lit in 1883, and it is now owned by a heritage society.[^1][^2]

## The place

Lighthouse Friends says the point is now commonly known as Green Point, and that the 1880 petition for a light called it Bay Roberts Point. It is the eastern end of the Port de Grave Peninsula.[^1] The peninsula's own page says the lighthouse's story "began with tragedy": the Huntsman, a vessel from Bay Roberts, sank in a storm with the loss of 45 lives.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives a different account of the Huntsman. It says the ship was lost on 28 April 1872 in the Strait of Belle Isle, not near the point, with its crew of forty-four men during the seal hunt.[^1] Neither page names a source for the loss, and Lighthouse Friends does not link the wreck to the light.[^1][^2]

The peninsula's page says the tower was built at the community's request.[^2] In 1880 Isaac Bartlett and other residents of Bay Roberts and neighbouring settlements on Conception Bay petitioned the Newfoundland House of Assembly for a light on Bay Roberts Point.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1882 the Inspector of Lighthouses, John T. Neville, noted that four cast-iron towers were ready for harbour lights, one of them for Bay Roberts Point. By the end of that year a store had been partly built on the point under contract, and the iron tower lay in storage at Bay Roberts until the weather opened enough to put it up.[^1]

Early in 1883 the tower was put up and fitted with its lamp. A keeper was named, and the light was lit.[^1] It was painted in red and white horizontal bands and showed a fixed white light from a sixth-order lens. A projecting lantern under the main light held three lamps. The Light List said the two outer lamps would show white, and the centre one, lined up with the southern rocks, red.[^1]

The height is given two ways. The peninsula's page gives 6.3 m (20.7 ft) for the tower, and the Coast Guard list gives 7.5 m (about 25 ft).[^2][^3] In 1885 a galvanized iron tank was partly buried at the station to hold the kerosene. The inspection report for 1895 said a porch was needed on the keeper's house.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends names Robert Andrews of Ship Cove as the first keeper. Its dated list has him from at least 1886 to 1901. A. Andrews followed in 1902 and 1903, then Richard Lear from 1904 to at least 1912. James Petten, Graham Petten and Richard French are named without dates.[^1]

The Heritage Society says Richard Lear lived in the keeper's house beside the tower.[^4] In 1931 the light was converted from kerosene to electricity, and a resident keeper was no longer needed. Lighthouse Friends says the dwelling was torn down a few years later, and that a nearby resident was hired as caretaker.[^1] The Newfoundland 3D gallery says the dwelling was demolished after the conversion.[^5] The Heritage Society, by contrast, says the house was taken apart and moved to Hussey's Cove, where it still stands.[^4] The sources do not agree on what became of the house.

## Today

Today the light is powered by solar-charged batteries.[^1] The peninsula's page says it is automated, and that the Coast Guard continues an operating presence.[^2]

Lighthouse Friends says the lighthouse was declared a Recognized Federal Heritage Building in 1990, and later a heritage lighthouse under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act, passed in 2008.[^1] The peninsula's page says Parks Canada recognized it as a heritage site in 2017 and put up a plaque to mark the designation.[^2] The dates differ, and they may describe different designations. On 5 August 2019 the community celebrated the transfer of the light and the land around it to the Port de Grave Peninsula Heritage Society.[^1]

The Heritage Society reports that a Reserve engineer regiment has been constructing viewing platforms and developing walking trails leading down to the lighthouse.[^4]

The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1] From Port de Grave, Lighthouse Friends says to head north-east on a road that begins across from the harbour. The road becomes rough and gradually disappears, so drivers should park near the last pair of houses and walk about a kilometre.[^1]
