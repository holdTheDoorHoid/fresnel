Sea Girt Light is a red-brick lighthouse on the New Jersey shore at Sea Girt, where the tower rises from the keeper's house as one building.[^2] It first showed its light on December 10, 1896,[^1][^2] and it is now a museum run by volunteers, open for tours on Sunday afternoons in season.[^13]

## The place

The coast between the Navesink and Barnegat lights was the gap the new light was meant to close.[^2] The two lights are 38½ miles apart,[^2] and LighthouseFriends prints the Light-House Board's report for 1887, which asked for a light at a point midway between them, estimated at $20,000.[^2] Congress provided the money on March 2, 1889.[^14]

The first site, close to Manasquan (Squan) Inlet, was found unfit before the sale closed.[^2] The acreage just south of Wreck Pond was examined next.[^2] The Board's report in the 1891–1892 volume records that a new site had been selected and that steps were under way to buy it and obtain the title.[^3] In 1894 the purchase was still held up by difficulties over a clear title.[^4] By 1895 the Board believed the difficulties had been removed, and it reported that the title papers were in the hands of the Department of Justice.[^5] The deed was signed on July 29, 1895.[^2]

The station was called Squan Inlet until the Board's annual report recorded its renaming to Sea Girt in February 1897.[^6] The Sea Girt News gives March 1, 1897 for the change,[^14] and LighthouseFriends says the name held until April.[^2] Before the light, the place had been called Wreck Pond, after a small body of water just north of where the Manasquan River reaches the sea.[^2]

## Building the light

The dwelling was an L-shaped red-brick Victorian house with an attached tower that rose just above the roofline.[^2] The Citizens Committee calls it the last live-in lighthouse built on the Atlantic coast.[^13] The brass work for the lamp was built at Tompkinsville on Staten Island, and the lens was ground in France.[^14] It was a fourth-order Fresnel lens, set in the lantern room.[^2]

Drifting sand troubled the grounds.[^2] In 1899 the grounds were seeded with lawn grass, and a flagstaff and a telephone were added.[^7][^2] In 1900 the exterior was repainted and a 240-foot (73 m) sand fence was put up to hold back the drifting sands.[^8] LighthouseFriends says that by 1906 the inlet beside the station had moved south, and that a short pile jetty followed in 1907.[^2]

## The keepers

The first keeper, Major Abraham Wolf, was a retired army officer who was paid $1.10 a day.[^12] The archive's roster has him from 1896 to 1902; the committee has him retiring in 1903.[^1][^12] Both pages say he had kept the Absecon light at Atlantic City before coming here, but they disagree on how long: twenty-three years according to LighthouseFriends, twenty-five according to the committee.[^2][^12] Both tell of his war, but they differ in detail. LighthouseFriends says that as a Union officer he would don a grey uniform and go undercover among captured Confederate soldiers, affecting a southern drawl.[^2] The committee says that his commanding officer had him dress in a Confederate uniform, that Union soldiers escorted him to Fort Delaware, and that he mingled among the Confederates there.[^12] Neither page gives a contemporary record for it, so it stays attributed to them. A later keeper described him as a "convivial soul."[^2]

Abram L. Yates followed.[^1] The committee says that he died at the lighthouse on May 29, 1910, at the age of 58, and that his wife entered his death in the logbook.[^12] Harriet Yates, his widow, took command of the light for about two months.[^12][^13] The committee's heading for her term gives 1903, but its text and the archive place it in 1910.[^1][^12] She applied for the permanent post, and the Board turned her down on the ground that she was not a member of the civil service, "although it was likely because she was a woman," in the committee's words.[^12]

John Hawkey arrived on July 23, 1910.[^12] The archive lists him as John L. Hawkey, and the committee as John W. Hawkey.[^1][^12] In 1912, the committee says, he changed the light from a kerosene wick lamp to an incandescent oil-vapor lamp.[^12] That same year he asked for permission to put a hen house on the north lawn.[^12] The reply told him to "take this matter up with the Inspecting Officer" at the next inspection, and the committee finds no evidence that a hen house was ever built.[^12]

William H. H. Lake, Jr., kept the light from 1917 to 1931.[^1] The committee says he was born on Staten Island, had served twelve years at Fire Island before two more postings, and arrived here in 1917.[^12] It also says that during his tour he oversaw the 1921 radio beacon.[^12] The Commissioner's report for 1921 lists Sea Girt, with the Ambrose and Fire Island lightships, among the stations whose radio signals were in commission.[^9] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports that bearings taken from the signals were "found accurate within 2 degrees."[^10] In 1924 the lamp was changed to an electric one, and the committee says that Lake then told a reporter: "The service is not what it used to be."[^12]

George J. Thomas, a widower, came in October 1931 and was the last keeper the Lighthouse Service assigned to Sea Girt.[^12] His last day of active duty was November 29, 1940.[^12] He retired on March 31, 1941, with a pension of $1,015 a year.[^12]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Service's Bulletin for 1915 notes storm damage at the station.[^11] LighthouseFriends says that more protection work was built that year, at a cost of $4,771.[^2]

On September 8, 1934, the liner *Morro Castle* caught fire in a raging storm three miles off the coast.[^12][^13] The committee says the crew took their bearings from the Sea Girt beacon, and that the lighthouse, under keeper George Thomas, served as a first-aid station.[^13] A son of keeper Lake was one of six Sea Girt lifeguards who, the committee says, saved 15 passengers.[^12]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service passed to the Coast Guard on July 1, 1939.[^13] When the United States entered the Second World War, the light in the tower was put out and the lens taken down.[^2] The Sea Girt News dates the lens's removal to 1941 or 1942.[^14] The Coast Guard fitted the house as quarters for its men.[^2] The committee counts as many as fifteen men; LighthouseFriends says the quarters held up to twenty.[^12][^2] Men stood watch in the tower and patrolled the beach with dogs.[^12]

After the war an automatic light was set on top of the tower and the lighthouse was decommissioned in 1945.[^12] In 1954 or 1955 the Coast Guard moved the light to a freestanding metal tower on the lawn.[^14] In 1956 the federal government sold the building to the Borough of Sea Girt for $11,000, leaving out the 22-by-24-foot (6.7 by 7.3 m) plot under the metal tower.[^12][^2] The borough used the house as a library and community center for more than two decades.[^13] The light on the metal tower was turned off in 1977, and the tower was dismantled.[^12]

By 1980 the building was in poor repair and closed.[^13] The Sea Girt Lighthouse Citizens Committee was formed in 1981 and took a 25-year lease at $1 a year; the lease has since been extended to 2056.[^13] The tower light was installed in the lantern room in 1982.[^14] In 2002 a fourth-order lens, formerly used at Crowdy Head Lighthouse in Australia, was bought on eBay for $20,000 and put on display.[^2]

## Today

The Borough of Sea Girt owns the building, and the Citizens Committee runs it under the lease.[^12][^13] Tours run on Sundays from 2 to 4 p.m., from mid-April to the Sunday before Thanksgiving, except on holiday weekends; the 2026 season runs from April 12 to November 22.[^13] Visitors are taken up into the lantern room.[^13] The committee says the building is in use some 200 days a year.[^13] The lantern light is listed as an active aid to navigation, and as a private one.[^1] The 22-by-24-foot (6.7 by 7.3 m) plot with the metal tower on it remains federal property.[^12]
