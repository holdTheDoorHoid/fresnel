Point Escuminac Lighthouse stands on a low, flat point at the southern entrance to Miramichi Bay, about 8 km east of the village of Escuminac on New Brunswick's north-east coast.[^1][^4][^5] The tower there now is a hexagonal concrete tower built in 1965 or 1966, the fourth light on the point.[^4][^5] The first, an octagonal wooden tower lit in 1841, was demolished in 1966.[^1][^2][^5]

## The place

The point marks the southern entrance to Miramichi Bay and, with North Point on Prince Edward Island, the northern entrance to Northumberland Strait.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the name Escuminac comes from a Mi'kmaq word meaning "watching place" or "look-out place".[^1]

During an address in 1820, George S. Smyth, the Lieutenant Governor, told the House of Assembly that he would take the requisite steps to see a lighthouse built on Point Escuminac.[^1] Twenty years later none had been built.[^1] Lighthouse Friends thinks the delay was because no law had been passed to levy duties on Miramichi shipping to pay for one.[^1] In 1840 a petition from the two most prominent Miramichi merchants, Gilmour Rankin and Joseph Cunard, and 100 other merchants or prominent citizens spurred the House to grant money for the project.[^1]

The sources disagree about the rock under the light. Lighthouse Friends calls the point "fragile sandstone", while the federal heritage register calls it a shale point.[^1][^4]

## Building the light

The light went into operation on 12 August 1841, the tenth lighthouse in New Brunswick and the first on its northern coast.[^1] Its octagonal tower was built of hand-hewn lumber held together with wooden pins and covered with pine shingles. Six Argand lamps and reflectors gave a fixed white light 21 m (70 ft) above high water.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the station was "quite isolated" until money was provided in 1846 to complete a road to it.[^1]

Before 1860 the shore lost about 46 cm (a foot and a half) a year to erosion.[^1] In a great easterly storm that year about 9 m (thirty feet) were lost in a few hours.[^1] The buildings stayed where they were until 1879, although a move to a spot about 120 m (400 ft) inland was recommended after the storm of 1862, and strongly backed by Keeper Hay.[^1]

In September 1869 the worn-out lantern and lighting apparatus were replaced by a third-order French Fresnel lens and a new lantern, at a cost of $1,301.[^1] A fog whistle went into operation on 1 June 1874 and sounded ten seconds in each minute of fog. It cost $4,871.[^1] A foghorn replaced it in 1887.[^1]

The fog signal was changed again in the 1900s. In July 1903 the steam fog-alarm was shut down for a few days for repairs.[^6] In 1906 and 1907 the old fog-alarm building was converted into an engine room for a new diaphone plant, which the Canadian Fog Signal Company of Toronto furnished for $4,600.[^7][^9] Lighthouse Friends calls it a three-inch diaphone.[^1] In 1909 a 55 mm Diamond vapour installation replaced the duplex lamp in the light.[^10] A new keeper's dwelling and boathouse were built under contract by Edward Rourke of St. John West in 1910, for $1,975.[^1][^12] An electric lighting plant was installed in 1914.[^1]

The present tower is a hexagonal reinforced-concrete shaft with a flared top, based on a prototype developed for the Cape Forchu light and built during a national upgrading campaign.[^4][^5] The register gives 1965 as the year of the build; Lighthouse Friends and Rowlett give 1966.[^1][^2][^4]

## The keepers

John McEwan was hired as the first keeper.[^1] William Hay kept the light from 1845 until his death in 1873. Lighthouse Friends says he died "after twenty-nine years of service", although its own keeper dates for him begin in 1845.[^1] Thomas Phillips took over in 1873 and tended both the light and the fog alarm until his death in 1888, when James Carter was appointed. Carter was paid $700 as keeper of the light and engineer of the fog alarm, and he paid an assistant out of that.[^1]

Carter and H. W. Phillips, who kept the meteorological and signal stations, were both dismissed in 1892 after charges against them were confirmed in an investigation.[^1] Carter had submitted several fraudulent accounts totalling $48. H. W. Phillips was dismissed for unauthorized absences from his stations. In his reply to the dismissal he asked the Department to reconsider, pointing to "being here for eighteen years in connection with this station".[^1]

Kenneth Ross McLennan took charge of all the operations at the point, including the signal and meteorological stations, in 1892, for a salary of $700 according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] The Department's keepers' table for the year ended June 1904 lists "McLennan, Kenneth" against "Escuminac Light and Fog Alarm".[^16] Lighthouse Friends gives his service as 1892 to 1939, then Kenneth Bliss McLennan from 1939 to 1969, Merrill William Trenholm from 1969 to 1983, and P. Durell from 1983 to 1987.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the early hours of 30 September 1901 the schooner *Levinia*, from Tignish, Prince Edward Island, was making for Miramichi in a thick haze. Her captain said he picked up the light but that the foghorn did not sound until he had passed north of it, thirty minutes later.[^1] Six pilots employed at Miramichi Bay gave testimony that the horn was often not audible south of the station.[^1] McLennan was cleared by his own logbook, which showed the fog alarm running from 11:10 p.m. on 29 September until 7:50 the next morning.[^1]

A life-saving station was established at Escuminac in 1908.[^8] E. F. Fleiger is listed against Point Escuminac, with the year 1908, in the Department's 1909 table of stations.[^11] The report for the year ended June 1913 records that on 16 November the life-boat crew rescued two men from the fishing smack *Cormier* in a gale of wind.[^14] That date falls in the 1912 part of that fiscal year.[^14]

The Chatham port warden's survey printed in the report for the year ended June 1912 concerns cargo salved from the schooner *Minnie F. Crosby*, stranded on Escuminac reef at the entrance to the Miramichi River. Its molasses and sugar were landed at Chatham.[^13] A survey printed in the report for the year ended June 1915 records that, by 2 June 1914, a St. John schooner had struck the ground off the light, unshipped her rudder and sprung a leak while waiting at Chatham to discharge a cargo of coal.[^15]

On the night of 19 to 20 June 1959 a storm struck the fishing fleet. The Labour History in New Brunswick project records that 35 fishermen, young boys as well as adults, died.[^17] Lighthouse Friends says 54 boats had set out from Escuminac Harbour that evening in pursuit of salmon. The forecast had not anticipated the strength of the storm, and the boats carried no radios. It says that twenty-two boats sank and that twenty-six widows and eighty-three fatherless children were left behind. It adds that "a powerful light and a working foghorn" could not prevent the disaster.[^1] A stone monument, *Les Pêcheurs*, was unveiled on the Escuminac Wharf in 1969, with a bronze plaque naming the victims and survivors.[^17]

## After the keepers

In early 1965 the Miramichi Historical Society learned that the old lighthouse was to be demolished. The Department of Transport said the lens was to go to another major light station and offered the society the lantern room. The society wanted only the lens. The old lighthouse was demolished in November 1966, and no remnant of it remains in the local community.[^1]

The keepers' dwellings were lost, the fog alarm building was replaced, and a generator and radio building was added, while the point took on a new role as a weather station.[^5] The station was de-staffed in 1989.[^1] The Government of Canada recognised the present tower as a federal heritage building in 2008.[^4]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse and is its site manager.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends and Rowlett both say the grounds are open and the tower is closed to visitors.[^1][^2] The Coast Guard's list gives the light a white flash every three seconds, at a height of 22 m, with a nominal range of 13 nautical miles, operating day and night, and a fog horn.[^3][^5] Rowlett describes the light as three white flashes every twenty seconds, so the two sources disagree on its characteristic.[^2]

The lighthouse is reached from Route 117 at Escuminac: the pavement ends after 4.6 km, and a dirt road runs another 5 km. By 2017, Lighthouse Friends says, the road had deteriorated significantly, and only a high-clearance vehicle was advised.[^1]
