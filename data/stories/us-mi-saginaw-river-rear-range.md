The Saginaw River Rear Range Light is a pair of range lights at the mouth of the Saginaw River, on the western bank just north of Bay City, where the river meets Saginaw Bay on Lake Huron.[^1][^2] The stone light that first marked the river mouth in 1841 was pulled down in 1876, when two new range towers replaced it. The rear tower and its dwelling still stand.[^1][^2]

## The place

Michigan's constitution of 1835 made it the state's duty to encourage internal improvements such as roads and canals. One canal of the time was to link the Saginaw River with the Grand River, which flows into Lake Michigan.[^1]

Congress approved $5,000 for a lighthouse at the river's mouth on 3 March 1837.[^1] When Lieutenant James T. Homans visited the following year, he noted that no preparations had been made for it, and he described the place's commerce as "considerable, and increasing."[^1]

The bar at the river's mouth is the feature the sources return to. In 1866 a project was proposed for dredging a twelve-foot (3.7 m) deep channel through the bar, and in October 1867 the Army Corps of Engineers cut one 6,800 feet (2,070 m) long and 195 feet (59 m) wide.[^1] In 1839 the government had bought forty acres at the mouth of the river from the Chippewa Indians.[^1]

## Building the light

The first light was a sixty-two-foot (19 m) stone tower, completed in 1841 by Levi Johnson, with a one-and-a-half-story dwelling beside it measuring thirty-four by twenty feet (10 by 6 m).[^1] Seven lamps in fourteen-inch (36 cm) reflectors gave a fixed white light visible for up to fifteen miles. A sixth-order lens replaced the lamps in 1856.[^1]

The first keeper, Harvey Williams, had built the first two steam sawmills on the Saginaw River, and he later lost his fortune in land speculation. He served two years and moved on in 1843.[^1]

In 1869 the Lighthouse Board recommended range lights "to show the way into the river," because the existing light "only shows its whereabouts." Congress appropriated $12,000 for them on 15 July 1870.[^1] The trouble was the swamp land the towers needed. When its owner asked $2,500 for the sites, the Board asked the governor of Michigan for a condemnation commission, and the commission set the compensation at $17,496.84. The Board then abandoned the project and returned the unused money to the treasury.[^1]

Congress provided $23,000 on 3 March 1875 for range lights and a new dwelling, and work on the range lights began in May 1876.[^1] On 15 September 1876 the old light was discontinued. Julia Brawn, the widow of the keeper, exhibited the lights of the new range, and the old tower and dwelling were demolished.[^1]

The front light was a square pyramidal tower, 34 feet (10 m) high, built on a timber crib, with a sixth-order lens showing a fixed red light. The rear light stood 2,310 feet (704 m) upstream, in one corner of a two-story dwelling. Its tower was 53 feet (16 m) high, of yellow brick, and its foundation was timber piles driven into the swampy ground.[^1]

On the night of 15 June 1891 the Board changed the rear light from fixed white to fixed red.[^3] In 1896 a plank walk 625 feet (191 m) long was built from the rear light to the front one.[^4]

## The keepers

Peter Brawn was appointed keeper in 1866. LighthouseFriends says he was reportedly disabled, and that much of the tending fell to his fifteen-year-old son Dewitt and his wife Julia.[^1] The archive roster lists him as Peter Brawn, Jr., from 1866 to 1873.[^2]

Peter died in 1873, and Julia took charge. LighthouseFriends says she was head keeper until 1877; the archive roster calls her acting principal keeper from 1873 to 1877.[^1][^2] She had married George Way in 1875. When he was placed in charge of the light in 1877, she became first assistant, a post abolished in October 1882. It was reinstated the next spring and filled by Leonidas Charlton (LighthouseFriends spells him Leonidus). George Way died in November 1883, and Julia, then sixty-seven, left the station after seventeen years there.[^1][^2]

The roster also lists Nellie Buzzard as first assistant from 1883 to 1886, under Edward John Buzzard as head keeper. The sources do not say how the two were related.[^2]

William A. Burke was appointed head keeper in 1900 and served until 1939, "far longer than any other keeper at the range lights," as LighthouseFriends puts it.[^1][^2] He had begun his career as third assistant keeper at Spectacle Reef in 1894 and became second assistant there. In 1896 he was appointed head keeper of Frying Pan Island Lighthouse.[^1] The roster carries the station on to Gustav W. Hanson, listed as keeper from at least 1942.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources on this station record no wreck tied to the range lights. The rescues recorded are the Lighthouse Service's own lists of people credited with saving life or property.

The Commissioner of Lighthouses listed "W. A. Burke, keeper, Saginaw River Range Light Station" among those credited with saving life or property in the fiscal years 1917, 1918 and 1919.[^7][^8][^9] LighthouseFriends says Burke towed a disabled sailboat to safety in 1917 and rescued stranded motorboats in 1918 and 1919. In 1925 he used the station's boat "to free a steam tug that had gone ashore near the range lights."[^1]

The Service's *Bulletin* gives the 1925 incident in more detail. On 10 April, Burke went to a steam tug "ashore near the lighthouse," and after "working with the station motor boat for one and a half hours" he succeeded in releasing it and towed it to deep water.[^10]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over the lights in 1939. Its crews stayed in the rear light until 1981, when a new station was built at Essexville.[^1]

The two range lights were changed from oil to electric incandescent lamps in 1916, according to the Service's *Bulletin*.[^5][^6] LighthouseFriends dates the electric lights to 1915, the year the front light was rebuilt, and says electric lights were then installed in both towers.[^1] The front light was replaced in 1915 by a black, skeletal steel tower, and its timber crib was replaced by concrete.[^1]

The rear tower light was deactivated in 1963, according to LighthouseFriends, when a new channel was dredged and a new set of range lights was needed to mark it. The US Lighthouse Society's place record gives 1960 as the year the station was discontinued, and the two dates are not reconciled here.[^1][^2]

After the Coast Guard left, the rear tower and dwelling were sold into private hands in 1981. Dow Chemical, which owned adjoining land, bought the lighthouse and its reservation in 1989, and the company boarded up the lighthouse's windows and doors.[^1] In 1999 the Saginaw River Marine Historical Society agreed with Dow to restore the abandoned lighthouse to its 1950s appearance. Its volunteers replaced the dwelling's roof and broken windows and removed the lath and plaster.[^1] The dwelling near the front light was demolished in 2018, after it had deteriorated beyond repair.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by Dow Chemical. The grounds, dwelling and tower are closed, and access is restricted to members of the Saginaw River Marine Historical Society during scheduled work sessions.[^1] The society has opened the structure for tours during the tall-ship celebrations in Bay City in 2006, 2010, 2013 and 2016.[^1] The Fresnel lens from the lighthouse is on display at the Coast Guard station in Bay City.[^1] Visitors should not enter the closed grounds or climb the tower.
