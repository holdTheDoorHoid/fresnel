Yerba Buena Light stands on the southern slope of Yerba Buena Island, an island of about 140 acres in the middle of San Francisco Bay, between San Francisco and Oakland.[^1] Its small octagonal tower, the only freestanding wood-frame lighthouse tower still operating in California,[^1] was finished in 1873 and first lit on 1 October 1875 with a fifth-order Fresnel lens.[^1][^7] The National Register nomination says that lens "has guided ships in the central bay for well over a century."[^1][^7]

## The place

More maritime traffic passed Yerba Buena than any other island in the bay, the nomination says, and thousands of commuters passed it every day.[^1] Ferry routes made the island dangerous, and fog was the bigger hazard: "Fog was the biggest hazard at Yerba Buena."[^1]

The Spanish named the island for the mint that grew on it.[^1] When Americans arrived in 1849 they brought goats, and LighthouseFriends says the island soon became Goat Island.[^1][^7] Goat-herding squatters stayed until 1870, when an Army base drove them off. The base burned, and the Army never came back.[^1]

In 1912 the station's name changed.[^3] The Lighthouse Service's bulletin reported that "The name of Yerba Buena light station" had been changed to Goat Island light station.[^3] LighthouseFriends quotes Harry W. Rhodes, the inspector of lighthouses in California, as remarking that the people of San Francisco "will regret that the geographic board has changed the name of the island."[^7] By 1932 the bulletin was describing "Extensive improvements to the Yerba Buena Depot (formerly Goat Island)."[^4]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $10,000 "to begin work on a tower, Keeper's residence, and bell house." The nomination dates that to 1872; LighthouseFriends dates it to 1874.[^1][^7] The nomination says the tower was complete in 1873, but the light waited for a lens.[^1] The lens was moved from the Yaquina Lighthouse in Oregon, and LighthouseFriends says it was a fifth-order lens from Yaquina Bay Lighthouse.[^1][^7] The nomination names Yaquina in one place and "Yakima" in another, and does not say which is right.[^1]

A fog bell from the Point Conception station, struck by a clock mechanism, came first.[^1][^7] The nomination quotes the 1873 Lighthouse Service report that the bell was "in place and operating in that year," but it also says a new steam fog-signal building "had been completed on October 1, 1875."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the light and fog signal went into operation on 1 October 1875, with H. D. Tuttle as the first head keeper.[^7]

The steam whistle needed water, which was often in short supply on the island.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the water was pumped by hand through iron pipes from a spring to a 10,000-gallon tank at the signal house.[^7] The nomination says that in the dry year of 1884 the fog-signal tanks "had to be filled by pumping from a harbor water boat."[^1] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900, the 10-inch steam whistle, in duplicate, "was in operation some 128 hours, and consumed about 17 tons of coal."[^2]

The light burned oil.[^7] LighthouseFriends says a Hains mineral-oil lamp replaced the lard-oil lamp inside the lens in 1880.[^7] LighthouseFriends also says a fourth-order lens replaced the fifth-order one in 1912, to increase the light's intensity, but the nomination describes a fifth-order lens still in use.[^7][^1]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists 55 keeper entries for the station from 1875 to 1958: head keepers, first and second assistants, an acting keeper and laborers.[^6]

Henry D. Tuttle was the first head keeper, from 1875 to 1877.[^6][^7] Herbert H. Luff was head keeper from 1904 to 1921, and was the chief keeper when the keepers' dwelling caught fire in 1909.[^6][^5] John P. Kofod served two stints at the station, first as an assistant and then as head keeper from 1921 to 1928.[^6][^7] LighthouseFriends says that in 1914 Kofod took the head keeper's post at East Brother Island, where he stayed until 1921. It also says he took his grandchildren to the fog signal on New Year's Eve, where the children could sound three long blasts on the steam whistle for the ferries going past.[^7]

Albert N. Speelman was head keeper from 1928 to 1939, and Lemuel C. Miner from 1939 to 1943.[^6] Wayne R. Piland was an assistant from 1941 to 1946 and head keeper from 1953 to 1958, when the light was automated.[^6][^7] The archive gives Frederick C. Zimmermann as head keeper from 1947 to 1953. LighthouseFriends gives his start as "at least 1946."[^6][^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the afternoon of 17 July 1909 the keepers' dwelling caught fire.[^5][^7] The *San Francisco Call* reported that the assistant keeper, Kofod, was in the kitchen when the flames broke through the roof, and that he gave the alarm to Chief Lighthouse Keeper Herbert Luff.[^5] The navy tug *Vigilant* sounded its alarm on its whistle, and the men on the island became "a Quick acting and determined fire fighting brigade."[^5] Sailors dragged a hose up the cliffs.[^5] The paper said a considerable part of the keepers' household goods was saved.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the roof and several upper rooms were destroyed.[^7]

## After the keepers

A tunnel was bored through the island in 1933 to link the east and west sections of the Bay Bridge, and the bridge was finished in 1936.[^7] The nomination says the bridge's completion "signalled the end of the ferryboat era."[^1]

In 1934 the fog signal was changed from a steam whistle to a diaphone, and the light was changed from fixed to flashing, according to LighthouseFriends.[^7] The tower was floodlighted in 1957 in an attempt to keep birds from crashing through the lantern-room windows.[^1] The Coast Guard automated the light in 1958.[^1] LighthouseFriends says personnel were removed from the station that year.[^7] The island was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1991.[^7]

## Today

The light still runs.[^7] LighthouseFriends says it remains operational, and the nomination says the lens, "where it has shone ever since," was moved here from Oregon.[^7][^1] The depot east of the light grew into Coast Guard Base San Francisco after 1960, and the nomination says the keepers' former quarters are now home to the district's Coast Guard commanding officer.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^6]
