Halfway Rock is a two-acre granite ledge in the middle of Casco Bay, Maine, with a stone tower on it that has marked the way to Portland since 1871. It stands roughly midway between Cape Elizabeth and Cape Small, which is how it got its name, and today the light is private property.[^1][^2]

## The place

The National Register nomination says the station was built to mark "a very heavily trafficked approach to Portland", on a shallow rock ledge in the bay.[^1] Lighthousefriends.com dates the demand for a light to 1835, when the brig *Samuel* of Bath struck the rock in a southeasterly gale; its captain and a crewman were swept overboard and lost, and the survivors sheltered in the ledges until morning. The same page reports a Navy officer's proposal that year for a stone monument on the rock, and the loss of the British bark *Boadicea* in February 1861 with all aboard.[^2] New England Lighthouses also names the *Samuel* in 1835 and notes that other ledges nearby had claimed many vessels.[^4]

## Building the light

The nomination quotes the Light-House Board's 1867 report: an estimate "to mark with a light-house this dangerous rock was submitted to Congress at its last session". Construction was authorized only in 1869, with $50,000 appropriated, and the nomination names the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers as designer.[^1] According to Lighthousefriends, granite blocks were brought from other Maine islands by boat and the surface of the rock was cut level; the balance of the money reverted to the Treasury on 30 June 1870, work was suspended, and Congress granted $10,000 more on 3 March 1871 to finish.[^2] The light was first shown on 15 August 1871, with a revolving third-order Fresnel lens, a white light with a red flash once a minute.[^1][^2] New England Lighthouses compares the dovetailed granite construction to Minot's Ledge Light in Massachusetts.[^4]

The sources disagree about the tower's height. The nomination puts it at nearly 66 feet (20 m) from base to the middle of the lantern, while the U.S. Lighthouse Society archive gives 76 feet (23 m) for the same measurement.[^1][^3]

The keepers lived inside the tower. Lighthousefriends describes a kitchen on the first floor, the principal keeper on the second, the two assistants on the third, and a watchroom with a small stove and desk beneath the lantern.[^2] Some years later D. P. Heap, writing in 1889, described the station as built on a rock "so swept by the sea that there is absolutely no soil".[^5] A pyramidal skeleton tower holding a 1,000-pound fog bell was added: Lighthousefriends and the Lighthouse Society archive give 1887, the nomination 1888.[^1][^2][^3] A wooden boathouse with sleeping rooms was built in 1889, though New England Lighthouses says 1888, and an elevated oil house followed in 1890.[^1][^2][^4] In 1905 a diesel-powered Daboll trumpet replaced the bell as the main fog signal, according to Lighthousefriends.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1912 lists Halfway Rock among the stations whose illuminant was changed to incandescent oil vapor.[^8]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society archive lists John T. Sterling as the first principal keeper, from 1871 to 1883; New England Lighthouses calls him Capt. John T. Sterling.[^3][^4] Three keepers were assigned, two on duty at a time and the third ashore, and Lighthousefriends says the tight quarters meant no families or spouses lived on the rock.[^2]

Isolation sometimes soured relations. In October 1885 a Lighthouse Board inspector reported on a quarrel between two men. Lighthousefriends quotes his account: the first assistant, William T. Holbrook, had complained that principal keeper George H. Toothaker "would often refuse to speak to him for a week or 10 days at a time without any apparent reason whatsoever". The inspector wrote that Toothaker admitted he "did get into a queer state of mind at times", and Toothaker resigned.[^2] The archive's roster has Toothaker as first assistant from 1873 and principal keeper from 1883 to 1885, with Holbrook succeeding him.[^3] New England Lighthouses names him George A. Toothaker, a Harpswell native, and quotes an interview long after he left: "Asleep or awake, the beacon haunts you."[^4]

John B. Dewyea had a long run: the roster has him as second assistant from 1892, then first assistant, then principal keeper from 1902 to 1921.[^3] Arthur S. Strout arrived in 1929 on the archive's dates (New England Lighthouses says 1928), became principal keeper in 1934 and stayed until 1944; the latter source says he became the first Coast Guard keeper there in 1939, when the Coast Guard took over the Lighthouse Service.[^3][^4] Coast Guard crews followed. When Kenneth Rouleau arrived in 1960, the small living quarters were in the upper floor of the boathouse, and the day was split into 12-hour shifts for the two men on the rock, so they saw little of each other.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The bell tower's first winter tested it: both Lighthousefriends and New England Lighthouses report a storm that put eight feet of water over the ledge, and the tower survived.[^2][^4] On 9 June 1899 the fog signal was disabled; the Light-House Board's report for fiscal 1899 says it was working again on 13 June.[^7]

The 1919 volume of the Lighthouse Service Bulletin records that on 15 November Dewyea and assistant keeper W. F. Reed "boarded the drifting wreck of the schooner Annie Conley and moored it near the light station." The Bulletin entry gives no year for the event itself.[^6]

In February 1934 the keepers reported ice an inch thick extending past the rock.[^4] In late 1935, according to Lighthousefriends, a storm cut the telephone cable to shore; Strout was ashore, and a nineteen-year-old amateur radio operator, Perley Swasey, raised assistant John Pendell and learned that waves had broken over the rock for eighteen hours and partly destroyed the boat slip.[^2] A 1942 magazine article, as summarised by New England Lighthouses, found Halfway Rock not blacked out in the war; it was later turned off and returned to service in 1945.[^4]

In October 1962 a combined nor'easter and hurricane swept away a porch, thirty feet of the boat-ramp track and half of the new helicopter landing pad, and keepers James E. Murray and Danny L. Harris moved into the granite tower for safety.[^2] A storm in February 1972 damaged the Fresnel lens and led to the decision to automate.[^2]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1975, as the nomination and the archive agree; the owner's book describes it as 1976.[^1][^3][^10] A solar-powered optic replaced the Fresnel lens, which Lighthousefriends reports went on display at the Coast Guard Academy.[^2][^3] A 2026 Portland Press Herald feature, however, refers to the original lens as having vanished, so its fate is unsettled.[^9]

Lighthousefriends reports that the American Lighthouse Foundation leased the station in 2000, that Maine Preservation listed it among the state's ten most endangered historic properties in 2004, and that when no public custodian came forward it was auctioned online, selling on 20 September 2014 for $283,000.[^2] Its buyer, Ford Reiche of Freeport, has repointed the tower and restored the interior and boathouse.[^9]

## Today

Halfway Rock Light is privately owned and is not open to the public; the archive lists it as an active aid to navigation.[^3] The Portland Press Herald reported in 2026 that only about 25 people a year reach the ledge, which lies about ten miles off Portland and is landed on with an amphibious boat.[^9] There is no public landing, and it should be seen from the water at a distance.
