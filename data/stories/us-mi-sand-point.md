Sand Point Light stands at the far west end of Escanaba, Michigan, on the shore of Little Bay de Noc. It is a brick tower on a story-and-a-half dwelling, built in 1867 to mark a sand reef at the approach to an iron-ore port. It was first lit in May 1868 and kept for just shy of eighteen years by Mary Terry, who died in a fire in the keeper's house in 1886.[^7][^8][^9]

## The place

Escanaba was founded in 1862 with a railroad linking the Negaunee iron mines to Little Bay de Noc.[^8] By the 1920s the Commissioner of Lighthouses described the port as "one of the most important on Lake Michigan," shipping about 7,000,000 tons of iron ore each season.[^6] The light was there to warn ships off Sand Point, a sand reef that reached out into the bay.[^7]

Congress had voted money for a light here before the station was built. In 1864 it appropriated $5,000 for a light on Sand Point, but the owner of the land "was unable to produce a clear title to the property," and the delay gave the Lighthouse Board time to reconsider its plans.[^8]

## Building the light

Congress provided another $9,000 "for the lighthouse on March 2, 1867."[^8] The Society says the station was built in 1867 "at a cost of $11K," and the Mining Gazette gives the cost as "$11,000."[^7][^9] The building was a "story-and-a-half, rectangular building" with an attached brick tower, "topped by a cast iron lantern room which housed a fourth order Fresnel lens."[^7]

The Society says that "the light, a fixed red signal, first showed on the night of May 13, 1868."[^7] LighthouseFriends describes the first lamp as a fixed white light, with the focal plane "forty-four feet above the surrounding water."[^8] The sources do not agree on the color.

In the 1920s the Commissioner's estimates asked for money to improve the light and fog signal and the dwellings: "Improving light and fog signal," with $71,000 in one year and $70,000 in the next.[^5][^6]

The Board first asked for a fog signal in the 1890s. In 1893 it wrote: "A fog bell struck by machinery in the light-station at Escanaba, on Sand Point, would be a valuable addition to the service of this station. It can be set up for about $1,100."[^2] The Board's 1896-97 report, quoting several earlier annual reports, said that "A steam signal here is not essential."[^3] The fog bell was finally installed in 1900. The Board reported "A bell struck by machinery April 18, 1900," and the bell tower came from the old Tail Point light station: "The bell tower at the old Tail Point light-station was taken apart and landed here for erection."[^4] LighthouseFriends says Keeper Rose "placed the fog bell in operation for the first time on May 1, 1900."[^8]

## The keepers

John Terry was the first keeper, appointed in 1867.[^1][^8] He died in 1868. LighthouseFriends dates his death to April 1868.[^8] The Mining Gazette says that "John died of consumption" before the lighthouse was finished.[^9] His widow, Mary L. Terry, lit the light on 13 May 1868 and kept it until 1886.[^1][^8] The Society calls her "one of the first women light keepers on the Great Lakes."[^7] The Mining Gazette says her appointment was controversial: the historical archives it cites say she was appointed "over the objections of government officials who strongly opposed the services of a woman in the lighthouse."[^9] She managed the light "for just shy of 18 years."[^9]

Lewis A. Rose followed her in 1886 and served for 27 years, the longest service of any keeper at Sand Point, according to LighthouseFriends.[^8][^1]

When Rose retired in 1913, Peter J. Peterson was brought from Wind Point Lighthouse to take charge.[^8] The accounts differ on what happened next. LighthouseFriends says that "just two months later he collapsed at the kitchen table and died," and that Captain Soren Kristiansen, master of the Eleven Foot Shoal lightship, was then made keeper.[^8] Peterson's widow was briefly temporary keeper, and LighthouseFriends says she "resigned shortly thereafter."[^8] The Mining Gazette dates Peterson's death to "his sudden death from a heart attack in April 1912."[^9] The archive lists Soren Kristiansen as keeper from 1913 to 1925.[^1]

Later keepers included James D. Armstrong (1925 to 1931), Elias Garrett (1931 to 1934) and Daniel A. Garrett (1934 to at least 1950), as the archive gives them.[^1] The Society says "Nine keepers and their families lived in the Sand Point Lighthouse."[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The best-documented event in the station's history is the fire of 1886. The Board's report for that year, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, reads: "The light-keeper was burned to death and the station damaged by a fire which originated in the dwelling on the night of March 4, 1886."[^8] The Mining Gazette gives the date as 5 March, "when a deadly fire broke out inside the lighthouse."[^9] The Society says the fire "severely damaged the building" and put the light out of commission for a time.[^7] The repairs cost $2,362.72, and "The light was shown on the opening of navigation."[^8]

The origin of the fire is not known. The Mining Gazette says that "The exact origin of the fire remains unknown," and that a local coroner's jury ruled the cause of Mary Terry's death unknown.[^9] It also reports that "People have since speculated" about murder, robbery or arson.[^9] None of the sources confirms any of these explanations.

No wreck or rescue at the station itself is named in these sources.

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over the lights in 1939. The Society says that "When the US Coast Guard took over all navigational lights in the country from the National Lighthouse Service in 1939, changes came to the Sand Point Lighthouse."[^7] The Mining Gazette gives the end of the station's operation as "from 1868 to 1938," a date that differs from the Society's 1939.[^9] By then the harbor had been dredged and filled, leaving the light "some distance from the hazard for which it had been giving warning."[^7] The Coast Guard built a crib light offshore, and "This crib light is still in use today."[^7]

After major alterations, the building "became the family residence for the Officer-in-Charge of the station." Its tower was reduced: "The lens and lantern room were removed, and the height of the tower reduced by ten feet."[^7] In 1985 the Coast Guard decided to stop using the building and "indicated that it might be razed."[^7] A lease from the Coast Guard was negotiated, and the Mining Gazette reports "nearly $100,000 in fundraising and volunteer efforts" for the restoration.[^7][^9] LighthouseFriends says that "the lighthouse opened to the public in 1990," and that a replica fourth-order lens "was installed in the lantern room of Sand Point Lighthouse" in 2014.[^8]

## Today

The Delta County Historical Society runs the lighthouse as a museum. It is open "every day from Memorial Day through Labor Day from 1:00 to 4:00 p.m."; the Society says "it is a good idea to call ahead to check on our hours."[^7] LighthouseFriends gives a different summer schedule, open "daily from 11 a.m. to 4 p.m. between Memorial Day and Labor Day."[^8]

Ownership is also described differently. The Society says a lease from the Coast Guard was negotiated.[^7] LighthouseFriends says "Ownership of the lighthouse was transferred to the society in 1998."[^8] The Mining Gazette says the property "remains technically under Coast Guard ownership," while "the Historical Society owns the surrounding land and maintains and occupies the site."[^9] The historic Fresnel lens "is now on exhibit at Delta County Historical Society's museum," and the lantern room holds a replica.[^8]
