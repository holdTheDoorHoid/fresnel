Sandy Point Shoal Light is a brick house on a round iron foundation, set in a few feet of water about 1,000 yards off the beach at Sandy Point State Park in the upper Chesapeake Bay. It replaced an earlier lighthouse on the shore, was tended by resident keepers until 1963, and has been privately owned and dark since 2019.[^1][^10]

## The place

Sandy Point juts from Maryland's western shore, and in the Light-House Board's words shoals run out from it into the bay for about a mile.[^1] The first lighthouse here went up on the land in 1858. Congress appropriated $8,000 for it on 3 August 1854, and about two acres, 600 feet north of the point, were bought from Baptist Mezick and his wife Mary for $526.[^1]

The shore site did not work. In 1874 the Light-House Board reported that vessels drawing more than ten feet of water could not come within about a mile of the light, and that the fog bell, "on account of its distance from the channel," could seldom be heard. Nearly all the passenger steamers bound for Baltimore changed course at this point, and the Board called this "a hazardous undertaking to boats crowded with passengers and running on time, when neither the light can be seen nor the fog bell heard."[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the shore station's fog bell, rung by machinery, to 1863.[^2]

## Building the light

The Board asked Congress for $40,000 to move the light to the outer edge of the shoal. It was refused, and the Board lowered the request to $30,000 the next year, then asked for $25,000 in 1876, $20,000 in 1877 and $25,000 each year until 1881, all without result. On 7 August 1882 Congress authorized $25,000. The Board now thought $40,000 necessary because of the exposed site and the need for a sturdier, ice-resistant structure. Refused more money, it went ahead with a caisson lighthouse but built a cheaper brick tower in place of the planned cast-iron one.[^1] The Governor of Maryland conveyed about five acres of submerged land on 21 April 1883.[^1]

The work was done in a single season. In August 1883 the contractor's crew built a working platform to hold the caisson in place, and by 24 August the wooden caisson and the first course of cast-iron plates had been assembled and were sunk the next day. Temporary quarters and a kitchen were put up on shore and joined to the work site by a "plank walk." By 26 September about 1,000 cubic yards of concrete had been poured. The brick tower was finished on 9 October and the lantern two days later.[^1] LighthouseFriends, citing a Light-House Board report, says the light was ready for exhibition on 18 October but was not shown until 30 October 1883, pending the official notice to mariners.[^2] The National Register nomination gives the same date for the light entering service.[^1] The nomination names W. J. Humes as the contractor of the 1858 house and as the builder of the present one for the Light-House Board.[^1]

What they built was a cast-iron cylinder 35 feet (11 m) across and 32 feet 6 inches (9.9 m) high, filled with concrete, carrying a brick dwelling 24 feet (7.3 m) square with its corners cut off, giving it an octagonal plan. The two lower floors held the living quarters. Below them, inside the cylinder, was a room for water, coal and oil, and a hollow iron column carried the weights that drove the fog bell.[^1]

The lens is a point on which the sources part company. The nomination calls the original lens a fourth-order one and gives the maker as "Henry Le Pante"; it dates a red sector to 1890.[^1] LighthouseFriends writes the maker as Henry-Lepaute, thinks the old fifth-order lens was probably moved from the shore light in 1883, and says the fourth-order lens was put in only in May 1890, to give a fixed light.[^2] The same writer reports that a red sector, added to the light on 15 April 1888 to mark the entrance to Craighill Channel, did not reliably define it when the light flashed.[^2] The nomination adds that by 1890 the brick had "scaled off in many places."[^1]

The shore lighthouse was abandoned. The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 records that the old material in the buildings at Sandy Point, with those of three other discontinued light-stations, was sold at public sale in June.[^3]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the National Archives' keeper registers, lists keepers and Coast Guard crew between 1859 and 1962. William J.W. Yewell is the first, for 1859 to 1860, and Mary E. Yewell follows him as head keeper for 1860 to 1861.[^4] The archive also lists two women as first assistant at the shore station, Alice E. Allen (1865 to 1867) and Mary Jane Whiteford (1867), and a Mary J. Parkinson (1874 to 1877) in the same post.[^4] The sources do not say how any of them were related to the men in the list.

John Reynolds was head keeper from 1886 to 1900, the longest stay of the 1800s.[^4] The winter of 1895 is the best-recorded moment of it. On 16 February 1895 the Annapolis *Evening Capital* reported that a tug captain had told of Reynolds being ill at the lighthouse and wanting to be taken off, and that the tug could not get near.[^5] An Annapolis dispatch to the Baltimore *Sun*, reprinted in Wisconsin in April, said that Reynolds, suffering from rheumatism, had sent his assistant ashore for medicine and was alone at the light for sixteen days while ice piled sixteen feet high around the house. His condition, the dispatch said, meant "the lights were regulated with difficulty, the keeper having to crawl on his hands and knees to the tower in the fourth story."[^6]

Edward Jansen was head keeper from 1908 to 1927, nearly twenty years.[^4] The Chesapeake Chapter of the U.S. Lighthouse Society, which spells his name Jensen, lists an efficiency silver star for him in 1915 and an inspector's efficiency star in 1918.[^7] Carl Geiling followed him from 1927 to 1940.[^4]

The assistants came and went much faster. The archive lists four men whose single term as first assistant began and ended in 1907 alone.[^4] When the nearby Baltimore Light was automated in 1923, a second assistant keeper was added here to look after it, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] A description of the station written in September 1938 found one keeper and one assistant,[^1] though the archive lists a second assistant, Emory W. Cherricks, from 1926 to 1944.[^4] The sources do not settle the difference.

In the 1940s the archive begins to list Coast Guard men, and its last head-keeper entry is Tom Medlin, at least 1959 to at least 1962.[^4] The nomination notes that while the Coast Guard lived in the house, the officer in charge used one of the second-floor bedrooms, 9 by 12 feet, as his quarters.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The winter of 1895 is the one storm story the sources tie to this light (see above). A second event was a failure of the sea floor. During a routine inspection in 1901, soundings showed "an extensive scour in the immediate vicinity" of the lighthouse below the foot of the caisson, which within weeks had grown nearly to the point of needing emergency work.[^1] The Board's report for fiscal 1901 lists Sandy Point among the Maryland lights where soundings were made around the light-house and repairs done.[^8] In 1902 the McClenahan Granite Company of Port Deposit laid about 670 cubic yards of riprap around the foundation.[^1]

## After the keepers

The light changed in step with the technology. The oil wick burner gave way to an incandescent oil-vapor lamp in 1913.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded that in December 1929 the station was electrified, with duplicate 2-kilowatt generators, storage batteries and flashers, and the light changed from fixed to flashing.[^9][^1]

The light was automated on 14 May 1963, after which the fog signal sounded every 30 seconds in fog.[^1] In June 1979 the Fresnel lens was found smashed by vandals. The Coast Guard offered a reward, but the nomination says the culprits seem never to have been found.[^1] Personnel of the cutter *Red Birch* rehabilitated the house in July 1988, replacing the eave cornice brackets, and painted it inside and out in 1990, and in August 1995 a new copper-sheathed roof and gallery repairs were done.[^1] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2002.[^10]

The federal government declared the lighthouse excess in 2005. When it was offered to non-profit groups under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, none came forward, and an internet auction closed with a winning bid of $250,000 on 28 June 2006.[^2][^7] In 2019 the Coast Guard notified mariners that it was discontinuing the light because "the deteriorating condition of the Sandy Point Shoal Light structure" had become "a severe health hazard and a hazard to access for maintenance crews," as LighthouseFriends quotes the notice.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is privately owned and not open to the public.[^10][^2] It stands offshore and the nomination says access is by boat; it is not a place to land.[^1] The best views are from the shore at Sandy Point State Park, or from the water.[^2]
