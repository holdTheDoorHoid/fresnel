Nauset Light stands on a bluff above Nauset Beach in Eastham, inside the Cape Cod National Seashore.[^1][^3] Its tower is the cast-iron tower that was built at Chatham in 1877 and moved here in 1923. In 1996 it was moved again, across the road, before the cliff could take it.[^1][^3] The keeper's house dates from 1875, and the 1892 oil house still stands beside them.[^2]

## The place

Shipwrecks went on along Eastham's shore, and citizens petitioned the government for a lighthouse, according to the Nauset Light Preservation Society's timeline.[^4] The first lights here were three towers, and the nickname "Three Sisters" came from a saying that, seen from the sea, they looked like three ladies in white dresses wearing black hats.[^4]

The tower that stands now was built as the twin of a tower at Chatham.[^1] The Massachusetts survey form records its measurements as identical to that twin.[^2]

## Building the light

In 1838 the government put up three brick towers, about 15 feet (4.6 m) tall and 150 feet (46 m) apart, with stationary lamps.[^4] The site, with a keeper's house, was bought from Sally and Benjamin H.A. Collins for $150, and the towers were erected for $7050.01, the survey form says.[^2] In 1858 the lamps were given sixth-order Fresnel lenses, and in 1873 those were replaced with fourth-order lenses.[^4]

A second keeper's house went up in 1875, the survey form and the Society's timeline both say. LighthouseFriends dates the dwelling built for the Three Sisters to 1876.[^2][^3][^4]

In 1877 the two Chatham lights were rebuilt as twin cast-iron towers, each 48 feet (14.6 m) tall and weighing 90 to 100 tons.[^4][^3] In April 1892 the Light-House Board reported that each of the three Eastham lights had been moved to a new tower about 30 feet (9 m) west of the old one, with the lights raised 4 feet (1.2 m).[^5] The survey form calls this replacement of the brick towers, which erosion had taken, with three wooden towers.[^2]

In 1911 the center tower was fitted with a revolving Fresnel lens that flashed three times in a sequence, and it came to be called The Beacon.[^4] In 1923 the north tower at Chatham was moved in seven sections and rebuilt in Eastham, about 200 feet (61 m) from the cliff edge.[^2][^1][^3] A 20,000-candle-power kerosene lamp went into a fourth-order Fresnel lens in the new tower.[^4]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends prints a keeper list for Nauset running from 1838 to 1950. It names seventeen head keepers and seven assistants.[^3] The US Lighthouse Society's archive, which has entries from 1929 to 1950, matches that list except for one start date, discussed below.[^7]

Isaac Dunham is the first head keeper on the list, from 1838 to 1842.[^3] Benjamin H.A. Collins kept the light in two stints, 1843 to 1849 and 1853 to 1861. The survey form records the 1838 purchase from a Sally and Benjamin H.A. Collins, but no source read for this story says the keeper and the seller were the same man.[^3][^2]

Nathan A. Gill, Jr., was head keeper from 1869 to 1883. The assistants' list also has a Nathan A. Gill, Sr., from 1873 to 1879, and two other Gills. It gives no relationship among them.[^3] Stephen S. Lewis, head keeper from 1883 to 1914, held the post longest on the list, thirty-one years.[^3]

After 1914 the head keepers changed more often. Thomas J. Kelley served from 1914 to 1918, and James Yates from 1918 to 1919. George I. Herbolt is listed from 1919 to 1932 by LighthouseFriends, but from 1929 to 1932 by the archive.[^3][^7] John E. Poyner served in 1932, Allison G. Haskins from 1932 to 1938, and Fred S. Vidler from 1938 to 1942.[^3][^7]

Eugene L. Coleman, who came from more than a decade at Cape Neddick, Maine, arrived in 1942. LighthouseFriends, drawing on Coleman's own recollections, describes a winter when the cold fell to eight below zero and vapor was so thick that the houses across the way could not be seen. The family had no electricity, though it had been promised.[^3] Coleman kept the light until 1950.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Life-Saving Service's report for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1888, which covers July 1887 to June 1888, has an entry for November 1, 1887. The patrol from the Nauset station, about a mile north of the Nauset Beach Beacons, found lumber and wreckage drifting ashore in large quantities. The man on patrol could see no vessel, and he went back to the station to report.[^6]

Erosion was the slow threat. The survey form says the brick towers had been lost to it by 1892.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that by the 1980s surf and storms had eaten away the cliff near the light.[^3] By 1993 the tower was about 50 feet (15 m) from the cliff, the Society's timeline says, and the Coast Guard declared it surplus, darkened it and planned to take it down.[^4]

By November 1996 the danger was close. The National Park Service says less than 35 feet (11 m) remained when the tower was moved in one piece, about 300 feet (91 m) across the road.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the tower stood twenty-five feet (7.6 m) from falling off the cliff, and puts the move at 336 feet (102 m) from the bluff's edge.[^3] The Society's timeline gives the cost as $330,000.[^4]

## After the keepers

The light was automated. The Society's timeline dates the change to 1952, when a keeper was no longer needed, and LighthouseFriends dates it to 1955.[^4][^3]

The keeper's house passed to private owners. It stayed in private hands until 2024, when it was transferred to the National Park Service.[^3][^4] In 1965 the Park Service bought two of the three old wooden towers, and it bought the third in 1983.[^4] In 1988 the Park Service restored the Three Sisters in their original configuration, in a park off Cable Road.[^4]

In 1981 two rotating aerobeacons replaced the lens.[^1] The fourth-order Fresnel lens that came out is now on display at the Salt Pond Visitor Center.[^4][^2] The signal changed to one red flash and one white flash, with five seconds between them.[^1][^4]

The Nauset Light Preservation Society was founded on November 10, 1993, to raise money for the move. In 1995 the Coast Guard granted the Society a lease to operate and maintain the tower.[^4][^3] On May 10, 1997, the light was relit as a private aid to navigation, and ownership passed from the Coast Guard to the National Park Service.[^4][^3]

In October 1998 the keeper's house was moved across the road to stand beside the tower, keeping the same orientation and relative position to the lighthouse that it had before, LighthouseFriends says.[^3][^4] The tower was repainted in 2013 with a grant from a snack-food company, and rust was removed and it was repainted again in 2021.[^3]

## Today

The tower is open for tours on Sundays from May through October, and on Wednesdays from June through September. The Nauset Light Preservation Society runs the property for the Cape Cod National Seashore, and the hours vary, so visitors should check the Society's website first.[^3][^1]

The keeper's house is a short-term rental. The Society began offering it in June 2024.[^3]

Visitors park in a lot off Ocean View Drive and follow a path to the lighthouse.[^3]
