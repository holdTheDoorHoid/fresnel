Cape Canaveral Light stands on Florida's Atlantic coast, on Space Force land.[^2] Its 151-foot (46 m) cast-iron tower was lit in 1868. In 1893 and 1894 it was taken apart and rebuilt about a mile inland, away from the sea.[^1][^2] The brick tower it replaced, lit in 1849, was blown up to provide rubble for the new foundation.[^1][^2]

## The place

The Foundation says dangerous shoals off the cape were a constant threat to mariners, and that is what the light was built to mark.[^1] Erosion was the station's other problem. In 1890 Congress appropriated $80,000 for moving the station to a safe site, should the encroachment of the sea make that necessary.[^3][^4] Before the money was spent, wooden protective works 800 feet (240 m) long were built along the high-water line.[^3] The Board measured the high-water line at several dates between 1890 and 1892, including the point opposite the tower. By 30 June 1892 the land had gained back what it had lost.[^3]

## Building the light

The first tower was brick, 65 feet (20 m) tall including a ten-foot (3 m) lantern room, and fifteen Winslow Lewis lamps burned whale oil in it.[^1] The Foundation says it was lit in 1849.[^1] Its first keeper, Nathaniel Scobie, left in 1849. The Foundation says he went to take his family to safety after requesting, and not receiving, protection against attack.[^2] The second keeper, Ora Carpenter, the Foundation says, thought the lighthouse was going to topple over in 1852.[^2]

Mills Olcott Burnham became head keeper in 1853, and the Foundation says he served until 1886.[^1] The archive's roster lists him twice, 1853 to 1863 and 1868 to 1886.[^10]

In 1859 Congress voted money for a taller tower, to be made of cast iron by a foundry in Cold Spring, New York.[^1] Work began, but the Foundation says the onset of the Civil War postponed construction until after the war.[^1] The Foundation's fact sheet is the only source for the war-time details here. It says the Confederate Lighthouse Board ordered Florida's lights dark for the rest of the war, and that Burnham partly complied: he sent equipment to St. Augustine and buried the reflectors in his orange grove.[^2]

When the war ended, a temporary fourth-order Fresnel lens was put into the brick tower until the iron tower was ready.[^2] The iron tower was lit in 1868.[^1] Its plates are bolted together and lined with brick inside.[^2] The Foundation says the keepers seldom lived in it, except in storms, because it was too hot in Florida and cottages had already been built. The fact sheet also records the destruction of the keepers' cottages in 1876.[^2]

The tower moved in 1893 and 1894. On 1 July 1893 a working party landed at the cape.[^4] By October the station had quarters, storehouses, a wharf on sheathed piles, a tramway two miles (3.2 km) long, and a temporary beacon with a focal plane of 57 feet (17 m).[^4] On 25 October work began on taking down the lantern.[^4] The Foundation says the tower was dismantled and its pieces moved on a tram pulled by mules.[^1] Its history gives a distance of a mile and a quarter inland; its fact sheet says a mile.[^1][^2] The tower was re-erected on a concrete foundation, and on 7 June 1894 it was ready for relighting.[^4][^11] The 1894 report gives 26 July as the planned date for showing the new first-order light and discontinuing the temporary fourth-order light.[^11] The Foundation gives 25 July 1894 as the relighting date,[^2] and the Board's 1895 list of changes gives 25 July as the date the light was moved.[^7]

## The keepers

The archive's roster has fourteen principal-keeper entries, from Scobie in 1848 to a keeper listed from 1952.[^10] They name twelve different men, since Burnham and David Swain each appear twice.[^10] The Foundation says the station had only nine head keepers in 155 years.[^1] The two counts do not match.

The Foundation says Mills Olcott Burnham and his descendants kept the light for eighty years.[^1] He had two sons, who died young, and five daughters; the husbands and sons of his daughters served as keepers and assistant keepers.[^1]

Clinton P. Honeywell served the station for a long run. He was second assistant from 1891, first assistant from 1893, and principal keeper from 1904 to 1930.[^10] Government bulletins and annual reports name him as keeper in 1913 and again in 1921.[^8][^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Board's report for 1894 says the August 1893 storm culminated on 27 and 28 August, with a tide two feet higher than any the district had recorded.[^5] Its force, the report says, was very great as far south as Cape Canaveral.[^5] The lighthouse schooner *Pharos*, then at anchor in the bight at Cape Canaveral, narrowly escaped being driven ashore after parting three anchor chains.[^5] In October a second storm caught the *Pharos* in its center off the Florida coast between St. Augustine and Cape Canaveral, while she was bound for Cape Canaveral with material and provisions.[^6]

In 1913 Keeper Clinton P. Honeywell assisted the six survivors of the wrecked yacht *Huntress* and brought them about four miles (6.4 km) to the light station.[^8]

In 1921 Honeywell sheltered the captain and 17 crew of the British steamship *Albert Soper*, which was wrecked about five miles (8 km) from the station.[^9] The same year Honeywell and the first assistant, J. B. Butler, fed and clothed ten men from two United States Navy hydroplanes that had been beached near the station.[^9]

During the Second World War the Foundation says the light was dimmed for the duration.[^2] It adds that the river and some adjacent land were used as a practice bombing range by the Banana River Naval Air Station, and that the lighthouse was almost bombed.[^2]

## After the keepers

The lighthouse was electrified in 1931.[^2] The Coast Guard took over the lighthouse in 1939.[^1] The Foundation says the last keeper left in 1941.[^1] The archive roster, however, lists a principal keeper from 1952 onward.[^10]

In 1993 the first-order lens was taken out of the tower, because launch vibrations were damaging the fragile glass, and a DCB-224 rotating beacon replaced it.[^2] In June 2021 a Sealite lantern replaced that beacon.[^2]

In 2000 the Air Force became the owner of the tower, and in 2020 the Space Force took over.[^1][^2] The Foundation says the Coast Guard continues to maintain the light.[^1]

## Today

The Cape Canaveral Lighthouse Foundation was formed in 2002 to preserve the lighthouse's history and provide tours to the public.[^1] The tower stands on Space Force property. Visits are arranged through the Foundation, which runs tours and takes group requests; do not go onto base ground or climb the tower on your own.[^1][^2] The current light is a Sealite lantern showing two flashes in five seconds, followed by a 15-second eclipse.[^2]
