Blockhouse Point Lighthouse stands on Rocky Point, on the west side of the entrance to Charlottetown Harbour on Prince Edward Island's south shore.[^1][^4] Its wooden tower is attached to the keeper's house, and the place it marks was a summer gathering ground of the Mi'kmaq long before anyone lit a lantern there.[^1][^2]

## The place

The point looks out over the Northumberland Strait at the mouth of Charlottetown Harbour. Lighthouse Friends records that the area hosted large Mi'kmaq gatherings each summer, that the French founded a settlement called Port-la-Joye nearby in 1720, and that the British built Fort Amherst in 1758 to assert control.[^1] The Register's heritage lighthouse statement adds that the 19th century brought growing marine traffic into Charlottetown, including trans-Atlantic ships and coastal steamships that arrived with immigrants and left with timber and other exports.[^3]

## Building the light

The sources do not agree on when the first light was shown. Lighthouse Friends quotes the 1846 Journal of the House of Assembly of Prince Edward Island, which records a payment of 10 pounds to Thomas Owen for building a lantern at the Blockhouse "as a Harbour Light for Charlottetown" and keeping it in operation.[^1] The Canadian Register of Historic Places, citing the province's heritage division, instead dates the first light to 1851 and says citizens petitioned the colonial legislature, which provided 10 pounds for a lantern added to the roof of a warehouse, with no money apparently set aside for upkeep.[^2] Lighthouse Friends adds a more substantial lantern room of 1856, built on the upper part of the blockhouse for 46 pounds.[^1]

By 1874, the year after Prince Edward Island entered Confederation, the federal superintendent of lighthouses found the buildings "so much decayed by age as scarcely to merit repair", according to the report as quoted by Lighthouse Friends.[^1] Parliament appropriated $4,000 for a replacement, and the present lighthouse went up in 1876, built by James W. Butcher under a contract of $2,750.[^1] The Register says it was built to a Department of Marine and Fisheries design.[^2]

The result is a square, tapered wooden tower, 12.8 m (42 ft) high, attached to a two-storey keeper's dwelling; the cliffs of red sandstone beneath it put the light about 18.3 m (60 ft) above the water.[^1] The Register calls it a rare survivor of a keeper's dwelling attached to the light tower, and describes the house as having four bedrooms upstairs and a kitchen, parlour and two more bedrooms below.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives the same ground floor but adds a bathroom to the four upstairs bedrooms.[^1][^2]

The light was originally catoptric (reflector-based); in 1909 a fourth-order Fresnel lens with a mercury-vapour source replaced it, and the present seven-foot octagonal lantern room followed in 1910.[^1] The 1876 station also showed a red light from a lower window to lead mariners toward a bell buoy in Hillsboro Bay. That red light was made unnecessary by the Hazard Range Lights and was discontinued when navigation opened in 1890.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists Thomas Owen (1846 to 1852), William Coates, John Jamieson, Thomas Smith and Neil Campbell as the early keepers, then Archibald McLaine from 1867 to 1900, Alexander S. McNeil from 1901, Nelson W. Currie from 1912 and William Stanley Taylor from 1936.[^1] The Register's list names A. S. McNeil, Nelson Currie and William S. Taylor.[^2] Currie's appointment is dated 6 May 1912 and Taylor's 15 October 1936.[^1]

Taylor was the last keeper. Both the Register and Lighthouse Friends date the automation of the light to 25 July 1962; Lighthouse Friends adds that Taylor remained keeper until 3 February 1963 and then served as caretaker until 1 January 1967.[^1][^2] His son Merrill Taylor later leased the lighthouse as a summer residence for several years, until the Coast Guard ended that lease, and one on the New London Rear Range Lighthouse, in 2000.[^1]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends reports that the lighthouse twice escaped planned replacement, in 1963 to 1964 and again in 1969 to 1970.[^1] A community group, the Blockhouse Lighthouse Preservation Society, formed in 2011 and petitioned for ownership under the Heritage Lighthouse Program; in 2015 it learned that the Mi'kmaq Confederacy had filed a claim for the lighthouse and other sites on the Island.[^1] In 2017, with the building neglected, members contacted their MP and Fisheries and Oceans; workers arrived that November, and when the scaffolding came down in March 2018 rotted wood and old shingles had been replaced and the foundation repaired.[^1]

On 30 August 2024, Parks Canada announced the designation of the lighthouse under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act.[^4] Abegweit First Nation's land use plan says the nation acquired the lighthouse from Fisheries and Oceans, together with more than 12 acres of adjoining land from the Province.[^5] The plan dates the acquisition to 2022 in one place and to 2024 in another.[^5]

## Today

The tower remains an aid to navigation, and Lighthouse Friends describes its present signature as a three-second flash followed by a one-second eclipse.[^1][^3] Ownership now rests with Abegweit First Nation, which describes a Blockhouse lighthouse renovation as part of its plans for the site.[^5] The last published visitor information, written while the Coast Guard owned the lighthouse, described the grounds as open and the tower as closed; the property has changed hands since, and no source seen confirms current access, so visitors should not assume the grounds are open or the tower can be entered.[^1]
