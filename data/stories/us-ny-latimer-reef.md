Latimer Reef Light is a cast-iron "spark plug" tower sitting on a rocky reef in Fishers Island Sound, about a mile north of the east end of Fishers Island, New York. It replaced a lightship in 1884 and was automated in 1954, so for about seventy years keepers lived in the iron cylinder.[^1][^2]

## The place

The reef lies between Fishers Island and Stonington, Connecticut.[^2] In 1838 Lieutenant George M. Bache of the U.S. Navy recommended an unlighted beacon on it, because it was "much in the way of vessels passing in either direction through the sound, or bound into Stonington."[^2] LighthouseFriends says its name is reportedly that of a Revolutionary War spy, James Latemore, who ran aground on it and was captured; the account is a reported tradition, not a documented fact.[^2]

A private lightship was kept near the reef from 1837 by the Stonington Railroad and Steamboat Company, and in 1849 the government's Eel Grass Shoal lightship replaced it. Several vessels served on the station until the lighthouse took over in 1884.[^2]

## Building the light

The Light-House Board, in its history of the Eel Grass Shoal lightship, recorded a plan "to replace the light-ship by the erection of a hght-house, at an exi^ense of $25,000, on Latimer Keef" (the quotation keeps the scan's errors), and said the work was done in 1884.[^3] LighthouseFriends describes the tower as a prefabricated cast-iron cylinder, about 49 feet (15 m) tall, on a cast-iron, concrete-filled base.[^2] It reproduces the Board's notice that from 1 July 1884 a flashing white light of the fifth order, flashing every ten seconds, would show from it, and that the Eel Grass Shoal lightship would be discontinued.[^2]

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive instead lists the original lens as fourth order.[^1] The Board's report for 1899 records the change from a fifth-order to a fourth-order lens, dated 24 December 1898, which supports the fifth-order account; LighthouseFriends places the change in 1899.[^4][^2]

## The keepers

The first head keeper was Charles E. P. Noyes, who had been captain of the Eel Grass Shoal lightship. LighthouseFriends adds that he came from the Stonington whaling trade and stayed twenty-five years, until he was transferred to Ram Island Lightship and replaced by William H. Smith.[^2][^1] The archive lists Samuel C. Gardiner as the first assistant; LighthouseFriends spells the surname Gardner.[^1][^2]

After Noyes, the roster shows long stays only among the head keepers: Smith from 1909 to at least 1919, Frank J. Raymond from 1925 to 1934, and George E. Durfee from 1934 to 1952.[^1] The assistants changed constantly. The archive lists forty first-assistant stints between 1884 and 1944, many lasting a year or less.[^1]

The keepers also tended a fog bell, struck by machinery. The Board's reports record the old Stevens striking machine replaced by a new one in its 1896-97 volume, and the bell-striking machine replaced again in its report for 1899.[^6][^4] In 1913 the lighthouse department announced that the fog signal would be moved from the west side of the tower.[^7] LighthouseFriends calls the station apparently one of the more uncomfortable for its keepers, noting that iron towers of the type were reportedly bitterly cold in winter and miserably hot in summer.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A storm on 1 March 1914 damaged lighthouse service property at the eastern end of Long Island Sound and in Narragansett Bay; the Norwich Bulletin reported minor damage at Plum Island, Point Judith, Orient Point and Latimer Reef.[^8] LighthouseFriends quotes Keeper Smith's log: "The sea was so high that it broke over the Platform and Pier."[^2] It adds that he tied down the station's two boats single-handed but his own skiff was smashed, and that the woodshed and then the outhouse disappeared under the water.[^2]

A newspaper item of June 1910, reprinted by LighthouseFriends without naming the paper, tells of an assistant keeper named John Andrews who tried to row back to the light after some days ashore, could not make headway against a strong ebb tide, and was picked up by two fishing captains in turn, the second of whom towed him up to the light. LighthouseFriends notes that the Service's records do not list him there.[^2]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists Latimer Reef among the Long Island Sound light stations where the principal damage occurred in the New England hurricane of September 1938.[^9]

## After the keepers

Durfee left in 1952, and a Coast Guard crew appears in the archive's roster in the 1940s.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the station was automated in 1954 and that in 1983 the fourth-order Fresnel lens was replaced with a 300 mm optic.[^2]

The Coast Guard declared the tower excess in 2008. After no eligible organization was awarded it, the General Services Administration sold it at auction on 23 August 2010 for $225,000.[^2]

## Today

The tower is listed on the National Register of Historic Places (2006) and still shows a flashing white light every six seconds.[^10][^2] It is privately owned and not open to the public.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says it is best viewed by boat.[^2]
