Terence Bay Light is a white tower with red trim on a granite point above the fishing community of Terence Bay, near Halifax, Nova Scotia.[^1][^8] The light has been shown from a lantern on a mast, from a square wooden tower built in 1903 that still stands, and from a new lantern set on that tower in 2018.[^2][^3][^8] It is a federal heritage lighthouse. The community was to have taken it over in 2017, and it is still waiting for the deed.[^5][^6]

## The place

The light was put up to mark the western side of the entrance to Terence Bay.[^2] Its first light stood on Shipley Head.[^2][^3] The Register of Historic Places says the tower was built "as a harbour light to warn of potential dangers in Terence Bay and to guide ships towards Halifax harbour."[^1] The point stands on granite, which the Register puts at 14.6 metres above sea level.[^1]

The bay was a working fishing place. The Register says that in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries Terence Bay "developed an extensive fishery with a fish plant where fish were salted, processed and shipped out for sale."[^1] The same entry says the sailors and fishermen were also involved in "rum running" to Maine during prohibition.[^1] A New York Herald account of the 1873 wreck described the waters in the vicinity of Mars Head as "thickly studded with rocky shoals and islands."[^11]

## Building the light

The first light here was a lantern on a mast. The Lighthouse Friends history says that in 1885 a small lantern was hoisted up a 7.6 m (25 ft) mast on Shipley Head, and that Patrick A. Fahey built the mast and the white shed at its base for a $175 contract.[^2] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society says the light was erected and lit in 1885, twelve years after the wreck of the SS Atlantic.[^5]

The Lighthouse Friends history dates the change to 1903, when the Department of Marine announced the completion of an enclosed tower.[^2] Its annual report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1903 (July 1902 to June 1903) says the light was improved "by substituting for the lantern on a mast a stronger light, shown from an enclosed lighthouse tower."[^3] The report describes the tower as "a square wooden building with sloping sides," with a square wooden lantern, "the whole painted white," and "32 feet high from its base to the ventilator on the lantern."[^3] The report says the building was "erected by day labour, at a cost of $471."[^3] The light was a fixed red, elevated 17.4 m (57 ft) above high water, with a dioptric lens "of the seventh order," and the report expected it to be seen 8 miles from all points of approach by water.[^3]

The Register calls the tower "a very good example of a square, tapered, wooden tower, with a flared cornice that supports the gallery."[^1] The sources do not agree on its size or its light. The Lighthouse Directory gives the tower as 8 m (26 ft), with lantern and gallery, a focal plane of 14.5 m (48 ft), and a continuous red light.[^8] The 1903 report's 9.8 m (32 ft) runs to the lantern's ventilator, and its 17.4 m (57 ft) is the focal height above high water. The Coast Guard now lists a white light.[^3][^9] None of these sources explains the change.

## The keepers

The first keeper was Peter Jollimore. The Lighthouse Friends history says he was paid $100 a year and served until 1900.[^2] The Register spells the name Jollymore, and says the light "is also associated with the Jollymore family." It describes the station as "Staffed from 1884 until 1957," with Peter Jollymore as the first keeper and Hezron Jollymore as the last.[^1]

After Jollimore came Henry Beck, from 1900, though the Lighthouse Friends history gives no end date for him.[^2] Samuel P. Slaunwhite kept the light from 1903 to 1918.[^2] The Department of Marine's statement of Nova Scotia light-keepers, printed in 1907 with its fortieth annual report, names "Slaunwhite, S. P" against Terence Bay.[^4] He was followed by Charles Slaunwhite, from 1918 to 1921, and then Zachariah Jollimore, from 1921 to "at least 1923."[^2]

Hezron Jollimore received his appointment on 18 February 1942.[^2] He was the last keeper. The light was fully automated in 1957, but he stayed on as caretaker for another decade, on a much smaller salary of $40 a year.[^2] The Society says the light had no keeper after 1967.[^5] The sources give no birthplaces, families or other personal details for these men beyond the names, the years and the Jollimore and Slaunwhite family links above.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The wreck most of the sources describe came before the light did. The SS Atlantic, a White Star steamer, left Liverpool for New York on 20 March 1873. Eleven days later, with her coal running low, her captain decided to steam for Halifax.[^11][^12] Early on 1 April she went ashore at Mars Head, near Lower Prospect.[^12] The Register says she "struck a rock just off Mars Head in the waters of Terence Bay," and calls the wreck "the largest marine disaster until the sinking of the Titanic in 1912."[^1] The New York Herald named the reef Meagher's Rock, one of "those treacherous rocks lying off" the coast, and called Mars Head "a huge mass of rock, which rises seventy feet above high water, just outside of the mainland."[^11]

The counts of the dead disagree. On 3 April the Herald reported that "the total number lost now foots up to 560; saved 418."[^11] On 2 April the Tribune had said that "upward of 700 were drowned."[^10] The Register gives 562.[^1] The Nova Scotia Archives gives "565 passengers and crew."[^12] The Society's newsletter gives about 550 in one place and about 535 in another.[^5][^7]

Survivors came ashore by line, and the fishermen's boats took off many more. The Herald reported that "many reached the shore by the aid of the lines, and the fishermen's boats rescued many more," and said that the kindness of the Prospect people was "universally acknowledged and praised."[^11]

The tower also took weather damage. The Lighthouse Directory says Hurricane Juan, in September 2003, cost the lighthouse some of its shingles.[^8]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1957. According to the Lighthouse Friends history, the lantern room came off around 1980, after the lighthouse had fallen into disrepair, and a deck went across the upper tower with a watertight light on top.[^2] The Society describes the same 1980 changes.[^5]

In 2011 local residents, with Halifax Regional Council's backing, said they wanted to take over the light, which the Coast Guard had put on its surplus list.[^2] In 2015 Ottawa gave the light heritage status under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act.[^2][^6] The Lighthouse Friends history says the committee was "reportedly granted ownership" in 2016, but that the transfer was held up when the owner of the land the right-of-way crosses "refused to compromise."[^2] The Lighthouse Directory dates the stall to 2014.[^8] The Society's 2022 newsletter says the transfer has stalled over public access to the site, because a neighbouring owner will not grant it.[^6]

Repairs came in 2018. The Lighthouse Friends history says the Department of Fisheries and Oceans began repairs in July 2018, and that a new lantern room went on the tower in late December 2018.[^2] The Society's newsletter dates the Canadian Coast Guard's renovations to 2018 to 2020, and elsewhere says the Department of Fisheries and Oceans made the repairs in 2019 and 2020.[^5][^6] The sources do not agree on who did the work or in which years.

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns and runs the light.[^2][^8] The Coast Guard's List of Lights gives a white flash of two seconds in every six, describes the tower as a white pyramid with a red top, and lists the light as lit year round.[^9]

The tower is closed, and the way to the light crosses private land.[^2][^7] The Lighthouse Friends history says the route is a narrow right-of-way between two private residences, and that the grounds are open while the tower is closed.[^2] The Society's newsletter calls the point apparently private, though it says the light can be seen from the turnaround at the road's end.[^7] The Terence Bay Lighthouse Committee runs regular walks to the light and an annual Lighthouse Day each September; the 2022 one drew more than 30 people.[^6] Anyone who is not on one of those walks should look at the light from the road's end and stay off private land.
