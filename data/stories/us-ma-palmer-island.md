Palmer Island Light stands on a six-acre island in the Acushnet River, at the entrance to New Bedford Harbor.[^8] Its conical stone tower, 24 feet (7.3 m) high, was built in 1849 and is still an active light.[^2][^1] The island is designated for walking, and a visitor listing warns that rising tides can cover the sand spit that leads to it.[^8]

## The place

New Bedford was the nation's whaling capital, and its shipowners and sea captains "vigorously petitioned" for a light to mark the harbor, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] An act of 1837 approved $2,000 for a beacon light at the harbor mouth.[^3] In 1842 the inspector I.W.P. Lewis wrote that a light on Palmer Island was also needed, LighthouseFriends reports.[^3]

Congress appropriated $3,500 for the lighthouse in 1848, LighthouseFriends says, and an acre of the island was bought the following year.[^3]

## Building the light

The nomination records that Charles M. Pierce built the masonry tower between 9 June and 22 September 1849 under contract to the Superintendent of Lights, at a cost of $1951.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the project came in $173 under budget.[^3] The tower is a conical stone tower with a birdcage-style lantern.[^3]

William Sherman first lit the lamps on 30 August 1849, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] In 1852 a 99-foot (30 m) seawall went up on the island's east side.[^3] That year the whaling merchant Samuel Rodman tried whale oil as an illuminant in the light. He billed the government $105.80 for the oil, and the government reimbursed him, although whale oil was not adopted for the light at that time.[^3]

LighthouseFriends says a fifth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps in 1856, and the Society's record gives the same order.[^3][^1] The nomination dates a lens change to 1857.[^2] A new lantern went onto the tower in 1863.[^3]

## The keepers

The Society's roster lists twelve principal keepers for Palmer Island, from 1849 to 1942.[^1]

James Stubbs was keeper from 1861 to 1862, and then Caroline Stubbs from 1862 to 1873.[^1] The roster gives her role as principal keeper, and she is the only woman's name on it.[^1] LighthouseFriends lists the same years in its list of head keepers.[^3] The sources do not say how the two were related, and this story does not assume that they were.

George Cowie kept the light from 1874 to 1891.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he complained of brackish well water and of soot from the city's factories, which blew across the island and fouled the cistern. It adds that "a 1931 inspection would list the same water problem."[^3]

Arthur A. Small came to the light in 1922 from the Narrows light in Boston Harbor, with his wife Mabel and two sons, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] He was a gifted painter who often painted the island.[^3]

George A. Faulkner kept the light from 1915 to 1922.[^1] In June 1920 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports him towing a power boat, "which was adrift and without gasoline," to the light station.[^7]

Frank J. Ponte kept the light from 1938 to 1939, and Andrew Zuius, Sr. from 1939 to 1942.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls Ponte "Franklin," says he filled in after Small, and says Zuius had been keeper at Bullocks Point Light in Rhode Island when that light was nearly destroyed in the 1938 hurricane.[^3] The nomination gives a different account. Its substitute keeper during Small's leave, Martin Maloney, had been stationed at the Bullocks Point light during the hurricane, and was still tending the Palmer Island light in August 1939.[^2] The sources do not agree on who was at Bullocks Point in 1938, or on how Maloney's service and Ponte's overlapped.[^2][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The nomination records a gale on 8 September 1869 that damaged the plank walk from the keeper's house to the tower.[^2] On 31 May 1900 a fog bell struck by machinery was set up in a new pyramidal wooden tower, the Board's report for that year says.[^4] LighthouseFriends gives the bell's weight as 1,260 pounds.[^3]

The worst of the damage came on 21 September 1938.[^5] The keeper's own report, quoted in a letter printed in the Lighthouse Service Bulletin, says he made preparations all that day and carried extra oil and lamp equipment up to the tower.[^5] His wife, Mrs. Arthur A. Small, had been told by him to stay in the upper part of the oil house. The report says she left it, and that she "tried to launch a boat to save the keeper" after he was swept overboard.[^5] It also says she was "swept away and drowned."[^5]

The Bulletin's texts disagree about where she was.[^5][^6] The Commissioner's letter says the keeper had helped his wife to the light tower for safety before the dwelling was destroyed, and that she then left the tower to try to launch a boat.[^5] A later text says that Mrs. Small had first taken refuge in the tower, and that she lost her life in an attempt to go to her husband's assistance when a heavy sea washed him away.[^6] The keeper's report, quoted in the same letter, puts her in the oil house.[^5] The nomination says the keeper was injured and that his wife drowned while trying to reach the light, and its historical narrative calls her Sara Small.[^2] LighthouseFriends and the visitor listing call her Mabel.[^3][^8] LighthouseFriends gives the keeper's own words about the storm, in part: "I was hurt and she knew it."[^3] It describes a wave that swept away his wife, the dory and the boathouse, and says that only the tower and the oil house survived on the island.[^3] Small reached the tower and kept the light and fog signal running until relief came the next morning, the Bulletin says.[^5]

LighthouseFriends says two friends rowed out the next morning and found him, and that he was taken under police escort to Chelsea Marine Hospital, where he learned that his wife had perished.[^3] The keeper's report, as printed in the Bulletin, says he was removed to St. Luke's hospital, suffering from exhaustion and exposure.[^5] The sources do not agree on the hospital. The Bulletin prints the Commissioner's letter of commendation to "Capt. Arthur A. Small."[^5] LighthouseFriends says he was granted a two-year absence with pay, and then retired.[^3]

## After the keepers

The nomination says the light was automated to operate from Butler's Flat Lighthouse in 1941.[^2] LighthouseFriends puts it a year later, in 1942, when Zuius was still in charge and the light was "automated and made unattended in 1942."[^3]

The US Army Corps of Engineers bought the island in 1962, the nomination says.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates a hurricane wall in the harbor to 1963. It says that after the wall went up the lighthouse was no longer needed, and that the wall made the island "more accessible to lighthouse seekers and vandals alike."[^3] The visitor listing gives 1966 for the completion of the hurricane barrier.[^8] The nomination says vandals set the fire that gutted the building in 1966. LighthouseFriends describes an arson fire that gutted the tower and nearly destroyed the lantern room.[^2][^3]

The island passed through several owners before the City of New Bedford took it over in 1978.[^3] Local residents finished a restoration in 1989, but vandals struck again, and the light stood dark for most of the 1990s, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] A new restoration, with repairs by inmates in a county pre-release program, led to a ceremony in 1999 marking the light's 150th year.[^3] A new solar light with a white flash every seven and a half seconds was switched on.[^3]

## Today

The City of New Bedford owns the island and the light.[^1] The Society's record lists the light as open to the public, but LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1][^3] The visitor listing describes the island as "designated for walking and other passive recreational uses such as bird watching."[^8] Fishing and shellfishing are prohibited, because the harbor is contaminated with PCBs.[^8]

The visitor listing warns that "access from the sand-spit at the southern end of the island" may be covered with several feet of water, and advises checking the tide charts before crossing.[^8] A visitor guide says the grounds are open, but that the lighthouse is open only by special arrangement with the city.[^9]

The 1938 hurricane washed away the other structures on the island, the visitor listing says, and the lighthouse is the only one left standing.[^8] The record gives the current optic as a 250 mm solar light.[^1]
