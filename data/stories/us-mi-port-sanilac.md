Port Sanilac Light stands by the harbor of the village of Port Sanilac, on the Lake Huron shore of Sanilac County, Michigan.[^1] The Lighthouse Board first asked for money for a light on this stretch of coast in 1868, and the brick tower was first lit in October 1886.[^1][^2] The keeper's dwelling was sold in 1928 and the tower in 2000, but the Coast Guard is still responsible for the light.[^1]

## Building the light

The gap was the reason for the light. Fort Gratiot, at the head of the St. Clair River, was built in 1825, and Pointe aux Barques, at the tip of Michigan's thumb, in 1848. The seventy-five miles of shore between them remained without a light for several years after that.[^1]

Congress appropriated $20,000 for a lighthouse at Port Sanilac in 1885, and the Board bought a site.[^1] Charles Diem, the contractor, began the brick tower and keeper's dwelling on 7 June 1886.[^1] The tower, dwelling and outbuildings were finished on 13 October 1886.[^1] The Board gave notice in the *Lake County Star* of 30 September that a fixed white light would be shown from the new lighthouse on and after 20 October.[^2]

The light was a fourth-order Fresnel lens, made by Barbier & Fenestre.[^1] The tower is 59 feet (18 m) tall and octagonal, with an hourglass profile. The lamp stands 69 feet (21 m) above the water.[^1][^3] A covered passage links the tower to the two-story brick dwelling.[^1]

## The keepers

Richard W. Morris was the first head keeper, from 1886 to 1893.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he had been first assistant at Thunder Bay Island. On 13 October 1886 he was told he had been promoted to head keeper at Port Sanilac. Stormy weather held him and his family on the island until the sixteenth. They boarded a steamer at Alpena on the eighteenth and reached Port Sanilac early on the nineteenth.[^1]

William H. Holmes succeeded Morris and served from 1893 to 1926.[^3] Before coming to Port Sanilac he had served at the Thunder Bay Island lifesaving station and then as first assistant at the Thunder Bay Island light. In 1885 he married Grace Sinclair, daughter of that island's head keeper.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Morris reportedly stepped down around the end of 1892, so that his niece Grace could stay with her husband while their children attended school.[^1] The archive's roster ends Morris's service in 1893.[^3]

Holmes began to suffer from neuritis around 1920. LighthouseFriends says that on many nights Grace carried the lamp oil up the lighthouse stairs.[^1] Holmes died of apoplexy on 13 October 1926, at the age of sixty-four.[^1] Grace Holmes, the daughter and granddaughter of lighthouse keepers, was then appointed keeper in his place and served until the light was automated in 1928.[^1][^3] The sources do not agree on when the light was automated. The U.S. Lighthouse Society's place record gives 1925, and LighthouseFriends gives 1928.[^3][^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 1 October 1888 the steamer Lowell left Sand Beach, Michigan, towing six barges for Bay City. The weather grew worse, and the tow was forced back. The schooner-barge St. Clair anchored off the Sand Beach breakwater.[^4] The Sand Beach crew rowed out to her, but the captain refused to abandon his vessel and the crew went back to the station. Around ten that night a torch showed on the St. Clair. The crew took off her people, and because they could not get back that night, they ran for Port Sanilac, thirty miles to leeward.[^4]

Early on 2 October, as the boat tried to beach at Port Sanilac, a wave rolled it over just as it passed the end of the pier. Five people died, among them the barge's captain and a woman cook. The St. Clair foundered and went entirely to pieces.[^4] The report says that witnesses agreed the life-saving crew were "in no way culpable."[^4] The keeper of the Sand Beach station had never been into Port Sanilac, though several of his crew knew the harbor. The report does not mention the village's lighthouse.[^4]

## Today

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports the change from fixed to flashing, and from oil to electric incandescent, among its 1925 changes.[^5] Its 1924 changes had already listed Port Sanilac's move from oil to electric incandescent.[^6] LighthouseFriends dates the flashing change to 1 April 1925 and gives the new characteristic as a group of three white flashes. In another passage it says the light had been electrified in 1924.[^1] On 13 September 2016 the Coast Guard removed the historic Fresnel lens from the tower and installed a multi-tier LED beacon in its place. The restored lens now sits in a case near a window at the base of the tower.[^1]

The lighthouse is privately owned, and its grounds, dwelling and tower are closed to the public.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends notes that the Sanilac County Historical Society conducted tours of the lighthouse on Fridays in July and August 2017.[^1] Its visitor notes point to public parking at the end of Cherry Street, a short way south of the lighthouse.[^1] Visitors should keep off private land and should not try to climb the closed tower.
