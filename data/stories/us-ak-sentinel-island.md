Sentinel Island Light stands on a small island at the north end of Favorite Channel, south of the mouth of Lynn Canal and about 25 miles (40 km) north of Juneau, Alaska.[^1] It was lit on 1 March 1902, one of the first two lighthouses built in Alaska, in the years when gold rush traffic crowded Lynn Canal.[^1]

## The place

The station sits on the northeastern half of Sentinel Island. In 1900 the federal government reserved 6.55 acres there for a lighthouse.[^1] Lynn Canal ran from near Juneau north to Haines and Skagway, and the gold rush put it under heavy traffic. The National Register nomination says fog, rain, strong tides and a rocky shoreline made the passage hard for steamers overloaded with prospectors and freight, and that more than three hundred accidents were reported in Inside Passage waters in 1898 alone.[^1]

In a report to Congress dated 13 October 1900, the inspector and engineer of the Thirteenth Lighthouse District gave Sentinel Island top priority. Congress paid for two Alaskan lighthouses that year, and Sentinel Island was one.[^1]

## Building the light

The Light-House Board's annual report says bids for the Sentinel Island light and fog-signal station were opened on 6 June 1901. The lowest, $21,267, was accepted.[^2] The same report had listed the station among its proposed Alaskan works at an estimated $31,500.[^2] The nomination names George James of Juneau as the contractor and says he began work in 1901.[^1]

The first station was a wooden, square, two-family keepers' dwelling with an integral light tower, and a steel-and-glass lantern holding a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^1] Separately, a fog-signal house stood 60 feet (18 m) west of the oil house, with a third-class Daboll trumpet.[^1] The light burned 82 feet (25 m) above mean high water.[^1] A dock, a boathouse, two hoist houses and a 360-foot (110 m) tramway of steel rails and wooden ties linked the landing to the buildings.[^1] The nomination records the start as 1 March 1902, shared with Five Finger Islands light to the south; George R. Putnam's 1917 survey gives the same date for both.[^1][^3] Putnam adds that seven stations of this kind, each with a fog signal, were built on the passage between 1902 and 1905.[^3]

The light changed over the years. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1918 volume lists Sentinel Island among lights changed from fixed white (oil) to group flashing white (acetylene).[^6] The nomination says the candlepower rose from 600 to 1,600 in 1926, and rose again in 1929 when electricity replaced acetylene.[^1]

In 1935 a new two-story Art Deco building in reinforced concrete, 28 by 34 feet (8.5 by 10.4 m), was built to hold the light and the fog signal. An 11-foot-square tower rises 25 feet (7.6 m) above its roof, and the nomination gives the total cost as $35,310.[^1] The old lantern was moved onto the new tower.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the lantern room was slid into place along a wooden trestle built between the old and new buildings, and dates the work to 1933 to 1935.[^5]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the National Archives' keeper registers, lists head keepers and first assistants at the station from 1902 to the early 1960s, a new name every few years in the early decades.[^4] The first, Thomas N. McBride, appears in both lists in 1902, as assistant and as head keeper, and stayed until 1903.[^4] Carl E. Peterson held the post for six years from 1904.[^4]

Charles B. Bohm served the island three times: as first assistant from 1905 to 1908, then as head keeper from 1914 to 1923 and again from 1925 to 1930.[^4] In between, Frank Edlin, who had been first assistant from 1918, was head keeper for two years.[^4] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1924 to 1929 lists an efficiency flag awarded to the station and, separately, to Bohm as keeper.[^7] The index does not say what the inspectors saw.

Charles L. Shepardson was head keeper from 1934 to 1941, the years of the concrete rebuilding.[^4] Trueman E. Cook is listed as head keeper from 1946 to 1956.[^4] The archive lists Coast Guard crew members at the station from 1959 to 1965, and the nomination says the last keepers left in 1966.[^4][^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 5 August 1910 the Canadian Pacific steamer *Princess May* ran onto a reef at Sentinel Island. The Seattle Star, relaying a United Press dispatch from Juneau, put the grounding at 2 o'clock that morning and said there was "a dense fog at the time."[^9] The Salt Lake Tribune's headline reported that the passengers were being cared for at the Sentinel Island lighthouse, and its story said all passengers and their baggage had been taken there.[^8] Early reports called the ship a total loss.[^8] Lighthouse Friends reports that she was refloated on 3 September after rock was blasted away and sliding ways were built.[^5] The same account says Louis Betteker, head keeper at the time, remembered the episode as the most vivid of his career.[^5] The archive dates his term as head keeper from 1910.[^4]

The station's best-known night came on 24 October 1918, when the *Princess Sophia* struck Vanderbilt Reef in a storm. The nomination says rescue boats from Juneau had to retreat to shelter, and some found it by the light from Sentinel Island; by the next morning only a twenty-foot piece of the ship's mast showed above water and nobody survived.[^1] Lighthouse Friends puts Sentinel Island a little over four miles southeast of the reef.[^5] The two sources disagree on the dead: the nomination gives 353 passengers and crew, Lighthouse Friends 343.[^1][^5]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1966, after a project approved in 1962 changed the optic to a rotating lantern.[^1] The fog signal, which had sounded five seconds in every thirty, ran until 1996.[^1] In 1971 the Coast Guard burned the original dwelling, by then deteriorated.[^1] In June 1987 the crew of the buoy tender *Woodrush* installed fifteen solar panels on the roof, and the light now runs on solar-charged batteries.[^1]

The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2002.[^5] For about six years before 2004 the Gastineau Channel Historical Society had been leasing it from the Coast Guard.[^10] On 26 April 2004 the Interior Secretary announced that the station would be transferred to the society, the first Alaskan lighthouse to go under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000.[^10] The society says the transfer of ownership took place in 2006.[^11]

## Today

The island is reachable only by boat or helicopter, and the lighthouse is owned by the Gastineau Channel Historical Society.[^5][^11] The society says it can arrange overnight stays on advance request, and since 2022 it has partnered with a local tour company on visits to the lighthouse.[^11] The light is still an active aid to navigation.[^4] Frank Murkowski, who served on Coast Guard buoy tenders in the early 1960s, recalled it as "particularly difficult" to service, with no good landing area and exposure to the swells of Lynn Canal.[^5]
