American Shoal Light is a brown iron skeleton of a lighthouse standing in a few feet of water on a reef 9.6 miles southwest of Summerland Key, in the Lower Florida Keys. It was the last of the six tall Florida Reef Lights to be built, and it was first lit on 15 July 1880.[^1]

## The place

The nomination says the reefs and shallows of the Florida Keys have always been hazardous to vessels, and that the area's storms, hurricanes above all, make them more dangerous still.[^1] By 1878 five offshore towers already marked the reef along the Keys, but a stretch of roughly 51 miles between Sand Key and Sombrero Key had none. That gap held several hazards, among them the Sambo Reefs, Maryland Shoal, American Shoal and Looe Key.[^1]

The Lighthouse Board first recommended a light at Looe Key in 1875. The following year it proposed American Shoal instead and asked for $75,000, and Congress took no action in 1876 or 1877. Approval came on 20 June 1878.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the Board's first request much earlier, to 1855, and says the Board argued that strong currents tended to push ships onto the reefs. The same source reports that a 36-foot white screwpile daymark had been put on the shoal in 1852, which gave no help at night.[^2] On 6 June 1879 the governor of Florida deeded to the federal government whatever rights the state held in a four-acre parcel of submerged land on the shoal.[^1]

## Building the light

The Board did not draw a new design. It reused the plans of Fowey Rocks Light, and the nomination calls the two towers virtually identical, with lanterns as the only significant difference: here the middle of three tiers of glass is offset sideways by half a pane.[^1] The Phoenix Iron Company of Trenton, New Jersey, won the contract for the metalwork at $47,000. The parts were assembled at the factory to check the fit, taken apart, and shipped to the lighthouse depot at Key West.[^1] The foundation is nine iron disk piles, one at the centre and eight in an octagon, in a footprint 56 feet (17 m) across. Each pile is a 12-inch wrought-iron shaft driven through a seven-foot cast-iron disk.[^1] LighthouseFriends adds that the crew first built a working platform of iron-tipped mangrove piles.[^2]

Work on the site began in late 1879. A fair season and a mild winter let it go quickly, and the tower was finished the following year.[^1] D. P. Heap's 1889 book on lighthouses summed it up as ironwork finished in the North, shipped to Key West, and erected and lighted in one year.[^4]

The optic was a first-order Fresnel lens made in Paris by Henry-Lepaute in 1874, with 24 bull's-eye panels. It turned on 39 steel ball bearings of one and a half inches, made one revolution every two minutes, and showed one flash every five seconds from a kerosene wick lamp.[^1] The tower, dwelling and lantern were painted brown, with only the stair cylinder white.[^1] The sources give two heights: the nomination says the tower is 124 feet (38 m) tall, while the Lighthouse Society's archive records 109 feet (33 m) as the height of the light above mean high water, a figure the GSA's 2022 sale notice also gives for the tower.[^1][^3][^12]

Changes came slowly. On 30 April 1893 the light was changed from flashing white to flashing white with three flashing red sectors.[^5] An oil house measuring 14 by 14 by 7 feet was built and hung from iron rods under the tower in the fiscal year 1899.[^6] The wick lamp gave way to an incandescent oil vapor lamp in 1912 and to a brighter kerosene vapor lamp in 1923.[^1] By 1931 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin was explaining American Shoal's single white flash every five seconds as the start of a second series of flash-count signatures along the Reef, and noted there was little danger of confusing it with the Hillsboro Inlet light.[^11]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society archive, working from the keeper registers, lists a head keeper and two assistants for most of the station's early history.[^3] William Bates, who had earlier served at Garden Key, Sand Key and Sombrero Key and came to the new tower from Sombrero Key, lit the lamp for the first time as head keeper, and the archive lists him until 1889.[^1][^2][^3] The sources disagree about where his first assistant, Dudley Richardson, came from: the nomination says Sombrero Key, LighthouseFriends says Alligator Reef.[^1][^2] The archive lists no head keeper between Bates and Henry P. Weatherford, who began in 1899, so that decade is unaccounted for.[^3] Henry Spencer appears in the register as second assistant from 1890 to 1894 and then as first assistant until 1910, some twenty years in all.[^3]

William H. Curry, head keeper from 1910 to 1917, kept the station that won the Seventh District's efficiency flag for the fiscal year 1915.[^3][^7] Richard C. Roberts, who was head keeper from 1919 to 1939, won the flag for the station for 1924 and again for 1925.[^3][^8][^9]

Radio reached the Florida Reef keepers in the late 1920s. A 1929 Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that, after an appeal by Secretary of Commerce Herbert Hoover, nearly 300 donated radio receiving sets had gone out to keepers, and it printed a letter from the American Shoal keeper, who is not named. In the old days, he wrote, news of a presidential election had sometimes taken a month to reach the station. When Secretary Hoover was elected, he said, the station heard the result as soon as anybody else, and listening to ministers preaching and to singing was, in his words, "almost the same as being in church."[^2][^10] The Bulletin names, among earlier donors, a generous lady of New York City, whereas LighthouseFriends says a woman in Key West gave sets to four of the Reef lights.[^2][^10]

In 1939 the Lighthouse Service was folded into the Coast Guard, and Coast Guard crews took over. The normal complement was four men, each serving three weeks at the lighthouse and one ashore, with three always on the tower.[^1] LighthouseFriends records the recollections of George Maroney, who served from April 1955 for almost two years. In his time the four-legged platform beside the tower was not yet in place, so crews reached the station by a walkway and a rope ladder. The station's motorboat hung from davits and was cranked up by hand. The kitchen oven was the only heat, and the one television signal they could receive came from Cuba.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In December 1924 the British steamship *New Toronto*, bound from Hull to Galveston, ran aground on American Shoal close to the lighthouse. The Coast Guard cutter *Saukee* and a salvage tug came from Key West and refloated her, and she continued her voyage.[^1]

In the October 1926 hurricane the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that Sand Key and American Shoal had little damage, while at Sombrero Key the barometer fell to 28.82 and the keepers estimated the wind at 125 miles per hour.[^9]

In January 1997 the container ship *Houston* ran aground about two miles west of the light and damaged coral in the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary. In the legal settlement the owners paid for eight RACON radar beacons for light towers in the Keys, and one went on American Shoal.[^1]

## After the keepers

The sources give different dates for the end of the resident crews. LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard left the lighthouse on 9 July 1962, while the nomination says the station was automated in 1963.[^1][^2] The archive's keeper list ends in 1962.[^3] In 1980 the Fresnel lens was removed, according to the nomination, and replaced by a small acrylic beacon charged by solar panels. Its present whereabouts are uncertain; the nomination suggests it may be an unlabelled first-order lens in the Coast Guard's storage in Forestville, Maryland.[^1] In 1998 a VRB-25 rotating beacon replaced the acrylic one, and a RACON radar beacon was installed the same year.[^1]

The tower was crewed once more, from July to November 1980, during the Mariel boatlift. Six Coast Guard personnel watched vessel traffic from it.[^1] Termites had meanwhile damaged the wooden interior, and quick repairs were needed.[^2]

The sources differ on the light's status today. The nomination, written about 2010, describes it as an active aid to navigation, whereas LighthouseFriends says it was deactivated in 2015, when a nearby 30-foot tower with an automated light took over.[^1][^2] The National Park Service holds a National Register of Historic Places registration form for the lighthouse (reference 10001189), stamped December 2010.[^1] The Coast Guard declared it excess on 1 February 2019, and no organization was chosen to take it over, so the General Services Administration offered it at online auction in 2022.[^2][^12] Bidding closed on 23 May 2022 with a top bid of $860,000, ahead of ten other bidders. The winner had until 7 July to complete the purchase.[^13]

## Today

LighthouseFriends describes the lighthouse as privately owned, with the tower closed; its page is undated, and the sources read do not confirm that the 2022 sale closed.[^2] The nomination notes that it can be reached only by boat.[^1] On a clear day it can be seen from the public road near Sugarloaf Creek, and anything closer takes a boat or a plane.[^2] It is private property standing in open water, so it is best admired from afar.
