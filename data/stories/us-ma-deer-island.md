Deer Island Light is a light in Boston Harbor, off the southern end of Deer Island, Massachusetts. Its cast-iron tower on a caisson was established in 1890 and torn down in 1982. Since 2015 a square skeletal tower on a nearby pile-supported platform has replaced the fiberglass tower that followed it.[^1][^2]

## The place

The Boston Marine Society petitioned Congress in 1832 for $3,000 to put a stone beacon on the southern point of Deer Island, which was covered at high tide. The granite pyramid that resulted marked the northern side of the Broad Sound channels.[^1]

The Board's request for a replacement rested on those channels. LighthouseFriends quotes its reports for 1884 and 1885: "The steamers plying between Boston and the northern ports make use of the Broad Sound channels," and a light and fog signal at the point were "particularly desirable, because of the narrow and devious passages."[^1] Congress appropriated $35,000 on 4 August 1886, and added $6,000 on 2 October 1888, LighthouseFriends says.[^1] The title to the site came from the City of Boston.[^1]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends quotes the 1890 Board records for the work. The tower stood on a cylinder iron caisson 33 feet (10 m) across and 30 feet (9 m) high, filled with concrete. Test borings had found sand and gravel mixed with a little clay.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the completion to 31 December 1889, and gives the light as "of the fifth order."[^1] The archive gives 1890 as the year the light was established, and records a fourth-order lens installed that year, so the sources disagree on the lens order.[^2]

The station had a three-floor keepers' residence inside the tower, with an iron circular staircase made in Detroit, and cisterns in the base to hold rainwater from the veranda.[^1] The Board's report for fiscal 1891 says that "A 1,200-pound bell, with striking machine, was placed at the station for temporary service until the installation of the" Daboll trumpet.[^3] The first keeper was John Farley, with Michael J. Curran as his assistant.[^1][^2] In 1922 the Service changed the station's illuminant from oil to electric incandescent.[^9]

## The keepers

The archive lists 96 keeper and crew lines for the station, from 1890 to 1972: 35 principal keepers, 38 first assistants and 23 Coast Guard names.[^2]

Joseph B. McCabe was assistant from 1908 to 1912, and keeper from 1912 to 1916.[^2] Elno C. Mott was assistant from 1914 to 1916 and keeper from 1916 to 1918.[^2] Merrill B. King kept the light from 1923 to 1931, and Thomas Small from 1931 to 1939.[^2] Frederick A.C. Bohm, from 1941 to 1943, was the last civilian keeper, LighthouseFriends says. For one and a half years during the Second World War he cared for the light and also patrolled the nearby waters for German submarines.[^1][^2]

Keeping the light was not easy. The Lighthouse Service's inspector in 1910, William W. Buchanan, recommended higher pay for the keepers and wrote that "Deer Island is the most disagreeable station in Boston Harbor." The sewer outfall was only 150 feet (46 m) away, and at times its spray reached the tower, LighthouseFriends quotes his report.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sea damaged the station early. The Board's report for fiscal 1891 says that "A window in the pier, which was stove in by the sea, was replaced by a 15-inch ship's side light."[^3]

In August 1895 the steamer *John Endicott* ran into a sloop yacht with three people aboard and sank it. The steamer *Philadelphia* managed to rescue one person, and LighthouseFriends says the other two "owed their lives" to the keeper of Deer Island Light, who put off in the station's dory.[^1]

The Board's report for fiscal 1899 mentions a channel through the mine fields between Deer Island and Long Island Head lights. Two black can buoys placed to mark that channel were discontinued on 6 August 1898.[^4]

LighthouseFriends says a powerful storm in November 1898, which it calls the Portland Gale, sank forty ships in the harbor. Keeper Wesley A. Pingree reported that it smashed the windows of the kitchen and let sea water in, spoiling the drinking water in the cistern. The waves also swept about the station's dory and landing ladder.[^1]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin records a run of rescues. On the night of 11 July 1914 Assistant Keeper Mott "rescued two men from a burning motor boat, beached the boat," and brought the exhausted men to the station, where they had food, dry clothing and shelter for the night.[^5] On 11 September 1915 Mott brought in the disabled power boat *Alice*, and the eleven men aboard were given food and lodging for the night.[^6] On 22 September 1915, McCabe and Mott towed the power boat *Madeline* to a safe anchorage. She had been "disabled in a strong northwest breeze."[^7] On 23 September 1917 Mott brought a disabled motor boat, with five people aboard, to a safe anchorage.[^8]

On Saturday night, 19 February 1916, the keeper of Deer Island light drowned on the bar between the island and the light.[^10] The Daily Kennebec Journal of Augusta, Maine, reported that he was running across the bar to light the light when a wave swept him into the harbor, and that "he never rose again."[^10] Both newspapers print his name as Joseph R. McCabe. The archive and the Bulletins give Joseph B. McCabe, keeper here from 1912 to 1916, and this story treats them as the same keeper on the strength of the dates and the post.[^2][^10][^11] The Manchester Journal gave his age as 35, and said he slipped off a sandbar leading out to the light.[^11] LighthouseFriends tells it differently. In its account McCabe had left the light to help his fiancée address their wedding invitations, and slipped on a big rock on the walk back. It gives his age as twenty-eight, and says his body was never found.[^1] The sources differ on his errand, his age and how he fell, and the story gives each account.

On 27 December 1930, LighthouseFriends reports, Keeper Merrill King, fearing the tower would be flooded, "poked cotton into all the cracks in the walls."[^1] In the winter of 1936 ice cracked the cast-iron caisson, and the foundation was then encircled with interlocking steel sheet piling and riprap.[^1]

In February 1972, during the storm that preceded the light's abandonment, the power failed. LighthouseFriends prints the account of Coast Guard Keeper Pedro D. Marticio, whose stint the archive ends in 1972.[^1][^2] He says all power was lost and the tower began to tip, and that the cutter *Pendant* "rammed its bow into the tower" before he and his crew jumped aboard.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard abandoned the light on 19 February 1972.[^1] LighthouseFriends says repairs were estimated at up to $400,000, and that because the light was "ineligible for the National Register," the decision was made to tear it down.[^1] Demolition began on 14 June 1982 and was finished three weeks later.[^1]

A white fiberglass tower went up on the old base in 1982, according to LighthouseFriends. The archive dates it to 1983.[^1][^2] It was built in England to withstand 110 miles an hour winds. Complaints about its poor visibility led to a brown fiberglass tower in 1984.[^1] In November 2015 the fiberglass tower was removed "from the foundation of the 1890 lighthouse," and replaced by "a square, skeletal tower mounted atop a nearby pile-supported platform."[^1][^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and the tower is closed.[^1] Deer Island is part of the Boston Harbor Islands park.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the offshore light "can be seen from the island" from its public walkway, and that it is best seen from the water.[^1] The archive lists the place as not open to the public.[^2] Since 1995 the island's primary occupant has been a wastewater treatment plant.[^1]
