Offer Wadham Island Light stands on the outermost of the Wadham Islands, a small group off the northeast tip of Newfoundland, at the eastern entrance to Hamilton Sound.[^1] It was placed in permanent operation on 4 October 1858 from a brick tower.[^1] The old tower still stands near the light, which is now shown from a square steel skeleton tower.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

## The place

The Wadhams lie just offshore from Newfoundland's northeast tip, and Offer Wadham is the outermost of them.[^1] Greenspond Historical Society places the light on a small island between Fogo Island and Musgrave Harbour, reachable only by private boat or helicopter.[^2]

In 1841 some 276 merchants, planters and other residents of Twillingate and the Fogo district petitioned the governor for a light on the Wadham Islands.[^1] They wrote of "the dangers to which vessels employed in navigating the coast are exposed."[^1] No action followed that year.[^1] An act of 1856 provided that a light should be built on Offer Wadham Island once the money was found, and the wording, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, is that "a Light House shall be Erected on the Offer Wadham Island."[^1]

The island was also a fishing station.[^1] By the 1870s a summer settlement of shacks and fish-drying stages had grown up on it, and its salt cod was counted the best in Newfoundland.[^1]

## Building the light

In March 1857 a lantern and lighting apparatus were ordered from David and Thomas Stevenson of Edinburgh.[^1] The Stevensons found the lens they received from Paris defective and had to get a second one, and the delay held the lens and lantern back until they reached St. John's on 16 September.[^1]

Robert Oke, a lighthouse inspector, designed the light.[^1] Alexander Smith, a stonemason, and William Campbell, a builder, put up the tower during 1857, the same year they built a light on Dodding Head near Burin.[^1] When Oke came ashore on 1 October 1857, he rated the red brick tower and its attached wooden dwelling satisfactory.[^1] Greenspond and Rowlett date the tower to 1858.[^2][^4]

Landing the gear proved harder.[^1] Oke had left St. John's late in September after a run of strong gales.[^1] Over the next two weeks several landings were tried, and only the two keepers, with their luggage, food and fuel, got ashore.[^1] "Incessant high seas and the lateness of the season" forced Oke to store the lighting apparatus at Seldom-Come-By, and the lighting planned for 1 March 1858 was put off.[^1]

Oke sailed again in July 1858 aboard the schooner Medway.[^1] The apparatus reached the island on 7 August and was installed on the tower the following week.[^1] Its fixed white light, from a double Argand burner behind a fourth-order lens, did not go into permanent service until 4 October.[^1] Its focal plane was 30.5 m (100 ft).[^1][^3]

The tower was changed several times.[^1][^4] In 1863 both faces of the tower received a coat of Portland cement.[^1][^4] By 1878 it was in an "extremely defective condition": it was encased in a new layer of cement brickwork, and a cast-iron gallery replaced the stone cornice.[^1] In 1886 the keepers' dwelling got a mansard roof in place of its pitched one.[^1] In 1890 a six-sided, fourth-order lens changed the signal to a white flash every thirty seconds.[^1] In 1893 the tower was encased in iron.[^1][^4] In 1961 it received a reinforced-concrete casing, octagonal in shape.[^1][^4]

## The keepers

Thomas Hennessey was the first head keeper.[^1] After repeated complaints to the Board of Works about the light's quality, Charles Prowse was sent to the island in November 1862 and again the following May.[^1] Hennessey and his son, then serving as assistant, were dismissed, and Prowse took charge of the light.[^1] Greenspond Historical Society gives Prowse's start as 1862 and Lighthouse Friends gives it as 1863.[^1][^2]

By Lighthouse Friends' reckoning, Prowse served from 1863 to 1899 and had many assistants.[^1] One was Henry Waterman. Inspector Nevill's report, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, says Waterman "came to an untimely end in the spring [of 1880] by falling through the ice," and describes him as "an officer who had always conducted himself worthily."[^1]

Elias Abbott kept the light from 1899 to 1904, Stephen Abbot from 1905 to 1908 (Greenspond spells it Abbott) and William Pomeroy from 1909 to 1916.[^1][^2] James Ford Mouland was keeper from 1916 to 1936.[^1] Lighthouse Friends also names Arch Way, without dates.[^1] During the Second World War Greenspond names Walter Hicks and Herbert Abbott as keepers.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives Hicks as keeper from at least 1941 to at least 1945.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1844 a select committee of the Assembly took testimony from mariners about the islands.[^1] John Slade told it that "during the previous four years no less than fourteen vessels had been lost on the Wadham Islands and several souls had perished."[^1] Thomas Knight, who had sailed numerous times between St. John's and Notre Dame Bay, said Offer Wadham was the only island in the group with a harbour, and that he had seen as many as twenty fishing boats at anchor there.[^1]

The sources record few dated storms at the station itself.[^1] The strong gales of 1857 held up the work (above), and the ice that took Waterman in 1880 is in the keepers' section.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1961 the iron tower received its concrete casing, and the station was then de-staffed. A former keeper, Walter Hicks, was taken on as caretaker.[^1] Greenspond says the old light was replaced by an automated light on a skeleton tower.[^2] The keepers' dwelling was pulled down in the 1990s on safety grounds.[^1]

Rowlett's directory gives the old tower as "Inactive since early 1990s", with a question mark, and calls it abandoned and clearly endangered.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says the old tower still stands nearby, minus its lantern room.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard list, last modified 23 June 2026, gives a light of Fl W 5s, with the focal plane at 30.5 m, a nominal range of 5 nautical miles, and a square skeleton tower with a red and white rectangular daymark, 7.1 m high. It marks the light "Seasonal."[^3]

Lighthouse Friends, Rowlett and Greenspond all give a white flash every three seconds, and Lighthouse Friends places it on a square skeletal tower.[^1][^2][^4] The sources therefore disagree on the flash interval, and the list is the most recent of them. None of the sources says when the characteristic changed, if it did.

Greenspond says the site is open and the tower closed, and that the island is accessible only by private boat or helicopter.[^2] Lighthouse Friends suggests seeing the light from a boat or the air, and says the Canadian Coast Guard owns it.[^1]
