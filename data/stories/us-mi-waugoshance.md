Waugoshance Light is a brick tower and keepers' house built on a reef in the open water of Lake Michigan, roughly two miles northwest of Waugoshance Island, off the tip of Waugoshance Point. Lit in 1851 and discontinued in 1912, it spent its working life with the lake all around it, and it is now an offshore ruin.[^1]

## The place

Waugoshance Point and the chain of small islands and reefs at its tip reach about seven miles into Lake Michigan from the northwest corner of the Lower Peninsula. Lighthouse Friends, drawing on the Light-House Board's reports, describes the whole stretch as a danger to mariners.[^1] Congress first set aside $5,000 for a light here in March 1837, and a naval officer, Lieutenant G. J. Pendergrast, was sent to choose the spot.[^1] He reported that the point itself was too far from the main channel, with a reef running out from it for nearly half a mile. He preferred a small detached reef lying between the point and the Mackinaw lightship, about 50 yards wide and 200 long, with five to seven feet of water over it.[^1] That reef is where the light was built. The Mackinaw Straits lightship, established in 1832, was then the only lightship on the Great Lakes, and Pendergrast suggested it could be put to use elsewhere once the lighthouse was built.[^1]

## Building the light

The work took longer than the appropriations did. Congress voted $25,000 in 1838, but nothing was done until 1847, when the project passed to the Bureau of Topographical Engineers and the money was voted again; later grants in 1848 and 1850 brought the total to $83,945.[^1] Captain Augustus Canfield of the Corps of Engineers was put in charge.[^1] In 1848 fifty-foot-square wooden cribs were built on St. Helena Island, towed fourteen miles to the shoal and filled with stone. The next season's work raised a circular tower of yellow Milwaukee brick, twenty feet across at the base and fourteen at the top, joined to a one-and-a-half-story brick dwelling.[^1] The stair, lantern and lighting apparatus went in during 1850 and the station began operating in 1851.[^1] Lighthouse Friends thus has the structure finished in 1850 and ready to begin operating in 1851; it does not give the night of first lighting.[^1]

An 1854 light list shows a fixed third-order Fresnel lens; by 1858 a fourth-order lens giving a flash every ninety seconds was in place.[^1] Canfield also fitted a copper tube that cooled and contracted if the single lamp went out and, through levers, set the fog bell ringing. It was tested each morning when the lamp was put out.[^1]

The lake kept attacking the foundation. The Light-House Board said in 1865 that the station was "second to none in the lake region" in importance and raised its estimate for protecting the cribwork to $200,000.[^1] In 1867 work began on a stone pier under the engineer William Sooy Smith, using an iron coffer-dam with an air lock, which Lighthouse Friends calls the first use of the pneumatic process for a lighthouse foundation in the United States.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1937 agrees that the old wooden pier around the lighthouse had "seriously deteriorated" and that work on replacing it with a stone pier began in 1867.[^9] Digging stopped in 1868 without reaching bedrock; the stone pier was finished in 1870, and a new dwelling for the keepers followed at a cost of $12,000.[^1]

In 1883 a crew of thirty-nine spent nearly two months encasing the tower and dwelling in iron and building a fog-signal house for duplicate ten-inch steam whistles, which first sounded on 19 October 1883.[^1] The tower was painted in horizontal red and white bands in 1893, and in 1894 the light changed from a flash every ninety seconds to one every forty-five.[^1] A square concrete protecting pier followed in 1896. The Board's report for that year says a working party was landed at Waugoshance on 21 May, that old timber was cut out and steel casings and concrete placed, and that in nearly six weeks after the landing there had been only two weeks of fair weather. High winds and seas interfered with the work, and "the swamping of the lighters and loss by stranding on the reefs was often imminent."[^4] The year before, plans and contracts for the concrete protection had been made.[^5] In the report for the year ended 30 June 1900, the Board records that the fog-signal boilers were sent to the station and the fog-signal house was nearly completed.[^6]

## The keepers

The station needed a head keeper and two assistants.[^1] The Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the keepers' registers, lists seventeen head-keeper terms between 1852 and 1912. The longest were John M. McHarry (1865-1877), John Herman (1890-1900) and Ingvald Olsen (1902-1910).[^2] Henry G. Graveraet, Jr., served as first assistant from 1856 to 1861.[^2] The same family appears again and again. George W. and Charles M. Marshall both served here, and another Marshall, James M., was first assistant from 1904 to 1910 and again from 1910 to 1912.[^2] The archive describes Thomas Marshall, head keeper here from 1882 to 1886, as brother to keepers George W. and Charles M. Marshall.[^2][^3]

Two head keepers died in service, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] Thomas Marshall drowned on 28 May 1886. A newspaper item transcribed by the archive says he left Mackinac Island in a small boat in tow of the propeller T. S. Faxton; about a mile out, "the sea being heavy, he had the steamer let go of his tow-line. An hour after his boat was found empty." He left a family on the island "in poor circumstances."[^3]

John Herman's end is told as a legend. Lighthouse Friends says he perished in 1900, and the retelling says he locked an assistant in the lantern room as a joke and was gone when the man got out, and that later odd happenings were put down to his ghost.[^1] The register shows Herman's tenure as head keeper ending in 1900 and James O. Gallagher's starting that year.[^2] The story rests on that one retelling; no contemporary account of his death is cited.

Another tale, set in 1871, concerns assistant keeper James Davenport, who appears in the register as first assistant for 1871 to 1873.[^2] In one version Keeper Davenport rang the fog bell for three days through smoke from the Great Peshtigo Fire; in another he entertained the crew of a schooner stranded on the reef with his violin.[^1] These are told as stories, and two versions disagree.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The station's fog signal was a regular part of the year's work. The Board's reports give the steam whistle's running time: some 391 hours in fiscal 1895, 320 hours in fiscal 1896 and 200 in fiscal 1897.[^5][^4]

In August 1881 the Cheboygan *Northern Tribune* printed a captain's report of counting "35 vessels" in under an hour "off Waugoshance" on a Wednesday night with the wind "blowing a living gale."[^7] In July 1884 the same paper noted a plan to look for the boiler and machinery of the propeller *Canisteo*, "sunk near Waugoshance light a couple of years' since."[^8]

## After the keepers

White Shoal Lighthouse, two miles north, began operating in September 1910, and Waugoshance was discontinued two years later.[^1] In August 1943 the Navy took over several islands in northern Lake Michigan, including some off Waugoshance Point, for training. The lighthouse became part of a bombing and gunnery range for Naval Air Station Traverse City and was bombed and strafed; one attack is reported to have left the structure badly burned.[^1]

The Coast Guard declared the light excess, and in 2009 it was offered free to eligible public and nonprofit bodies. In October 2011 Michigan's senators announced it would go to the Waugoshance Lighthouse Preservation Society, which had been working toward its restoration.[^1] The National Register of Historic Places had listed the light station on 4 August 1983.[^10] In January 2021 the society's board voted to dissolve, concluding the lighthouse could not be saved. High water was eroding the base, and a temporary fix quoted at over $300,000 would have lasted only a year or two.[^1]

## Today

The ruin stands in open water and is best seen from a boat or the air; nearby waters are hazardous, and the tower is closed.[^1] Lighthouse Friends lists the preservation society as owner, but the society has since dissolved, so who now holds title is not established here. Do not attempt to land on or climb the structure.
