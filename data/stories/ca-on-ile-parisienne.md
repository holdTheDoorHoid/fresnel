Ile Parisienne Light stands at the southwest end of Ile Parisienne, an uninhabited island in the middle of Whitefish Bay on Lake Superior.[^1][^7] The Department of Marine had a hexagonal concrete tower built there in 1911, and the light was lit in 1912.[^1] In April 1922 the government tender *Lambton*, carrying keepers for the lights of eastern Lake Superior, was lost with all hands in a severe storm off Caribou Island.[^5][^1]

## The place

The island lies near the middle of Whitefish Bay.[^1] In the early 1900s vessels sailing between Whitefish Point and a buoy on Gros Cap Reef would pass within about two miles of its southern tip, and the Canadian government eventually decided to place a lighthouse there.[^1] The island was held by the Crown in its entirety until 1902, when a fishing company bought 114 acres on its south-east side.[^1] The register links the light to the early-twentieth-century upgrading of navigational aids on the Great Lakes, and to the growth in marine traffic that followed the opening of the Sault Ste. Marie Canal.[^2]

## Building the light

In the year to 31 March 1911 the Department inspected sites for proposed lighthouses on Lake Superior, at Michipicoten Island and at Ile Parisienne.[^3] On 29 August 1911 William Fryer, an architect and builder from Collingwood, signed a contract for $14,650 to build the light, a dwelling, an oil shed, a boathouse and a wharf on the island.[^1] W. H. Carson, an engineer-draughtsman, was at the island as inspector of work from September to November 1911.[^4]

The tower is a hexagonal, reinforced-concrete structure with buttresses, and a red iron lantern.[^1] The 1912 description, as LighthouseFriends prints it, gives its height as 17.7 m (58 ft) from base to lantern vane.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory gives 9 m (30 ft) for the hexagonal concrete tower, so the sources do not agree on its height.[^7] The light was of the second-order dioptric type, shown 16.5 m (54 ft) above the lake, and visible 12 miles from all points of approach by water.[^1] Its fog signal was a diaphone, worked by compressed air.[^1] The station commenced operations on 15 September 1912, according to the same 1912 description.[^1]

## The keepers

John Douglas was hired as the first keeper in 1912.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that he and five other keepers on Lake Superior were lost in the spring of 1922 while returning to their stations. The departmental report on the loss does not name them, so that part is unconfirmed.[^1][^5] The station had a keeper and an assistant keeper, and in 1922 the *Lambton* was to land both at Ile Parisienne.[^1]

After Douglas came J. D. McKinnon (1922 to 1924) and A. Mitchell (1924 to 1934). In 1934 E. J. Dayrymple and A. Hurley each served a short term, and C. N. McDonald was there at least until 1937.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 28 June 1916 a dispatch from Sault Ste. Marie reported that the steamer *Panther* had sunk after a collision in a dense fog with the steamer *James J. Hill*, which arrived at Sault Ste. Marie with the *Panther*'s crew. The dispatch places the collision "off Persian island" in Whitefish Bay.[^9] The spelling is the paper's, and the dispatch does not name Ile Parisienne, so the link to this island is an inference.

The *Lambton* left Sault Ste. Marie on 18 April 1922 with all the lightkeepers for the eastern end of Lake Superior on board. Heavy ice met the vessels in Whitefish Bay, and gales were sweeping the lake. Ice floes damaged the *Lambton*'s steering, and her two escorts turned back.[^5][^1] She was lost with all hands off Caribou Island on 19 April, in a severe storm.[^5] A dispatch printed in the *Milwaukee Leader* on 26 April said she was believed to have carried about 17 men, and that she had left to light the lights on Michipicoten and Caribou islands.[^8] LighthouseFriends gives a crew of sixteen and five keepers.[^1] The two counts do not agree, and the departmental report names no one aboard.[^5] The Department's expenditure list for the year to 31 March 1923 includes an allowance to relatives of the *Lambton*'s crew.[^6]

## After the keepers

The light was declared a recognised federal heritage building in 1991.[^1] The register calls the tower a very good example of early modernism in concrete.[^2] The rest of the island became the Ile Parisienne Conservation Reserve in 2001.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory says the two keepers' houses are staffed in season, as the first contact for eastbound ships entering the Soo Traffic Control Area.[^7]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light.[^1] The island is reached only by boat, and the Lighthouse Directory says the site and tower are closed to visitors.[^7] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower closed, so treat the site as closed unless the Coast Guard says otherwise.[^1] The light shows a white flash every ten seconds from a focal height of 16 m (53 ft).[^7] About 46 ha (114 acres) at the south-east corner of the island is privately owned.[^7]
