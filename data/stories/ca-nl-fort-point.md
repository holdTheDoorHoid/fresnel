Fort Point Light stands on a promontory at the south side of the entrance to Trinity Harbour, on Trinity Bay, and has been shown since 1 January 1874.[^1] For eighty-eight unbroken years the keeping of it belonged to one family, the Rowes. In November 1938 the schooner *Marion Rogers* was lost a few metres from the fog alarm.[^1]

## The place

Lighthouse Friends says Sir Richard Whitbourne called Trinity's harbour, in 1620, "the best and largest Harbour in all the land", and that the fishery made Trinity a significant fishing centre in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.[^1]

The point at the harbour mouth was fortified early.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates the first battery there to 1744.[^1] The Trinity Historical Society says the fort "was fortified in 1746" and that it "boasted three batteries mounting 18 guns".[^3] The two dates disagree, and these sources do not settle which is right.

In 1762 the French took Trinity.[^4] Community Stories says the town surrendered on 17 July.[^4] The Society says that before the French left on 1 August they split the heavy guns at Fort Point and rolled them over the cliffs into the sea, and that the magazine was destroyed and the other buildings were set on fire.[^3] During the War of 1812 Trinity's two chief merchant firms, Slade's and Garland, agreed to rebuild a battery at Fort Point.[^3]

The Society also tells a story, which it gives as a story rather than a record, of a Sunday morning when a gun fired from the fort during morning prayers and the volunteers were called out for an enemy that was not there. It says two mischievous youths had crossed to the fort, and while one distracted the watchman, the other fired the gun.[^3]

## Building the light

Work on the light began in 1872, Lighthouse Friends says, when the Scottish firm D. & T. Stevenson was paid $653 for the lighting apparatus and G. H. Cole was paid $158 for a wharf and road to the site. A further $2,345 was spent in 1873.[^1] A notice to mariners announced that "a fixed white Light will be exhibited nightly from sunset to sunrise at this place upon a wooden tower painted white", at 23 m (75 ft) above the sea, and visible in clear weather for eleven miles.[^1] The Newfoundland 3D Lighthouse Gallery gives the first tower's range as 9.5 nautical miles.[^5] The two figures differ, and the sources do not say which kind of mile the notice used. The apparatus was an eighth-order dioptric lens with a single argand burner, throwing light over an arc of 320 degrees.[^1]

The light changed in 1908, from fixed white to an occulting white showing 6.25 seconds of light and 1.25 seconds of darkness.[^1] In 1910 a fog alarm building was added, on lower ground south of the lighthouse, with a diaphone sounded by air compressed by oil engines.[^1] In 1921 the wooden tower was replaced by a circular iron tower about half its height. The gambrel-roofed building the wooden tower had stood on was kept, and the iron tower was joined to it by a covered passageway.[^1]

## The keepers

William Harve was paid $16 on 3 January 1874 for instructing William Rowe, the first keeper, in his duties.[^1] Rowe was the first of a long line of Rowes at the station.[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives the keepers as William Rowe (1874 to 1884), James Rowe (1884 to 1905), a second William Rowe (1906 to 1910), Arch. Rowe (1911 to 1920) and Henry James Rowe (1920 to 1962).[^1] James Rowe took over "in place of his father".[^1] The sources do not say whether the two William Rowes were the same man, and no source links Arch. Rowe to the William Arthur Rowe of 1938.

Inspector John T. Nevill visited in 1884 and found the station "in its usual scrupulously clean condition".[^1] In 1889 he wrote that James Rowe could "certainly claim the distinction of having the best-kept station in the whole service".[^1] Lighthouse Friends lists two more names under the fog alarm: Thomas Pike for 1910, and W. Connolly for 1911 and at least 1912.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1877 Nevill reported the lens damaged when a lamp's glass chimney exploded.[^1] He wrote: "Every one knows that a lamp glass, on a carefully trimmed and regulated lamp may break at any time from unascertained causes."[^1] Lighthouse Friends says no blame fell on Keeper Rowe, because according to the inspector his pay was not enough for, and his instructions did not require, a continuous nightly watch.[^1]

In 1882 Keeper Rowe complained that the square wooden tower "shook considerably during storms", and that many bolts had worked loose as the timbers shrank.[^1] The fog alarm of 1910 sounded, in thick weather, a pattern of "three-second blast, ten seconds of silence, three-second blast, ninety-four seconds silence".[^1]

On 27 November 1938 the schooner *Marion Rogers* sailed from St. John's with six crew, one passenger and a cargo of goods.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says she was bound for Trinity, her home port, and that as she neared it a "blinding snow squall" struck.[^1] She ran aground a few metres from the fog alarm building.[^1] The *Fisherman's Advocate* reported that the schooner "struck the rocks near the Fort Point Lighthouse, at the entrance to Trinity, where there is also a fog alarm", and that "Seven men went to watery graves".[^2] The Advocate was less sure of her destination. It said the particulars "are indefinite", and that she may have been bound for New Bonaventure, with the weather perhaps forcing the master to try for Trinity.[^2] The Advocate's crew list names the master, William Hogarth, with his son Lester, Alfred Pitcher and his son Simeon, William J. Butler, Ellis Butler and Edward McGrath.[^2] Elsewhere the Advocate calls William J. Butler and Edward McGrath passengers, where Lighthouse Friends counts one passenger in all.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends says the seven men aboard all perished, that it took several days to recover the bodies, and that one body was never found.[^1][^2]

A magisterial inquiry followed.[^1] Magistrate Arthur Squarey heard Henry James Rowe, keeper of the light and fog alarm, and William Arthur Rowe, the assistant keeper.[^1] He found their evidence "contradictory and confusing", and wrote that "the duties of both the Keeper and the Assistant Keeper have been performed in a slip shod manner". He also wrote that the keeper "must have spent over one third of his watch away from the fog alarm".[^1] The Superintendent of Lighthouses, T. S. Devine, concluded that the keeper had put the fog alarm on when it was needed. He recommended that the keeper and assistant be disciplined for leaving the fog alarm unattended while it was in operation.[^1] With no survivors, Lighthouse Friends says, "much uncertainty about the incident remains".[^1]

## After the keepers

Henry James Rowe retired at 65 in 1962, "ending eighty-eight unbroken years of lightkeeping at Fort Point by the Rowe family".[^1]

The short iron tower was demolished on 25 June 1971, and a red skeleton tower 4.3 m tall was erected in its place.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates the present tower to about 2003.[^1] The Newfoundland 3D Lighthouse Gallery calls it "the current wooden structure" of 2003.[^5] The Coast Guard's list describes a "Square skeleton tower" 12.4 m high, and names no material.[^6]

In 2011 the Trinity Historical Society announced plans to convert one of the two keepers' homes into an interpretation centre, with completion timed to coincide with the 250th anniversary of the French invasion of 1762.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says Fort Point is still a staffed station, and that the second keeper's building is still occupied by the Coast Guard.[^1] A Fisheries and Oceans Canada brochure (page modified 2019) also lists Fort Point among the staffed lightstations.[^7] The Coast Guard's list gives the light as operating year round, 24 hours a day, with a fog horn that sounds a 2-second blast and then 18 seconds of silence.[^6] Because the station is staffed, visitors should keep to the open grounds and not climb the tower.[^1]

The Trinity Historical Society runs the Fort Point Military Site.[^3] Its website gives the summer hours as "open from June 24 to August 30", 9:30 am to 4:30 pm daily, and says the site explores "the history of the salt cod trade in the harbour, lighthouse keepers, and local shipwrecks".[^3] Several cannons stand on the grounds.[^1] The road is dirt for the last 4.4 km, from Dunfield to the end at the lighthouse.[^1]
