The Grand Haven South Pierhead Inner Light is the inner of two red lights on the South Pier, where the Grand River meets Lake Michigan.[^1][^2][^8] Its cast-iron conical tower, with a focal plane of 52 feet (15.8 m), was first lit in 1905, and the Conservancy says it stands 600 feet (183 m) back from the end of the pier.[^2][^3][^4]

## The place

Grand Haven sits on the south side of the mouth of the Grand River.[^1] The port's first light, which the Conservancy dates to 1839, stood on the beach.[^3] Shore erosion was a long-running problem.[^1][^3] The Conservancy's history says a late fall storm took out the seawall on 6 December 1852, and that the tower and cottage collapsed on 17 December.[^3] Lighthouse Friends puts the wall's collapse on the night of 17 December, and says the dwelling wall fell early the next morning and the tower later that day.[^1] The port had no beacon for the next three years.[^3]

A bluff-top tower replaced it in 1854 and 1855 and remained the main port light until 1905.[^1][^3] The South Pier was extended over time.[^1] When it reached its present length in 1893 it was given a pier-head light.[^3] The tower this story follows grew out of that pier light.[^1][^3]

## Building the light

The pier light was never one tower. Lighthouse Friends says a steam siren in a fog-signal house under the pierhead light began operating in December 1875, that the light and fog signal moved lakeward in 1883 and again in 1885 as the pier grew, and that a tubular lantern was set up on 30 November 1889 to make a range with it.[^1]

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1895 records the range moves. On 6 July 1894 the front range light was moved 278 feet (85 m) nearer the outer end of the south pier.[^5] On 4 May 1895 the pierhead light itself was moved 511 feet (156 m) further out, and the front range light was discontinued "on account of the removal of the pierhead light to the outer end of the pier."[^5]

The present tower came ten years later. The Conservancy says that in the fall of 1904 a 52-foot (15.8 m) steel tower was placed on the end of the South Pier, and that on 12 January 1905 the Fresnel lens was moved from the bluff to the new tower.[^3] The U.S. Lighthouse Society record gives the tower as built in 1905, of cast iron and conical in shape.[^4] The National Park Service inventory gives cast iron, 51 feet (15.5 m) tall.[^2] The Conservancy's "steel" and the Park Service's "cast iron" disagree, and this story follows the two sources that say cast iron. The Conservancy adds that the ship captains had not been told of the switch, and that the captains of the steamers coming in that night "were totally confused."[^3]

The tower then moved. Lighthouse Friends says a temporary red light was set up shoreward of the metal pierhead tower on 28 April 1906 to form a range, and that over the next year the cylindrical tower was moved nearly 600 feet (183 m) to a new concrete foundation.[^1] The Conservancy dates that move to 1907.[^3] The Park Service inventory answers "NO" to whether the tower has been moved.[^2] The two accounts that describe the move do not fit that answer, and the sources do not settle it.

The lens is also disputed. Lighthouse Friends says the 1854 Fresnel lens from the bluff tower was used in the 1855 tower and the cylindrical pierhead tower, and that the cylindrical tower carried a fourth-order lens.[^1] The Park Service inventory and the Lighthouse Society record give the original optic as sixth order, and the present one as a 250 mm lens.[^2][^4] Lighthouse Friends says the bluff lens is now on display in the Train Depot Museum in Grand Haven.[^1]

Cyberlights gives 1839 as the station's year of establishment, as the Park Service inventory does, and says the inner light was "originally built in 1839, then moved and rebuilt in 1905."[^2][^8] The 1839 date belongs to the first light on the beach.[^1][^3] The other sources cited here date this tower to 1904 or 1905, so the story does not give the inner tower an 1839 date.

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society keeps one roster for the whole pier range, not one for each tower. Its 97 entries run from 1872 to 1969, so the roster does not say which light a keeper worked in during any given year.[^4]

The roster's earliest woman keeper is Mary Miller, first assistant from 1872 to 1875, when the pierhead light was the wooden tower put in place in 1871.[^1][^4] Lighthouse Friends' list for the whole Grand Haven station gives "Mrs. Harry Miller" in the same post for the same years, but neither source says the two names belong to one woman.[^1]

Frank Fraga went from first assistant (1892 to 1900) to principal keeper (1900 to 1911).[^4] Joseph Kilgore was principal keeper from 1911 to 1920, and Charles Bavry from 1920 to 1926.[^4] Nels Engberg followed from 1926 to 1942, and Rolla J. Paris served as first assistant from 1918 to at least 1945 before becoming principal keeper in 1947.[^4] Michael F. Wickman's name is the last entry in the principal-keeper column, from 1969.[^4]

The roster lists David E. Sparks as principal keeper in 1961.[^4] Lighthouse Friends describes him as a Coast Guard crewman on duty in the light that year, when he went into the water after a man who had been swept off the pier.[^1] The rescue is described below.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Lighthouse Friends says a gale on 9 December 1886 forced the pier beacon twelve feet (3.7 m) backward and crushed the elevated walk behind it.[^1] That beacon was the pier light of its day.[^1] The present tower was built in 1904 and first lit in 1905.[^1][^2]

On 25 January 1916 keeper Joseph Kilgore rescued two Coast Guard men whom the ice threatened to carry out into Lake Michigan.[^6]

On 14 September 1961, during the remnants of Hurricane Carla, two young men walked out on the pier to watch the waves.[^1] One was washed off between the front and rear range lights.[^1] Coast Guard crewman David E. Sparks, on duty in the light, threw a life ring and went into the water after him. The large waves thwarted the rescue, and Sparks held onto the ring until Coast Guard men reached him.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the Coast Guard awarded him the gold lifesaving medal in 1962.[^1] The two young men are not named here.

## After the keepers

On 15 May 1917 the rear light was changed from white to red and the front light from red to white, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] The Lighthouse Service's index for 1918 to 1923 lists a change of illuminant at the Grand Haven Pierhead Range Rear.[^7]

The Park Service inventory says both pier lights were automated in 1969.[^2] The Coast Guard declared the pier lights excess in 2009, and the City of Grand Haven received a deed to the two pierhead lights in December 2012.[^1] A community conservancy was formed to care for them, and it has raised money to restore them.[^1][^3]

## Today

The Park Service inventory, as archived in 2007, lists the inner light as operational and the station as open to the public.[^2] The Lighthouse Society record also marks the station open to the public.[^4] The City of Grand Haven owns the pierhead lights.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends' visitor note says "Grounds open, tower closed."[^1] The note covers the whole Grand Haven page and does not name a tower, and none of the cited sources says the inner tower can be climbed. Lighthouse Friends' directions say visitors can walk the breakwater to the lights.[^1]
