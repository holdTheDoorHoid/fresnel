Cape Norman Lighthouse stands on the cliffs at the northern tip of Newfoundland, where the Great Northern Peninsula ends above the Strait of Belle Isle, in Newfoundland and Labrador.[^1][^9] Its light was first exhibited on 1 October 1871.[^1] The tower standing there now is concrete, finished in 1964.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the station was automated in 1992 and that a keeper was hired again in 2002.[^1]

## The place

Cape Norman is the northernmost point of the island of Newfoundland. LighthouseFriends says French explorers named it Cape Normand because its high cliffs reminded them of their homeland in Normandy.[^1]

The Strait of Belle Isle was a busy route.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes an 1870 Department of Marine statement that the Strait was used by the Ocean Mail steamers of Canada, and that Canada's policy was to make the St. Lawrence the great highway to the west.[^1] Two first-order lights already stood on the Strait, at Belle Isle and Point Amour, both set up in 1858. Cape Norman would mark the eastern entrance.[^1]

The need had been raised before. LighthouseFriends quotes John Page, Chief Engineer of Public Works for Canada, who wrote in 1860, after a visit, that Cape Norman was the site he would pick to make the Strait safer and easier to navigate.[^1] When Canada approached Newfoundland, the Governor wrote that his government would give a free grant of land for the lighthouse and its dwelling.[^1]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says work on the wooden hexagonal tower began in 1870, and that the tower measured 12 m (40 ft) from base to vane.[^1] The light was first exhibited on 1 October 1871.[^1] Its revolving apparatus had two faces with three lamps each, giving a white flash every two minutes.[^1] The light's focal plane was 42 m (138 ft) above the sea.[^1] The whole station cost $10,141.96.[^1]

## The keepers

Henry Locke, a local man, was appointed the first keeper at $500 a year.[^1] In 1890 John William Campbell, a man from Pictou, Nova Scotia, who had worked with steam engines, oversaw the new steam fog alarm and stayed on to tend it.[^1] When Locke retired, Campbell took charge of the light too.[^1]

The Department's keeper tables for the fiscal years ending 1904 and 1906 list Campbell as keeper at Cape Norman, light and fog alarm.[^4][^5] The report for the fiscal year ending 1900 records that a small building for coal, 8.5 m by 3.7 m (28 by 12 ft), had been bought in the locality by the keeper and set up near the fog alarm building.[^2]

LighthouseFriends follows the Campbell family at the station. The roster gives Alexander Warren Campbell from 1911 to 1946, Jacob S. Campbell from 1946 to at least 1961, Alvin Campbell from 1984 to 1992, and Warren Campbell from 2002 to at least 2017. It counts five generations of Campbells as keepers.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On Sunday 4 August 1889 the Dominion Line steamer Montreal ran aground on Belle Isle in fog.[^1][^10] The New-York Tribune reported from Quebec that Captain Wall and about sixty people, crew and passengers, were on the island awaiting help.[^10] The wreck was at Belle Isle, not Cape Norman.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the loss prompted the steam foghorn at Cape Norman, which began to sound a year later.[^1]

The Department's monthly notes for Cape Norman record the weather and the ice. They note that the first snow fell on 22 October 1899, and that one iceberg was sighted daily that month.[^2] For October 1900 they record that eleven icebergs were sighted.[^3]

## After the keepers

In the fiscal year ending 1906 the Department decided to replace the old tower. Its report said a segmental cast-iron tower would be erected at the station. The light was changed to triple flashing, because Cape Bauld was to become a double flash, and a third-order apparatus procured for Seal Island, Nova Scotia, was used here.[^5]

Construction of the new tower, fog-alarm machinery and double dwelling ran through the fiscal years ending 1906 to 1908.[^5][^6][^7] LighthouseFriends says the new light began operating in 1907.[^1] In the fiscal year ending 1909 the old wooden tower was razed to its first floor and turned into a storehouse.[^8]

LighthouseFriends says the fog alarm was converted from steam to oil in 1923.[^1] A Marine report for the fiscal year ending 1924 also records the change at Cape Norman.[^11] LighthouseFriends also says a contract for a new concrete tower was let in 1963, and that the tower, which stands today, was completed in 1964.[^1]

The station was automated in 1992.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse ran automated for ten years, and Warren Campbell was hired as keeper in 2002.[^1]

The Canadian Coast Guard's List of Lights gives the present light as Fl(3) W 27s, with a focal height of 38.7 m, visible 21 nautical miles, from a white octagonal tower with a red top.[^9]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard.[^1] LighthouseFriends lists keepers at the station to at least 2017, and says a small building beside the lighthouse serves as the keeper's office. The sources read do not confirm whether keepers still work there.[^1] The grounds are open and the tower is closed, so do not climb the tower.[^1]
