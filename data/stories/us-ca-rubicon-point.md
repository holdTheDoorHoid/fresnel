Rubicon Point Light is a lighthouse on the west shore of Lake Tahoe, California. The Lighthouse Service put an acetylene light there in 1919 and moved it north in 1921. The lighthouse now stands in D. L. Bliss State Park.[^1]

## The place

Lake Tahoe lies on the California and Nevada line, and the Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for 1917 names it "Lake Tahoe, Cal. and Nev."[^2] The same report gives the lake's length as 21 miles and says that "a great many passengers are carried on numerous steamers and launches there annually."[^2] After three years of requests from the Lake Tahoe Protective Association, Congress authorized aids to navigation for the lake.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress authorized aids to navigation on Lake Tahoe by an act of 28 August 1916.[^2] No money came with it. The Commissioner's report for 1917 says "no appropriation was made therefor," and that funds had not been available from the Lighthouse Service's general appropriations.[^2]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1919 lists a new light at Rubicon Point, Lake Tahoe, California, as flashing white, shown by a lens lantern on acetylene.[^3] The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1920 records the work under its Eighteenth District heading: an acetylene light on a small lantern house, at a cost of $1,352, with its focal plane 200 feet above the lake and flashing white every five seconds.[^4]

## After the keepers

In 1921 the light at Rubicon Point was discontinued and moved about four and a half miles north to Sugar Pine Point, where it was set on a square, pyramid-shaped wooden tower.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's list of new lights for 1921 includes "Sugar Pine Point, Lake Tahoe, Calif.," flashing white from a lens lantern on acetylene.[^5]

In 1929 the D. L. Bliss family donated 744 acres to the State of California, and D. L. Bliss State Park was created to include the lighthouse.[^1] After the nonprofit Tahoe Heritage Foundation helped raise about $25,000, the lighthouse was renovated and stabilized in 2001.[^1]

After further vandalism, plans for another repair project began in 2015.[^1] A $48,000 grant from the Tahoe Fund to California State Parks in 2017, part of a plan to reroute a steep stretch of the Rubicon Trail, helped pay for work on the lighthouse. The work included timber braces on the inside walls and an iron frame fitted inside the door to prevent unwanted entry.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse stands in D. L. Bliss State Park. Lighthouse Friends describes a walk of just under a mile from the park's parking lots.[^1] Nothing in the sources says the inside is open to visitors. The iron frame inside the door is there to prevent unwanted entry, so visitors should not try to enter or climb the building.[^1] The most recent work in the sources is from 2017, and they do not show the lighthouse's condition since then.
