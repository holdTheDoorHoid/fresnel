Mile Rocks Light stood on two rocks off the south side of the Golden Gate, at the mouth of San Francisco Bay.[^2] It was first lit on 15 February 1906, after the steamer *City of Rio de Janeiro* sank in fog at the Golden Gate in 1901.[^1][^2] The upper floors of the tower came down in 1965 and 1966, and what remains is the lower part of the tower on its concrete base, with a helicopter landing pad on top.[^2][^3]

## The place

The two rocks, known together as Mile Rocks, lie roughly half a mile from the nearest shore.[^2] LighthouseFriends suggests the name comes from their position: the rocks are one mile south of the main shipping channel into the bay.[^2]

The Lighthouse Service tried a buoy first. In November 1889 it placed a bell buoy near the rocks, but the currents pulled the buoy under and even set it adrift, and the bell was withdrawn in May 1890.[^2] Buoys in these waters were hard to keep in place.[^2][^4] The Light-House Board's report for fiscal 1901 describes a large nun buoy marking Fort Point Ledge that the tides dragged off its ledge on 18 May 1901. With its 11,000-pound sinker and all its moorings intact, the buoy "anchored itself in the vicinity of Mile Rock."[^4]

On 22 February 1901 the steamer *City of Rio de Janeiro*, coming in through fog, struck Fort Point and went down. NOAA reports that "Of the 210 on board, 128 lives were lost."[^1] The sources differ on how fast she sank. NOAA says she sank "within 10 minutes," and LighthouseFriends says she was lost in eight.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends reports that the Lighthouse Board concluded the wreck might not have happened if a fog signal could be heard well seaward of the ledge.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $100,000 on 28 June 1902 for a light on the rocks.[^2] Colonel Thomas H. Handbury, the engineer of the Twelfth Lighthouse District, drew up the plans.[^2][^6] A San Francisco paper described the design that November. The base would be concrete "faced with granite blocks extending to 24 feet above low water plane," with a tower "having a base diameter of 36 feet" above it, and "Three keepers will be required."[^5] The same paper noted that the coast's marine interests had long urged Congress "to make an appropriation to build a lighthouse at this point."[^5]

James A. McMahon of San Francisco got the foundation contract in 1904. The first crew quit once they had seen the rock. A second crew, of "deep-sea sailors recruited from San Francisco," did the work, and the small schooner *Rio Rey* anchored nearby to serve as floating quarters.[^2] The crews blasted a level place for the base and built reinforced-concrete walls four feet (1.2 m) thick, enclosed in steel plates, up to 35 feet (11 m).[^2] LighthouseFriends quotes the Board's own report on the difficulty: "There was hardly a day during the year on which at some time the waves did not break over the rock."[^2]

By July 1905 the work was close to done. The San Francisco Call reported that the compressed-air engines "were hoisted to their positions," and that "The three keepers who are to manage the light have been selected."[^6]

The light was first lit on 15 February 1906, with a third-order, L. Sautter & Co. Fresnel lens in the lantern.[^2] The station's ten-inch whistle was to sound a three-second blast every thirty seconds in thick or foggy weather.[^2]

## The keepers

Head Keeper Isaac Knutsen took charge of the station on 28 December 1905.[^2] Three officials from the Board had come out with him. When they tried to leave, the sea was too rough for the launch to reach them, and they had to spend the night at the lighthouse.[^2] The archive's list of keepers begins with "Isaac Knutsen (1905-1907)."[^3] He was followed by Gottfrid T. Olson, "Gottfrid T. Olson (1907-1916)," who had first served as an assistant from 1906.[^3]

Later head keepers included William H. Hicks, listed as "William H. Hicks (1917-1920)," and Lyman A. Woodruff, listed as "Lyman A. Woodruff (1943-1949)" after serving as a first assistant from 1932.[^3] Most of the names in the archive's list are assistants.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Less than a month after the light was activated, the station nearly lost an assistant. LighthouseFriends reports that Assistant Keeper William P. Holmes "nearly lost his life when heavy seas swamped his skiff" while he was returning to the station. The other keepers had no boat to reach him and could only watch. A pilot boat spotted him and pulled him from the water.[^2] The archive lists him as "William P.D. Holmes (1905-1906)."[^3]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1919 reports that on 1 February, W. H. Hicks, keeper of Mile Rocks, "rescued from drowning three young men whose canoe had capsized near the light station, two of whom were being carried out to sea with a strong ebb tide."[^7]

The same year's Bulletin also lists an improvement to the fog signal: "Fog signal improved: Mile Rocks," which changed the signal from an air whistle to an air diaphone.[^8]

## After the keepers

Automation came in stages. The work in 1964 included a submarine cable, which failed that November, and generators had to run the light for a time.[^2] On 18 August 1965 the light was deactivated, and the Coast Guard removed its personnel for rebuilding. The lantern room and the top two tiers were dismantled and a landing pad was built, at a cost of $110,000.[^2] The archive dates the helicopter platform, which replaced the upper floor of the tower, to 1966.[^3]

San Francisco Supervisor William Blake and the Conference of California Historical Societies protested the automation plans, LighthouseFriends reports, but the Coast Guard went ahead with the cost-saving measure.[^2] The San Francisco Maritime Museum tried to acquire the top forty feet (12 m) for display. Saving that part would have added $87,000 to the $110,000 contract, so the living quarters were scrapped.[^2] The original third-order lens went to Old Point Loma Lighthouse in San Diego, where, according to LighthouseFriends, it is still in use.[^2]

## Today

The tower survives only in its lower part, with a helicopter landing pad on top.[^2][^3] The Coast Guard owns the light, and LighthouseFriends says the tower is closed.[^2] The archive lists the station as not open to the public, and its record shows the light is no longer an active aid to navigation.[^3] LighthouseFriends notes that the base is now painted in orange and white bands.[^2] Mile Rocks is a rock offshore, and no one should try to land on it.
