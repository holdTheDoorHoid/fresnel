Devils Island Light stands on the north end of Devils Island, the northernmost of the Apostle Islands in Lake Superior, in Ashland County, Wisconsin. It was the last lighthouse built in the Apostle group, and it was the last of them to lose its resident crew.[^1][^2]

## The place

Devils Island sits at the northern edge of the archipelago, and in the account of Terry Pepper, a Great Lakes lighthouse historian, it was an important turning point for vessels running east or west along Superior's south shore. By his account the island stayed unlit for more than thirty years while traffic grew.[^3] The Light-House Board's report for 1891 called Devils Island "an isolated station with no adequate harbor" and expected its flashing third-order light to become "one of the most important turning points in Lake" Superior.[^4] A count made informally from the tower, in a year the National Register nomination does not give, found 120 vessels in plain sight at once.[^5]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $15,000 for a light station on 2 March 1889, and the Board reports that a further $5,500 for a fog signal followed on 3 March 1890.[^4] (Pepper gives the fog-signal sum as $5,000 and the date as 2 March.[^3]) The money did not stretch. By 1891 the Board needed another $22,000 to finish the station, so it put up a temporary wooden tower to get a light burning.[^1][^3]

That light was first shown on 30 September 1891: a fourth-order light, with a 10-inch steam whistle starting the same day.[^6][^7] Pepper describes the tower as a timber skeleton about 60 feet (18 m) high, showing a fixed red light.[^3] Henry J. R. Baker, who had been first assistant at Passage Island, came over as the first head keeper.[^1][^8] Pepper reports that work stopped at the end of the 1892 season for want of funds, and the Board repeated its request each year until Congress acted in 1895.[^3]

The island itself had to be bought. The Board reported in 1894 that the condemnation of the island "has at last been perfected" and the title was now the government's, and the $1,600 award was paid in August 1895.[^9][^10] Pepper's account places the close of the condemnation proceedings in the August after the June 1896 act instead.[^3] An act of 11 June 1896 allowed $4,000 of the tower money to go to a second keepers' dwelling, and the Board had an iron tower designed.[^10] A work party reached the island on 1 July 1897 to begin that dwelling, and the tender *Amaranth* carried material for it.[^1][^10] The tower itself was finished by contract on 17 October 1898.[^11]

Sources disagree about the tower. Lighthouse Friends gives its height as 80 feet (24 m) and calls it steel; the National Park Service says 82 feet (25 m) and a steel cylinder; the Light-House Board called it iron.[^1][^2][^10] It shook in the wind. According to the Park Service, keepers complained that the motion sometimes put the lamp out, and in 1914 the Lighthouse Service braced it from outside, giving it the look it has now.[^2]

The lens, a third-order Fresnel made by Henry-Lepaute of Paris, took three more years to arrive.[^2][^1] Lighthouse Friends and Pepper say it came ashore on 22 June 1901 and was lit on 20 September 1901; the Park Service has it arriving in April 1901.[^1][^3][^2] The old wooden tower came down in 1901 (Park Service), 1903 (Lighthouse Friends) or 1904 (Pepper).[^2][^1][^3] In 1914 the lamp was changed to incandescent oil vapor.[^12]

The steam whistle ran for 398 hours and burned about 19 tons of coal in the year reported in 1894, and 256 hours and 9 tons in the report of 1899.[^13][^14]

## The keepers

The archive of the U.S. Lighthouse Society lists, for the civilian years, head keepers Baker (1891-1893), Charles H. Brown (1893-1898), Alexander McLean (1898-1909), Frank W. Marshall (1909-1921) and John Garrity (1921-1925), among many assistants.[^8] John Mattson and Marcus Baker came in 1892, Mattson as first assistant and Baker as second.[^1][^8]

Alexander McLean came from Michigan Island after four months there; he married Cecelia Carlson in 1903, and she spent her honeymoon on Devils Island and some thirty years as a keeper's wife.[^1] Lighthouse Friends, drawing on her recollections, records that on the island in storms "the spray used to dash against my living room windows."[^1] Lighthouse Friends adds that she never grew fond of life at a lighthouse.[^1]

Hans F. Christensen was head keeper from 1925 to 1934.[^8] In the Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1 September 1928 he reported that President Calvin Coolidge and Mrs. Coolidge had visited the station on 22 August, arriving at noon by yacht from Bayfield, and the keeper said they were well pleased.[^15] The *Evening Star* of Washington reported that the party went ashore shortly after noon and spent 45 minutes at a picnic lunch.[^16]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 30 September 1939, according to Lighthouse Friends, Marjorie Bard, the sixteen-year-old daughter of head keeper James W. Bard, fell while scrubbing the stairs of the keepers' dwelling and fractured her hip. A strong northwest wind had made the sea too rough for the island's 26-foot boat, so Bard called for help on the station's radiotelephone. The call was relayed to the lighthouse tender *Diligence*, which reached the island at 11 p.m. Marjorie was lashed to a bedspring and carried across the island to the tender; the trip took five hours, where it normally took twenty-five minutes. The *Diligence* landed her at Bayfield, and she went by ambulance to hospital in Ashland. She recovered.[^1] Lighthouse Friends cites the *Milwaukee Journal* of 1 October 1939 for the story.[^1]

## After the keepers

A radiobeacon began working on 30 October 1925, and a diesel generator brought electric light in 1928; Pepper adds that because the stations closed every December, an acetylene winter light was fitted that year.[^1][^3] Lighthouse Friends dates the new air diaphone to 13 April 1927, Pepper to the year after the beacon.[^1][^3] In 1939 the Coast Guard took over from the Bureau of Lighthouses.[^17]

Devils Island became the base from which the Coast Guard maintained the other Apostle lights, and in 1978 it was automated, the last in the group.[^1] In 1989 the Coast Guard took the Fresnel lens out and set a modern optic on the gallery rail; local residents sued over it.[^1][^3] The Park Service restored the lens and, with a helicopter and a tractor-powered hoist, put it back in the lantern at the start of September 1992.[^3][^1]

## Today

The station is in Apostle Islands National Lakeshore; Congress moved the Coast Guard's lighthouse reservations to the Park Service in 1986.[^3] The Coast Guard still maintains the automated light and radiobeacon.[^17] The station was part of the Apostle Islands Lighthouses listing in the National Register of Historic Places on 8 March 1977.[^17] About sixteen acres on the island's north shore make up the station: two keepers' dwellings, two oil houses, a fog-signal building and a pump house still stand.[^17]

Visitors reach the island by boat; Lighthouse Friends says the light is best seen from the water, or by landing on the island, and reports the grounds open, the tower open in season and the dwellings closed, and the Park Service has staffed the lighthouse with a summer volunteer.[^1][^2] Check with the Apostle Islands National Lakeshore for current conditions before planning a landing.
