The Hilton Head Range Rear Light, also called the Leamington Lighthouse, is a cast-iron skeleton tower in a pine grove on Hilton Head Island, South Carolina.[^1][^7] It stands between the fifth and fifteenth greens of a golf course.[^9] It was the rear light of a pair of range lights that guided ships into Port Royal Sound. The front light has gone. The rear tower and a small brick oil house are what survive.[^1][^7]

## The place

Port Royal Sound's channel would not stay put, and the lights had to follow it. The Lighthouse Board's report for 1896 and 1897 says the bar kept moving south, "so as to make necessary a change in the position of its front beacon."[^2] The Board's report for 1891 and 1892 says the channel had moved south far enough to need more land, "to make the purchase of additional land necessary."[^4] The 1985 nomination puts it the same way: "The navigation channel and the Hilton Head Beach frequently shifted location, requiring several relocations of the front beacon."[^1]

The need for a light went back a long way. A petition of 1853 asked for a light and beacon at the Port Royal entrance.[^7] Congress appropriated $10,000 on 3 August 1854 for a light and beacon on or near Hilton Head.[^7] Nearly $3,000 was spent in 1856 and 1857 trying to obtain title to the land, and then the project was abandoned.[^7]

During the Civil War, after Union forces had gained control of Hilton Head, Congress appropriated $7,000 on 3 March 1863 for range lights at Port Royal Harbor.[^7] A detail of Union soldiers was assigned to open up a vista to the rear range light.[^7] LighthouseFriends says the range lights were discontinued in 1869, reportedly after a storm destroyed them.[^7] The NRHP says "This system was inactive by 1869."[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $40,000 in 1876 for a new system of range lights at Hilton Head.[^1] The nomination says the "Plans for the system were approved by the United States Light House Board in May 1877."[^1] The two sources differ on when the contract was let. LighthouseFriends dates the contracts to 1878, while the nomination says the contract "was finally let in 1879."[^7][^1]

LighthouseFriends says both lights and dwellings were completed in June 1880. The Board then delayed activation until 1 August 1881, when the Parris Island range lights were due to be finished.[^7] The press kit gives the activation date as 1 August 1881.[^8]

The rear tower is what remains. The nomination calls it "a ninety-five-foot cast-iron tower" (about 29 m), and says it "consists of a cast-iron skeleton, a cylindrical stair tower, and a wooden watch room and lantern room structure."[^1] The resort's press kit gives its height as 94 feet (about 29 m).[^8] The focal plane was "ninety-two feet above sea level" (about 28 m), and the light "could be seen from fifteen miles away."[^1]

The NRHP calls the front light "a shorter tower on a second keeper's house."[^1] LighthouseFriends says it rose from the roof of the keeper's dwelling.[^7] It also says the range lights were originally fixed red, and that the front light was changed to fixed white on 1 February 1883.[^7]

## The keepers

The archive's roster for the station runs from 1863 to 1933.[^10] The first head keeper was Henry H. Thomas, who served from 1863 to 1866.[^10] The archive also lists two first assistants of that period, Sarah E. Gillespie (1865-1866) and Ann Calvert (1866-1869), by name and role only.[^10] Bernard J. Calvert was head keeper from 1866 to 1869. The sources do not say whether Ann Calvert and Bernard J. Calvert were related.[^10]

The roster lists no keeper between Bernard J. Calvert's stint and Robert A. Sisson's, which begins in 1879.[^10] After Sisson, the head keepers were John R. Turner, Paul Schwarzer, Frederick H. Bruggeman, Charles L. Sisson and Paul Wright.[^10] Schwarzer was an assistant from 1889 to 1891 and head keeper from 1891 to 1905.[^10] Paul Wright is the last head keeper the roster lists, from 1922 to 1933.[^10] The archive gives Sisson's first year as head keeper as 1879, while LighthouseFriends gives 1881.[^10][^7]

LighthouseFriends also lists four keepers for the front light: Stephen Roper (1863 to 1867) and Richard Pope (1867 to 1869) as head keepers, and Dom. Pappa (1865 to 1867) and William John Pope (1867 to 1869) as assistants. These names do not appear on the archive's roster for the rear station.[^7]

On the night of 31 August 1886 the Charleston earthquake was felt at the range lights.[^7] LighthouseFriends reports that the head keeper, Robert A. Sisson, who lived at the rear light with his family, described the dwelling as heaving the way a small boat does in rough seas.[^7] The assistant at the front light said that he and his wife took the tremor for the ghost of a former assistant buried near the house.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Board's report for 1899 records storm damage: "The structures at the front beacon were injured by the storm of October, 1898." Seven new piers were put under the dwelling.[^5] The next year's report says the storm of 1898 had cut the beach away in front of the front keeper's dwelling, so far that "the next storm would carry it away, together with the outbuildings."[^6]

## After the keepers

The front light was moved again and again. It was moved to the westward on 1 September 1890, and on 15 April 1891 it was moved 73 feet (about 22 m) south.[^3] In January 1897 it was moved again, to "a point 63 feet to the southward" (about 19 m).[^2] The nomination says "several such moves were required in the 1890s."[^1] The Board's report for 1900 says "There being no healthy site nearer than a point near the rear beacon," so the front beacon's structures were moved there.[^6]

Changes to the rear station followed. The nomination says "A small brick oil house was built in 1892," and that in 1893 "the Hains lamps were replaced with Funck-Heap lamps."[^1][^10] It says the system was "thoroughly renovated around 1913," and was "taken out of service and the Rear Lighthouse abandoned sometime in the 1930s."[^1] The archive's place record gives 1932 as the year of discontinuance.[^10] LighthouseFriends dates the end of the range lights to 1932.[^7]

LighthouseFriends says the tower and the land around it were sold to Greenwood Development Corporation, and that in the mid-1980s the lighthouse became part of the Arthur Hills golf course.[^7] The 1985 nomination names the Greenwood Development Corporation as owner, and says the lighthouse is "currently vacant."[^1] The keepers' dwellings were moved: LighthouseFriends says that in the 1960s the two dwellings were split in two and moved to Harbour Town.[^7] The resort's press kit says Greenwood Communities and Resorts, the parent company of Palmetto Dunes, restored the structure in 1985 and opened the lighthouse grounds to the public. It also gives the date of the lighthouse's National Register listing as 12 December 1985.[^8]

## Today

The lighthouse is on private land. The operator's visitor page says visiting is free, but the inside tower is closed, and the lighthouse sits on private property in a golf-course setting.[^9] Visitors cannot climb the tower. To visit the grounds, they stop at the resort's guard house and ask for a pass, which is not given after 5 p.m.[^9]

The sources differ on the owner. The 1985 nomination names Greenwood Development Corporation. LighthouseFriends says Palmetto Dunes Resort owns the lighthouse,[^7] and the press kit describes Greenwood Communities and Resorts as the parent company of Palmetto Dunes.[^8] The press kit says the lighthouse underwent a significant renovation in 2019, including a new cedar shingle roof.[^8]
