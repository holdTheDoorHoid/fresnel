Breakers Point Light is a small flashing light on a headland on the east side of the entrance to Pago Pago Harbor, on Tutuila, the main island of American Samoa.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory calls it the landfall light for Pago Pago.[^1]

## The place

The Directory calls Pago Pago Harbor "one of the best in the South Pacific."[^1] The islands of Samoa were divided in 1899: the large western islands went to Germany, and the smaller eastern islands, the group that includes Tutuila, went to the United States.[^1] The Lighthouse Service's jurisdiction was extended to the American Samoan Islands on July 3, 1905.[^2] Its own history says that up to 1910 the island lights were run "as a subbranch of what was then" the twelfth district, with its headquarters in San Francisco, and that naval officers and Army engineer officers had local charge.[^2]

Breakers Point is also the name of a Second World War gun battery on Papatele Ridge, on the same east side of the harbor entrance. Its two six-inch guns were emplaced in 1941, and the battery was listed on the National Register of Historic Places as Breakers Point Naval Guns.[^3]

## Building the light

The year the light was first lit is not recorded. The 1911 inspection report on the station, dated 3 March 1911 and made by Lieutenant C. S. McDowell, USN, says the station was built or established "Prior to September 1, 1900."[^4] The Directory notes that a 1907 light list does not include the light, and that a 1922 light list describes a white square pyramidal tower 16 ft (5 m) high.[^1]

The 1911 report gives the tower as 16 feet (4.9 m) from base to ventilator ball, with its focal plane 188 feet (57 m) above mean high water.[^4] Its frame was Oregon pine around an angle-iron frame.[^4] A foot trail of about 600 feet ran from the tower to the road.[^4] The naval station was five miles away, and the nearest village, which the report spells "Laulu," a quarter mile.[^4]

In 1914 the Bulletin reported that the light had been improved. A fourth-order lens had replaced the old lens lantern, and the candlepower had risen "from 160 to 490."[^5]

For the fiscal year ended 30 June 1915, the Commissioner of Lighthouses reported that the aids in American Samoa, like those at Guantanamo and on Guam, were "maintained under the supervision of the naval commandants," by allotments made from Lighthouse Service appropriations. The report gives an approximate annual expense for Samoa of $2,100.[^6]

The Bulletin for 1927 describes the next change. After a field inspection in November 1925 by the superintendent of the nineteenth lighthouse district, "plans were developed for radical changes" in the Samoan lights. The former lights were fourth-order lenses with kerosene wick lamps.[^7] The Bulletin ties the change of material to rapid corrosion of metal structures in that climate.[^7] The towers were designed of wood, "fabricated at the Tutuila Naval Station and transported knocked down" for field erection.[^7] The light at Breaker Point was reconstructed and newly equipped on 24 August 1927, flashing white every five seconds, 190 feet (58 m) high.[^7]

In a letter printed in the 1927 Bulletin, Commander W. B. Howe, United States Navy, the retiring officer in charge of lights in American Samoa, wrote to the superintendent of lighthouses at Honolulu that the new towers for Breaker Point and Taputapu Island "have been constructed at Pago Pago and will be erected in the near future."[^8]

## The keepers

No keeper is named for this light. The 1927 Bulletin says the work was supervised by the captain of the yard at the Tutuila Naval Station, "who is the officer in charge of lights in American Samoa." It adds that a mechanician trained in the Honolulu shops "will be the regular attendant of these lights in future."[^7] The Bulletin does not name the mechanician. The only officer the 1927 letter names is Commander W. B. Howe, the retiring officer in charge of lights, and the sources do not say he kept this light.[^8]

## After the keepers

According to the 1927 Bulletin, automatic acetylene lights were provided for all the Samoan stations except Pago Pago Range, and the acetylene stations were controlled by sun valves. Their gas cylinders were recharged in Honolulu, which is six days by steamer from Pago Pago.[^7]

## Today

The Directory describes the light as active. It flashes every six seconds, white or red depending on direction, from a 27 ft (8 m) post carrying a diamond-shaped daymark.[^1] The site is reached by road, although the entrance is unmarked.[^1] The site is open and the tower closed.[^1] The Directory says the lights are maintained by the U.S. Coast Guard Oceania District, and a 2009 photograph, contributed by Elinor DeWire, shows a Coast Guard aids team checking the light after the earthquake and tsunami that struck the islands that September.[^1] The Lighthouse Society's place record gives "Existing: No" and "Open to Public: No," which do not agree with the Directory.[^9]
