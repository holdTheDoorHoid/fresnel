Sankaty Head Light stands on a bluff about a mile north of the town of Siasconset, on the eastern side of Nantucket.[^1] Its brick tower was first lit on 1 February 1850.[^11] The nomination says its Fresnel lens was one of the first installed in a United States lighthouse.[^1] It also quotes a writer, Samuel Adams Drake, who visited in 1875 and recorded that fishermen called the light the "blazing star."[^1][^11]

## The place

LighthouseFriends says Sankaty Head is the most southeastern headland in New England.[^11] The nomination says the tower stands on a cliff 160 feet (49 m) above the water.[^1]

The danger lay offshore, in the Southern Shoals.[^1][^11] Drake wrote that the light warned mariners to "steer wide of the great Southern Shoal."[^1][^11] Lieutenant Charles Davis conducted soundings off Nantucket in 1847 and discovered shoals that were named Davis South Shoals in his honor, LighthouseFriends says.[^11] Money for a light at Sankaty was not appropriated until 14 August 1848.[^11]

## Building the light

Ten acres on the head were bought from George Myrick in May 1849 for $250.[^11] LighthouseFriends says Cabet King was contracted to build the brick tower, and that supplies were landed at Nantucket Harbor and carried across the island.[^11] The nomination says the materials came to the site by ship.[^1] The two accounts differ on how the materials reached the site.

Benjamin F. Isherwood, a Navy engineer, supervised the construction of the tower and the installation of the lens, LighthouseFriends says.[^11] The nomination spells his name "Isherwoode," says he directed the construction at a cost of $10,333, and says he bought the lens in France.[^1] The archive's roster lists a contractor named Benjamin R. Isherwood, with no dates. Nothing in these sources links him to the engineer, so the two are not treated as one man here.[^2]

The nomination describes the tower as the 60-foot (18 m) brick tower, built in 1849 and 1850.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes a brick section rising 53 feet (16 m), with a granite section six feet (1.8 m) high above it.[^11]

The lens was a second-order Fresnel from Henry-Lepaute of Paris, first lit on 1 February 1850, LighthouseFriends says.[^11] The Lighthouse Board, formed by Congress in 1852, issued a report that year praising the lens and its first keeper, Alexander D. Bunker, LighthouseFriends says.[^11]

The Board's report for 1853, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, said the assistant keeper had to travel about seven miles a day, because the dwelling had room only for the principal keeper and his family. A dwelling for the assistant was built in 1855.[^11]

General context, not a record of this station: the Board's general rules for keepers, issued in 1864, said assistant keepers must not be under 18 years of age and must be able to read and write.[^13]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists 52 stints at Sankaty from 1849 to 1964.[^2] The first principal keeper it lists is Joseph Allen, for 1849 alone. Alexander D. Bunker (1849 to 1860) followed.[^2]

Joseph G. Remsen was head keeper from 1894 to 1919.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he was the longest-serving head keeper at Sankaty, with over twenty-five years there and seven different assistants. He had started at Brant Point Lighthouse in 1887, and captained the South Shoals Lightship for two years before coming to Sankaty.[^11]

Charles W. Vanderhoop was keeper, and Eugene N. Larsen his assistant, on 11 April 1920, when a fire broke out near the station.[^6] That year Larsen became principal keeper, replacing Vanderhoop, who was transferred to Gay Head.[^11][^10]

LighthouseFriends says Larsen arrived at Sankaty in 1914, after service at Minot's Ledge, Cape Ann and The Graves.[^11] The roster has him as principal keeper until 1944.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he received the Commissioner's Pennant in 1939, "the highest honor a light station could receive," and retired in December 1943 after twenty-nine years at Sankaty.[^11] The roster and LighthouseFriends give different end dates for his service.

Archford "Ted" Haskins was the station's last civilian keeper, until 1947, when Coast Guard crews took over.[^2][^11]

James E. Dolby was first assistant from 1924. LighthouseFriends says the assistant's post was eliminated in 1933, when the light was electrified.[^2][^11]

The roster's entries from the 1950s and 1960s are for later keepers and Coast Guard crews. They are not named here.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the morning of 20 January 1892, LighthouseFriends says, Keeper Remsen telephoned Walter N. Chase, keeper of the Coskata life-saving station. Rockets had been seen off the coast during the night, and the masts of a schooner stranded on Bass Rips could now be made out.[^11]

The Life-Saving Service's annual report for 1892 gives the British schooner H. P. Kirkham, of Liverpool, Nova Scotia.[^14] Its wreck table lists the Kirkham against the Coskata station, dated 20 January.[^4][^15] The report's services table gives a different place: Rose and Crown Shoal, about fifteen miles from the Coskata station. Its awards list gives 21 January 1892 for the same wreck.[^4][^5]

The Service gave Chase a gold medal dated 2 April 1892 for rescuing, during a heavy gale and sea, "the crew of seven men from the rigging" of the schooner.[^5] The award text says the men were brought to land after twenty-three hours of battling wind and sea.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the rescue boat rowed nearly fifteen miles out, could make little headway against the offshore wind on the way back, and had its crew taking turns at a few minutes of sleep. It landed on the beach twenty-six hours after setting off.[^11] The award counts twenty-three hours of battling; LighthouseFriends counts twenty-six hours from setting off to landing.

On 11 April 1920 a fire broke out about a quarter of a mile north of the light station.[^6] The Bulletin says it burned about 80 acres, came to within 5 feet (1.5 m) of the oil house and 75 feet (23 m) of the keeper's dwelling, and that Vanderhoop and Larsen helped preserve lighthouse property.[^6]

## After the keepers

The Bulletin for 1923 says 1,550 visitors registered at Sankaty Head Light Station in 1922.[^7]

A Bulletin item for 1925 reports that on 15 July, at 11:45 p.m., the master of the Nantucket Lightship plainly saw Sankaty's light from his station, about 42½ miles away.[^8]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 records a brick oil house built at Sankaty Head.[^3] The nomination and the Lighthouse Society's record give 1887 for the oil house.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends gives 1892.[^11]

The 1933 Bulletin says the light was electrified on 14 May and that its candlepower rose from 99,000 to 720,000. LighthouseFriends gives 15 May.[^9][^11] The Bulletin adds that the change allowed one keeper to be discontinued, and that the light had been seen on several occasions from the Nantucket Shoals Lightship, 41 miles away, though its normal range is only 19 miles.[^9]

The nomination records a Fresnel lens removed in 1938 to the Nantucket Whaling Museum, and another Fresnel lens removed in 1950. It does not say whether these were one lens or two.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the 1950 removal was followed by rotating aerobeacons. It also says the 1887 keepers' duplex was torn down in 1953 and replaced with two one-story houses.[^11]

The light was automated in 1965. Personnel continued to live at the station until 1992, LighthouseFriends says.[^11][^2]

Severe erosion of the bluff began in the 1980s. The 'Sconset Trust says the tower stood 68 feet (21 m) from the edge before it was moved.[^12] LighthouseFriends says the 1991 storm tore away large pieces of the bluff, that residents formed Save Our Sankaty to save the tower, and that the 'Sconset Trust acquired the lighthouse and raised the money to move it.[^11] In 2007 the Trust moved the tower 405 feet (123 m), to a spot 250 feet (76 m) from the edge.[^12] LighthouseFriends gives 390 feet (119 m) for the move, to the northwest.[^11]

## Today

The 'Sconset Trust maintains the lighthouse and occasionally opens it to the public. The grounds are open daily from dawn until dusk. Traditionally the tower is open twice a year for tours, in June and October, and reservations are required through the Trust's online registration system.[^12]
