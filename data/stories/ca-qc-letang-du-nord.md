The Phare du Borgot, also known as the L'Étang-du-Nord lighthouse, stands on the point of Cap Hérissé, set a little back from the village of L'Étang-du-Nord on Cap-aux-Meules in the Magdalen Islands (Îles-de-la-Madeleine), Quebec. A wooden keeper's house with a lantern on its roof was first lit on the site in 1874. The white fibreglass tower that stands there now went up in 1987.[^1][^2][^3][^9]

## The place

The federal government began a series of lights in the Magdalen Islands in 1870, at Rocher-aux-Oiseaux, then at Anse-à-la-Cabane in 1871 and Île-d'Entrée in 1874. The aim was to make the Gulf of St Lawrence shipping lane safer, because wrecks were frequent there.[^1] The region's heritage records count more than 700 shipwrecks over the years.[^1][^3]

The light was built to warn mariners of Dead Man's Island (Le Corps Mort) and to mark the western shore of the islands, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3]

## Building the light

The first keeper's house was a two-storey wooden building, lantern on top, housing the keeper and his family.[^1][^9] LighthouseFriends describes it differently, as a square wooden building 8.5 m (28 ft) high with an attached dwelling. Its light, first exhibited on 20 April 1874, had three 20-inch reflectors and circular burner lamps that revolved every three minutes, giving a focal plane of 61 m (200 ft) and visible for 32 km (twenty miles).[^3] The lighthouse cost $5,641.57.[^3] A steam fog whistle followed on 10 November 1874, but hills on both sides of the station cut off its sound seaward, and it was dismantled in 1890.[^3]

Two officials, the Marine engineer William P. Anderson and William H. Smith, chairman of the Board of Examiners of Masters and Mates, concluded that the location was not optimal. The light stayed where it was.[^3]

In 1898 a circular lamp overheated in the lantern and started a fire that slightly damaged the building.[^3] The Department's report for the year to 30 June 1900 says the station's tower, dwelling and other buildings were painted by the keeper, with local assistance.[^4]

The heritage sources date a restoration of the old house to 1913.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says a new dwelling was built in 1912 and 1913 and topped with a new lantern room, fitted with a double-flashing catoptric light.[^3] The two accounts describe the same period of work differently, and the story gives both.

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends names Timothy O'Brien as the first keeper, from 1874 to 1891.[^3] The Department's list of lightkeepers records the appointment of Nectaire Arseneau at Étang-du-Nord on 21 July 1891.[^5] LighthouseFriends then has Arseneau keeping the light until at least 1899, Edward Chiasson from 1900 to 1902, Arseneau again from 1903 to 1911, and N. Arseneau from 1911 to 1921.[^3] The Society's heritage account instead names a single keeper, Néciphore Arsenault, from 1874 to 1921.[^2] Radio-Canada gives the same 1874 to 1921 span for Nicéphore Arsenault, the first keeper of the light.[^6] The sources do not agree on who kept the light between 1874 and 1891.

From 1921 the Turnbull family kept the light. LighthouseFriends names Philip F. Turnbull to 1950, Jason Alfred Turnbull from 1950 to 1954, Frank Turnbull from 1954 to 1955, Alfred A. Arseneau from 1955 to 1957, and Norman H. McKay from 1957 to 1965.[^3] The Society's list differs in its years and spellings, with Alfred Arseneau from 1950 to 1951, Fred and Frank Turnbull from 1951 to 1960, and Norman Mackay from 1961 to 1967.[^2]

During the Second World War, the Society says, Philip Turnbull reported enemy submarines to a military base in Sydney, Nova Scotia.[^2] Radio-Canada's 2024 report says the keeper of the time, Philippe Turnbull, did the same.[^6] Neither gives a date or a record for the report.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 22 November 1988, during a winter gale, two sections of the Duke of Connaught dry dock broke loose while being towed from Montreal to Sydney, Nova Scotia. They washed ashore at Étang-du-Nord.[^3] A travel site says the towlines broke in a storm and forced the dock onto the rocks south of the harbour.[^7] The forward section still lies there.[^3]

## After the keepers

The wooden house was replaced by a steel tower in 1967, according to the heritage sources and LighthouseFriends.[^1][^2][^3] LighthouseFriends says the wooden house and its outbuildings were pulled down in the 1970s, when the light was automated and a keeper was no longer needed.[^3] The heritage register says the federal lights began to be automated in the 1970s.[^1] The Society dates the steel tower's replacement to 1987, and the Secteur record says the steel structure was demolished that year.[^2][^8]

The current tower, a round, tapering shaft with an octagonal lantern painted red, was built in 1987.[^1][^3][^9] The register describes its material as plastic; the Society and Les amis des phares say fibreglass. Its height is 11.5 m.[^1][^2][^9]

The municipality of Îles-de-la-Madeleine cited the lighthouse as heritage property in 2006.[^1][^2] Radio-Canada credits the Quebec government with the 2006 citation.[^6] The sources disagree about when the light went out. The Society says it went out in November 2011. Radio-Canada says the Magdalen lights, apart from two, went out in 2011. LighthouseFriends says the light was discontinued in 2015.[^2][^3][^6] The heritage register says the light is still working.[^1] The story does not settle which account is right.

The lighthouse and its neighbour at Île-d'Entrée were both built in 1874, and celebrations of their 150 years were planned for 2024.[^2][^6]

## Today

The federal government owns the lighthouse, and Radio-Canada reports that the Canadian government has judged it surplus.[^6] The municipality of Îles-de-la-Madeleine is listed as its manager.[^9] An observation site has been laid out around the tower for visitors, on chemin du Phare.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^3] Keep to the viewing area; no source says the tower can be climbed.
