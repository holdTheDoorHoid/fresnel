Hooper Strait Light was a screw-pile lighthouse that marked the shoal at the entrance to Tangier Sound, on Chesapeake Bay.[^3] Ice carried its first house away in 1877,[^3][^5] its second house was staffed until 1954,[^3] and in 1966, after the Coast Guard had marked the old light for demolition, a young museum had the house cut in two and barged forty miles up the Bay.[^2][^3][^4] It now stands on the grounds of the Chesapeake Bay Maritime Museum in St. Michaels, Maryland.[^1][^2]

## The place

Hooper Strait is the passage that carries boats from Chesapeake Bay across Tangier Sound toward Deal Island or places along the Nanticoke and Wicomico rivers.[^1][^2] The light was put on the north side of the entrance, to mark the shoal between the mainland and Bloodsworth Island.[^3]

Lightships marked the entrance before the screw-pile. The Chesapeake Chapter of the Lighthouse Society dates the light station to 1827, with several lightships in service, and says a lightship marked the entrance from 1827 or 1828 until 1845.[^3][^4] The sources disagree on the county. The Screwpiles book places the original light in Dorchester County,[^5] while the Lighthouse Society's archive gives the station's county as Talbot, where the house now stands.[^13]

## Building the light

The first light was a square screw-pile house, erected in 1867.[^3][^5] Ice destroyed it in January 1877 (see below).

The second house was built in its place.[^2] The Chesapeake Bay Maritime Museum says the present lighthouse "was built in 1879".[^1] Its timeline describes a hexagonal wooden cottage 44 feet (13 m) across, a story and a half high with six rooms, "constructed at Lazaretto Depot" and set on ten-inch (25 cm) wrought-iron piles, with a fifth-order Fresnel lens.[^3] The Chesapeake Chapter's own page gives 1880 instead. It says the house was "designed and built in Baltimore" and then "dismantled and re-erected at the site in October of that year".[^4] The Screwpiles book has the new light opening two years after the 1877 loss, which fits 1879.[^5] The sources do not settle the year.

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1926 obituary of William H. Davis, a retired keeper, says he "entered the Lighthouse Service in 1881" as a carpenter in the fifth district's field working party and that he "assisted in the construction of the following lighthou es : Hooper Straits".[^12] He entered the service after both dates, and the obituary does not say when he worked at Hooper Strait.

The light was changed in later years. A red sector was added to the lantern in 1882.[^3] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1899 says that at Hooper Strait "New model fifth-order lamps were supplied."[^9] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1925 lists a light at Hooper Strait, "flashing white, minor acetylene light established," among its changes to aids to navigation.[^11]

## The keepers

The Chesapeake Chapter says that keepers were not allowed to have their families living at this lighthouse. They collected water for drinking, bathing and cooking from the roof's rain gutters, and groceries "were rationed and arrived once a week from the mainland."[^4]

The Lighthouse Society's archive roster lists John S. Cornwell as principal keeper from 1869 to 1885, and Alexander S. Conway as first assistant from 1869 to 1878.[^13] On 8 January 1877 ice took the house off its piles with both men inside. The Screwpiles book says they pulled a lifeboat several miles toward shore across the ice in a 24-hour trek, sheltered on a nearby island, and stayed stranded for two weeks before they could contact the authorities and their families.[^5] The book says that when the new light opened two years later, Cornwell was back at his post. Conway, it says, "disappeared from the Lighthouse Service with no record of his fate."[^5]

Zebedee Harper appears on the roster three times: as principal keeper from 1867 to 1869, as first assistant from 1879 to 1884, and as principal keeper again from 1885 to 1891.[^13] Henry T. McGrath was first assistant from 1909 to 1911 and principal keeper from 1918 to 1924.[^13] The roster's last principal keeper is George Henry Leikam Sr., from 1945 to 1954.[^13]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The ice of January 1877 took the first house. In September 1877 the Middletown Transcript, quoting the Salisbury Advertiser, reported that the Lighthouse Board had "not yet taken steps to replace the lighthouse at Hooper's strait, which was carried away by the ice last winter."[^6]

In late October 1878 a gale struck the Bay. Under the headline "TERRIFIC CYCLONE", the New York Herald printed a dispatch from Baltimore, received at Washington on 24 October 1878, reporting that the steamer Express, of the Potomac line, had been lost off Hooper's Strait at five o'clock on the morning of 23 October.[^7] The Alexandria Gazette printed the loss a day earlier. Its first dispatch placed the wreck near St. Mary's County, a short distance above the mouth of the Potomac. Its reprint of Captain Barker's own account, from a Baltimore newspaper extra, put the Express off Hooper's Strait.[^8] The dispatch in the Herald named her commander, Captain Barker. The Herald's headline said twenty of her passengers and crew drowned, but its dispatch said that all her crew and passengers were lost, except for a few who were picked up.[^7] The papers do not agree on the toll. Neither paper's account mentions the Hooper Strait light.[^7][^8]

On 21 March 1923, according to the Service's Bulletin, keeper Henry T. McGrath and assistant Walter McDorman rescued the occupant of a small boat that had sprung a leak and sunk near the station.[^10][^13]

## After the keepers

The Chesapeake Chapter's timeline records the station's full automation in 1954, after which the house began to deteriorate.[^3]

By 1966 the Coast Guard had designated Hooper Strait Light for destruction.[^2] With support from the Historical Society of Talbot County and the designated demolition funds, the young Chesapeake Bay Maritime Museum stepped in to save the house.[^2][^3] The museum hired the Arundel Corporation to lift the forty-two-ton lighthouse from its pilings in two pieces and barge it forty miles up the Bay to St. Michaels.[^2] A Baltimore firm, Pile Drivers, built a new foundation of tubular steel on Navy Point.[^2] The light arrived on 9 November 1966 and opened to the public on 20 May 1967.[^2] The Chapter's timeline says it was later fully restored.[^3]

## Today

The lighthouse stands on Navy Point, on the museum's campus in St. Michaels, as an exhibit.[^1][^2] Visitors can go inside to see how a keeper lived and worked and can climb the sea hatch; the exhibit invites them to "tend the huge lens."[^1] The Chapter notes that it is open to the public.[^4] The Chapter's page also carries a 2021 photograph by Greg Krawczyk, captioned "Hooper Strait Lighthouse Foundation remains with skeletal tower."[^4]
