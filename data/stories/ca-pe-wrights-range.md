Wright's Front Range Light is a squat white wooden tower, 3.7 m (12 ft) tall and with no lantern at all, that stands in an open field on a point known locally as Paul's Bluff, about a kilometre southwest of Victoria on the south shore of Prince Edward Island.[^1][^2] It was the front half of a pair of range lights that guided vessels into Victoria Harbour from the Northumberland Strait, and it carries the name of its keeper from 1894 to 1935, on whose land it was built.[^1][^4]

## The place

Victoria Harbour, also called Crapaud Harbour, was the outlet for a very fertile district, and in the early 1900s Lighthouse Friends describes it as the most important harbour on the island's southwestern side after Summerside, with a 486-foot (148 m) pier and a weekly steamer from Charlottetown.[^3] Parks Canada's heritage statement likewise says the lighthouse supported Victoria's role as a key shipping outlet for the southwestern part of the island.[^1] In 1894 two new pairs of range lights, Palmer's and Wright's, were added to work with the older Leard's Range, and mariners were given a published sequence of alignments to follow in from the strait.[^3]

## Building the light

The light was first shown on 15 June 1894.[^3] Lighthouse Friends describes the first front light as a mast on a square shed-roofed tower on the south side of Paul Bluff, carrying a fixed red light 9.2 m (30 ft) above high water.[^3] The Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society, quoting an older description, calls the first structure "a box shed about 6 feet high with a short mast about 10 feet in total height."[^2] The present tower replaced it in 1903.[^2][^3] It is a pepper-pot design with a pyramidal roof, shingled walls and a single sea-facing window from which the light shone, and the heritage statement notes that this economical form was favoured because it was cheap to build and easy to maintain.[^1] The Society calls it the simplest and least expensive enclosed lighthouse on the island.[^2]

## The keepers

Charles L. Wright was appointed keeper on 14 June 1894, and the Department of Marine's 1910 list of stations gives his salary as $130.[^3][^5] Lighthouse Friends adds that he was paid $20 for the land on which the rear light stood and began at $100 a year.[^3] The lights took his name, and the front light was built on his land.[^1][^4] He stayed until 1935, and the Society records that he received an Imperial Long Service Medal for his many years of lightkeeping.[^2] His son Charles Evan Wright succeeded him and kept the lights until 1959.[^3][^4] The Society says he retired when electricity was installed in the lights.[^2] Lighthouse Friends lists two further names for 1959, Edward H. Boswell and Bryce Boswell, without saying more.[^3]

## After the keepers

Because of shoreline erosion, the tower was moved a short distance in 1986.[^1][^4] The Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society says it now stands on private property.[^2] The provincial register and Lighthouse Friends say the light was deactivated in 2011, and the Society gives the month as August 2011.[^2][^3][^4] The light had been solar-powered before that.[^2]

## Today

The tower was recognised as a heritage lighthouse under the federal Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act on 29 January 2025.[^1] Lighthouse Friends lists it as owned by the Canadian Coast Guard and closed to visitors; the Society notes that the Victoria Seaport Museum has petitioned for ownership under the Parks Canada Heritage Lighthouse Program.[^2][^3] The Society says it can be seen distantly from the waterfront in Victoria, and Lighthouse Friends says the grounds and tower are closed.[^2][^3] The two sources also differ on ownership, the Society saying private property and Lighthouse Friends the Coast Guard.[^2][^3]
