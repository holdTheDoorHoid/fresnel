A short brick tower joined to its fog-signal house stands at the end of a low sandy spit near Hansville, in Kitsap County, Washington, where Admiralty Inlet opens into Puget Sound. Its lantern was first lit on 1 January 1880, and it is usually called the oldest lighthouse on Puget Sound.[^2][^3][^15]

## The place

The spit runs out for more than a quarter of a mile at the northern tip of the Kitsap Peninsula. Lieutenant Charles Wilkes of the U.S. Exploring Expedition gave it the name Point No Point in May 1841, and local Native people already knew it as *Hahd-skus*, "long nose".[^3] On 26 January 1855 officials of the United States met tribes of the Kitsap Peninsula here to fix payment for land and to designate reservations, a treaty meeting that the National Register nomination counts among the reasons the area is historic.[^1]

The Lighthouse Board, expecting Puget Sound commerce to grow with the Northern Pacific Railroad, asked for a light here in 1872.[^3] HistoryLink names two wrecks that made the case: the bark *Iconium*, aground in fog in 1868, and the bark *Windward*, wrecked on Whidbey Island in 1875 while trying to avoid the shoal.[^3] The National Register nomination adds that in 1878 the bark *Osmyn* struck the *Aureola* in the fog and "went down with three members of the crew."[^1] 

The money and the land took years. Lighthouse Friends says Congress granted $25,000 on 3 March 1873; HistoryLink says Congress appropriated $25,000 in 1877.[^2][^3] The owner of the point, Francis James, was reluctant to sell. Lighthouse Friends has him selling ten acres for $1,000 in March 1879, while HistoryLink says he sold 40 acres for $1,800 in April 1879.[^2][^3] HistoryLink adds that James, a Port Townsend councilman and storekeeper, had once been a keeper at Cape Flattery, and that the Board chose the spit over Foulweather Bluff, 3.5 miles to the northwest.[^3]

## Building the light

Work on the buildings began in September 1879, according to Lighthouse Friends, and the lighthouse tender *Shubrick* brought the materials, according to HistoryLink.[^2][^3] The result was a brick tower and attached office, a metal oil house, a wooden structure housing the fog bell's clockwork and a two-story duplex for the keepers.[^3] The sources do not agree on the tower's size: Lighthouse Friends calls it eleven feet (3.4 m) square, HistoryLink says 30 feet (9.1 m) tall, and the National Register nomination, describing it after later changes, gives 10 feet (3 m) on a side and 28.5 feet (8.7 m) high.[^1][^2][^3]

The glass for the lantern and the lens had not arrived by the end of 1879. The Notice to Mariners had promised a light on 1 January 1880, so the first keeper, John S. Maggs, hung an ordinary kerosene lantern in the lantern room.[^2] HistoryLink says he hung canvas over the south windows to keep out the wind.[^3] A fifth-order Fresnel lens arrived on 10 January and the storm panes on 1 February.[^2][^3] A 1,200-pound fog bell from New Dungeness arrived in April 1880, struck by weight-driven machinery. Lighthouse Friends says a winding ran the bell for two and a half hours; HistoryLink says the mechanism had to be rewound every 45 minutes in fog, and that when it failed, which it often did, the keepers struck the bell with a hammer.[^2][^3] The light was visible for about ten miles.[^3]

By 1893 the Light-House Board judged the bell inadequate, writing that it "does not satisfy the needs of the service."[^6] Lighthouse Friends says the request for a first-class fog signal was then repeated every year until Congress approved it.[^2] Congress appropriated $6,000 on 1 July 1898; the brick fog-signal house was finished on 30 November 1898.[^8] A Daboll trumpet began sounding on 1 April 1900.[^8] In the fiscal year ended June 1897 the old bell had run some 558 hours; the trumpet's first full year, to June 1901, was 205 hours.[^7][^9] HistoryLink says the old bell went to Browns Point in 1903.[^3]

The lens is another place where the sources part. Lighthouse Friends and HistoryLink both say the first lens was fifth-order, but HistoryLink dates the present fourth-order lens to 1898, while Lighthouse Friends dates it to 1915 and the Lighthouse Service's own *Bulletin* for 1915 lists the light as changed that year from fixed white to group flashing white with an incandescent oil-vapor lamp.[^2][^3][^10] The National Register nomination mentions specifications for a fourth-order lens dated 1898 in the Coast Guard's files.[^1]

## The keepers

Maggs, who also kept a dental practice in Seattle, arrived with his first assistant, Henry H. Edwards, in December 1879.[^2][^3][^4] His wife Caroline and two young children came in February 1880, and a daughter, Mollie, was born at the station in July 1880.[^2][^3] The first year was not peaceful. Lighthouse Friends reports that an assistant, Abram Manning, quarrelled with Maggs over running the fog bell, took up a pistol, and had to be removed by the district inspector; Maggs wrote in his log of "great relief" when the Mannings had gone.[^2] The Register of keepers lists Manning in 1881.[^4]

Isolation shaped the work. HistoryLink says there were no roads to the station for its first forty years, and that supplies and people came by boat.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says a four-mile horse trail to the post office at Port Gamble was opened in 1880, and that an 1881 inspection found the station unhealthy, with "fevers" among its residents.[^2] Another source, HistoryLink, says the mail run was nine miles by rowboat to Port Ludlow.[^3]

Edward Scannell, born in Ireland, became principal keeper and stayed 26 years at $800 a year with one assistant, by HistoryLink's account; the Register of keepers lists him from 1889, after a spell as acting keeper from 1888.[^3][^4] His wife Mary served as postmistress from 1893 to 1914; HistoryLink says she was the first postmistress of the post office the station opened in 1893.[^2][^3] The nomination notes that the first entry in the office's guest register is from Lafayette Heath of Port Gamble, who visited on 29 August 1895.[^1]

William H. Cary, assistant from 1910, took over as principal keeper in 1914.[^4] His wife Cora ran a store in Hansville and took weather readings at the station three times a day, telephoning them to the weather service at Boeing Field in Seattle.[^2][^3] In 1917 the Lighthouse Service's *Bulletin* listed Point No Point and Cary among the stations awarded a lighthouse efficiency flag for the fiscal year 1916.[^11] The Register of keepers gives Cary's term as ending in 1939, while HistoryLink says the Carys left in 1937.[^3][^4] After 1939, when the Coast Guard took over, the Register lists Coast Guard personnel from 1942.[^3][^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A severe storm struck the station on 9 March, the Light-House Board reported in its fiscal 1891 volume, which puts it in March 1891, doing "much damage" to the bulkhead and grounds.[^5] On 20 May, the Lighthouse Service reported in its 1930 Bulletin, lightning struck the tower; instead of following the rod to the ground it passed through the lantern and pedestal, the copper oil tubing and the air tubing.[^12] The Bulletin says no damage was done to the lens or pedestal.[^12] HistoryLink separately says the lens was slightly damaged in 1931, that many sources blame lightning, and that contemporary records point instead to explosive vapor from a faulty oil vaporizer tube.[^3]

On the morning of 26 August 1914 the passenger steamer *Admiral Sampson* was rammed in dense fog by the Canadian Pacific's *Princess Victoria*, off Point No Point and 18 miles north of Seattle.[^13][^14] The Seattle *Star* of that day reported that the *Sampson* sank within ten minutes (another passage in the same paper says three).[^13] It said Captain Z. S. Moore directed the rescue, and that he was last seen "raising his hand as though in token of farewell."[^13] That first day's count was three passengers and eight crew lost; the National Register nomination and HistoryLink give 16 lives.[^1][^3][^13] The Tacoma *Times* the next day reported the oil from the *Sampson*'s broken tank had caught fire and for a time threatened the *Victoria*.[^14] The accounts place the collision off the point but say nothing about the station's own part that morning.

During the Second World War, HistoryLink says, the station carried extra Coast Guard personnel who watched for submarines and patrolled 236 miles of Kitsap shoreline.[^3]

## After the keepers

In 1975 the Coast Guard built two modular buildings and a 90-foot (27 m) radar and radio tower on the point for Puget Sound's vessel traffic service, and in August 1977 it automated the light and fog signal.[^3] Lighthouse Friends adds that the dwelling continued to house Coast Guard personnel for several years; the Register's roster ends in 1977.[^2][^4] HistoryLink says the last Coast Guard member assigned to the station left in 1997.[^3]

Kitsap County first expressed interest in the property in 1992 and received a long-term lease in 1998.[^2][^3] On 16 June 2006 the Fresnel lens stopped turning, replaced by a modern beacon on the railing outside the lantern room, and in the same year the Friends of Point No Point Lighthouse were formed.[^2] The U.S. Lighthouse Society moved its headquarters into half of the duplex in April 2008, with the other half let as a vacation rental.[^2] The Coast Guard declared the lighthouse excess in 2009; in July 2012, Lighthouse Friends says, the Department of the Interior announced its transfer to the county, and a re-opening celebration at the restored lighthouse had been held on 12 May 2012.[^2] The Lighthouse Society says it and its partners raised more than $200,000 in state and federal grants for the keeper's workshop and the lighthouse.[^16]

## Today

The station is in Point No Point County Park, managed by Kitsap County Parks, and Lighthouse Friends lists the grounds as open daily in daylight.[^2][^15] Volunteers from the Friends give tours on Saturdays and Sundays from noon to 4 p.m., April through September; the 2026 season has ended and tours resume in spring 2027.[^15] The tour does not include the lantern room, and the keeper's dwelling is closed to visitors, though half of it is a vacation rental.[^2][^3][^15] The light is still an active aid to navigation, a modern beacon on the lantern-room railing since 2006.[^2][^3] In the fall of 2026 the county scheduled replacement of the fog-signal room roof for mid-to-late October, with some areas around the lighthouse closed for safety, and the Coast Guard is planning lead-paint remediation on the exteriors.[^15]
