Point Reyes Lighthouse sits on a ledge cut into the cliff at the western tip of the Point Reyes headlands in Marin County, California, built low on purpose so that it would burn beneath the fog rather than inside it.[^3][^2] Its first-order Fresnel lens was first lit on 1 December 1870, and the light ran for 105 years, until the station was automated in 1975.[^3][^4] The Park Service now keeps the tower, lens and clockwork as a museum, and the Coast Guard's automated beacon was placed on a separate building below it.[^4][^3]

## The place

The Point Reyes headlands jut about 10 miles (16 km) out into the Pacific, across the route of ships going in and out of San Francisco Bay.[^9] The Park Service calls the point the windiest place on the Pacific coast and the second foggiest on the North American continent, and says winds of 133 miles an hour have been recorded there.[^3] The first recorded shipwreck on the California coast, the Manila galleon *San Agustin*, was lost here in 1595.[^5] After the Gold Rush the losses came steadily: Dewey Livingston's history of the station lists the *Oxford* (1855), *Oregon* (1860), *Sea Nymph* (1861), *Monterey* (1862), *Bengal* and *Norvick* (1863), *Labouchere* (1866) and *S.F. Blunt* (1868) among them.[^5]

Congress appropriated $25,000 for a light here on 3 August 1854, $10,000 of it for the lighting apparatus and $15,000 for construction; LighthouseFriends instead says the light was authorized in 1855.[^5][^2] Nothing was built for fifteen years, because the United States did not own the ground. Livingston traces a tangle of rival claims on the old rancho: its buyer, Andrew Randall, was shot dead in a San Francisco hotel in July 1856 by a man he owed money, and the land finally passed to the lawyer brothers Oscar and James Shafter, who asked a price the Lighthouse Board thought exorbitant.[^5] California's senator William Gwin told the Senate in April 1860 that the owners wanted the whole $25,000 for a few acres that were "not, in fact, worth twenty-five cents an acre."[^5] The appropriation lapsed, and Congress later voted money again.[^5]

The cost of the wait is counted differently by different sources. Livingston puts the maritime losses at almost a million dollars, and the Park Service's brochure at about a million; LighthouseFriends counts fourteen shipwrecks while the price was disputed.[^5][^4][^2] The standoff ended in 1869. The Board accepted the Shafters' $6,000 offer, and a deed signed on 28 July 1869 gave the government 83 acres, with a landing place on Drakes Bay and a right of way across to the point.[^5]

## Building the light

The first plan was a house with a tower built into it, on top of the bluff. Experience at Point Bonita had shown that fog could hide a light set that high, so the design was changed.[^2] In June 1869 the Coast Survey's George Davidson walked the headland and picked a shelf about 275 feet (84 m) below the proposed site.[^5] He also urged that "a powerful fog trumpet or steam whistle" go on the ledge below the light, because in very thick weather it would be "of greater use than the light itself."[^5]

Congress added $45,000 in July 1870, and the *Shasta Courier* of 23 July 1870 printed the line in its list of appropriations: "For lighthouse and fog signal at Point Reyes, California (in addition to former appropriations,) $45,000."[^8][^5] The Park Service puts the whole cost at nearly $95,000, against the $25,000 first set aside.[^4]

The work began in January 1870 with a road from the Drakes Bay landing to the point, and a tramway was built to carry tools and materials down the cliff.[^5] Phineas Marston, a carpenter from Maine, built the keepers' two-story dwelling at the top.[^5] Joseph Bien, a San Francisco machinist, made the sixteen-sided cast-iron tower, a near twin of the one he had just finished at Cape Mendocino.[^5][^2] The lens had been made in Paris in 1867 by Barbier and Fenestre. It arrived at San Francisco with one section missing, and the engineer and the shipping company searched for it for weeks before it turned up in time.[^4][^5] The iron and the lens were landed at Drakes Bay and hauled up to the bluff above the site by 9 October 1870, and a crew of seven men was hired to lower the pieces down the tramway.[^5] The Park Service says it took six weeks to lower the materials and build the light.[^3] A notice announced that it would show a white flash every five seconds from 1 December 1870.[^5]

Sources disagree about how high the light is. The 1870 notice put it 273 feet (83 m) above the sea, an 1880 county history that Livingston quotes says 296 feet (90 m), the *Chronicle* of 1887 says about 300 feet (91 m), the Lighthouse Society's record says 294 feet (90 m), and the Park Service brochure gives 271 feet (83 m) for the site found in 1858.[^5][^6][^1][^4] Livingston says kerosene replaced lard oil in the lamp in September 1887.[^5] The *San Francisco Chronicle* described the new burner in its issue of 25 September 1887: five circular wicks, a flame so bright that no one could look at it from the tower without smoked glasses, and a 120-pound weight that forced the oil up to the wicks.[^6]

The fog signal was harder. Work on the ledge began on 1 February 1871, and a large cistern had to be built because there was no water within two miles.[^5] The signal was tried in June 1871 but could not be put into service until winter rains had filled the cistern.[^5][^2] The steam boiler burned about 140 pounds (63 kg) of coal an hour in fog.[^4] A fire destroyed the building in the first years; LighthouseFriends and Livingston date it to 28 April 1872, after which Congress voted $10,000 for a replacement.[^4][^2][^5] Livingston records that the cistern ran dry in the fall of 1875 and that more than 20,000 gallons had to be hauled in by a local dairyman.[^5]

Mariners complained that they could not hear it. In a letter Livingston quotes, the district engineer, Colonel Williamson, wrote that he had "frequently investigated complaints made by masters of vessels, of not hearing the signal when passing within a mile of it in thick weather."[^5] Two steam sirens replaced the whistle, in April 1880 by Livingston's account and on 1 June 1881 by LighthouseFriends.[^5][^2] In 1890 twelve-inch steam whistles replaced the sirens, and the Light-House Board's report for fiscal 1891 records the change.[^5][^11] A compressed-air diaphone was installed in 1915.[^5][^2] After a circular to shipmasters, the Lighthouse Service reported that the horn could be heard 7 miles south and 4 to 5 miles north.[^13]

The station kept changing. A power house was built in late 1928 and the fog-signal machinery was moved up beside the tower; in 1934 the diaphones themselves were moved to a new building directly below the lighthouse.[^5] Livingston dates the arrival of commercial electric power to 1939 and LighthouseFriends to 1938.[^5][^2] In April 1925 the master of the steamer *Richmond* reported seeing the Point Reyes light from nearly 40 miles away, though the lamp had only 80,000 candlepower; the *Bulletin* credited an unusually clear atmosphere.[^17]

## The keepers

The station was staffed by a head keeper and three assistants.[^2][^3] The roster begins with John C. Bull as head keeper, in 1870, and his wife Melissa C. Bull, who is listed as second assistant for 1870 to 1871.[^1][^5] The Park Service says the Bulls and their family arrived in November 1870; Livingston puts the move into the new house in August.[^4][^5] In 1887 the *Chronicle* gave the pay: $800 a year and rations for the keeper, $600 for the first assistant, and $500 each for the second and third.[^6] The original dwelling held all four keepers until two cottages were built in 1885 for $5,787.35.[^5]

The post had a poor name. The Lighthouse Society's summary of Livingston's article says the first fifty years brought "insanity, alcoholism, violence and insubordination," along with the wind, the fog, the stairs, the isolation and the low pay.[^20] The *Chronicle* of 1887 gave its readers a few stories without naming the people. A cave near the keeper's house bore the name of a former assistant, an Englishman whose room was full of curiosities and who had reared a cat on an experimental regime; its tomb read "I loved Jodie." A recently removed keeper, the paper said, was known to local ranchers for his drinking, and it could not say whether inspectors' negligence or employees' reluctance to "tell tales" had let the situation last so long. The writer thought the present keepers "quite exemplary."[^6] The same article says the station's first assistant wrote his feelings into verse, and that the writer heard the poems read by their author.[^6] LighthouseFriends credits Keeper Edwin G. Chamberlin with a poem in the station logbook that longs for city, friendship and love.[^2] The Park Service's brochure prints two lines of it, "Better to dwell in the midst of alarms / than reign in this horrible place," and says only that a keeper wrote them.[^4]

The Park Service has transcribed the station log for 1888. It opens on 21 January, when John C. Ryan took charge as head keeper and wrote that the station was "broken, filthy, and almost a total wreck from end to end of it" and "more like an old saw mill than a light station."[^7] His third assistant signed the statement, and Livingston reports that the whole entry was scrawled over with the word "Rats."[^7][^5] Ryan's crew spent the year repairing and painting: the log records carpenters on the windows and chute, work on the new boiler, whitewash on the coal chute and stairs, and in October the tower sealed, primed red and painted.[^7] The assistants had troubles of their own. On 14 April the papers arrived dismissing First Assistant James Twohey from the service, and on 20 April he left with his wife, after "7 years, 10 months and 16 days at this station"; the log gives no reason.[^7] On 20 September Second Assistant James Nightwire (spelled Nightwine on the roster) was transferred and promoted to first assistant at Pigeon Point.[^7][^1]

George A. Hussey came to Point Reyes through the surrounding dairies. Livingston writes that he was one of the tenant dairymen on "A Ranch" and that he became head keeper in 1889 after many years of contract hauling for the station and service there as an assistant.[^5] The roster has him as head keeper from 1889 to 1891.[^1] The *Chronicle* of 1887 also names J.A. McFarland, then keeper at Alcatraz, as a former assistant at Point Reyes; the roster has a John A.F. McFarland as an assistant there from 1871 to 1875, but no source says the two are the same man.[^6][^1]

Paulus Nilsson came as first assistant in 1897 and became head keeper in 1909. LighthouseFriends says he fell on 18 February 1921 and died three days later.[^2] His own words survive in the *Lighthouse Service Bulletin*: after a hurricane on 27 January 1916 he sent the inspector a list of the damage.[^15] Gustave V. Zetterquist's span at the station runs from 1930 to at least 1951 on the Lighthouse Society roster.[^1][^2] The last Coast Guard keeper was Thomas Smith, who turned off the old light on 12 June 1975.[^2][^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Wind is the station's constant. A gale unroofed the keeper's house soon after it was finished, the *Chronicle* says, and LighthouseFriends dates a blow that took off the roof and brought down chimneys and fences to 1878.[^6][^2] A 1937 bulletin article said the Weather Bureau's records at Point Reyes showed a maximum annual air movement of 205,884 miles, in 1915, equal to a steady 23.5 miles an hour for the year, and that winds over 80 miles an hour were not uncommon even in clear weather.[^19] On 27 January 1916 a northwest hurricane blew at 104 miles an hour "the whole afternoon," and Nilsson's list of damage included a chimney on the large dwelling, a telephone post and wires, part of the roof of the tank house, and three stretches of fence, one of which was "lifted out of the ground and landed against the rain shed."[^15] The *Chronicle* says the keepers sometimes had to prostrate themselves on the stairs in gales, and that spray reached them even at the fog signal, 150 feet or more above the water.[^6]

Fog sets the other rhythm. When the *Chronicle*'s reporter visited in 1887 the sirens had run 176 hours without a break. Their blast lasted five seconds every seventy seconds, "enough to drive any ordinary man mad," he wrote, and the attendants "looked as if they had been on a protracted spree."[^6] The Lighthouse Service later gave the station's record as 2,070 hours of fog in 1887, and in 1912 an annual average of 1,350 hours, about 15 percent of the time, over 27 years.[^14][^12] By 1915 the average over 31 years was 1,337 hours.[^14] In the fiscal year 1917 the station logged 1,984 hours of fog, about 23 percent of the time, and for the fiscal year ended June 1930 it had 2,129 hours, its highest since 1918.[^16][^18] The service's 1937 bulletin gives 2,360 hours for the fiscal year 1934, the most in the district.[^19]

The wrecks near the point ran on through the keepers' years. On 20 August 1885 the British bark *Haddingtonshire* came ashore on Point Reyes Beach just north of the lighthouse. The San Francisco *Daily Alta California* blamed the light, calling the machinery "old and useless" and saying the siren "does not keep its time and frequently fails to blow at all during foggy weather," and the district inspector, Commander J.W. Philip, answered it.[^5] The 1888 log notes a bark ashore at 3 a.m. on 21 July about 3 miles north, and a schooner aground 2 miles south on 11 September.[^7] On the road to the lighthouse the *Chronicle*'s driver pointed out where the *Warrior Queen* was lost in a fog in 1875, lying one morning high and dry on the sand with all sails set; in 1887 her figurehead stood in a ranch garden.[^6] The Park Service says a life-saving station opened on the Great Beach north of the lighthouse in 1890, and that a new one was built on Drakes Bay in 1927.[^3]

The 1906 earthquake struck at 5:12 a.m. on 18 April.[^3] The Park Service says the lens slipped off its tracks, the keepers repaired it, and the light was working again that evening.[^3] Livingston says the guide-roller spindles were bent and the lamp stand displaced, and LighthouseFriends adds fallen chimneys and cracked cisterns.[^5][^2]

LighthouseFriends tells of a rescue in 1927 that rests on its account alone. Fred Kreth, listed on the roster as first assistant for 1926 and 1927, is said to have lowered himself about 200 feet down the cliff on a rope to three fishermen stranded when their boat hit the rocks, and to have hauled all three to safety; the Coast Guard, it says, found the surf too high to launch and tried to reach them by land.[^2][^1]

## After the keepers

Before automation, dwellings that had stood for nearly a century were replaced in 1960 by a modern apartment block and garage.[^5][^2] In 1967 a Coast Guard division chief ordered the station closed to visitors, effective 15 May, over staffing problems and despite public protest.[^5]

The Coast Guard automated the light on 12 June 1975, ending 105 years of manned operation. The Coast Guard put the automated equipment in a new concrete building on the old fog-signal site; when Livingston wrote in 1991 it held a revolving light, a radiobeacon and an electronic fog signal, all on timers.[^5][^2] The first-order lens, its clockwork and the tower were left in place.[^2] Land at the station passed to the Park Service for Point Reyes National Seashore, and the station opened to the public on 15 August 1977.[^2] Accounts differ on what stayed with the Coast Guard: the Park Service says the Coast Guard transferred ownership of the lighthouse to it, Livingston says the Coast Guard kept title to two small parcels and the transformer house, and LighthouseFriends says the tower and keepers' residence were left out of the transfer and lists the lighthouse as Coast Guard owned but park managed.[^3][^5][^2] The station was placed on the National Register of Historic Places as the Point Reyes Light Station in 1991.[^2][^10] A six-month renovation in 2003 cost $1.2 million, and a thirteen-month restoration in 2018 and 2019 cost $5.7 million, restoring the lens and clockwork, cleaning and repainting the ironwork, and replacing the roof and glazing.[^2] The Park Service says the lens was rebuilt in July 2019.[^3] The restored lighthouse reopened on 8 November 2019.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse lies inside Point Reyes National Seashore and is run by the National Park Service, which describes itself as the owner (the accounts above differ).[^9] There is no entrance or parking fee.[^9] From the parking lot, which holds 38 cars, it is a mostly uphill walk of about 0.45 mile (0.7 km) to the Lighthouse Visitor Center, then 313 steps down to the lighthouse. The stairs are closed to visitors when the wind passes 40 miles an hour. Visitors are asked to stay on the paved road and marked paths, and everything west of the parking lot away from them is closed, including the areas beyond the railings on the stairs.[^9] Hours change with the season, so check the Park Service's Point Reyes Lighthouse page before you go.[^9]
