Big Shippagan Lighthouse stands on a sand bar at the southern entrance to Shippegan Gully, on Lamèque Island in New Brunswick, near the village of Chiasson. The first light there was a small wooden tower lit in 1872. The tower standing now dates from 1905, and it still shows a white light for ships using the gully.[^1][^2][^6]

## The place

In the usage of the 1800s, a gully was a narrow opening between barrier islands or between points of land. Such openings often needed marks and lights to guide mariners through to safe harbour.[^1]

The gully at Shippagan was the way in for fishing boats, and in 1871 John Harding, the Department of Marine's agent at Saint John, pressed for a light there. In his 1871 report, as LighthouseFriends prints it, Harding recalled an inquest on three brothers. They had tried to enter the gully in a southern storm, touched the side of the channel, and drowned when their boat overturned.[^1]

## Building the light

The Department's first light, put up the following year, was a square wooden tower 6 m (20 ft) tall, with a wooden lantern on top. Its four flat-wick lamps sat behind 41 cm (16 in) reflectors. Keeper Francis X. Dumaresque, paid $240 a year, first showed the light on 21 October 1872.[^1]

A breakwater went up around the station in 1875 to protect it from the sea. A storm in October 1879 damaged the tower's foundation. In 1880 Michael Hayden moved the tower for $288 from an island called L'Islet to Alexander Point, on the northeast side of the entrance to the gully. A lantern hoisted on a pole gave range lights to show the course through the gully.[^1]

A keeper's dwelling was built in 1883, and in 1897 Dumaresque had it enlarged by an addition 5.2 by 3.7 m (17 by 12 ft).[^1]

The tower standing now came in 1905. The Department reported that the old light had been "replaced by a new and much higher tower." It stands 91 m (300 ft) south of the old one, is an octagonal wooden building 16 m (51 ft) high, and sits on a square wooden crib 2 m (6 ft) high. Honore Duguay of Caraquet built it under a $1,500 contract.[^1][^2] The new light was to show a fixed white light of the fourth order, 16 m (53 ft) above high water, from the opening of navigation in 1906.[^2]

In 1907 the fourth-order lens was replaced by a single-flashing third-order lens, and the tower was altered at a cost of $315 to take the revolving light.[^1]

## The keepers

Dumaresque kept the light from 1872 to 1902. LighthouseFriends counts his service at 31 years.[^1] The keepers' table for fiscal 1901 lists "Dumaresq, Francis X" at Shippegan.[^3] The inspection reports that LighthouseFriends prints describe him as "a very obliging and efficient officer." In 1875 he was away from the station, leaving the light in another man's charge so he could look after his crops, and the inspector wrote to him. In 1876 he was away again, visiting a sick wife, and the station was "found in very bad order." In July 1877 the inspector found everything "neat and in good order."[^1]

Aug. Robichaud followed Dumaresque in 1902. The keepers' table for fiscal 1902 lists "Robichaud, Aug Shippegan."[^4] LighthouseFriends says a Mrs. A. Robichaud kept the light from 1904 to 1906.[^1]

Adélard Savoie kept the light from 1906 to 1910. The keepers' table for fiscal 1906 lists "Savon, Adelard" at Shippegan, and LighthouseFriends gives the keeper as Adélard Savoie.[^5][^1] After him LighthouseFriends names Louis de Grace (1910 to 1913), A. T. DeGrace (1913 to 1919), Jerome Noel (1919 to 1921) and Stanislaus Hachè, who is recorded from 1921 to at least 1937.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The one storm that LighthouseFriends records at the station is the October 1879 gale, which damaged the tower's foundation. The tower was moved the following year.[^1]

The three brothers drowned in the gully before the light was built. Harding cited their deaths when he asked for a light.[^1]

## After the keepers

A new keeper's dwelling went up at the station in 1924, built by P. G. Robichaud, and the foundation crib was repaired.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light.[^1] Its List of Lights gives the characteristic as a long-flashing white light, flashing every six seconds, visible 13 nautical miles, from a white octagonal tower with a red top that is 16.1 m high. The light is listed as burning year round.[^6]

Parks Canada lists the light in its federal heritage designations under the name "Big Shippegan Shore Light."[^7] The Canadian Register of Historic Places describes the tapered, medium-height octagonal tower, its wood shingles, its cast-iron lantern, and its white tower with red accents. It calls the tower a prominent landmark for mariners and tourists.[^8]

The grounds are open, but the tower is closed. The Coast Guard owns the site.[^1] To get there, LighthouseFriends advises taking Route 113 from Shippagan across the causeway to Lamèque Island, then Chemin Chiasson. Turn right just past the Village of Chiasson onto the dirt road. It becomes rough after 0.8 km (0.5 miles), so park there and walk the last 0.9 km (0.6 miles).[^1] Do not climb the tower while it is closed.
