The Pointe au Baril range lights are two towers on the eastern shore of Georgian Bay, in the Township of The Archipelago, Ontario. In line, they lead ships through a channel toward the harbour at Pointe au Baril.[^1][^2] The front light is a square wooden tower with a keeper's dwelling attached.[^3] Since 1995 the township has leased the building from the Coast Guard and runs a museum and a summer attraction there.[^3]

## The place

The point's name means "Barrel Point." LighthouseFriends tells how fur traders from Penetanguishene lost a canoe along the eastern shore of Georgian Bay in the 1870s. When the canoe was found the following spring, the finders took a barrel of whiskey from it. They left the empty barrel on the point to mark the place, and the name followed.[^1] The present light was the first building put up on the site, although a rudimentary beacon came before it.[^3]

## Building the light

In 1887 the Department of Marine drew plans for range lights to lead into the channel, then decided on better buildings than the first plans called for. Charles Mickler of Collingwood was given a contract of $1,515 to erect them. The lights were first displayed on 10 October 1889. The total spent on them, with their lighting apparatus, was $2,260.37.[^1]

The Department's own description, as LighthouseFriends prints it, gives the front light as a square wooden tower with a kitchen attached. The tower is 9.8 m (32 ft) high to the vane. The light is 11.6 m (38 ft) above the bay and visible for 10 miles from all points seaward.[^1] The rear light stood on an island 1.5 km (4,800 ft) away. It was an open square frame with an enclosed lantern, 13.4 m (44 ft) high to the vane.[^1]

In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1903 the back range tower was replaced by a new one on the old site. It had a skeleton steel frame, an enclosed wooden watch room and a square wooden lantern. "The steel frame is painted red, and the woodwork white," the report says.[^4] LighthouseFriends gives the reverse colours, with the steel white and the wood red.[^1] The annual report is the primary record, so the story follows it.

The next year the report notes that wooden slats had been painted white across the tower's channel side, and that the trees around the tower had been cut down "so that the building may be more easily picked up from seaward."[^5] LighthouseFriends says mariners still found the back light hard to see from outside the reefs, and that this is why it was replaced in 1909.[^2] In the fiscal year ended 31 March 1909 the 1903 tower was taken down and removed to Owen Sound. "It was replaced by a new 4-section steel skeleton tower, square in plan," the report says, and the old tower became the front light of that range there.[^6]

## The keepers

Samuel Edward Oldfield was the first keeper, from 1889 to 1897. LighthouseFriends reports that he was dismissed in 1897 for "fishing with illegal nets which are wholly prohibited in Ontario waters." His successor, Charles McDevitt of Parry Sound, received the same salary, $300 a year.[^1]

Ole Hansen (1907 to 1930), James A. Vail (1930 to 1940), Kenneth Malcolm Evans (1941 to 1949) and Carl Madigan (1949 to 1977) followed.[^1] After Madigan's death in 1977, Emmaline Madigan took over the light. She kept it until the Coast Guard automated it in the fall of 1983 and boarded up the house.[^1][^7]

In 2000 the Pointe au Baril Islanders' Association and the township asked her back to live and work at the lighthouse in the summer months as a local historian and museum curator. She retired from lighthouse duties in 2024.[^7]

## After the keepers

The register says the light was destaffed in 1983 but still houses automatic coastal and range lights. The building has been leased to the township since 1995, which runs a seasonal attraction, museum and shop there.[^3] In 1998 local groups, including the Islanders' Association and the township, opened the lighthouse in summer as a local attraction and information centre.[^7]

LighthouseFriends reports that the Coast Guard had listed the range lights for decommissioning.[^1] The township has the right of first refusal and has made a proposal to the Coast Guard to buy them.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard's current list still gives both range lights. The front light is a fixed red light with a focal height of 13.1 metres, a nominal range of 16 nautical miles, and a white square tower. The rear light has a focal height of 26.7 metres and a white skeleton tower with a red stripe daymark. Both are marked seasonal, in place year round, and operating 24 hours.[^8] The sources differ on the front light's colour. The 1889 Department description gives it as fixed white. LighthouseFriends' table gives both lights as fixed red, as does the current list.[^1][^8]

The lighthouse is reached by boat only, and the township describes a panoramic view of the channel from the site.[^7] LighthouseFriends gives summer hours for the front light, from mid-June through Labor Day, and says the rear's grounds are open with its tower closed.[^1][^2] The WPSD Museum's exhibition "Lights of the Bay" ran from 26 June to 18 October 2025. Its notice says the exhibition was prompted by a project with the township to store artifacts from the lighthouse temporarily while it undergoes renovations.[^9] The access notes above are undated, so check with the township before going.
