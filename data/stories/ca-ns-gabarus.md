Gabarus Lighthouse is a white hexagonal tower with a red lantern, standing on Harbour Point at the south shore of Gabarus Bay in Cape Breton. It was lit in 1890 to guide boats into the harbour, and in 2015 a local society had it moved about 12 m (40 ft) back from a cliff that the sea was eating away.[^1][^2][^5]

## The place

Gabarus Bay is on the southeast shore of Cape Breton.[^9] The entrance to Gabarus Harbour lies between Rouse Point on the east and Gabarus Point on the west.[^2] The Department of Marine's 1890 notice, as Lighthouse Friends prints it, said the light was for "aiding vessels navigating Gabarus Bay, and leading to a safe anchorage behind Harbour Point."[^2] The Register of Historic Places classes it as a medium harbour light with a range of eight nautical miles, meant for small fishing boats and pleasure craft that work their way in through shallow water.[^1] The village of Gabarus, which Lighthouse Friends says was founded in 1716, lies 3.5 km (2.2 miles) east of Route 327 along Gabarus Highway.[^2]

## Building the light

The 1890 notice described a hexagonal wooden tower painted white, with an iron lantern painted red and about 9.8 m (32 ft) from the ground to the vane.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says Mr. Neil McNiell built it under contract for $650. With other expenses the work came to $748.33, and the light went into operation on 1 November.[^2] The Register says the tower was built to a standard plan and dates it "circa 1890".[^1] A tourism guide gives the tower's date as 1891, and says that the tower standing today was built between 1955 and 1962 as a replacement.[^8] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society's list gives the same 1955 to 1962 span for the present tower.[^6] The Register and Lighthouse Friends both date the tower to about 1890, and the 2015 news calls the structure 125 years old when it was moved, so this story follows the 1890 date.[^1][^2][^5] The Preservation Society's list also gives a seventh-order dioptric lens in use from 1922 to 1941.[^6]

## The keepers

John Hardy was appointed the first keeper on 22 November 1890, at an annual salary of $150.[^2] The keeper table in the Department's 1904 annual report lists "Hardy, John" against Gabarus, with the same appointment date, and gives his salary as $200 a year.[^4] The table's figure differs from the $150 that Lighthouse Friends gives at his appointment. James H. McDonald succeeded him in 1908 and served until 1930, when his salary was $420.[^2] Lighthouse Friends also names a J. McDonald for 1930 and 1931, and Michael B. Morrison for 1950 to 1964, but gives no keeper between 1931 and 1950.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Preservation Society says the light was automated by 1988.[^6] Lighthouse Friends says that in 1982 a light and foghorn were placed on Rouse Point, but the Coast Guard's list still places this light on Harbour Point. The same list also has a separate light on Rouses Point.[^2][^3]

The lighthouse had been standing on land that was eroding into the ocean.[^5] The Gabarus Lightkeepers Society was formed in 2014 to raise funds to move and restore the lighthouse, and it petitioned the government for permission to save it.[^2][^7] In 2015 the society entered This Lighthouse Matters, a Nova Scotia contest run by the National Trust of Canada and the Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society and decided by public votes. Gabarus came second with 21,403 votes and was awarded $50,000.[^2][^9] On 26 and 27 November 2015 a contractor, T&J Demeyere Properties, slid the 22-tonne lighthouse about 12 m (40 ft) across metal rails, using liquid dish detergent as a lubricant, and set it on a new foundation.[^2][^5] The society's chair, who lives next door to the lighthouse, said that after one rainstorm the cliff edge was about 2 m (7 ft) from the tower, and that members woke up and watched whenever it rained.[^5] A later case study gives the distance as 15 metres.[^9] In 2016 the tower was restored to its original design, with new windows, repaired framing and repainting.[^7]

In 2017 the society asked the Cape Breton Regional Municipality to accept ownership of the lighthouse under the divestiture programme of the federal Department of Fisheries and Oceans, which offered a one-time payment of $40,000.[^2] The municipality had a policy against owning buildings, but was open to leasing the lighthouse from the department.[^2] The society declined to sign the memorandum of agreement on its lawyer's advice. It was willing to take on insurance and maintenance, but could not accept the liability terms in the agreement.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard's list describes a fixed red light, shown all year from a white hexagonal tower with a red top, with a range of eight nautical miles.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says the Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and the Preservation Society gives the owner as the Government of Canada.[^2][^6] The Preservation Society's page gives two answers on whether the light still works: its light details say it is operational, but its summary table says it is not still operating.[^6] The tower is not open to the public. The grounds are open all year for walking and photographs, and visitors reach the point by turning onto Harbour Point Road from the village.[^7][^2]
