Princes Bay Light stands on a bluff above Raritan Bay, at the southern end of Staten Island, New York.[^1][^2] Its first tower, of rubblestone, showed a light in 1828. The brownstone tower that replaced it was finished in 1864.[^2] The light was discontinued in 1922, and a statue of the Virgin Mary now stands where its lantern room was.[^1][^2][^6]

## The place

The bluff rises about 85 feet (26 m) above the water, on the highest point along the shoreline of Staten Island.[^2] Congress appropriated money for a light there in 1826.[^2] In 1828 three sites were chosen for new beacons, to guide the ever-growing ship traffic calling at New York City: Princes Bay, Fort Tompkins on Staten Island, and the Highlands of Navesink in New Jersey.[^2] Oystering had been one of the main trades in the bay since the late 1700s. Overfishing and pollution ended it as a commercial trade by the early 1900s.[^2]

In 1837 a Navy commission reported that the light could be seen only from north-northeast to south-southwest.[^2] It recommended reflectors facing west, for night traffic on the upper bays, which in winter had to pass south of Staten Island when ice blocked the Kills.[^2]

## Building the light

The first tower stood a little over 30 feet (9 m) high and showed its light 107 feet (33 m) above the sea.[^2] Its eleven lamps were set in fourteen-inch reflectors.[^2] Around 1843 a visitor, I. W. P. Lewis, found that the tower's eighteen lamps gave passing coasters only part of their benefit.[^2] He recommended that eight lamps be suppressed, and the change was soon made.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says a third-and-a-half-order Fresnel lens went into the lantern in 1857.[^2] The archive gives 1857 as the year the original lens was installed, but describes the first optic as reflectors later replaced by a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^1] In 1863 the Lighthouse Board reported that the tower needed rebuilding. A wooden tower carried a temporary light while a new brownstone tower went up.[^2] It was finished in 1864, and the lens was moved into it.[^2] The keeper's brownstone house, linked to the tower by a passage, was finished in 1868.[^1][^2]

Congress gave $12,000 in 1870 to protect the station from the sea.[^2] A seawall and three jetties went in over the next few years. By 1880 the wall had given way in several places, and $3,500 went on repairs that year.[^2] In 1884 the interval between flashes was cut from two minutes to 45 seconds. From 1 January 1890 a fourth-order lens showed a white flash every five seconds.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists nineteen principal keepers from 1828 to 1922.[^1]

LighthouseFriends reports that Abel Rawson, a former whaling captain from Nantucket, was promised the post by Andrew Jackson while the light was being built.[^2] Rawson kept the light until his death in 1840.[^1][^2]

The archive lists Harriet Riell as principal keeper from 1848 to 1849, after Henry E. Riell (1847 to 1848).[^1] The sources read here do not say how she came to the post or what she did in it. Silas Bedell, keeper from 1849 to 1853, was found in an 1851 inspection to be "ignorant of his duties."[^2] The same inspection found the lantern glass broken and cracked. LighthouseFriends also says Bedell often left his daughter in charge while he went off to pursue other interests.[^2]

William G. Davison was keeper from 1886 to 1917, the longest tenure on the archive's roster.[^1][^2] In 1916 a reporter visited the light while accompanying Inspector J. T. Yates on an inspection tour of New York Harbor lights.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that Davison, then seventy-two, had the best-kept station in the area, and that his wife was still doing the housework.[^2] John F. Anderson, keeper from 1917 to 1922, stayed on after the light was discontinued, until November 1922, to guard the station against vandals.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the year ended 30 June 1893, ice carried away the bridge across the small creek north of the light station during the winter.[^3] The Board had it rebuilt.[^3] The next year the Board's report records a picket fence of some 350 feet, and a bridge over the inlet to the swamp, raised and extended by 21 feet.[^4]

On 4 and 5 October 1918, explosions at the munitions plant at Morgan, New Jersey, damaged lights in New York harbor.[^5][^7] The Bulletin's account names the Princess Bay light station (the spelling the Board and the Bulletin use for this light), where the dwelling was damaged.[^5][^7] The sources read here record no wreck tied to this station.

## After the keepers

On 31 August 1922 the old lighthouse was discontinued, because a new system of channel lights in Raritan Bay had made it unnecessary.[^6] LighthouseFriends says that lighted buoys in the bay had made the bluff-top light redundant earlier that month.[^2]

In 1926 the lighthouse reservation was sold at public auction.[^2] The highest bid came from the Mission of the Immaculate Virgin, a residence and school for orphans that already surrounded the light on three sides.[^2] The mission placed a statue of the Virgin Mary atop the tower where the lantern room had been.[^1][^2] A rear range light went up on the mission's land in 1953. The federal government paid the mission $32 a year for the use of a small piece of land, and a front range light stands on the beach 45 yards away.[^2]

In 1999 New York State and the Trust for Public Land bought the lighthouse and about 145 acres of upland from the Archdiocese of New York.[^2] The area is now the Mount Loretto Unique Area, managed by the state's Department of Environmental Conservation.[^2] In 2006 the lighthouse was renamed the John Cardinal O'Connor Lighthouse, and a VRB-25 rotating beacon was placed atop it.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is not open to the public, and its grounds are closed.[^1][^2] The views come from trails in the park, and LighthouseFriends says the best are in winter, when the trees are bare.[^1][^2]

The archive lists the station as not an active aid to navigation.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports the 2006 beacon, and this story does not settle whether the light is an official aid today.[^1][^2]
