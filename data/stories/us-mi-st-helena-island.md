St. Helena Island Light stands on a small island in the Straits of Mackinac, about two miles off Michigan's Upper Peninsula.[^2] It was first lit in September 1873 to mark a shoal that runs out from the island, and it kept resident keepers until 1922, when it was automated.[^1][^9] The Great Lakes Lighthouse Keepers Association says it owns and runs the station. Today the island is reached by boat.[^2]

## The place

St. Helena Island lies about ten miles west of Mackinac Island, off Gros Cap.[^1][^2] Its north shore has a natural harbor that had long sheltered Native Americans, voyageurs and vessels fleeing the lake's southwesterly storms.[^1] The brothers Archie and Wilson Newton bought the 266-acre island from William Belote in 1853 and set up fishing, trading, lumbering and cooperage businesses on the shore of the harbor.[^1] The island once had a community of more than two hundred people.[^2]

The hazard was the shoal. Pepper says St. Helena Shoal lies just under the surface for almost a mile and a half to the island's northwest, which left the western passage unsafe for all but the shallowest vessels.[^1] Dangerous shoals also reached out from the eastern end.[^1] Pepper says the Lighthouse Board recommended a light at the island's southeast point in its 1867 annual report, with $14,000 for construction.[^1] Congress did not act on that request, or on the Board's later ones, until 10 June 1872.[^1]

Because the island lay close to the growing town of St. Ignace, Pepper says, it drew visitors. In winter many of them crossed the ice by sleigh, a trip of about three hours.[^1]

## Building the light

Orlando M. Poe, the engineer of the Eleventh District, chose a three-acre reservation on the island and advertised for materials and labor.[^1] Work began in September 1872 and stopped on 9 November, when Pepper says conditions became too cold for the mortar to set properly.[^1] The limestone base of the tower and the basement for the keeper's dwelling were finished before winter.[^1] The work party came back on 9 May 1873, and over the next month the double-walled brick tower went up course by course.[^1] A cast-iron lantern capped it. From the foundation to the center of the lens the tower stands 65 feet (19.8 m).[^1]

A covered way joined the tower to the brick dwelling. An iron door at the tower end was meant to stop a fire spreading between the two buildings.[^1]

The lens came from Paris. By 30 June 1873 the station was complete apart from minor finish work and the lens, and in August the district lampist, Mr. Crump, arrived to install the cast-iron base and assemble the lens.[^1] It was a third-and-a-half-order Fresnel lens.[^1][^3] Thomas P. Dunn, the first keeper, reported for duty on 29 July 1873.[^1] On the evening of 20 September 1873 he first showed the light.[^1]

Later work is recorded in the Board's reports. The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1895 records a new landing crib and boat way built at the station.[^7] The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1896 records a brick oil house, with a metal door and roof, erected there.[^8] Pepper says the oil house was part of a ten-year Board program to store oil apart from the tower, because kerosene had made fire more dangerous.[^1]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists thirteen lines of service at St. Helena, from 1873 to 1922.[^3] Dunn kept the light until 1875, when he exchanged posts with Charles Louisignau, the keeper at McGulpin's Point. Pepper spells the name Lousigneau.[^1][^3] Louisignau resigned from the service on 30 May 1888. Pepper notes that no keeper was listed at the station until 7 July that year.[^1]

Charles M. Marshall, first assistant at Waugoshance, came to St. Helena as acting keeper in July 1888.[^1] Pepper says he was made permanent keeper on 6 July 1892.[^1][^3] On 23 November 1900 Marshall moved to Old Mackinac Point as first assistant.[^1]

George E. Leggatt, who had been first assistant at Old Mackinac Point, took over the station that November. Pepper, who spells the name Legatt, says he drowned the following June, which left the station without a keeper again.[^1][^3] The archive also lists a laborer, LaSalle S. Leggatt, for 1901. The sources do not say whether the two men were related.[^3]

Joseph Fountain arrived as keeper on 1 July 1901.[^1][^3] Pepper says he had eighteen years of service at other island lights in Lake Michigan.[^1][^3] He stayed until 1918.[^3]

In 1909 the Board decided to add an assistant at the station.[^1] Louis Beloungea, transferred from Squaw Island on 21 October, lived in a small one-room cottage a scant ten feet (3 m) from the privy.[^1] Pepper calls that a less than desirable situation, and says four assistants left the post in as many years.[^1] Pepper says the cottage was later moved to a site about a hundred feet (30 m) north of the oil house.[^1] Beloungea ate his meals in the main dwelling with Keeper Fountain and his family.[^1]

Wallace S. Hall was first assistant from 1914 and keeper from 1919 until he transferred to Little Point Sable on 30 June 1922.[^1][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 7 February 1913 Keeper Joseph Fountain rescued two men who had become lost on the ice and were nearly frozen, and brought them to the light station.[^4] Putnam's history of the lighthouses, published in 1917, gives more detail. He says the two men were trying to cross the Straits in a blizzard, and that the keeper went out with a hand sled and brought in the exhausted men.[^6] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin does not mention the blizzard or the sled.[^4]

In August 1914 Fountain and his assistant, Wallace Hall, helped repair a motor boat that had become disabled near the station.[^5]

## After the keepers

Hall's transfer in 1922 ended resident keeping.[^1][^9] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's list of changes for 1922 records the station's light as changed from oil to acetylene and from fixed to flashing.[^9] Pepper says the new lamp had a sun valve, which turned it on in the cool of evening and put it out with the warmth of day. He says the station was then boarded up, and keepers from Old Mackinac Point took their boat over whenever passing vessels reported trouble with the light.[^1]

Without a full-time keeper, the buildings deteriorated. By the 1980s the station was in extremely poor condition: everything of value had been stripped, and vandals set a fire on the second floor.[^1] The Coast Guard demolished the boathouse and the assistant's dwelling to reduce their liability, and was considering taking down everything except the tower.[^1]

The Great Lakes Lighthouse Keepers Association took a thirty-year license to the reservation in 1986.[^1][^2] The lighthouse was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1988.[^2] Boy Scout troops from Ann Arbor and Calumet began to work on the station each summer. Pepper says this began in 1989; the GLLKA says the Ann Arbor troop began two years after the 1988 listing.[^1][^2] Pepper says that in 1996 Representative Bart Stupak sponsored a bill that transferred the station buildings and reservation to the GLLKA, as part of a Coast Guard authorization act.[^1] In September 2001 the Little Traverse Conservancy bought the entire island as a nature preserve.[^1]

## Today

The GLLKA says it owns and operates the station. It says about 250 people visit the lighthouse in a summer, and it takes volunteer keepers for tours of duty from three days to two months, between mid-June and mid-August.[^2]

Pepper's page, last modified in 2007, says the Conservancy bought the entire island in 2001. The sources do not show how that fits with the GLLKA's ownership of the station.[^1][^2]

The island is reached by boat. The GLLKA takes volunteers out by charter boat from Mackinaw City, or by a two-mile run from the south shore of the Upper Peninsula.[^2] Pepper notes that a Mackinaw City ferry company's summer lighthouse cruise passes the island.[^1]
