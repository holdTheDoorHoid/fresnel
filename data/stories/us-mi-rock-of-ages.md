Rock of Ages is a strip of bare rock about 50 feet (15 m) wide and 210 feet (64 m) long, rising some 16 feet (5 m) above Lake Superior two and a half miles (about 4 km) off the western end of Isle Royale, in Keweenaw County, Michigan.[^1] Between 1907 and 1910 the Lighthouse Service put a brick tower on a steel and concrete pier there, with quarters for four keepers and a fog siren in the base.[^1][^5]

## The place

For more than fifty years the reef had claimed one ship, the 205-foot (62 m) wooden sidewheel steamer *Cumberland*, according to Terry Pepper's history of the light; in the 1890s, as Duluth grew into the lake's great port, more captains began to steer along the north shore in bad weather, and the Rock of Ages lay directly in that track.[^1] The Lighthouse Board first asked Congress for $50,000 for a light and fog signal there in its report for 1896.[^4] By the Board's account in 1900, vessels bound to and from Duluth ran along the north shore in the lee of Isle Royale when the lake was too rough for the more southerly course, and a light on the rocks there would be, in its words, "a valuable aid to these vessels."[^3]

Congress did not act. On 20 October 1898 the 257-foot (78 m) wooden propeller *Henry Chisholm* was lost on the reef,[^1] and the National Park Service's account lists both the *Cumberland* (1877) and the *Chisholm* (1898) among the reef's victims.[^2] The Board kept repeating its request. In its 1900 report it raised the estimate from $50,000 to $125,000,[^3] and in 1903, Pepper writes, it changed tack and asked for $25,000 only to survey the site and plan the work.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress gave $25,000 on 3 March 1905, $50,000 on 30 June 1906 and another $50,000 on 4 March 1907, with the approval of the work conditional, Pepper says, on keeping the cost under $100,000.[^1][^5] The sources disagree on what the station cost: LighthouseFriends gives $138,328.50 as the amount finally spent, and the National Park Service gives $125,000.[^5][^2] Plans came from Charles Keller, who is called a captain of the Army Corps of Engineers by the National Park Service and a major and Eleventh District engineer by Pepper; the design borrowed from skyscraper practice, a steel skeleton carrying brick walls.[^2][^1] The National Park Service names Walter F. Beyer of Detroit as builder; Pepper says the contract for the steel skeleton went to the Russell Wheel and Foundry Company of Detroit.[^2][^1]

The accounts differ on the year work began. LighthouseFriends has a chartered steam barge leave Detroit on 21 May 1907 and reach Washington Harbor, Isle Royale, on 27 May; Pepper gives the same days in 1908; the National Park Service puts the start of the crib in May 1907.[^5][^1][^2] All agree on the method. Old mining buildings at Washington Harbor were leased as a shore depot,[^5][^1] and out on the rock the workmen blasted a flat footing and riveted heavy steel plates into a ring 50 feet (15 m) across and about 25 feet (8 m) high, which was then filled with concrete.[^5][^1] Once a timber platform at pier-deck level carried a bunkhouse for thirty men, the crew no longer had to make the twice-daily, ten-mile trips to and from the rock.[^5] In the autumn gales, LighthouseFriends reports, supplies could be landed only when the sea was calm, and more than once the men were put on short rations.[^5]

A temporary third-order fixed red light and a six-inch siren were put into service on 22 October 1908, according to LighthouseFriends and Pepper; the National Park Service gives 11 October.[^5][^1][^2] With storms arriving, the station was closed on 4 November 1908 and work stopped until the next season.[^1] On 4 March 1909 Congress added $15,000 for a proper lens, and an order went to the Paris makers Barbier, Bénard and Turenne later that month.[^5][^1] The second-order lens was first shown on 15 September 1910.[^1][^2] A Lighthouse Service description of 1912, as LighthouseFriends prints it, gives four flash panels, each of seven refracting and seventeen reflecting prisms, turning on a bath of mercury to give a double white flash every ten seconds, visible 19 miles in clear weather from a focal plane 117 feet (36 m) above the lake.[^5] The National Park Service instead gives a range of 29 miles and a rotation of once every twenty seconds.[^2]

The tower was eight storeys high. The 1912 description puts the fog-signal plant on the first floor, the office on the second, the mess room and kitchen on the third, the keeper's and first assistant's rooms on the fourth, and the second and third assistants' rooms on the fifth.[^5]

## The keepers

Thomas E. Irvine was the first head keeper. Pepper says he had eight years in the service, at Outer Island and Au Sable Point, before this posting; the second assistant, William Duggan, reported on 2 October 1908 and Irvine and first assistant Lee Benton arrived on 27 October.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society archive lists Irvine as head keeper from 1908 to 1914, followed by John Garrity (1914 to 1921), Joseph V. Metivier, Emil Mueller and John F. Soldenski, who held the post from 1930 to 1935.[^6]

Four keepers lived in the tower for the open-water season, which LighthouseFriends puts at about eight months, April to December.[^5] Pepper describes a rota in which one keeper in turn took a week's leave each month, with reading, cards and fishing around the rock to fill the hours.[^1] In 1932 the Bureau of Lighthouses noted that mail for Rock of Ages and Passage Island had to go by steamer from Duluth, a steamer "rarely operative beyond the latter part of October."[^7] One Rock of Ages keeper, Robert Morrill, appears in the archive as a first assistant from at least 1926.[^6] LighthouseFriends tells of a December in which the tender *Marigold* arrived six days overdue to take the keepers off and found a note on the door saying that the men had left the day before because fuel and food were low. It dates this to 18 December 1926 and says Morrill reached Duluth days later with word that all four were safe, after ice kept them from landing at Grand Portage and they took shelter with fishermen at Pigeon Point.[^5] The Bulletin's 1918 to 1923 index also lists "Rock of Ages keepers picked up by tender Marigold" on page 113, among similar entries for other Lake Superior stations; it gives no date and does not describe the event. The index covers 1918 to 1923, so the entry cannot be the December 1926 event, and either that date or the identification is in doubt.[^15]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The wreck that made the station's name came in late May 1933. The passenger steamer *George M. Cox*, bound from Houghton, Michigan, to Port Arthur and Fort William, Ontario, stranded in fog on the reef a short distance from the light.[^8] A newspaper report dated 28 May 1933 says she went aground "early Saturday evening", which is 27 May.[^11] Pepper, who gives 28 May, adds that the fog signal was sounding and that the ship, then on her first voyage after refitting, came out of the fog at seventeen knots.[^1] The Bulletin records the signals running at the time: the radiobeacon for three hours and the sound signal for one.[^8]

The sea was calm, and the passengers and crew reached the light station. The Bulletin says there was not room inside the tower for everyone, so people took turns to warm up and spent the rest of the night "huddled on the bare rocks outside."[^8] The numbers differ by source. The Bulletin gives 127 people on board and says 116 were landed at the station, "without accident of any kind," many of them in the station boat;[^8][^9] Pepper has 125 rescued.[^1] A news report of 29 May described a night "in the penetrating chill of the reef where the tiny quarters of the light house furnished heat for only a few at a time."[^10] Next day everyone but a few representatives of the ship's owner was taken ashore.[^8] Pepper says the Coast Guard cutter *Crawford* carried the last of them to Two Harbors, Minnesota; LighthouseFriends says Houghton, Michigan.[^1][^5]

The Secretary of Commerce commended head keeper John F. Soldenski and his assistants, named in the Bulletin as L. M. Whipple, J. B. Marshall and S. E. Heinsberg, for the landing.[^9] Pepper names the assistants Whipple, Marshall and "Marrow"; the Bulletin gives S. E. Heinsberg and the archive's roster lists an Edward Heinsberg as third assistant from 1929 to 1935, so the surname agrees.[^1][^9][^6] The light's radiotelephone sent out a general call about the wreck and handled messages for the vessel's owners; the district office learned from it that the aids to navigation were not involved.[^5] The *Cox* never came off the reef. Pepper notes that her wreck still lies there and is visited by divers.[^1]

The station's signals served ships in clear weather too. On 13 June 1936, by special request, the radiobeacon and whistle were run regardless of weather so that the large steamer *City of Detroit III* could find Grace Harbor with a cruise party visiting the proposed Isle Royale National Park. The pilot reported hearing the whistle about eight miles off and knowing the ship's position from then until she anchored.[^13] The light could also be seen from afar: a 1922 Bulletin item reports that the first and second assistant keepers at Outer Island, about 76 miles away, plainly saw it flash on the night of 7 May.[^14]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service passed to the Coast Guard in 1939. Pepper says about half the keepers chose to join, and that William Muessel was assigned in 1949 as officer in charge, serving until 1954.[^1] The archive lists Muessel as head keeper for those years.[^6] Interviewed by Pepper in 2002, Muessel recalled a rota of three weeks out and one in, and the 24-foot (7 m) boat in which the men ran across the roughly nineteen miles to Grand Portage, Minnesota, when going ashore on liberty or coming back from it.[^12] Of the tower itself he recalled that when the crew went out in spring after the winter it took about a week to warm the place up, and that the *Cox* lay about four feet under water, slipping a little deeper each year.[^12]

The archive's roster shows Coast Guard personnel at the station in the 1960s, and John Brecke as head keeper from at least 1976.[^6] The National Park Service says a four-man crew served until 1977; the archive and the Rock of Ages Lighthouse Preservation Society give 1978 for automation.[^2][^6][^18] About 1975 the tower was resurfaced and whitewashed, and the moulding and pilasters of its lower storey were smoothed over, giving it the plain "spark plug" shape it has now.[^2] The second-order lens was taken out in 1985, Pepper says over five days, and a solar-powered 300 mm optic took its place.[^1][^2] The station was entered on the National Register of Historic Places as no. 83000881, published on 4 August 1983.[^16]

## Today

Rock of Ages is an active aid to navigation, standing in waters that belong to Isle Royale National Park.[^6][^18] LighthouseFriends lists the lighthouse as owned by the Coast Guard, with the tower closed; the National Park Service lists it as not open to the public, and Pepper says landing at the dock is considered dangerous.[^5][^17][^1] The old second-order lens is on display at the Windigo visitor station on Isle Royale, about five miles away.[^1][^2] The wreck of the *George M. Cox* remains a dive site on the reef.[^1]
