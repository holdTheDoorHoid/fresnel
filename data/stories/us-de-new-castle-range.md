The New Castle Range lights are two Coast Guard towers beside the Delaware River at New Castle, Delaware. They were built to help mariners avoid Bulkhead Shoal, a shoal in the river just north of Pea Patch Island.[^1] Both lights were first exhibited on 15 November 1876.[^1] The original wooden lights are gone and the rear light's house burned in 1982, but the steel tower that replaced the front light and the skeleton tower on the rear site still mark the channel. They are closed to visitors.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The government bought the land for the pair. It paid $6,000 for a 4.07-acre parcel on the river's western bank, from L. Taylor Dickson, and $2,500 for a one-acre parcel about half a mile inland, from Joseph I. Taggert.[^1] The front light stood beside Deemer Beach, which had a bathing beach, a dance hall and a picnic area, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1]

## Building the light

Lieutenant Colonel William F. Raynolds of the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers was responsible for erecting the buildings for both lights, LighthouseFriends says.[^1] Its quotation of an 1878 description of the Fourth District's sites gives the front light a ten-foot-square tower, 38 feet (12 m) high, attached to the south end of the keeper's two-story dwelling. The archive's record gives 15 feet (4.6 m) from the base of the front structure to the center of its lantern, which does not match the 1878 figure.[^2] The rear light was a square tower attached to its own dwelling, and it stood fifty feet (15 m) from its base to the focal plane.[^1] Each light used a 24-inch range lens, a silvered reflector and a Funck lamp.[^1]

Later work on the site came in order. In 1880 a small gallery was built outside the front lantern so the outside of the glass could be cleaned.[^1] In 1884 a large clump of trees that blocked the view of the rear light was cut down, with the landowner's consent. In the years after, both stations sank wells for drinking water.[^1] In 1886 the front tower was detached from the dwelling and moved thirty feet (9 m) west, so that the range line would be farther from the shoals off Finn's Point. An enclosed passage was built to connect the tower and house, and a detached frame oil house went up.[^1]

On 31 October 1894 the front light's characteristic changed from fixed white to a light that was fixed white for 2 seconds, with an eclipse of 1 second between. The Light-House Board's report for the year gives the date.[^5] In the year to 30 June 1901 the front tower was repainted solid white, and the barn, dwelling and elevated walk were painted light brown.[^8]

## The keepers

Alexander Jarrell was the first head keeper of the front light, from 1876 to 1883.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends says Alexander Jarrell tried to cultivate crops at the front light.[^1] The station logbook, as LighthouseFriends prints it, records that on 17 April 1877 he went to New Castle to see about manure, and found that none could be had "at any price."[^1]

Oliver L. Bell served from 1883 to 1888, and George A. Smith is listed for 1888 alone.[^2] Joseph Cofield came next. The archive's roster has him from 1888 to 1916, the longest stint on the front light's roster.[^2] Arthur W. Hopkins followed from 1916 to 1933.[^2]

The sources disagree about the last keeper. LighthouseFriends calls Arthur W. Hopkins the last keeper, and says that after the light was automated, sometime in the early 1930s, he went on living in the dwelling as a custodian, rent-free, until at least 1940.[^1] Both the archive's roster and LighthouseFriends's own keeper list also give Milton A. Duffield as head keeper from 1933 to 1943.[^2][^1] The two sources do not explain how those accounts fit together.

In 1948 the archive lists Arthur W. Davis for that year alone, and then Glen J. Loberger from 1948 to 1949.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives the first of these men as William A. Davis.[^1]

The rear light had its own line of keepers. William L. Heal served from 1876 to 1877, and Jacob W. Wallace from 1877 to 1887.[^3] Bankson T. Holcomb kept the rear light for eighteen years, from 1887 to 1905.[^3] Thomas Wright served from 1905 to 1906, and George W. Duncan from 1906 to 1919.[^3] Joseph M. Harrison (1919 to 1926) and John W. Hill (1926 to 1933) complete the list, and LighthouseFriends prints the same names.[^1][^3]

The Board's grounds work shows how the stations were kept up. In the year to 30 June 1900 the front station received about 800 square feet of sod and about 96 ornamental trees and shrubs. The rear station received about 76 trees and shrubs.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Between midnight and one in the morning on 15 February 1877 the steamship Ontario ran ashore off the station, the keeper's log says.[^1] The log records the work of getting her off. On 17 February the log says the three Philadelphia ice boats, "after working faithfully at each high tide, failed to move her." On 19 February her cargo was taken off, and one of the ice boats towed her up the river. On 20 February the log has her passing down the river, outward bound.[^1]

The Board's report for the year to 30 June 1894 says that at the front light the sea wall was rebuilt, and jetties were thrown out to protect it.[^4] Two years later, in the year to 30 June 1896, storms had torn down about 90 feet (27 m) of the front station's jetties, and they were rebuilt.[^6]

The lights were discontinued from 3 May to 26 July 1898. LighthouseFriends suggests the pause was a precaution during the Spanish-American War.[^1]

When the United States entered the First World War, the river was put under new rules. The Evening Journal of Wilmington reported on 9 April 1917 that navigation on the river must stop between set hours, and that the first regulations for shipping out of the Philadelphia port since the declaration of war had been issued. Patrol boats were to be stationed for inward and outward traffic at the points where the New Castle range crossed the Finns Point and Deep Water Point ranges.[^14]

A Lighthouse Service bulletin of September 1929 recalled a fire that had started at the rear light some years before. A paint burner had been used on the outside of the frame dwelling. About two hours after the day's work ended, smoke was seen, and it was due to a fire inside the wall behind the siding. The bulletin says a serious fire was avoided only with some difficulty.[^13]

## After the keepers

The lamps changed several times. In 1914 the rear light's illuminant changed from oil to incandescent oil vapor, and in 1917 the front light's was changed the same way.[^9][^10] In 1923 the front light was electrified. A small generator and storage battery in the lower story of the tower supplied a locomotive headlight that marked the range in the upper part of the tower, and a standard lens lantern below it, which showed sectors marking obstructions and safe water near New Castle and Delaware City.[^11] The rear light was electrified in 1925.[^12]

LighthouseFriends says the front light was automated in the early 1930s. In 1953 the Coast Guard put the front property up for auction, and the high bidders paid $5,000. LighthouseFriends notes that this was less than the government had paid for the land decades earlier.[^1] The buyers' family has lived in the dwelling since, and LighthouseFriends says five generations have occupied it.[^1]

The archive's record for the front station says the original tower was demolished and replaced with a modern steel skeletal tower in 1964, and that the keepers' house survives.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the replacement to the 1960s.[^1]

The rear light was replaced by an automated beacon atop a 110-foot (34 m) skeleton tower. The combined tower and dwelling was then declared excess and sold to the public.[^1] The archive's rear record gives 1964 as the year the rear light was discontinued.[^3] The new owner rented it out, and later it was used as a meeting hall. After years of neglect and a fire that reportedly damaged the roof, it was condemned. In November 1982, after the owner hired the Goodwill Fire Company of New Castle to do it, the structure was burned to the ground.[^1]

## Today

Both current towers still mark the channel for vessels, LighthouseFriends says.[^1] The front light is a steel tower that shows its light 56 feet (17 m) above mean high water. It stands on Coast Guard property surrounded by private land, next to a private house. The grounds, the dwelling and the tower are closed.[^1] A historic well, a privy and a brick oil house also remain at the front station.[^1] The rear house is gone. The Coast Guard's skeleton tower stands on that site, and the archive records the rear station as not open to the public.[^1][^3]

Visitors should keep off the private land at both sites. The towers are not open for climbing.[^1][^2][^3]
