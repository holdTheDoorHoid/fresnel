North Brother Island Light was a wooden tower on the southern tip of North Brother Island, in the East River between the Bronx and Rikers Island. It was first lit in 1869 and discontinued in 1953. The archive records that the tower was removed from the dwelling and replaced with a skeletal light in 1953, and that the dwelling collapsed.[^2] The island is closed to the public today.[^2][^9]

## The place

The island had little use before the light went up. Vanity Fair, reprinting an interview with the photographer Christopher Payne, says the Dutch navigator Adriaen Block named North Brother and its smaller sibling, South Brother, while exploring the Atlantic coast between 1611 and 1614. The same piece says the island seems to have had no formal use until the lighthouse was built in 1869.[^9]

The light was meant for dangerous water. LighthouseFriends says the northern approach to Hell Gate had seen many shipwrecks, and that the New York superintendent of lights made a light there a priority.[^1] Congress approved $5,000 in 1829 for a light on the Brothers, but the island's owner, Edward Ackerson, would not sell. The money was returned to the treasury after the state did not act. Later appropriations followed, and title to the southern tip was finally acquired in 1868.[^1]

From the 1880s the island also held a city hospital. LighthouseFriends says a fence went up in 1883 to separate the lighthouse from Riverside Hospital, which the City of New York ran for infectious diseases.[^1] Vanity Fair says that from the 1880s through the 1930s the island was used mainly as a quarantine hospital, and that Typhoid Mary died there in 1938.[^9]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 proposed a beacon at the island's north end, where a light would mark the channel between North Brother, South Brother and Barretto Point. The report noted that such a site would need no expense and no delay "in obtaining cession of jurisdiction."[^3]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says Congress appropriated $8,500 in 1866 and $7,500 in 1867. The design was a two-story dwelling with a mansard roof and an octagonal tower that rose from the front of the house to about fifty feet (15 m) above the river.[^1] The archive gives the tower as wood, 38 feet (12 m) tall, with the light 46.5 feet (14 m) above high water.[^2]

The first lens is a point of disagreement. LighthouseFriends says the original beacon was a sixth-order lens showing a fixed white light, first shown in November 1869.[^1] The archive lists a fourth-order Fresnel lens installed in 1869.[^2] The Board's report for 1900 describes a fifth-order lens being replaced by a fourth-order lens in 1899, which shows what was in use that year but not what was there in 1869.[^6]

LighthouseFriends says a fog bell was established at the station in April 1889.[^1]

In 1894 the Board recorded a new lens with an occulting clock, a new bell tower and a Gamewell fog-bell apparatus.[^4] LighthouseFriends dates the change in the light's character to 15 May 1894, from a steady white light to five seconds of light and five seconds of darkness.[^1]

The Board's report for 1900 records that the intensity of the light was increased on 29 November 1899.[^5] The island's brick oil house was built in the fiscal year 1901.[^7]

## The keepers

The archive's roster begins with Finlay Fraser, 1869 to 1871, and Daniel Kelly, 1871 to 1890. Arthur Donnelly (1890) and John Joyce (1890 to 1892) followed, and Samuel Wright held the post from 1892 to 1902.[^2]

Joseph D. Meade was appointed keeper in 1902, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1][^2] On 5 January 1903 he left the island to visit his mother in New York City. Police found his boat tied at a city dock, and the authorities concluded that foul play was most likely involved. His body was never found.[^1]

John F.P. Jacobi was keeper from 1903 to 1906, and LighthouseFriends says he witnessed the disaster of June 1904 described below.[^1][^2]

Axel Douglas Hallgren kept the light from 1906 to 1930, the longest closed term on the archive's principal line.[^2] William J. Murray held it from 1930 to 1946, and Albert G. Possell's entry on the archive runs from 1946 without an end date.[^2] The roster's last entries, from the early 1960s, include Edward P. Dolshun, Jesse R. Pope and several Coast Guard names.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In June 1904 the excursion steamer General Slocum burned close to the island. The New York Tribune's front page of 16 June 1904 called it "the burning of the General Slocum near North Brother Island yesterday morning," and described a Sunday-school excursion aboard.[^8] LighthouseFriends says the steamer left its New York dock on 15 June 1904 for a church picnic on Eatons Neck, and that smoke began pouring from her bow as she passed Hell Gate.[^1] The Tribune placed the start of the fire when she was passing Sunken Meadows.[^8] Either way, Captain Van Schaick took the burning boat toward North Brother Island.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he ran her aground in a small cove on the far side of the island, and that doctors and nurses from the island's hospital came to the scene to help.[^1]

The Tribune's headline gave "Possibly Seven Hundred" as the toll, and its text estimated, unofficially, that more than seven hundred had perished.[^8] LighthouseFriends says more than 1,000 passengers died that day.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1953 the light was moved onto the station's metal fog-bell tower. The lantern room and the top of the old tower were taken away.[^1][^2] The archive records the dwelling as collapsed.[^2] Vanity Fair says the island's last institutional use, a juvenile drug-treatment center, closed in 1963, and that the island was abandoned from then.[^9]

LighthouseFriends says the station's fog bell is displayed at the New York City Police Department's Harbor Unit at College Point, as a memorial to officers who died in the line of duty.[^1]

## Today

The island is closed to the public.[^2][^9] Vanity Fair says the island became a bird sanctuary after the facility closed in the 1960s, and that the New York City Parks Department oversees it today.[^9] Visitors should not try to land on the island.

Little of the station is left to see. LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse has collapsed, and only a glimpse of its roof shows above the growth on the island.[^1] Payne describes the island's buildings as very dilapidated, with few artifacts left to show how they were used.[^9]
