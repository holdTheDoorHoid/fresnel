Fort Washington Light is a wooden fog-bell tower on the Potomac River in Maryland, at the fort of the same name.[^1][^2] A light has been shown from this spot since 1857.[^1] From 1901 it was carried on the bell tower, and in 2020 the Coast Guard took the light off the tower.[^1][^2][^3][^8]

## The place

The Potomac was the waterway into the new capital, and Fort Washington was finished in 1824 to defend it.[^1] Hazards in the river could do serious damage to a ship.[^1] The National Park Service says the channel for large ships on the river must come within 50 yards (46 m) of the shoreline.[^1]

In 1856 the Treasury Secretary, James Guthrie, asked for a light to be placed on Digges Point, and the Secretary of War, Jefferson Davis, agreed on conditions.[^1] The light was to stand on the wharf, not inside the fortifications, and its keeper was to answer to the post commander.[^1][^2][^8] The Lighthouse Board appropriated $500 for the light.[^1][^8]

## Building the light

The first light was an 18.5-foot (5.6 m) iron post, placed on 7 November 1857.[^1][^8] The USLHS archive gives 1857 for the original sixth-order Fresnel lens.[^3] The NPS places the sixth-order lens in the 1870 tower instead.[^1]

In 1870 the Board replaced the post with a 16-foot (4.9 m) tower set nearer the water, at the end of the wharf.[^1][^8] LighthouseFriends says the tower was built at the Lazaretto lighthouse depot and carried to the fort by the tender Tulip in February 1870.[^8] A small addition in 1874 gave the keeper a room to stay in during stormy weather.[^8]

LighthouseFriends says a 32-foot (9.8 m) fog-bell tower went up in 1882, and that the keeper's dwelling was completed in January 1885.[^8] The NPS place page says the current tower was built in 1889 as the fog bell tower.[^2] The two dates disagree, and neither source explains the difference.

In 1900 the Light-House Board reported that the lantern was small and its ventilation poor.[^4] It wanted the tower built 6 to 8 feet (1.8 to 2.4 m) higher, so the light could show above a structure the War Department had just put up at the post, and it estimated a new tower at about $1,600.[^4] The money was never granted.[^8] In 1901 the Board put the light on the fog-bell tower instead, and the old 1870 tower was demolished.[^2][^8]

## The keepers

Ordnance Sergeant Joseph Cameron was the first keeper, from 1857 to 1869.[^1][^3][^8] The NPS says he had joined the Army in 1806 and served 66 years, 37 of them at Fort Washington, where he died in 1872.[^1]

His successor, Edward Kelly, asked for a raise.[^8] LighthouseFriends says the superintendent recommended lifting the keeper's pay from $180 to $300 a year, and Kelly got it.[^8] Two years later he asked for $480 and threatened to resign.[^8] He left the station in 1872.[^8]

Keeper Daniel McCahan was reprimanded in 1890 for failing to salute a Lighthouse Service tender and for being out of uniform.[^8] In 1896 he was judged unable to do the work, and Samuel Jones replaced him, according to LighthouseFriends.[^8] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1896 a brick cistern was built to give the keeper drinking water.[^5]

William S. Stinchcomb was keeper from 1900 to 1919 and Gary E. Powell from 1919 to 1925.[^3] LighthouseFriends says Powell was moved to Cedar Point Lighthouse in 1925.[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports that Secretary Hoover commended keeper Gary E. Powell for helping, on 2 January 1922, a gasoline boat of the Army's Quartermaster Department that had sunk in the Potomac, and its two occupants.[^7] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report, in a bound volume covering 1915 to 1922, also has Powell helping float the gas boat Lillian, which had run onto submerged piling near the fort.[^6]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the light was electrified on 1 February 1920. In 1921 an electric striker made the fog bell sound one blow every fifteen seconds.[^8]

In 1946 the fort passed to the Interior Department, but the light station stayed under the Coast Guard.[^1] In 1948 the station comprised the fog-bell tower with its light, a seven-room keeper's house, a timber dock and a boathouse.[^1][^8] In 1950 it was recommended that the dwellings go to the Park Service, with the tower kept by the Coast Guard.[^1]

The keepers' service ended in 1954, when the light became an unmanned automatic flashing red light.[^1][^2] In the late 1970s the fog bell's mechanism broke, ending the ringing.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the tower was turned over to the National Park Service in 2005, and that the Park Service's Historic Preservation Training Center restored it in 2009.[^8] Ongoing maintenance is by the Chesapeake Chapter of the U.S. Lighthouse Society.[^8] In 2020 the Coast Guard removed the triangle red marker and the light from the tower and moved them out to the river, ending more than a century of active duty.[^2][^8]

## Today

The tower remains in its original place, in Fort Washington Park. The light and bell tower is all that is left of the station.[^1][^2] The NPS lists the lighthouse as not open to the public, and the USLHS archive says the same.[^2][^3] The NPS page says the lighthouse is due for restoration in 2023 and 2024, and does not say whether that work has been done.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^8] Visitors walk from the park's parking area down to the river bank to see the tower.[^8]
