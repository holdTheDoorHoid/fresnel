Whitehead Light marks the east end of Whitehead Island, a rocky island at the western mouth of Penobscot Bay in Knox County, Maine.[^1][^6] The station was established in 1804, the third oldest in Maine,[^1] and the granite tower seen today dates from 1852.[^1] It was automated in 1982,[^1] and it now belongs to a summer camp that runs adult programs there.[^2][^3]

## The place

The Maine Historic Preservation Commission's National Register nomination places the station at the west entrance to Penobscot Bay, where its light guided shipping toward Rockland, Camden, Belfast and Searsport, ports that depended for decades on the sea.[^1] The case for a light was made early. LighthouseFriends reports a petition of 1798 from sea captains, who wrote that about two hundred coasting vessels of fifty to one hundred and twenty tons used the bay about once a week through nine months of the year.[^3]

Muscle Ridge Channel ran from Whitehead Light Station on the south to Owls Head on the north. An estimate in the Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for 1920 describes the channel as bordered on both sides by dangerous ledges and submerged rocks, hard to navigate at night and in thick weather, and busy with passenger traffic in summer. It adds that records kept by the life-saving station at Whitehead showed more than seventy accidents in the channel and its neighbourhood over fifteen years; the scanned text gives the last year as 1935, which cannot be right in a report of 1920.[^4]

## Building the light

The first light was built by contract. In 1803 Benjamin Beal and Duncan W. Thaxter won a $2,150 contract for an octagonal wooden tower on a stone foundation and a wood-frame dwelling, according to LighthouseFriends, and Ellis Dolph was appointed keeper in June 1804 at $200 a year.[^3] The sources do not agree on the starting date. The National Register nomination says the station was established in 1804,[^1] the US Lighthouse Society's archive and the Lighthouse Digest database both give 1807,[^2][^5] yet the Society's keeper roster starts with Dolph in 1804.[^2]

LighthouseFriends reports that a $6,000 contract went to Jeremiah Berry in 1831 to replace the worn wooden buildings with split, undressed stone, a tower 29 feet (8.8 m) high.[^3] Fog was a standing hazard; LighthouseFriends calls Penobscot Bay notorious for dense fogs, especially in summer. A fog bell and its clockwork, which had to be wound every six hours, were put up in 1830, and in 1838 an inventor's bell driven by the rise and fall of the tide was installed; LighthouseFriends reports that when I. W. P. Lewis visited in 1842 he judged the tide-powered scheme "a complete failure", and that Keeper Joshua Bartlett had gone back to ringing the bell by hand.[^3]

The present tower came in 1852. The nomination calls it a replacement of the original structure,[^1] and attributes its design to the architect Alexander Parris, on the strength of its resemblance to the 1851 tower at Monhegan, while admitting that documentary proof had not been found.[^1] LighthouseFriends states the attribution without that caveat.[^3] A third-order Fresnel lens replaced lamps and reflectors in 1855 according to the US Lighthouse Society's archive and LighthouseFriends;[^2][^3] the Lighthouse Digest database gives 1857.[^5]

A reliable fog signal arrived with a ten-inch steam whistle in 1869, according to LighthouseFriends;[^3] the nomination dates it 1869-70 and cites it as part of what earned the station its "secondary sea-coast light" classification.[^1] The brick fog signal building appears to date from 1888, when the Light-House Board's report mentioned a new boilerhouse and cistern.[^1] In 1894 the Board's report records a wooden water tank of about 4,000 gallons set up for the fog signal.[^6] In 1891 the old rubble-stone keeper's dwelling was replaced with the present frame double house on its old foundation, and a brick oil house and a service room at the tower were added.[^1] A second assistant keeper appears on the roster from 1895; in 1899 the Board noted that the tank house had been rebuilt to give him temporary quarters, and Congress appropriated $3,400 for a keeper's dwelling.[^2][^7] The Board's next report says the temporary dwelling was converted into a permanent one.[^8]

## The keepers

The first keeper did not last. LighthouseFriends reports that Ellis Dolph was dismissed in 1807, after the local superintendent of lights learned that he had been selling the station's oil.[^3] In 1816 Rebecca Otis wrote to the customs collector that her husband, Keeper Ebenezer Otis, was gravely ill, and asked that she and her fourteen-year-old son be allowed to look after the light if he died. He died that June, and Charles Haskell was appointed in his place.[^3]

By LighthouseFriends's account, Joshua Bartlett counted 2,397 vessels passing in three months of 1842, and with no cistern or well on the station he gathered fresh water from hollows in the rocks after rain, and went to the mainland when that ran short.[^3] Hezekiah Long kept the light from 1865 to 1872 and again from 1873 to 1875, with his daughter Abbie H. Long on the roster as first assistant.[^2] LighthouseFriends adds that he was a disabled Civil War veteran and that she helped him, and that the keeper was paid an extra five dollars a month to haul coal across the island from the dock.[^3]

The best-known family arrived in 1875. Isaac H. Grant became head keeper, and his wife Abbie E. Burgess Grant, already known for keeping the light on Matinicus Rock when a storm delayed her father's return, became first assistant; both are listed through 1890.[^2][^3][^9] The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists her salary at Whitehead as $480 and gives her reason for leaving as "Resigned".[^9] LighthouseFriends, drawing on a recollection by Clara Norton, daughter of the life-saving station's keeper, says Captain Grant taught school to the island's children for six to eight weeks each winter, and that pupils were told not to turn up on inspection days.[^3] When Horace Norton left the island in 1885, Grant bought a cart and a donkey named Jack to haul coal, the same source says, and a local newspaper noticed the animal.[^3]

Frank N. Jellison, first assistant from 1890, was head keeper from 1892 to 1905, and Elmer Reed, who had been first assistant from 1902, followed him until 1919.[^2] An August item in the 1914 volume of the Lighthouse Service Bulletin credits Reed with recovering a motor boat that was drifting near the station.[^10] After him the roster runs through Arthur B. Mitchell (1919 to 1929) and Arthur J. Beal (1929 to 1950); Frank William Alley was first assistant from 1928 and George L. Alley second assistant from 1926 to 1945.[^2] Coast Guard men are listed from 1945.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the morning of 7 August 1881 two men from the schooner Vicksburgh of Bangor went out in a yawl from their vessel, anchored in Seal Harbor, in dense fog and a heavy sea, and capsized about a mile east of the light. The Life-Saving Service's report says they clung to the bottom for three hours while the ebb tide carried them out, and that "Their cries for help were drowned by the roar of the waves". When the fog lifted, Keeper Grant saw them, sent his daughter with the alarm to the life-saving station about a mile away, and with his son Frank launched his own boat; so rough was the sea beyond the lee of the island that he threw over sail and ballast to stop the boat swamping. The two men were too weak to climb in and had to be lifted aboard. The Service awarded Grant its silver medal.[^11]

The life-saving station's crew figures in three further entries for November 1880. The Service's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1881 records the schooner Addie Ryerson, bound for Pembroke with iron ore, stranding on the island's northeast point in a gale on 11 November 1880 after her captain tried a seldom-used passage; the station's patrolman saw her and signalled with a red light.[^12] On 20 November the Camden schooner Helen Maria dragged her anchors onto the same point and the crew hove her off on the rising tide.[^12] On the night of 30 November the becalmed schooner Loella drifted near the light, and the station crew towed her into Seal Harbor.[^12]

Weather reached the station too. On 11 January 1891, according to the Light-House Board, an unusually high tide and storm lifted the superstructure of the bridge off its foundations; it was rebuilt.[^13] The steam whistle ran 2,243 hours in the fiscal year ended June 1891, 2,324 hours in the year to June 1892 and 2,272 in the year to June 1894.[^13][^6] In its 1894 report the Board noted that the keeper of Pumpkin Island light-station heard the Whitehead whistle at a distance of 20 miles in a north-east snowstorm, and at no other time.[^14] On 11 August 1896 the whistle's signal was changed to blasts of three seconds separated by alternating silences of nine and forty-five seconds.[^15]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists Whitehead among the lights changed to incandescent oil vapor in 1913.[^16] LighthouseFriends reports that in 1933 oil-engine air compressors and an air tyfon replaced the steam whistle, with generators installed in the fog signal building.[^3] The station was automated in 1982.[^1][^2] The two dwellings were then boarded up, and LighthouseFriends and the station owner's history both say the head keeper's house was torn down in the mid-1980s; public protest at that and similar losses is credited with the creation of the Maine Lights Program.[^3][^17] Pine Island Camp, a nonprofit that had long been on the island, was granted the station in 1997, and it has been renovating the buildings since 1998.[^17]

## Today

The tower is still an active aid to navigation operated by the Coast Guard, while Pine Island Camp owns and manages the station.[^2][^17] The camp runs adult programs at the station, which LighthouseFriends says has been the case since 2009,[^3] and its programs leave from Emery's Wharf on the mainland.[^17] The Lighthouse Digest database notes that the station is on a 90-acre island with three owners, and that most of it is held by the Swan family under a conservation easement.[^5] LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds, dwelling and tower are closed, and that the light is best seen from the water, while the Lighthouse Digest database lists the grounds as open to the public; the sources disagree, so a visitor should ask the camp before planning a landing.[^3][^5] The island lies about a mile off the mainland.[^5] The third-order Fresnel lens is on display at the Maine Lighthouse Museum in Rockland.[^3]
