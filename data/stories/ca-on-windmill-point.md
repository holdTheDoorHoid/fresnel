Windmill Point Lighthouse is a stone tower on the St. Lawrence River, just east of Prescott, Ontario. It began as a windmill, became a lighthouse in 1872, and is remembered as much for the battle fought around it in November 1838 as for its light.[^1][^2]

## The place

A stone windmill stood on the point, in the small community of Newport. LighthouseFriends says a West Indian merchant, Thomas Hughes, built it about 1830, and that it became the most prominent feature of the village.[^1] The site is now the Battle of the Windmill National Historic Site.[^1][^5] Parks Canada describes it as "a windswept hill, overlooking the St. Lawrence River".[^5] The federal register calls the mill a gristmill, one of the few wind-powered mills in English Canada. It gives no date for the mill.[^2]

The Department's annual report for 1873 explains how the tower came into the lighthouse service.[^1] As LighthouseFriends quotes it, the report begins: "Numerous applications had been made to this Department from time to time for a lighthouse at Windmill Point, near Prescott, in the River St. Lawrence".[^1] The sources do not name a hazard the light was built to mark. What they record is the request.

## Building the light

The Department bought the stone tower for $600, with a right of way to the river and from the main road, and fitted it out as a light. The report gives the total cost of fitting it out, together with the tower and land, as $3,266.27.[^1]

The light was a white fixed light on the catoptric principle, with four mammoth flat-wick lamps and 16-inch reflectors.[^1] The tower is 18.9 m (62 ft) high. The report says the light could be seen about 15 miles, without saying whether that means nautical miles.[^1]

The register describes walls of coursed rubble-stone about 0.9 metre (three feet) thick at the base. It says an octagonal cast-iron lantern was added when the tower became a light.[^2]

The register dates the conversion to 1872.[^2] The 1873 report says the light was "exhibited for the first time on the 15th day of June last".[^1] Read as June 1872, the report agrees with the register.

## The keepers

Bernard Kean was appointed keeper on 31 May, at a salary of $150 a year.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he was often praised as an excellent keeper, and that he resigned in 1881 at the age of seventy-two.[^1]

Samuel Robert Scarlot succeeded him and resigned after just over a year. LighthouseFriends' narrative calls him Samuel Scarlot.[^1] Ward Skinner Plumb kept the light from 1882 until 1901. In that year the minister of Marine and Fisheries recommended that "in the public interest the appointment of Mr. Plumb be cancelled".[^1] John Fraser was made keeper in 1901 and served until at least 1903.[^1]

The sources do not say where these men came from, or what became of their families.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the fall of 1838, Hunter leaders in northern New York "decided to invade Upper Canada and chose Prescott as their point of attack".[^1] The Hunters were a secret paramilitary organisation. Many rebels had fled to the United States after the rising in Upper Canada in 1837, and helped set it up.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says roughly 250 Hunters landed at Windmill Point in the early morning of 12 November. Their hope that local people would join them was not met. Nearly 2,000 local militiamen and British soldiers surrounded them.[^1] The fighting went on until 16 November, when a large force, with armed steamers on the river, attacked the windmill. Many Hunters fought to the death, and most of the rest surrendered or fled under cover of darkness.[^1]

The register says the tower "was the scene of an important battle against Americans who supported the Rebellion". British troops occupied it for a year afterwards, and milling there ceased permanently.[^2]

The newspapers do not agree on the numbers, and their accounts are partial. The Daily Herald of New Haven printed on 21 November a letter from the Prescott postmaster, A. Jones, dated 13 November. It says one of the schooners "landed from 100 to 150 men" at the windmill, one and a half miles below the village, and that the invaders began "throwing up an entrenchment".[^3] A correspondent of the Courier & Enquirer, writing from the steamer Telegraph off Ogdensburg on 14 November, described the invaders "occupying a strong stone mill", a little below Prescott.[^3]

Dr. A. Trowbridge, Jr., of Watertown, an eyewitness to the fighting, said that when he left Ogdensburg on the evening of 14 November the Patriot force was "confined to the windmill and adjoining stone buildings, in number from 100 to 150".[^3] On the back of a proclamation from J. Ward Birge, sent from Clayton, New York, the same paper printed a brief note dated 16 November. It said the Patriot force embodied at Windmill Point, below Prescott, were "all killed or taken prisoners".[^3]

The Phenix of Bristol, Rhode Island, printed on 24 November a Sackett's Harbor Journal extra, which reported news brought by the steamboat Oneida from Ogdensburg. It gave "About 500 patriots and 300 British regulars" as the forces engaged.[^4]

The figures do not agree. LighthouseFriends gives about 250 Hunters, the Prescott postmaster and Dr. Trowbridge give 100 to 150 at the windmill, and the Phenix gives about 500 patriots. None of the accounts confirms another.[^1][^3][^4]

## After the keepers

The light got a triple-mantle burner through the installation of one in 1922, and was electrified in 1928, LighthouseFriends says.[^1] It stayed in service "until 1978, when it was discontinued".[^1] The site's national historic designation is dated 1920 by LighthouseFriends and 1923 by the register.[^1][^2] In 1989 the lighthouse was declared a Classified Federal Heritage Building, and in 2013 the federal government designated it a heritage lighthouse.[^1]

The Friends of Windmill Point opened the lighthouse to the public in 1996.[^1] In 2004 Parks Canada repaired cracks that had worsened after an earthquake two years earlier.[^1] In 2015 the lighthouse was closed so that its roof could be repaired, with help from a $373,000 federal grant.[^1]

## Today

Parks Canada owns the tower, which stands within the Battle of the Windmill National Historic Site.[^1][^5] The site is "closed from September 2026 to May 2027 and will reopen in June 2027", so the tower is not open to visitors at present.[^5] When the site is open, its visitor page lists hours of 10 a.m. to 5 p.m. on Saturdays and Sundays in June, and Thursday to Monday in July and August. Admission is "by donation".[^5]

LighthouseFriends describes the grounds as open and the tower as open in season. That page may be out of date, given the current closure.[^1][^5]
