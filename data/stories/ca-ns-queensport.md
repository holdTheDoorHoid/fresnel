The Queensport light stands on Rook Island, a small island at the mouth of Queensport harbour on Chedabucto Bay, on Nova Scotia's Eastern Shore.[^1][^2][^4][^5] A light was first shown there in 1882.[^1][^4] The lighthouse on the island dates from 1936 according to the register, or 1937 according to the Nova Scotia list. It is a two-storey wooden house with a square lantern on its roof, and it has been a heritage lighthouse since 2015.[^2][^3][^4]

## The place

LighthouseFriends places Queensport on the southern shore of Chedabucto Bay, about halfway between Canso and Guysborough.[^1] The first settlers were fishermen, drawn by the herring, cod and mackerel in the nearby waters.[^1] After the American Revolution, loyalists from the third and fourth battalions of the 60th Regiment of Foot received land grants at Queensport.[^1]

The harbour is protected by Brodie Point, and Rook Island lies roughly in the middle of the harbour entrance.[^1] The register of historic places says the first lighthouse was built "in response to the booming fishery that operated out of Queensport harbour."[^3]

The place was known as Crow Harbour until 1898. LighthouseFriends says the legislature changed the name that year to honour Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee.[^1] The Department of Marine's report for the year ended 30 June 1901 lists "Name of Crow harbour" as changed to Queensport, among the changes it embodied in notices to mariners.[^6] That is three years after the 1898 date LighthouseFriends gives, and the two dates are not reconciled.

## Building the light

In 1882 James McDonald of Guysborough built a lighthouse on Rook Island: a square, tapered tower 12 m (40 ft) high, attached to a small keeper's dwelling. He also built a boathouse, an oil store and an outhouse, for a contract of $1,429.[^1] The first keeper, John Ehler III, put the light into operation on 25 November 1882.[^1] In 1883 the light was listed as a 6th-order light at the entrance to Crow Harbour, fixed white, 12 m high.[^4]

In 1899 the lighthouse's foundation was cemented, and new stays were fitted to the tower to steady it.[^1]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends counts seventeen keepers, none serving longer than eight years.[^1] The Ehler name appears on three of the keepers' lines (John Ehler III, John Ehler V and William E. Ehler). The sources do not say how those men were related.[^1]

David S. Hensbee kept the light from 1897 to 1902. In 1900 he was supplied with a hand-operated foghorn, which he had to sound in response to vessels' signals during periods of limited visibility.[^1][^7]

The keepers' list includes a married couple. Curtis C. Munroe, a World War I veteran, kept the light from 1932 to 1940, and his wife Lavinia served from 1940 to 1948.[^1] The two are among the three keepers on the list who served eight years, and Lavinia Munroe is the only woman named as keeper.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Department's annual report, dated Ottawa, December 1900, lists the hand fog-horns set up that season at several stations, which are "used whenever the fog signals of vessels are heard." Crow Harbour, Queensport, is one of them.[^7]

## After the keepers

A Notice to Mariners dated 8 December 1955 announced three changes: the light would change from fixed white to flashing white, the hand foghorn would be discontinued, and the light would be unwatched.[^1] A caretaker, Colin Hendsbee, was hired for the automated light, but a resident keeper was soon judged necessary, and Osborne O'Leary was sent to the island in 1956.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Austine R. Creamer was keeping the light when it was de-staffed for good in 1967.[^1] The Nova Scotia list dates automation to 1967.[^4]

The Nova Scotia list gives 1937 for the present building, and notes that it replaced the 1882 tower.[^4] Its history for the first light also has an entry for 1955 to 1962 that says the present tower replaced it. The register gives 1936, and says that in that year the earlier lighthouse "was replaced with a more modern design."[^3] The sources give three different dates for the replacement, and this story does not choose between them.

In April 1968 the boathouse and oil shed were declared surplus. They were sold and removed from the island within two months.[^1] The lighthouse itself was declared surplus in 1979 and offered for sale for $1,000, on condition that the buyer removed it from the island.[^1] A local resident wanted it kept on the island and restored, and the municipal council asked the Department of Transport to spare it.[^1] The Fishermen's Lighthouse Association was formed the same year and took a five-year lease on the property, to develop the island as a historic site and park. The municipality has maintained the lighthouse since then.[^1]

The lighthouse was placed on the Canadian Register of Historic Places in 2005, and was recognised as a heritage lighthouse on 12 February 2015.[^1][^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard list gives the light as flashing white every four seconds ("Fl W 4s"), on Rook Island, Chedabucto Bay.[^5] The register lists its current function as a "Historic or Interpretive Site."[^2] The Nova Scotia list gives site access as by boat.[^4]

Sources disagree on who owns the island and the light. The Nova Scotia list gives the Municipality of Guysborough as owner.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, but its own history says the municipality has maintained it since 1979, so this story treats the municipality as the working owner.[^1] LighthouseFriends' visitor note says the grounds are open and the tower closed, and it describes a roadside pullout on Route 16 towards Canso with a good view of the light.[^1] A group called Keepers of the Beacon is working to preserve the lighthouse.[^1]
