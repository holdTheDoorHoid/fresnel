Romer Shoal Light is a brown-and-white cast-iron tower standing in about 14 feet (4 m) of water in Lower New York Bay, north of Sandy Hook, New Jersey: the National Register nomination puts it 3.8 miles (6 km) out, while Lighthouse Friends gives 2.4 miles (4 km) from the hook's northern tip.[^1][^2] It marks a shoal beside the main approach to the port of New York, and it began life as a testing tower on Staten Island.[^1]

## The place

Romer Shoal is a patch of shallow water north of the Swash Channel, a natural route into New York harbor, and south of the Ambrose Channel, the dredged main shipping lane completed around 1912.[^1] The National Register nomination says it is unclear how the shoal got its name, though the name was in use by 1837.[^1] Lighthouse Friends reports that the common story, a pilot boat called *William J. Romer* lost in 1863, cannot be right, because a daymark stood on "Romer's Shoal" in 1838; it points instead to an 1870 note in *The Historical Magazine* naming Colonel Wolfgang William Romer, who sounded New York Bay in 1700.[^2]

Congress appropriated $15,000 in 1837 and a further $10,000 in 1838 for a day beacon, a granite stone cone with a wooden mast and case on top.[^1] Lighthouse Friends adds that two naval officers complained the beacon was being built in the wrong place, and that in 1842 I. W. P. Lewis testified that he had designed it and that it stood in eleven feet of water on the northwest end of the shoal rather than on the southeast end, where it would have helped more.[^2] The nomination says the granite settled unevenly and that by the early 1880s a replacement was needed.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1883, Lighthouse Friends reports, the owners of several steamships petitioned the Light-House Board for a light on the shoal, citing "the long list of vessels wrecked and seriously injured thereupon."[^2] The first light was an iron skeleton tower built in 1886 on the old granite foundation, with a compressed-gas lamp that, Lighthouse Friends says, could burn for up to ninety days with no keeper.[^1][^2] The nomination calls its lens a fifth-order Fresnel showing a fixed red light, while Arnold Burges Johnson's 1890 description of the "lighted beacon" gives a fixed white fifth-order light.[^1][^5] Johnson described the structure as an iron pier 30 feet (9 m) across and 16 feet (5 m) high.[^5] Gas tanks had to be carried out to it by water; one Light-House Board report lists ten days spent filling them in a single year.[^12][^4]

In May 1898 the Light-House Board decided to replace the beacon with a manned light, because the planned Ambrose Channel would bring ships close to the shoal.[^1] It chose to reuse a tower built in 1883 at the Tompkinsville Lighthouse Depot on Staten Island as a testing platform for equipment and lamp fuels.[^1] The board's report says the old light was discontinued as a wartime measure on 28 April 1898 and relit on 1 August; the removal of the old tower and erection of the new one was completed on 14 September 1898; and a fourth-order lens light flashing white every four seconds was established on 1 October.[^4] A 1,300-pound bell was installed as the fog signal.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates the bell's first striking to 20 February 1899.[^2]

The result, in the nomination's words, is a "sparkplug" lighthouse: a cast-iron caisson filled with concrete rising 10 feet (3 m) above the sea, carrying a four-story conical tower with the keepers' quarters inside it, the top of the lantern about 60 feet (18 m) up, and the light 54 feet (16 m) above the water.[^1] The caisson is painted black, the tower's lower half white and the upper half brown.[^1]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive, drawing on the keepers' registers, lists Albert H. Porter as the first principal keeper, from 1898 to 1901, followed by Charles W. Oliver (1901-1904).[^3] William F. Rhodes, who held the post from 1914 to 1916, was the seventh head keeper by Lighthouse Friends' count.[^2][^3] Henry R. McCarthy served as first assistant from 1910 until May 1920, then as head keeper for a few months that year.[^3] George E. Durfee was head keeper from 1926 to 1934 and Herman F. Westgate from 1934 to 1946.[^3] The archive records a long run of short assistant postings, many lasting only months.[^3]

Several rescues by the station's keepers are recorded. On 9 May 1915, the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports, Rhodes and assistant Vernon H. Stanton rescued two men from a canoe that capsized in a sudden squall near the station; the report says the men would probably have drowned without them.[^6] On a September day that year Rhodes saved two more men upset in a squall, bringing them to the station and giving them dry clothing, food and shelter for the night.[^6] The Bulletin for 1926 records Durfee and first assistant Joseph T. Covo helping a disabled motor boat on 6 July.[^8] A report printed in the Bulletin's 1931 volume has Durfee assisting two men in a small sailboat on 24 November; the passage read gives no year.[^9] Lighthouse Friends adds a 1937 episode in which assistant keepers Alfred Auger and Lester Thompson helped a couple whose propeller had failed, and were thanked by letter for the ropes, tools and hot coffee.[^2]

Lighthouse Friends also tells of 1916, when Rhodes was alone at the station for nearly two weeks after his assistant went ashore for two days' leave and did not return; the station's skiff washed up at Great Kills and the assistant's body was found at Coney Island, identified by his lighthouse buttons.[^2] This account is Lighthouse Friends' only; the sources read here do not name the assistant.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Navy men replaced the keepers for a time. Lighthouse Friends says that after the First World War six Navy signal quartermasters were squeezed into the tower alongside the three keepers, and that in 1920 the Navy took the station over entirely.[^2] The National Register nomination agrees that the Navy staffed it with three quartermasters, to run the light and report on shipping, until control returned to the Lighthouse Service in October 1921.[^1] On 13 November 1920 one of the three drowned. The Bulletin says the sea was too rough for the submarine chaser to deliver supplies directly, and that he was drowned trying to load the station's boat with provisions.[^7] Lighthouse Friends names the man as William Walker and says the chaser's propeller wash swamped his launch; the Bulletin and the nomination name no one.[^2]

The Bulletin describes a serious fatal accident on 16 September 1935 in landing construction materials at the station, which broke what it calls a perfect record of 25 years in the Third Lighthouse District.[^10] Lighthouse Friends says a launch from the tender *Tulip*, overloaded with supplies, sank and five men drowned.[^2] On the night of 18 July 1937, according to the Bulletin, tremors reported to have been an earthquake were felt at the station and at Lloyd Harbor on Long Island Sound.[^11]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over in 1939 and, the nomination says, kept a crew of three until the light was automated in 1966, when the Fresnel lens was replaced by a 190-millimetre acrylic lens.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says four men were posted, three always on duty while the fourth took a week's leave, and quotes a coastguardsman telling a reporter that "About the only excitement we have is running the launch out in heavy seas."[^2] The fog bell was replaced by a diaphone horn, dated 1936 by Lighthouse Friends and "by 1939" by the nomination.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends dates an incandescent oil vapor lamp to 1928; the nomination dates it to 1939.[^1][^2]

After a storm in December 1992 damaged the tower, Lighthouse Friends reports, the Coast Guard considered replacing it with a steel skeleton tower, and the caretaker of Staten Island Lighthouse, Joe Esposito, fought to save it.[^2] The main-gallery awning was removed in 1997.[^1] That year, Lighthouse Friends adds, the submarine power cable was retired and the light was solarized.[^2]

## Today

When the nomination was written in 2006 the tower belonged to the U.S. Coast Guard and worked as an automated aid to navigation, flashing white twice every 15 seconds with a fog signal of a 2-second blast every 15 seconds.[^1] The Coast Guard declared it excess in 2010, and the General Services Administration sold it at auction in 2011 for a winning bid of $90,000.[^2] Lighthouse Friends now describes it as privately owned, with the tower closed, and says Superstorm Sandy in 2012 blew out the windows and doors of its lower two levels and broke chunks off the caisson foundation.[^2] The only access is by boat, and the U.S. Lighthouse Society archive lists it as not open to the public.[^1][^3]
