Huron Island Light stands on the westernmost of eight small, rocky islands in Lake Superior, about three miles off the Upper Peninsula shore of Michigan.[^2][^13] Its granite tower, attached to the keeper's house, was first lit in 1868; the light was automated in 1972 and still shows today.[^2][^1][^13]

## The place

The Huron Islands sit roughly three miles offshore, and the coast here turns: ships bound west for Portage Lake or east for Marquette changed course near them.[^2] A committee that toured the lakes in 1863 wrote that "a light of good size on one of the Huron islands would render valuable service as a coast light," because no light was visible between Marquette and the Portage river light, and the latter was shut off by the islands themselves, as quoted by Lighthouse Friends.[^2]

The islands had already taken ships. Lighthouse Friends tells how the side-wheel steamer *Arctic*, crossing in fog on 29 May 1860, ran onto one of the western islands; passengers and cattle got ashore before she sank, and one island has been called Cattle Island ever since.[^2] The Lighthouse Board asked for money in 1865, and Congress appropriated $17,000 on 28 July 1866.[^2]

## Building the light

The site was the westernmost island, now called Lighthouse Island. Title could not be settled until September 1867, so building waited for the early spring of 1868.[^2] To reach the 103-foot (31 m) summit, the builders cut a road from the shore through the rock, with wooden bridges over the gaps that were made from the island's small birches; the tower and dwelling went up on a levelled site, in rough granite from the island trimmed with limestone at corners, doors and windows.[^2] Granite Island Light, nearby, was built at the same time to the same plans.[^2]

The square tower is nearly 40 feet (12 m) tall and joins the north end of a one-and-a-half-storey dwelling.[^2] A fixed third-and-a-half-order Henry-Lepaute Fresnel lens first showed on 20 October 1868, at a focal plane of about 198 feet (60 m); the U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive gives 197 feet.[^2][^1] Congress added $2,000 on 3 March 1871 for a "proper road" from the boat landing, and the first wooden bridges were replaced then.[^2]

A ten-inch steam whistle was built on the northwest tip of the island in 1876, giving an eight-second blast every minute when visibility was poor; a duplicate followed in 1881.[^2] A rock-and-cement dam meant to hold water for the whistles in 1882 was knocked down by sea and ice the next winter.[^2] The Light-House Board's report records that the water supply for the fog signal was rebuilt and finished in July 1890, and that the north corner of the dwelling had been struck by lightning on the afternoon of 27 June 1890.[^3] The Board's report volume covering 1896 and 1897 lists a new brick oil house with an iron roof, door and shelving, and a repaired landing crib; Lighthouse Friends adds a brick fog-signal building for two boilers in 1898.[^6][^2] The whistle's workload is on record: about 252 hours of steaming and 14 tons of coal in the 1895 report, and about 300 hours and 20 tons in the 1899 report.[^4][^5]

## The keepers

The archive's roster names Abel Hall as the first head keeper, serving 1868 to 1879.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says he had come to the Upper Peninsula with a surveying party in the early 1840s, that the 1860 census listed him as a farmer, and that his sons Charles and Frank helped him as assistants.[^2] The roster agrees: Charles F. Hall is first assistant from 1869, and Frank Hall from 1871 to 1877.[^1] Duncan Cameron followed (1880 to 1891), then Francis Jacker (1892 to 1897) and Charles Schulz (1897 to 1900).[^1]

Frank F. Witte took charge in 1901 and stayed until 1914.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says he had been an assistant at Raspberry Island, Granite Island and Spectacle Reef first, was known as "Big Sneed", and was made a warden of the island's nesting gulls after President Roosevelt named the Huron Islands a bird sanctuary in 1905.[^2] In 1912 he received a Lighthouse Service efficiency pennant, which Lighthouse Friends describes as the prize for the model station of the district.[^12][^2]

Most assistants did not stay long: the roster shows many second assistants for a year or less, among them Fred Koehler in 1905 and William H.M. Murphy in 1912.[^1] Later long stays include James E. Collins as head keeper from 1935 to 1951 and Martin A. Petersen as an assistant from 1932 to 1944.[^1] The roster's Coast Guard lines run from 1955 to 1970, with head keepers changing nearly every year in the 1960s.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A newspaper of 8 September 1905 reported that the keeper on Huron Island had seen a large schooner founder four miles north of the light, and "had no means of rendering assistance" in the sea that was running.[^9] The report does not name the keeper; Witte held the post that year.[^1]

The worst day on record is 30 April 1909, when the schooner barge *George C. Nester* broke from her tow, the steamer *Schoolcraft*, and was driven onto Lighthouse Island.[^8][^7][^2] The *L'Anse Sentinel* of 8 May 1909 reprinted the Marquette *Mining Journal*, which quoted Witte as saying a snowstorm and a sixty-mile-an-hour gale drove spray 150 feet over the light, and that "about 11:45 Friday morning I heard the distress whistle of a vessel."[^8] He ran toward the sound and saw the barge. The paper reckoned it "not more than five minutes" from his first sight of her adrift until she was wreckage and her crew gone.[^8] His efforts to get a line down to the men failed; the gale blew it back upon the rocks.[^8] He was hurt; the *Mining Journal* said a dislocated shoulder and bruised ankle, while Lighthouse Friends gives a broken collarbone and sprained ankle.[^8][^2] The tender *Marigold*, which Lighthouse Friends says was riding out the storm in the island's lee, brought him to Marquette about 8 o'clock Saturday morning.[^8][^2]

Accounts of the loss disagree on details. The Mining Journal and the Daily Kennebec Journal gave seven men lost, while a Washington Herald dispatch gave nine and put the parting of the tow line shortly after midnight rather than in the late morning; the Kennebec Journal, for its part, dated the wreck to Friday night, not Witte's 11:45 in the morning.[^8][^7][^14] The *Sentinel* named only the captain, George Debeau, and said the rest of the crew were not yet known.[^8] Lighthouse Friends adds that on 5 June Witte found a body from the barge floating near the north end of the island and took it to Skanee.[^2]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded two later acts. Its 1916 volume reports that on 1 September, keeper Alexander McLean and first assistant Charles G. Wright rescued a disabled 35-foot launch, the *Petrel*, "at great personal risk," keeping it off the rocks and saving two men aboard.[^10] Its 1918 volume reports that on 1 November, second assistant Michael Nolan took an officer of the stranded steamer *Vulcan* to town for provisions and to report the accident, then returned him to the steamer.[^11]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends reports that an air diaphone replaced the steam whistle in 1928 and a radiobeacon followed in 1929; in 1934 a frame dwelling and generators were added for $5,488; dormers and indoor plumbing came in 1950, and new Coast Guard quarters in 1961.[^2] The station was staffed through 1972 and has been automated since.[^1][^2] It was placed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1975.[^2]

In 1997 the Coast Guard excessed the lighthouse, and it passed to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, which manages the islands as Huron National Wildlife Refuge.[^2] Local citizens formed the Huron Island Lighthouse Preservation Association in 1999, and the group's fundraising paid for a new roof in the summer of 2006.[^2]

## Today

The tower still carries an active light.[^13] The Fish and Wildlife Service says all of the refuge's islands are closed to the public except Lighthouse Island, and that the only way there is by boat.[^13] The refuge's pages do not say whether the tower or house can be entered, so check with the refuge before planning a trip.
