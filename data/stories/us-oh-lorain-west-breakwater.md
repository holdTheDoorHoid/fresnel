Lorain Harbor, where the Black River meets Lake Erie in north-central Ohio, has had a light at the end of its pier since the 1830s.[^1][^21] The first tower's date is disputed: the Lorain library gives 1837, and LighthouseFriends 1836.[^1][^21] The lighthouse that stands there now is a concrete tower on a timber crib at the north end of the West Breakwater. It was completed in 1917 and given its permanent light in 1919.[^1][^6] Keepers and Coast Guardsmen lived in the building until it was closed as a working light in 1966.[^1] Volunteers and a non-profit foundation have since restored it.[^1][^20]

## The place

The harbor the light guards had an entrance 175 feet (53 m) wide in 1838. In that year Lieutenant Charles T. Platt of the U.S. Navy inspected the light at the end of the west pier. Platt's report, as the Lorain library prints it, calls the harbor an excellent one, with room for at least fifty vessels.[^1] He recommended that the pier be raised at least two feet (0.6 m), because the waves had been so violent that people lighting the beacon were washed off the pier three times in one year, and one of them drowned.[^1]

From the 1850s the beacon served both the harbor and the lake. The library says that in 1855 the beacon served as both a harbor light and a lake-shore light.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for 1891 and 1892 gives the commerce there for 1890 as 539,239 tons.[^9]

Congress appropriated $2,600 on 9 May 1836 to establish and build the Black River light-station.[^1] The first tower was a cylindrical brick tower with a lantern room, lit by eight lard-oil lamps.[^1]

## Building the light

The tower was rebuilt of brick and fitted with a fourth-order Fresnel lens in 1857.[^1] In 1875 the Lighthouse Service tore the brick tower down and put a 46-foot (14 m) wooden tower at the end of a new 600-foot (183 m) extension of the pier.[^1] A rear range tower near the shore end of the west pier followed.[^9] Congress appropriated money for it in 1891, and the Board's report records that "a skeleton iron tower was erected by contract."[^9]

The breakwater on which the present tower stands was a federal engineering project. The Army Corps of Engineers began the west breakwater in 1903 and finished the outer harbor west breakwater in 1908.[^1] The library describes the crib that carries the tower, built in 1910, as twenty-two feet (7 m) high and fifty feet (15 m) square; it was floated out to the end of the breakwater and filled with stone so that it sank.[^1] In April 1912 the Lighthouse Service replaced the wooden structures at the breakwater's end with steel skeletal towers topped by acetylene lights.[^1] The Service's bulletin records that two Lorain Harbor lights changed "from oil to acetylene."[^8]

Congress provided $35,000 for a light and fog-signal station on 22 October 1913.[^2] By the fiscal year 1915 a fourth-order lens and diaphone fog-signal apparatus had been ordered.[^2] On 30 June 1916 "the steel framework under contract was well advanced in the shop."[^3] By 30 June 1917 "the concrete structure had been erected," a temporary light was in commission in the new lantern, and $31,546.13 had been spent.[^4] A year later the Commissioner reported the temporary light in operation in the new lantern and the interior still unfinished.[^5] The Commissioner's summary described the light as "at the end of the west breakwater, to mark the entrance to the harbor."[^7]

The permanent light went into commission on 7 April 1919 and the fog signal on 13 May 1919. Spending to 30 June 1919 came to $34,971.54.[^6] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded that the west breakwater light changed from acetylene to incandescent oil vapor, and that its lens was now fourth order.[^10] According to the library, the fog signal gave three blasts in each group, with one group every thirty seconds.[^1]

The library describes the walls as very thick poured concrete, reinforced with steel.[^1]

## The keepers

The first head keeper of the Black River light was Captain Augusta Jones, who served from 1837 to 1841, according to the Lorain library's list of keepers.[^1] The keeper had to make daily trips out to the light during the shipping season.[^1] The longest-serving keeper, James Connolly, served from 1871 until he resigned in 1903 at the age of 75.[^1] The library gives his first name as James; LighthouseFriends gives John.[^1][^21] In 1905 the Lighthouse Board asked for a keeper's dwelling. Its report said the keeper received $600 a year for tending the two range lights and paid $144 a year in rent.[^1] A residence for keepers' families came in 1922, a double house on Augusta Avenue in Lorain, transferred from the United States Shipping Board.[^1]

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's keeper register lists Frank L. Sellman as principal keeper of the new tower from 1919 to 1921, and Olin W. Stevens from 1921 to 1937.[^17] The library's keeper list also gives Sellman and Stevens as first assistants for 1919 to 1921.[^1] In August 1923 Stevens rescued the occupants of an overturned canoe near the light.[^11] On 24 July 1925 he and assistant keeper Ralph L. McGue helped the crew of a Coast Guard boat after an explosion near the lighthouse.[^13] On 14 June 1926 he and Albert Williams, first assistant, rescued two men from a capsized sailboat about half a mile (0.8 km) from the station.[^14]

Other assistants in the register include John W. Bennett (1922 to 1925), Albert Williams (1925 to at least 1926), Ralph L. McGue (1925) and Dawson E. Fellner, second assistant from 1926.[^17] The Bulletin spells Fellner's first name Dawsen.[^15] In 1924 Bennett, first assistant, and Albert Williams, second assistant, rescued three men who were clinging to their capsized boat near the light.[^12] On 5 August 1926 Fellner saw a boy struggling in the water, lowered the station boat and rescued him.[^15]

From 1939 the Coast Guard ran all the lighthouses, and at Lorain it stationed three men at the light.[^1] The register lists William Deverick as principal keeper from 1957 to 1960.[^17] The library's history reports that Deverick estimated the light had received over 60 distress calls a month in the late 1950s.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The library's chronology dates several accidents at the pier. On the evening of 21 August 1888 a scow became unmanageable while entering the harbor and ran its jib boom into the east face of the tower.[^1]

The library dates the strike on the schooner Alta to 16 August 1894: the schooner, being towed away from the pier, struck the rear tower, which fell and shattered three lens lanterns. A new tower showed its light on 25 August.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the fall of the rear tower to 22 June 1894, and places a strike by a vessel being towed out of the harbor on 16 August 1895.[^21] The two accounts disagree on the year of the strike and on the date the tower fell.

On 21 February 1912 high winds blew the rear range tower off the temporary foundation on which the Baltimore & Ohio Railroad had placed it.[^1]

The Bulletin recorded several of the keepers' rescues described above. Its 1925 issue records the explosion on the Coast Guard boat.[^13] The library adds that in 1969 a Canadian freighter coming into the harbor accidentally rammed the lighthouse.[^1]

## After the keepers

The $22 million harbor improvements scheduled for 1965 included an outer breakwall, with an automated modern tower at its western tip to take over the light's work. The library says the Coast Guard then no longer needed the Lorain Lighthouse and scheduled the old building for demolition.[^1]

The Coast Guard decommissioned the lighthouse in 1966. The last light shone on 10 January, and the lamp's wiring was disconnected the next day.[^1] The Ohio historical marker's wording gives 1965 as the year of decommissioning, and LighthouseFriends says the last crew left in 1965.[^1][^21]

Local people organized a Save-the-Lighthouse Committee, headed by Wayne Conn, and on 25 January 1966 a not-for-profit association signed a five-year lease with the Coast Guard. The lease led the Coast Guard to cancel the demolition contract.[^1] The association disbanded before the lease ran out. In 1973 the Coast Guard took the building back and turned it over to the General Services Administration as surplus.[^1]

In 1977 the General Services Administration gave the deed to the Lorain County Historical Society for a token one dollar, on the condition that the society keep the building as a historic landmark.[^1] The National Register of Historic Places lists the lighthouse with a date of 29 December 1978; the library says it was placed on the Register in December 1978.[^18][^1]

In 1981 "Operation Lighthouse," a volunteer effort, repainted the exterior with 160 gallons of paint donated by the Glidden Company.[^1]

In 1989 the Lorain Port Authority created the Port of Lorain Foundation to preserve the lighthouse and the waterfront.[^1] On 12 February 1990 the foundation bought the building for $1.00.[^1] The library credits federal money with most of the restoration cost, including $500,000 for the base.[^1]

In October 1993 the Army Corps of Engineers awarded a contract to stabilize the base, which had been leaning. The Coast Guard declared the work complete when the grouting cost had reached $850,000, above the estimate.[^1]

The library places the return of a navigational light to the lantern room in 2001 or 2002, after 35 years of darkness. The first year visitors toured the building was 2002.[^1]

In 2011 the fourth-order lens, which had been on display for twenty years at the Charlotte-Genesee Lighthouse near Rochester, New York, returned to Ohio on loan from the Coast Guard, until 1 September 2028.[^1] In March 2014 the lens went on display in the lobby of the Lorain Port Authority offices at Black River Landing.[^1]

The 2017 centennial was marked on 30 June 2017 with the dedication of an Ohio Historical Marker.[^20]

## Today

The lighthouse stands at the north end of the West Harbor Breakwater.[^20] Its foundation's history page says its light "is no longer lit" and its fog horn no longer sounds.[^20] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record lists it as no longer an active aid to navigation.[^17]

The tower is reached by boat. The Foundation's tours and sunset dinners have run that way in the warm months, and its home page says the lighthouse was closed for the winter of 2026 and that tours and sunset dinners will resume the following June.[^1][^19]

The marker wording in the library's history says the lighthouse became the property of the Lorain Lighthouse Foundation, formerly the Port of Lorain Foundation, in 1990.[^1] The 2017 history says the Coast Guard retains ownership of the lens, on loan until 1 September 2028.[^1]
