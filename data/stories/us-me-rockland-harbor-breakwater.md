This light sits on a granite pad at the far end of Rockland's breakwater, just under a mile out into the harbor mouth (the city gives the breakwater's length as 7/8 mile). The breakwater came first; the light was added later to warn ships off the breakwater itself.[^3][^8]

## The place

Rockland, in Knox County, Maine, was a shipping town whose chief product was lime. In 1856, Lieutenant John Newton of the Corps of Engineers argued to the President for a breakwater, citing about three hundred locally owned ships and the limekilns along the waterfront, which storm seas sometimes reached.[^2] The city's account says the 1850s nor'easters did heavy damage to the waterfront and that Congress did not approve a breakwater until 1880.[^3] The National Register nomination says the work was laid in nine sections between April 1881 and November 1899, designed and engineered by the Army Corps of Engineers.[^1]

The record of what stood at the harbor before that is thin. Lighthouse Friends says a small lantern was set at Jameson Point, on the north side of the harbor entrance, in 1827; the US Lighthouse Society archive also gives 1827 as the year the station was established.[^2][^4]

## Building the light

The unfinished breakwater was itself the problem. The Lighthouse Board reported in 1888, as quoted by Lighthouse Friends, that the work was "entirely submerged at half-tide and presented a serious obstruction to the navigation of the harbor", and that with the engineers' money spent the Board put a temporary wooden beacon on the outer end.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives 23 April 1888 as the date Eba Ring, a part-time laborer, took responsibility for that light and rowed out to tend it.[^2]

As the breakwater grew, the beacon moved with it. The Board's reports record the lights shifting to a new beacon post at the end of the extension (the fiscal 1893 report), to two fixed red lens-lanterns one above the other on 15 August 1892, and again on 25 November 1895, when a small temporary shelter was also built at the end of the pier.[^5][^6][^7] On 15 March 1899 a foundation pier was built near the end of the finished work and the lights moved to it; the fiscal 1900 report adds a move of 785 feet southerly on 20 November 1899.[^8][^9]

In its 1899 report the Board asked for a proper station. Its argument was the harbor of refuge the breakwater made: sailing vessels would enter in the stormy season and needed warning of the outer end of the breakwater so as not to be carried onto it in easterly and northeasterly snowstorms, and steamboats entered the harbor throughout the year, carrying passengers between Boston and Bangor in summer, when fogs were frequent.[^8] An act of 6 June 1900 appropriated $30,000. The temporary lights were shown from a new temporary structure in August 1900, and in June 1901 that structure was moved to the extreme end of the breakwater to clear the site.[^10] Lighthouse Friends adds that a four-foot cap raised the breakwater after severe storms in the winter of 1899-1900; the city's account dates its completion to 15 October 1901.[^2][^3]

A Maine newspaper reported in February 1902 that the W. H. Glover Company of Rockland held the government contract and that work, stopped by winter, would resume.[^11] Lighthouse Friends gives 19 September 1902 for completion.[^2] The Portland Daily Press carried the Notice to Mariners: on or about 30 October the two red lights would be discontinued and a fourth-order light flashing white every five seconds shown from the new structure.[^12]

What was built, by the National Register description, is one connected complex on a rectangular platform of dressed granite: a brick fog-signal building with a square brick tower rising from its roof, and a 1½-story frame keeper's house with a gambrel roof.[^1] Lighthouse Friends adds a first-class Daboll trumpet fog signal, two 1,500-gallon rainwater cisterns under the dwelling, and a coat of white paint over the red brick in 1906 that stayed until 1991.[^2]

## The keepers

Llewelyn C. Ames became attendant of the beacon in 1895 at $300 a year; once the breakwater was complete he walked out unless it was iced over, and in fog he struck a metal triangle.[^2][^13] In September 1902, a month before the light was first shown, the Republican Journal of Belfast reported that W. P. Robbins, long keeper of Baker's Island light, had been appointed keeper with his son as assistant, and that the berth was "regarded as one of the most desirable on the coast" for its close proximity to shore.[^14] The archive's roster names them Howard P. Robbins and Clifford M. Robbins, 1902 to 1909.[^13] Clifford later said that after "Three or four winters like that in a row" of heavy ice he had had enough of lighthouse keeping, according to Lighthouse Friends, and father and son resigned together in 1909.[^2]

Charles W. Thurston followed and died on Christmas Eve 1909 after surgery; assistant LeRoy S. Elwell was promoted, and returned as head keeper again from 1925 to 1928.[^2][^13] The station began as a "stag station" without families. Lighthouse Friends says that in August 1915 the men were allowed to bring their wives, and that Elwell soon asked the inspector to cancel the permission because two families in the same quarters was not agreeable.[^2]

On Thursday afternoon in October 1916, the Daily Kennebec Journal reported, assistant keeper Albert Tribou set out from Rockland by rowboat with a sail hoisted; halfway to the lighthouse a heavy squall capsized it. His wife saw the accident from their Main Street apartment, took a jitney, and got men with a boat to him; he was still clinging to the overturned boat, so nearly drowned that he was revived with difficulty at Knox Hospital.[^15] (Lighthouse Friends places the apartment in Rockport; the newspaper says Rockland.[^2][^15]) In 1921 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded Tribou going to the aid of the disabled motor boat *Colon* near the station.[^16]

George E. Woodward, the last civilian head keeper, served 1934 to 1945, and the station passed to the Coast Guard in 1939. Lighthouse Friends says extra Coast Guard personnel kept a lookout from a tower on the dwelling roof during the Second World War.[^2][^13]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources give the weather mostly as an outline: the harsh, icy winters that Lighthouse Friends says preceded the Robbinses' resignation, and a fog signal that Lighthouse Friends says could sound as many as 900 hours a year.[^2] It also tells of a heavy fog in 1926 when the fog signal's engine was running but the horn was silent, and Elwell reported finding his assistant, who was meant to be tending it, asleep on a coat on the floor; he was told to keep a close watch on the man.[^2] In 1949 a pair of powerful air horns drew complaints from the nearby Samoset Hotel, and the volume was reduced.[^2]

## After the keepers

Sources differ on when the light was automated. The National Register nomination says 1964; the archive and Lighthouse Friends say 1965 (Lighthouse Friends: February).[^1][^4][^2] The fourth-order Fresnel lens was removed shortly before and an aerobeacon installed. When the Coast Guard proposed selling the station and removing the dwelling, the city objected and the Samoset Resort took over its care until 1989.[^2] The lighthouse passed to the City of Rockland in 1998 under the Maine Lights Program, and in 2001 was leased to Friends of Rockland Harbor Lights, volunteers who restored it. That group did not renew the lease in 2021. A storm in January 2024 tore shingles and roof tiles, and a $125,000 Save America's Treasures grant followed for the slate roof.[^2]

## Today

The City of Rockland owns the lighthouse, and the light remains an active aid to navigation: a VRB-25 optic 39 feet (12 m) above the sea showing white every five seconds, with a range of 17 nautical miles.[^4][^3] The breakwater and grounds are open in daylight, reached from Marie Reed Park at the end of Samoset Road, and the city advises avoiding high tide because waves can wash over the breakwater.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says the building has opened on seasonal weekends with volunteers; check with the city before relying on it.[^2]
