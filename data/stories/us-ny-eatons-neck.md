Eatons Neck Light stands on a bluff at the north end of Eatons Neck, in Suffolk County, New York, where Huntington Bay opens onto Long Island Sound. A stone tower finished in 1798–99, it is the second oldest lighthouse on Long Island, and it still shows its light through a third-order Fresnel lens.[^1][^2]

## The place

The point takes its name from Theophilus Eaton, who bought the land from the Matinecock people in the 1640s.[^1] In 1795 a part-time surveyor, Joshua Hartt, described "a great reef of rocks which run off into the sound more than a half a mile and are very dangerous to shipping," and added that a lighthouse was expected there.[^1] A sign near the lighthouse, which LighthouseFriends transcribes, says the site was acquired to mark the reef off the north coast of Eatons Neck.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that the rocks off the point saw more wrecks than any other spot on Long Island's north shore, and that at least sixty ships were lost in one December 1811 storm. That figure is that site's account and is not checked here against a primary record.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $13,250 on 14 March 1798 to build the lighthouse and fit it out, and on 16 June 1798 John and Joanna Gardiner sold the government a ten-acre site for $500.[^1] The Lighthouse Friends history says the contractor was John J. McComb, who had built Montauk Point Light two years earlier, and that his contract price was $9,750; a sign at the site, which that page also transcribes, gives $9,250 and an authorising act of 4 March.[^1] McComb wrote beforehand that no building materials were to be had near the spot and that everything would have to be landed west of the reef and carted up by a new road of about half a mile.[^1]

The tower is octagonal and built of stone, about 73 feet (22 m) high, and was first painted in black and white stripes; by an 1854 Light List it was all white.[^1] The first appointee as keeper, John Squire, refused the post in December 1798. A week later Thomas Burgher of New York City accepted it.[^1] The sign at the site, quoted by LighthouseFriends, says the tower was first lit in January 1799, while the page's own text says only that it was completed in 1799.[^1]

The lighting apparatus changed twice. An inspector in 1837 found every lamp defective, and in 1838 a new arrangement of twelve lamps and reflectors was fitted. After the tower was strengthened for a larger lantern, a third-order Fresnel lens made by Henry Lepaute of Paris was installed in 1857.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive still lists that third-order Fresnel lens as the station's optic.[^2]

Later work changed the station. After $11,800 was voted on 2 March 1867, major repairs followed in 1868: the wooden stairs became iron steps inside a brick cylinder, and the keeper's dwelling was partly torn down and enlarged.[^1] A steam siren fog signal was installed in 1871 and a duplicate soon after. A new first-class automatic siren in a brick building replaced them on 30 June 1904.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 shows that the duplicate second-class steam siren ran for about 628 hours and used some 40 tons of coal. The next year's report gives about 529 hours and 39 tons.[^5][^6] The light's illuminant was changed from oil to incandescent oil vapor on 28 February 1907.[^1]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the keeper registers, lists twenty-three principal-keeper entries between 1798 and 1962.[^2] Names recur. John Gardiner, who sold the land, is listed as keeper from 1804 to 1813, and his son John H. Gardiner held the post in 1813–14 and again from 1821 to 1843.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the elder Gardiner died in 1812, a year that does not match the archive's 1813 end date.[^1][^2]

Michael T. Burke is listed as principal keeper from 1879 to 1909, the longest term in the archive's roster, ahead of Arthur Jensen's.[^2] Arthur Jensen served first as first assistant (1909 to April 1911) and then as principal keeper from 1916 to 1942.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 13 January 1840 the steamer *Lexington* caught fire off Eatons Neck. According to LighthouseFriends, only four of the more than one hundred aboard survived.[^1]

On 25 August 1850, during a northeast gale, Keeper Benjamin Downing watched a small vessel capsize off the reef. In his letter to the Life Saving Benevolent Association, which LighthouseFriends reproduces, he wrote that he "could not prevail upon one single person to go in the boat," so he launched it with his sixteen-year-old son and saved one of the two men, the other having tried to swim ashore. He added that he had left "a good substitute, in the shape of my wife" at the light. The association awarded Downing and his son silver medals and $100.[^1]

In 1873 the schooner *Florence E. Turner*, bound from Honduras to New York, struck Stratford Shoal and capsized on a stormy night, according to LighthouseFriends; her master's card of thanks dates the gale to 28 November, when she was thrown on her beam ends. Her master, George E. Graves, credited the keeper of Eatons Neck, then listed as William Parott (the page spells it Parrott), with raising a volunteer crew and launching a lifeboat to take off him and his wife.[^1][^2]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded two rescues by Keeper Arthur Jensen. On 15 August 1923 he rescued the occupants of a rowboat that capsized near the station.[^3] On 28 January 1926 he and assistant keeper Ole Andersen (the Bulletin's text, as scanned, prints the name "Andel'sen") helped save the occupants of two barges that broke adrift from a towboat in a heavy westerly storm.[^4]

## After the keepers

The archive records the station as automated in 1961.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the keeper's dwelling was demolished in 1969 and eleven Coast Guard housing units were built, and that the tower itself was slated for demolition until local activists secured its listing on the National Register of Historic Places in 1973.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse belongs to the U.S. Coast Guard and sits within the Coast Guard station at the end of Lighthouse Road. LighthouseFriends states that the grounds and tower are closed to the public and that the best view is from the water, though the U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive record marks the site "Open to Public: Yes." Treat it as closed unless the Coast Guard says otherwise.[^1][^2]
