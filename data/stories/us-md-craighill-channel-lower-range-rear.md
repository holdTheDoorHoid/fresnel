The Craighill Channel Lower Range Rear Light is a wooden tower inside a cast-iron frame, standing in 2 to 3 feet (0.6 to 0.9 m) of water where the south entrance of the Craighill Channel meets northern Chesapeake Bay, near Edgemere in Baltimore County, Maryland.[^1] It was built in 1873 to 1875 as the rear light of a pair of range lights that led ships up the channel toward Baltimore.[^1] The Coast Guard owns it, and access is by boat.[^1]

## The place

The channel stretches about five miles (8 km) between the mouth of the Magothy River and the southern end of Belvidere Shoal.[^1] It is the first stretch of the maintained route to the Patapsco River and Baltimore Harbor.[^1] The channel took its name from William Price Craighill, an Army Corps of Engineers major who also sat on the Lighthouse Board and oversaw the surveys for widening and deepening it.[^1]

Congress appropriated $50,000 in 1870 to widen the channel to 500 feet (150 m) and deepen it to 22 feet (6.7 m).[^1] Before the range lights, the channel had no lights to guide ships at night.[^1]

The nomination quotes the Board's 1871 report on the channel.[^1] The report said the channel would save large vessels about five miles on the run from the lower bay to Baltimore, and would avoid much, if not all, of the danger from ice that built up in the lower Brewerton Channel in winter.[^1] It also said the channel was "much easier navigated, or would be if range beacons were established."[^1]

The land beside the rear light was also disputed.[^1] In 1888 the owner of land near the light, on Miller's Island, sued the United States for damages over what he called "unauthorized occupation of the site."[^1] The courts ruled against him.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $45,000 on 10 June 1872 "for two range lights for Craighill Channel into Patapsco River," to take the place of the two lighthouses at North Point.[^1] The Board first planned screw-pile foundations for both range lights.[^1] The severe ice of the winter of 1872–73 led it to build a small caisson for the front range and a granite pier foundation for the rear.[^1]

The rear site had a soft bottom, so the builders drove piles and laid a grillage of timbers on them.[^1] In April 1873 they enclosed the site in a sheet-piling cofferdam 60 by 60 feet (18 by 18 m), in 2 feet (0.6 m) of water.[^1] The nomination says heavy gales damaged the dam twice.[^1]

Nine piers of Port Deposit granite were built on the timbers, and the frame went up on them.[^1] It was a four-sided frustum with cast-iron corner columns, with an enclosed stairway to the lantern.[^1] The keeper's dwelling was built within the frame, at the base of the pyramid.[^1]

Money ran short.[^1] The appropriation did not cover the cost of finishing both range lights.[^1] A temporary light was shown from the unfinished structure on 20 November 1873, and the keepers were housed ashore in quarters the construction crew had used.[^1] Another $45,000 was approved on 23 June 1874, and the work was completed in March 1875.[^1]

The nomination, quoting the Board's report for 1884, says the structure received "extensive repairs" that June.[^1] The decayed wooden braces were replaced with iron swivel and Phoenix beam braces.[^1] In 1897 the base timbers, where they rested on the stone piers, had decayed, and they were replaced and strengthened with iron rods and beams.[^1] For the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899, the Board reported that new model fourth-order lamps had been installed, with minor repairs.[^2]

The archive gives 1886 as the year the present tower was established.[^3] The nomination does not mention an 1886 tower.[^1] It dates the structure to 1873–75, with alterations in 1884, 1888 and 1897.[^1] Neither source explains the difference.[^1][^3]

## The keepers

The archive's roster begins with Henry Buckless as principal keeper for 1873–74.[^3] Joel W. McDonald followed for 1874–1887, then Bernhard Berends for 1887–1901, William K. Slacum for 1902–1908 and Henry H. Wills for 1908–1923.[^3]

The keepers' dwelling was a one-and-a-half-story, mansard-roofed wooden house at the base of the tower, with pedimented windows and a wooden gallery.[^1] The nomination calls it a "mansard roofed, square, wooden structure."[^1] It was removed shortly after automation in 1937.[^1] Inside the tower, the watch room had a fitting for a heating stove, and a spiral stairwell of 16 wooden steps led up to the lantern room.[^1]

Robert Williams served as first assistant from 1874 to 1918, the longest stint on the archive's roster.[^3] Uriah R. Nichols was first assistant in 1873–74.[^3] From 1920 to 1923 four first assistants followed: Eldred J. Cherrix, William W. Bozman, Joseph D. Barnett and John M. Stowe.[^3]

The 1914 Bulletin gives the head keeper's first name as "Harry H. Wills."[^4] The Bulletin's 1912–1917 index and the archive's roster give "Henry H. Wills."[^5][^3] The sources here do not say which is right.[^3][^4][^5]

The archive also lists Bernhard Berends as first assistant at the Craighill Channel Lower Range Front, 2.4 miles (3.9 km) south of this light, in 1886–87.[^1][^6] The archive's records link the two stints to one man.[^6] That link rests on the archive alone and has not been checked against a register.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 21 August 1888 a "cyclone" carried away the roof of the dwelling, the copper smokestack and part of the galvanized iron sheeting on the tower.[^1] Doors, windows and the gallery were damaged.[^1] The nomination says all repairs were completed that year.[^1]

In May 1914 the Bulletin reported that on 17 May Harry H. Wills, keeper of the rear light, and Robert Williams, assistant keeper, "furnished food and shelter for the night to two men" from a disabled gasoline launch.[^4] The next day they assisted one of the men, who was slightly injured, in obtaining medical treatment.[^4]

## After the keepers

The rear light was electrified in April 1929, during what the Bulletin calls "a program of electrification" that year.[^7] A submarine cable connected it to land lines, so that it could use commercial current.[^7] A 94-watt railway headlight lamp went into the fourth-order lens, and an astronomic clock and lamp exchanger were fitted.[^7] Candlepower rose from 20,000 to 140,000, at a total cost of about $1,050.[^7]

The nomination gives the station's period of significance as 1873 to 1937, from completion of construction until automation.[^1] It says the keeper's house was removed shortly after automation in 1937.[^1]

In April 1994 a Coast Guard engineering survey called the wooden tower "a serious safety hazard to servicing personnel."[^1] It proposed demolishing the tower and putting a light-lowering device in its place.[^1] The nomination quotes the survey and notes that the tower was eligible for the National Register but was not listed.[^1]

In 2000 the Coast Guard repaired the tower.[^1] It fitted single-pane Plexiglas with vents in the window openings and a new banister in the watch room.[^1] A spare range light, which the nomination thinks was probably kept in a canvas-covered box on the gallery, was removed that year.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns and manages the light, and access is by boat.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists it as not open to the public.[^3]

The 2002 nomination describes the lantern room's range light as "a classic Fresnel lens in poor condition."[^1] Its brass frame is marked "HENRY - LEPAUTE a PARIS."[^1] It is closed to visitors.[^3] Do not try to land on it or climb it.
