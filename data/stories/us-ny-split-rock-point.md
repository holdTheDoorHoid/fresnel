Split Rock Point Light was a lighthouse on the New York shore of Lake Champlain, at Whallon Bay near Essex. It was first lit in 1838, its stone tower was replaced by a limestone one in 1867, and the station was discontinued in 1928, when a steel tower took over its light. The station is a private property today and closed to visitors.[^1][^2]

## The place

LighthouseFriends describes the lake narrowing to about three-quarters of a mile at this point. The rock that gives the station its name stands thirty feet above the water and is split from the cliff beside it by about twelve feet.[^2] The same account says that as commerce increased along the lake, the need for a light to mark the spot became apparent.[^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends reports that on 3 March 1837 Congress appropriated $5,000 for a lighthouse at "Split Rock Point," and that a parcel just south of the rock was bought that year from Congressman Reuben Whallon for $500.[^2] Peter Comstock built the lighthouse, which cost $4,016. The stone tower stood thirty feet (9.1 m) tall, and the light was first lit in 1838 with eleven lamps and fourteen-inch reflectors.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives its focal plane as 110 feet (34 m) above the lake. The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record gives the height of the light as 100 feet above water, so the two sources differ and this story does not choose between them.[^2][^1] The archive gives 1838 as the year the station was established.[^1]

In 1857 a new dwelling was built for the keeper, and the tower was fitted with a new lantern and a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^2] The archive also dates the original lens to 1857.[^1]

The original tower was replaced in 1867 by a 39-foot (12 m) octagonal tower of rough-hewn blue limestone, quarried at Willsboro Point.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says it was paid for from a congressional appropriation of $9,600 made in 1866.[^2] The archive records the 39-foot height and the 1867 tower.[^1]

A freshet destroyed the boathouse in 1869. A new boathouse, boatways and a capstan were added in 1871.[^2] The dwelling was a recurring problem. LighthouseFriends quotes the Lighthouse Board's 1874 report calling it "in a state of decay," and says it was asked for again in 1875 and 1876 before it was finally rebuilt in 1898.[^2]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1893 records "a survey of the reservation and various repairs" at the station.[^3] The report for the fiscal year 1899 says the work of raising, repairing or rebuilding the dwelling, under contract, began on 26 July 1898 and was completed on 6 December 1898.[^4] The archive dates the keeper's quarters to 1899.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists eighteen keepers' stints at the station, from David Delance (1838 to 1841) to John A. Tatay (1928).[^1] LighthouseFriends says Delance was paid an annual salary of $350.[^2] Henry E. Wyman appears twice, for 1866 to 1870 and for 1870 to 1871. The archive does not say why.[^1]

James Wakefield, Jr. kept the station from 1892 until 1927, the longest service on the roster. LighthouseFriends says he had spent "more than thirty-five years" there.[^1][^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin named him for the efficiency award three times: a pennant for the fiscal year 1914, a flag for the fiscal year 1916, and a flag for the calendar year 1921. The Bulletin prints his name as "James Wakefield."[^5][^6][^7]

LighthouseFriends reports that in August 1895 Wakefield killed a rattlesnake forty inches long with five rattles, which "made the local papers."[^2] In October 1927, at seventy-two, he died of an infection from a dog bite he got while separating two fighting dogs. His wife died the same day at their home in Essex.[^2]

After him the roster lists John W. Wakefield (1927 to 1928) and John A. Tatay (1928).[^1] The archive does not say how John W. Wakefield was related to James, and no other source here connects them.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends describes one wreck on this stretch of shore. On the night of 16 July 1875 the paddle steamer Champlain left Ticonderoga bound north up the lake, made a brief stop at Westport, and then ran onto the rocky shore of Split Rock Mountain. Passengers were taken off onto the Adirondack, and the Champlain was a total loss.[^2] The account rests on LighthouseFriends alone. The same page notes that the light "could not prevent all shipwrecks in the area."[^2]

## After the keepers

The archive gives 1928 as the year the station was discontinued.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that in 1925 the tender Beech brought a partly assembled steel skeletal tower to the point, and that it was smashed on the rocks while being erected. A replacement carrying an acetylene light went into service at Split Rock in 1928.[^2] A 2001 Plattsburgh newspaper calls the steel structure "the Split Rock tower, built in 1928."[^8]

LighthouseFriends says the government sold the property in 1931 for $2,700, and that it has been in one family's hands since 1959.[^2] The archive describes the keeper's quarters as Greek Revival and lists a carriage house among the other structures.[^1]

In December 2001 the Plattsburgh Press-Republican reported that the Coast Guard had "working beacons atop metal towers" at three Lake Champlain sites, Split Rock among them. The article says the metal towers replaced the stone lighthouses at those sites, and that the Cumberland Head and Split Rock lighthouses remain in private ownership.[^8] It also said the Coast Guard had "stopped its replacement plans," and that re-lighting the original lighthouses "seemed the perfect fit."[^8]

LighthouseFriends reports that on 19 March 2003 the light was moved from the steel tower back to the stone tower, "after seventy years of darkness."[^2] In January 2014 the Coast Guard said both metal towers were of an "obsolete design." It also said that "Neither of the towers is an active aid to navigation," and announced plans to remove them, as LighthouseFriends quotes the release.[^2]

## Today

Split Rock is privately owned, and the archive records it as not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is privately owned, that the grounds, dwelling and tower are closed, and that the gated road that leads up the hill to the light is flanked by warning signs.[^2] Nothing in these sources suggests going ashore or climbing the tower.

Whether the light is shown today is not settled by these sources. The 2014 statement says neither tower was an active aid, and the 2003 move that LighthouseFriends reports has no later confirmation in the sources here.
