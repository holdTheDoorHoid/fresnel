Guard Island Light stands on the larger of two small, rocky islands where Clarence Strait runs into the northern end of Tongass Narrows, the inside-passage channel that leads south to Ketchikan.[^1] The white concrete building with a lantern on its roof dates from 1923-1924, and it is the only fully intact structure left from a station that once had a keeper's house, a boathouse and a tramway.[^1]

## The place

The Guard Islands are two outcrops joined at lower tides, west of Point Higgins and north of Gravina Island, about 9.6 miles (15 km) northwest of Ketchikan.[^1] The station sits on the larger island, which rises roughly 50 feet (15 m) above mean water and measures 800 feet (240 m) from north to south by 300 feet (90 m) across. The smaller island reaches only about 22 feet (7 m), and in winter storms, the National Register nomination says, waves sweep and scour it.[^1]

The reason for a light here was traffic on the Inside Passage. By 1890, the nomination records, Alaska had only 27 buoys and 15 daymarks, with no lights or fog signals; the Klondike rush then multiplied the ships, and in 1898 and 1899 alone at least 46 vessels were lost in Alaskan waters, a figure the nomination takes from a federal shipwreck inventory.[^1] Mariners, it adds, counted the Tongass Narrows among the harder passages on the route.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for 1900 named Guard Island among eleven Alaskan places for which it asked Congress for $300,000,[^3] and its table in the 1901 report put Guard Island's estimate at $30,500, for a light visible 12 miles and a trumpet fog signal.[^2] President McKinley's executive order of January 1901 set the islands aside as a lighthouse reservation.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $100,000 for Alaska, and the nomination says Guard Island ranked sixth of fifteen proposed sites.[^1] Surveyors from the Thirteenth Light-House District mapped both islands in September 1902; Maj. W. C. Langfelt of the Army Corps of Engineers, the district's engineer, supervised the designs, and final plans went to the Board on 2 July 1903.[^1]

Accounts differ on what came next. The nomination says a crew hired directly by the Board cleared, graded and excavated until winter weather stopped work in January 1904, and resumed in June.[^1] LighthouseFriends says construction was authorized on 21 April 1903, that work began that November, and that a temporary light was shown from 1 December 1903 until the permanent one was ready.[^11] Both give 15 September 1904 for the first lighting of the permanent light.[^1][^11]

The first tower was a slightly pyramidal wooden frame, about 20 feet (6 m) square at the base, narrowing to a 12-foot (3.7 m) gallery roughly 30 feet (9 m) above ground, with the lantern 79 feet (24 m) above the water.[^1] It showed a fixed white light with a 34-degree red sector toward Vallenar Point and the Tongass Narrows.[^1] The nomination says the lantern held a buoy-style lens-lantern and no Fresnel lens, while the US Lighthouse Society's record gives the original optic as a fourth-order Fresnel; the sources disagree.[^1][^12] The fog signal was a 1,200-pound bell on the north face of the tower, struck every 20 seconds by clockwork, which the nomination calls the only station-mounted bell known among Alaska's lights.[^1] Around it stood a keeper's house of 28 by 38 feet, a boathouse and workshop, a tram house, and a tramway of 30-pound rail on a 3-foot gauge that carried supplies up from the water and launched the station's skiffs.[^1]

The wood did not last. The nomination says the Ketchikan depot designed a temporary structure for the light and fog bell as early as 1917.[^1] In its 1918 report the Commissioner of Lighthouses asked Congress for money "to replace the present unsatisfactory fog bell with an air diaphone and to construct a suitable permanent light and fog-signal building to replace present temporary wooden tower; also to erect another dwelling in order that two keepers may be assigned instead of one as at present."[^7] The 1921 estimates priced the station at $42,000, including $27,950 for a reinforced-concrete fog-signal building with the tower above it and $8,800 for a dwelling, which the estimate described as a frame house.[^8] J. T. Elliot's design, drawn at the Lighthouse Service's base in Ketchikan, was finished by 2 March 1922.[^1] That year's Bulletin reported orders placed for a diaphone and fourth-order lanterns for Guard Island and Point Retreat.[^9]

The new building was lit for the first time on 25 February 1924.[^1] The nomination gives it as 34 by 20 feet (10.4 by 6.1 m), standing 14 feet (4.3 m) to the cornice, cast in place from reinforced concrete painted white under a red roof, with a black metal lantern; LighthouseFriends gives the footprint as twenty by thirty-six feet.[^1][^11] In its engine room a Pennsylvania compressor powered a Type C diaphone,[^1] and a 1924 Bulletin lists the Guard Islands fog signal as changed from a bell operated by machinery to an air diaphone.[^10] The nomination calls the lantern the original 1904 one, yet the 1922 orders for new fourth-order lanterns leave its age uncertain.[^1][^9] The second dwelling, finished in 1924, had walls of concrete brick, unusual among Alaskan stations; it was built for an assistant but had become the main residence by 1940.[^1] The old wooden tower came down when the new building went up.[^1]

## The keepers

The nomination names the first keeper as John O. Carlson, who shared the house with assistant Gus Hall.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's roster, drawn from the keeper registers, lists John A. Carlson as head keeper from 1905 to 1912 and Gust J. Hall as first assistant in 1905-1906, so these are most likely the same two men under variant spellings.[^12] The roster then shows a new first assistant nearly every year until 1912, seven in all, before the post goes empty until 1924.[^12] LighthouseFriends says the assistant's post was dropped by 1913,[^11] and the Commissioner's 1918 request for a second dwelling, "so that two keepers may be assigned instead of one", fits a lone keeper at that date.[^7]

David Oliver Kinyon kept the light from 1915 to 1918 and George F. West from 1918 to 1934, the longest term on the roster.[^12] John C. Johnson, head keeper in 1914-1915, was named in the Lighthouse Service Bulletin among keepers at stations awarded the district's efficiency flag for the fiscal year 1915; Guard Island was the Sixteenth District's.[^4][^5]

The second house meant two families on a small island. The nomination, quoting a 1990 *Alaska Magazine* article, says that early in the century the island's two families were known around Ketchikan for bickering and petty grievances.[^1] One later family was the Gibbses: Otto Gibbs arrived in December 1947 with his wife, Dorothy, and two small children, and the radio quiz show *Truth or Consequences* later sent a contestant out with a bucket of ice as a forfeit.[^11] Civilian keepers gave way to Coast Guard personnel in 1939,[^11] and the station later became a "stag station" of four coastguardsmen, remembered for its pet deer.[^11]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

An inventory of Alaskan shipwrecks, as the nomination summarises it, lists only four near the station. The gas schooner *Laclabell* sprang a leak and foundered 1.5 miles west of the islands in 1912; a collision and a fire occurred near Vallenar Point in 1923, during the rebuilding; and the gas screw *Panama* sank in three minutes after a seam opened between Guard Island and Caamano Point. The nomination adds that the records do not show the keepers taking part in a rescue at any of them.[^1]

Other rescues are on record. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that on 28 June 1916, in a heavy wind and sea, Keeper Kinyon towed a disabled motor boat to the station from about a mile away.[^6] The nomination also tells, from the 1990 magazine article, of Prohibition years when the keepers launched a boat to a trolling boat drifting with no one at the helm and found two murdered men in its cabin; it gives no date or names.[^1]

## After the keepers

The station was updated piece by piece. Ship radio began in 1938, the lantern was electrified in 1939, a radio room was added in 1942, a dual-horn system with new compressors replaced the original fog-signal machinery in 1946, and power was converted to alternating current in 1959.[^1] The original keeper's house was razed in May 1962, and radio-beacon masts went up in 1963.[^1] The station was automated in 1969, and the radio room came off in the 1968-1969 work.[^1] The final entry in the station's Register of Visitors, written by William L. McVey on 31 July 1969, recorded the light as disestablished.[^11] In March 1970 the Coast Guard let a contract to demolish every building but the light and fog-signal building, though foundations and walkways can still be traced.[^1]

In 1997 the Southeast Area Council of the Boy Scouts weighed a long-term lease and decided against it, and a group called Guard Island Heritage hoped to take the lighthouse over under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, but the Coast Guard kept it.[^11] The light station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2004.[^11]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns Guard Island, and LighthouseFriends reports the grounds and tower closed.[^11] The island lies offshore, so a visit means a boat. LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse can be seen from the shore road north of Ketchikan near South Point Higgins Road, and that an excursion to it has been offered from Ketchikan.[^11] The light is automatic and solar powered.[^12] What is left on the island besides the 1924 building is its cistern and the foundations, walkways and tramway remains of the old station.[^1]
