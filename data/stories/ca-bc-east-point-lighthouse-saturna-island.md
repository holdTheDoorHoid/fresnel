East Point Lighthouse stands at the eastern tip of Saturna Island, in British Columbia's Gulf Islands, on a point where ships have to make a sharp turn.[^8][^9][^2] A wooden light was first shown there on 1 January 1888. A steel skeleton tower replaced it in 1948. The station has been unstaffed since 1996 and now belongs to Parks Canada.[^1][^2]

## The place

The Department of Marine and Fisheries studied the currents at the point in the 1920s. Its report printed in 1925 calls East point of Saturna Island and Turn Point "the two crucial points in this region," and says that at both "vessels have to make a sharp turn at a right angle."[^9] The report printed in 1924 says the two points are "the locations of most importance in the navigation of the pass."[^8]

The point was a hazard before it had a light. In February 1886 the ship *John Rosenfeld*, two years old and carrying nearly 4,000 tons of coal from Nanaimo, was in tow of the tug *Tacoma* when it grounded on a reef near East Point.[^3][^2] The Weekly Puget Sound Argus reported that the tide was high and began to fall soon after, and that the ship was left wedged.[^3] The Daily Morning Astorian reported that the wreck would be sold in Victoria next Monday.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the ship's back broke, that the owners sold her for salvage, and that Saturna residents used the coal from the wreck to heat their homes for years.[^2]

The Canadian Register of Historic Places says the light "was established following the wreck of a ship carrying coal from Nanaimo to San Francisco," and that it was "the product of lobbying by the maritime community, local members of parliament, and trade interests in the region."[^1] Parliament had appropriated money for a light on East Point in 1885, but tenders were not called until too late to help the *John Rosenfeld*.[^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says the contract was first awarded to George Fraser, and that Joshua Holland ended up building the lighthouse at a cost of $4,000. With the lantern and lighting apparatus the total came to $5,966.80.[^2]

The first tower was a square pyramidal wooden tower, 18 m (60 ft) high, with an attached dwelling. It stood on land obtained from the Gulf Islands pioneer Warburton Pike.[^2] Its revolving white light was first shown on 1 January 1888, with a focal plane 43 m (140 ft) above high water. Mariners were warned to keep a mile and a half off to the north and west, away from the ground that had caught the *John Rosenfeld*.[^2]

Rainfall on the point was scant, so the station relied on rain for its water. A tank shed with three large tanks was built in 1896 for $219.55.[^2] A post light went up on Patos Island, across Boundary Pass, in 1893.[^2]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends names John R. Wick as the first keeper. Its text says he served just over a year before he was moved to Sand Heads, though its keeper list runs from 1886 to 1889.[^2]

His replacement was James Georgeson, a brother of Henry Georgeson, who had kept the Active Pass light since 1885. James was born in the Shetland Islands and went back to Scotland to bring his wife and five children to Saturna.[^2] The Department's keepers' tables list him at "Saturna Island, East Point" for 1902 and 1907, and at "Saturna island" in 1912.[^5][^6][^7]

In June 1889 a brush fire nearly consumed the new lighthouse while James was away. His nephew George, visiting from Active Pass, fought the flames alone and was paid $30 for "saving the lighthouse and buildings at East Point."[^2] In 1909 James suffered a stroke, and his son Peter took on much of the work. When James retired in 1923, the Marine Agent in Victoria, Colonel Wilby, wrote to Ottawa that the light service was best maintained by the sons of men who had given long service. Peter was all but assured the job.[^2]

Peter Georgeson kept the light until 1939, when he transferred to Albert Head. LighthouseFriends's keeper list gives Henry Georgeson for 1939 and 1940, but its text says "his brother James" would keep the light for one more year. The sources read here do not settle which is right.[^2]

Andrew Ritchie moved to East Point from Race Rocks in 1940 and stayed until he retired in 1960.[^2] The Register says the early keepers were part of the original community of European settlers on Saturna.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

One November between 1940 and 1960, the American trawler *Neptune II*, homebound from Alaska, burst into flames as it passed the station. The keeper's wife ran to the radio, Victoria marine radio picked up the call, and a U.S. Coast Guard crew took a man off the stern before the trawler went under. LighthouseFriends does not give the year.[^2]

## After the keepers

Fog led to the next changes. In February 1935 R. W. McMurray, president of the West Coast Steamship Service, petitioned for a fog signal. The petition described East Point as the most dangerous point on the route between Vancouver and Victoria.[^2] A foghorn had been established on Patos Island, which let southbound vessels steer toward that island, but in fog captains were never sure when to turn west into Boundary Pass.[^2] An experimental acetylene fog gun in a small shack at the point was installed in September 1937. It often misfired and at times was inaudible to passing ships.[^2] In 1939 a fog alarm building with diaphone horns was built on the point. The Register calls the former fog alarm building "one of the most photographed buildings in the Gulf Islands."[^1][^2]

The wooden tower gave way in 1948 to a steel skeleton tower, 14 m (46 ft) high, with a standard revolving beacon, and the keeper's dwelling was repaired.[^1][^2] A second keeper's dwelling was built in 1960.[^2]

The Register notes the nearby capture of the orca *Moby Doll* in 1964, and that the point was a popular spot for liquor smuggling in the United States' prohibition era.[^1]

The station was de-staffed in 1996. Around 2006 it passed to Parks Canada, to be added to the new Gulf Islands National Park Reserve.[^1][^2]

## Today

The station is unstaffed and run by Parks Canada.[^1][^2] The red steel tower still displays a pair of lights for mariners.[^2] The keeper's dwelling now houses park staff, and the former fog alarm building has become a heritage centre.[^2] LighthouseFriends lists the grounds and tower as closed. The point is reached by ferry to Saturna Island and then along East Point Road to East Point Regional Park.[^2] Do not climb the tower.
