Matinicus Rock is a treeless islet of about 25 acres, six miles south of Matinicus Island, where two granite towers stand 180 feet (55 m) apart.[^1][^2] Only one still shows a light; the other has lost its lantern.[^2] A story from the station was in print by 1889: in the winter of 1856 the keeper's teenage daughter, Abbie Burgess, tended the lamps through a gale that cut the family off from the mainland.[^3]

## The place

The rock lies in the way of the coastal traffic. The National Register nomination describes the station as a guide along the shipping lanes off the southern entrance to Penobscot Bay.[^2] Writing in 1889, D. P. Heap placed it in the path of the steamers running from Boston and Portland to Eastport, Saint John, Yarmouth and Halifax, and of the coasting and fishing fleet trading with the British Provinces.[^3]

Sources differ on how far out it is. The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 1892 put it about 20 miles from the mainland as the crow flies, with Rockland, 25 miles away, the nearest harbour, and the coves of Matinicus Island 4 miles off.[^4] Heap said 25 miles from the mainland and gave the island 39 acres at low tide,[^3] George R. Putnam's 1917 history said eighteen miles,[^5] and the nomination says about 25 acres.[^2]

The Board described the place in plain terms: a bare, rocky islet about half a mile long, nowhere more than an eighth of a mile wide, its highest part not more than 50 feet above the sea, with "neither tree nor shrub and hardly a blade of grass." Its surface, the report said, "resembles in a large way a confused pile of loose stone," and the waves that sweep parts of it "move the huge bowlders into new positions." There was a little cove where material could be hauled up in pleasant weather, but no harbour. The keeper made a landing by steering his boat through the breakers on the top of a wave so that it ran up onto the boatways, where his assistants waited to draw it far enough up that a receding wave could not carry it back to sea.[^4]

According to the nomination, Matinicus Rock was the fourteenth light station on the Maine coast, the most distant from the mainland when it was built, and the first to use a pair of towers.[^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends, which draws on the annual reports, says Congress voted $4,000 for the station in May 1826, that the island was bought for $20, and that Jeremiah Berry contracted for $3,700 to build a stone dwelling with a wooden tower at each end.[^6] The nomination likewise describes a rectangular rubble-stone keeper's house with wooden towers,[^2] and Heap a cobble-stone dwelling with a wooden tower at each end.[^3] LighthouseFriends gives 15 July 1827 for the first lighting, by John A. Shaw, the first keeper;[^6] the US Lighthouse Society's roster begins Shaw's service in 1827.[^1]

The wooden towers did not last. LighthouseFriends reports that by the 1840s it was obvious the pounding surf had damaged the towers beyond repair, that Inspector I.W.P. Lewis wrote that the whole structure needed rebuilding, and that it was then rebuilt to a design by Alexander Parris.[^6] The sources disagree about the year. Heap dates the new granite dwelling, with a granite tower at each end, to 1846, and says the old dwelling was kept as an outhouse;[^3] the nomination dates the remodelling to 1847 and credits Parris with the dwelling's design;[^2] and the Light-House Board's report for fiscal 1900 speaks of "a stone dwelling built in 1847."[^7]

In 1857 the granite towers on the dwelling were cut down to the roof and two separate towers were raised farther apart than the old ones.[^3] The nomination gives the spacing as 180 feet and names the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers as builder.[^2] LighthouseFriends adds that the Light-House Board first meant to build a single tower with a revolving light, as the First District's inspector had proposed, and that two fixed third-order Fresnel lenses were installed instead.[^6] The Board's own reports of the 1890s call the station's lights second-order,[^13] while the US Lighthouse Society lists the original optic as a third-order Fresnel lens installed in 1857,[^1] and Russ Rowlett's directory says the original third-order lens from the south tower is on display at the Maine Lighthouse Museum in Rockland.[^9] The sources also differ on the height of the south tower: the nomination gives 41 feet from base to the middle of the lantern,[^2] and the US Lighthouse Society 48 feet.[^1] In 1917 Putnam described the pair as two granite towers sixty yards apart, one light five feet higher than the other.[^5]

The fog signal's start is also disputed. Heap says steam fog signals were placed here in 1856,[^3] the nomination says a steam fog signal was established in 1869,[^2] and LighthouseFriends gives a 1,500-pound bell in 1856 and a steam signal in 1870, with a brick fog-signal house built in 1876 and rebuilt in 1878 after the sea carried it away.[^6] By 1894 the signal was a 10-inch steam whistle in a brick house southwest of the south tower.[^10]

The two lights were changed over the years. LighthouseFriends records that the north tower's light was discontinued on 1 July 1883 and the south light changed to fixed red, that the north light was relit on 1 July 1888, and that the north light was permanently discontinued on 15 August 1923, when a revolving third-order lens went into the south tower and an oil-engine compressor plant replaced the steam fog signal.[^6] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin in 1932 likewise lists 1923 as the year the duplicate light at Matinicus Rock was discontinued;[^11] Rowlett has the north tower inactive since 1924.[^9]

## The keepers

The station's keepers lived on the rock with their families, and at times that meant a crowd. In 1842, LighthouseFriends reports, Keeper Samuel S. Abbott's household numbered nine.[^6] The US Lighthouse Society lists Abbott as principal keeper from 1839 to 1849, after Shaw, Abner Knowles, Phineas Spear and Thomas G. McKellar, and before William Young, who served until 1853.[^1]

**Samuel Burgess and his family.** Samuel Burgess took the post in the spring of 1853. His family, in Heap's account, was an invalid wife, four small daughters and a son who made his home on the rock but was often away fishing in Bay Chaleur and elsewhere. The eldest daughter, Abbie, was the keeper's only assistant, helping with the light and with the principal household duties, and in her father's absences the whole care of the lights fell to her. "I took a great deal of pride in my light-house work and tried to do my duty," Heap quotes her as saying.[^3] Her age is given variously: Heap says fourteen in 1853 and seventeen in January 1856, Putnam calls her fourteen in 1856, LighthouseFriends says seventeen, and Rowlett says sixteen.[^3][^5][^6][^9] The roster lists Samuel Burgess as keeper from 1853 to 1861 and Benjamin P. Burgess as first assistant from 1856 to 1861;[^1] LighthouseFriends identifies Benjamin as the keeper's son, appointed his father's assistant in August 1856.[^6]

**The Grants.** In 1861, according to Heap, Burgess was relieved by Captain Grant and his son; Abbie taught them their duties and that same year married the son, Isaac H. Grant.[^3] The roster lists John H. Grant as principal keeper from 1861 to 1867, Christopher Chase from 1867 to 1871, and Grant again from 1871 to 1890. It also lists Isaac H. Grant as first assistant from 1864 to 1875 and Abbie E. Burgess Grant as third assistant from 1870 to 1875, one of the station's women keepers in her own right, followed in that post by Mary B. Grant in 1875–76.[^1] Other Grants appear as assistants through 1890: William, William G., John Francis and Jarvis H.[^1] The Light-House Board wrote in its reports of 1892 and 1893 that "for more than twenty years, and until recently" the first assistant had been the principal keeper's son and the two had lived together in one dwelling.[^4][^8]

In 1875, Heap says, a vacancy at White Head light brought Isaac Grant the keeper's post there, with Abbie as assistant, and by Heap's writing in 1889 they were still there.[^3] An appendix to the Light-House Board's fog-signal investigations calls a "Mr. I. Grant" (initial only) a keeper of many years' standing at both Matinicus Rock and Whitehead and prints two notes from his journal kept at Whitehead, dated September 1875 and February 1876.[^12] Rowlett gives Abigail Burgess Grant's dates as 1839–1892.[^9] According to LighthouseFriends she wrote in 1892 that she still dreamed of the old lamps and wanted a gravestone shaped like a lighthouse or beacon; Edward Rowe Snow later had a small metal lighthouse placed on her grave.[^6]

**After the Grants.** The Board wrote that the principal keeper's resignation had broken up the arrangement of father and son in one house; LighthouseFriends dates the resignation of Keeper John Grant to 1890, and the roster's principal keeper changes that year.[^4][^6][^1] The Board began asking for money to build a fourth set of quarters. It wrote that the station had a keeper and three assistants, "the only people living on this rock," in two dwellings: two assistants and their families shared a double dwelling and the principal keeper had a single one. The Board estimated a proper dwelling at $3,200, to be built for strength "rather than the graces of architecture."[^4] The request was renewed in the report for 1893 and again in a volume covering 1896 and 1897, which called it a recommendation made in the Board's "last six annual reports."[^8][^13] LighthouseFriends says the third assistant was in the end housed by renovating the old stone house in 1894.[^6] The Board's report for fiscal 1900 describes that stone dwelling's upper story as wholly, and its lower story partly, rearranged, and says the double dwelling was altered so that its two families each had a separate entrance, stairs and hall, and a cellar holding a cistern.[^7]

**Twentieth century.** The roster names the principal keepers who followed: William G. Grant (1890–1900), James E. Hall, Merton E. Tolman, Charles G. Dyer, Arthur B. Mitchell, Frank O. Hilt (1919–1929), Ruel Walter Powers, Alvah Robinson, Roscoe L. Fletcher (1936–1945) and others, with Coast Guard crews listed from 1942.[^1] Edwina Davis, whose father, Assistant Keeper James Anderson, came to the rock in 1914 (the roster has James M. Anderson as third assistant from 1914), is quoted by LighthouseFriends as saying that the families made their own entertainment, with reading, cards, play on the rocks and roller-skating in the covered passage between the houses, and that the four families sent one order together to the mail-order catalogue at Christmas.[^6] In 1943, LighthouseFriends reports, Roscoe Fletcher's wife Dorothy told a reporter that the keepers had four days' leave a month, were on duty weekends and holidays, and stood four-hour watches, each man keeping the same watch for a week.[^6] Newspapers and magazines came by irregular mail, and the telephone line could be cut by a gale and stay down for months, according to the same account.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The first buildings took damage early. LighthouseFriends says the towers were already storm-damaged within weeks of the first lighting, and that on one occasion vibration from gale winds and leaking lanterns broke thirty glass chimneys in a single night. Keeper Abbott described two storms in which the sea broke into the southeast corner of the dwelling, destroyed the kitchen and filled the lower rooms waist-high with water.[^6]

**The gale of 19 January 1856.** The Light-House Board's report of 1892 and Heap's book both place the great storm on 19 January 1856. The Board wrote that the old dwelling was so thoroughly demolished that "not one stone was left upon another," and that the new dwelling, though in the most protected spot, was flooded and its heavy shutters had to be closed against the spray.[^4] Heap prints a letter that Abbie wrote to a friend. Early in the day, she wrote, the sea "made a complete breach over the rock, washing every movable thing away." "As the tide came, the sea rose higher and higher, till the only endurable places were the light-towers," she wrote. "If they stood we were saved, otherwise our fate was only too certain." Her father was away, and for four weeks, she wrote, no landing could be made on the rock. "Though at times greatly exhausted with my labors, not once did the lights fail."[^3] A Belfast, Maine newspaper summarising Gustav Kobbe's article in *The Century* in June 1897 said that after the storm the passage to Matinicus Island was so rough that Captain Burgess could not return until four weeks later.[^14]

The hens, in her telling, were the family's only companions. She ran out a few yards, in water knee-deep, after the rollers had passed and saved all but one, getting back inside just as her little sister cried out that the worst sea was coming. That wave, she wrote, "destroyed the old dwelling and swept the rock."[^3]

**The spring of 1857.** Heap writes that in the spring of 1857 Burgess left the rock to collect his salary and buy provisions and fuel, and weather kept him from returning. When the family ran short of food the son set out in a small skiff with a sail that his sister had helped to make. For twenty-one days, Heap says, the mother and the four girls were reduced to a cup of corn-meal and one egg each per day, with Abbie tending the light and her sick mother; at last father and son came back safely.[^3]

**Fog and sound.** The Light-House Board's acoustic investigations of the 1890s recorded a keeper's accounts from Matinicus Rock. In August 1890, in a calm after fog, he sounded the whistle again and again to attract an assistant keeper who was less than half a mile offshore and in plain sight of the signal; the assistant did not hear it. On another occasion, in a boat 100 yards from the northeast point, he saw the steam rise from the whistle but could hear nothing, then rowed about 150 yards southeast, heard it, and lost it again when he rowed back. Fishermen told the Board that in southerly to southwest winds and dense fog they had heard the whistle at 12 or 14 miles and followed it to within a mile of the station, then lost it until they were almost in the surf and could see the steam.[^10] The Board gave the coal burned by the signal as 85 pounds an hour while running in the year ended June 1893.[^10] In its fiscal 1900 report it recorded the 10-inch whistle running about 723 hours and burning about 33 tons of coal, and the 12-inch whistle about 340 hours and 23 tons.[^15]

**Later storms.** LighthouseFriends says a storm in November 1950 knocked out the machinery that powered the fog signal and electric light, and the station went dark; the keepers took shelter in the stone house, where spray blown off the wave-tops broke panes in the lantern, and a helicopter dropped supplies until a boat could come.[^6] David Brackett, a Coast Guard head keeper in the late 1970s, noted that the birds, waves, wind and above all the fog signal made the island very noisy, though in time he stopped noticing the horn.[^6]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard automated the south tower in 1983.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that the Fresnel lens was removed that year and a modern beacon installed, and that the Coast Guard's facilities on the rock were licensed to the Audubon Society in 1984 for the study and protection of seabirds.[^6] Rowlett records a solar-powered aerobeacon, installed in 1993, in the south tower, and the old lens at the Maine Lighthouse Museum in Rockland.[^9]

The nomination, prepared in October 1987 by the Maine Historic Preservation Commission as part of its "Light Stations of Maine" group, notes that from the 1950s many of the rock's outbuildings had been pulled down as new aids to navigation made them obsolete, and that the station had been automated in 1983.[^2] A continuation sheet, answering review comments dated 21 January 1988, redefined the period of significance as 1847 to 1937, beginning with the earliest surviving building or structure.[^2] The north tower, by the nomination, has lost its lantern room, workroom and frame assistant keeper's house.[^2]

## Today

The south tower is still an active aid to navigation, showing a white flash every 10 seconds from about 90 feet (27 m) above the sea, with a horn blast every 15 seconds.[^9] The US Fish and Wildlife Service owns the island, which is part of the Maine Coastal Islands National Wildlife Refuge; Rowlett lists the Coast Guard as operator.[^1][^9] A Project Puffin web page describes Matinicus Rock as the southernmost colony of Atlantic puffins, managed in association with the National Audubon Society.[^16]

The island is closed to the public. Rowlett says it can be reached only by boat or helicopter and that the site and tower are closed because it is a bird-nesting sanctuary.[^9] The US Lighthouse Society likewise records it as not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends lists a boat operator, Matinicus Excursions, offering trips to the rock, and Maine Audubon seabird excursions in June 2016 and 2017; the island itself stays closed.[^6]
