Marblehead Light stands at the northern tip of the Marblehead Peninsula, where it marks the mouth of Sandusky Bay on Lake Erie, in Ottawa County, Ohio.[^3][^8] An Ohio historical marker calls it the oldest continuously operating beacon on the Great Lakes.[^1] Its first keeper was Benajah Wolcott. After he died in 1832, his widow, Rachel, took over the light.[^3][^5] Ohio markers call her the first female keeper on the Great Lakes.[^2][^4]

## The place

The fifteenth U.S. Congress allocated $5,000 in 1819 for a light tower on the peninsula. LighthouseFriends says it was meant to guide vessels into Sandusky Bay and through the treacherous southern passage between the Ohio mainland and a cluster of offshore islands.[^3] The station was first called the Sandusky Bay Light Station,[^2] and LighthouseFriends says the tower was known as "Sandusky Bay Lighthouse" until 1870.[^3]

Benajah Wolcott, a Revolutionary War veteran from Connecticut, first arrived in what is now Danbury Township in 1806, as part of a survey crew for the Firelands. The Keeper's House marker explains that the Firelands were land Connecticut awarded to residents whose homes were burned by British forces in the war.[^4] Wolcott returned in 1809, the first of a small number of settlers on the north shore of Sandusky Bay, and he farmed there. The same marker calls The Orchards, or Danbury Settlement, the first American settlement in Ottawa County.[^4]

## Building the light

William Kelly, a stonemason from Sandusky, received the contract.[^2][^4] An Ohio marker says he used local limestone and completed the tower in eight weeks.[^2] Another Ohio marker credits John Kelly, his 13-year-old son and three helpers, with the work in eleven weeks.[^1] The two markers therefore disagree on who built the tower and how long it took. LighthouseFriends gives a crew of William Kelly and two men, starting in September 1821, and says a fifty-foot (15 m) tower stood by November. It cost $7,232.[^3]

Sources give the first tower's height as fifty feet, in LighthouseFriends and on one marker,[^3][^2] but the lamps-and-lenses marker says it stood 55 feet when built.[^1] The first light was thirteen whale-oil lamps set in reflectors.[^3][^1] The marker calls the apparatus crude for its day.[^1]

In 1858 a fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the reflectors. It was a barrel-shaped assembly of prisms around a single lard-oil lamp.[^1] In 1877, LighthouseFriends reports, the Lighthouse Board recommended $20,000 to rebuild the station. The keeper's dwelling was "old, leaky, and barely habitable," and the tower was in poor condition.[^3] Funds went to a new keeper's dwelling in 1880, and the limestone tower was covered with stucco and painted white.[^3][^8]

The tower grew in stages. In 1897 the top eight feet (2.4 m) were taken off and a brick extension went on. LighthouseFriends says a larger lantern from the discontinued Erie Land Lighthouse was placed on top in 1900.[^3] An Ohio marker dates the watch room to 1898 and the lantern to 1901, when it became the tower's third superstructure.[^1] The heights differ too. LighthouseFriends gives about sixty-five feet (20 m) after this work.[^3] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive record gives sixty feet (18 m) from base to the center of the lantern.[^8] The lamps-and-lenses marker gives a total of 73 feet 6 inches (22.4 m) after 1901.[^1]

In 1903 the Lighthouse Board equipped the tower with a three-and-a-half-order lens, 66 inches (1.7 m) across each face. Clockwork turned it around a kerosene lamp, and the keeper wound the weights by hand every four hours.[^1][^3] An incandescent oil-vapor lamp came in 1914 and electricity in 1923, but the lens still turned on clockwork.[^1]

## The keepers

Benajah Wolcott was appointed on 24 June 1822, an Ohio marker says, and kept the light until his death in 1832.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that each evening in the shipping season he climbed the tower to light its thirteen lamps, and that he kept a record of ships that passed and of the weather.[^3] A local history gives his death as cholera in August 1832, and one marker adds that a son died of the disease too.[^5][^2] His house, a hall-and-parlor on his farm, was built for him and his wife, Rachel Miller, by William Kelly.[^4]

Rachel Wolcott took over the light on 25 October 1832, according to a local history, and had official charge of it for about two years.[^5] She married Jeremiah Van Benschoten of Vermilion in 1834.[^2][^5] The local history dates his taking over her job to 14 February 1834.[^5] The Keeper's House marker says Rachel served until February 1854. The roster has her leaving the post in 1834.[^4][^8] The local history says the couple tended the light together for about eight years, after which Rachel drops out of the lighthouse records.[^5] The Keepers marker says Van Benschoten was officially appointed the third keeper, and that both tended the light until he resigned in 1841.[^2]

The keepers who followed served short terms. Roderick Williston was appointed in 1841 and served to 1843.[^5][^8] Charles F. Drake, Lodowick Brown, Jared B. Keyes, David L. Dayton, Thomas Dyer, Russell Douglas and Thomas J. Keyes each kept the light for a few years.[^8] LighthouseFriends says Lodowick Brown's wife, Margaret Kelly Brown, was a daughter of William Kelly, who built the lighthouse.[^3]

George H. McGee kept the light from 1873 until his death in 1896.[^2][^3][^8] His widow, Johanna, replaced him. The Ohio marker calls her the second female keeper, and she served until 1903.[^2][^8] Charles A. Hunter kept the light from 1903 to 1933, the longest tenure as head keeper, LighthouseFriends says. During the three-month winter season, when the light was inactive, he carved a grandfather clock and wove tapestries that showed the lighthouse. He also penned the words to a song called "The Lighthouse by the Bay."[^3]

An assistant keeper's position was added to the station in 1903.[^3] The roster lists Clinton D. Egelton that year, then Charles E. Perry (1903 to 1906), Earle O. Mapes (1906), Andrew Turinski (1907), and Mapes again from 1907 to 1913.[^8]

Edward M. Herman was an assistant from 1913 and head keeper from 1933 to 1943. The Keepers marker calls him the last civilian keeper and gives his dates as 1878 to 1964.[^2][^8] The Coast Guard took over the station after Herman retired.[^3] The roster also lists George Krause as principal keeper from 1943, with no end year, and Robert E. Jones from 1944 to 1947.[^8] Under the Coast Guard heading it names Frank Skorupa, "at least 1952." The roster's "at least" marks a documented span, not an appointment date.[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 1 May 1875 the schooner *Consuelo*, heavily laden with blocks of stone, capsized in the passage between Kelley's Island and Marblehead, about two miles north of the town. The Life-Saving Service's report says the sea was "tremendous" and a gale was blowing from the northeast when her cargo shifted. Five of her crew perished at once, and two clung to the cross-trees of her mainmast for nearly an hour.[^6]

Three brothers of Marblehead, one of them Lucien Clemons, went out in a small skiff, twelve feet long and three feet wide.[^6][^3] Their wives and children were among the forty or fifty people watching from the shore.[^6] After nearly an hour's struggle with the waves, the brothers reached the wreck and brought the two men out of the rigging. With the extra weight they could not reach the shore, until a steam-tug from Kelley's Island came to their assistance.[^6] The Life-Saving Service gave the three brothers its first-class medals.[^6]

LighthouseFriends says the Life-Saving station was built half a mile west of the light in 1876, and that Lucien Clemons became its first commander.[^3] The station was Station No. 9 in the Service's Ninth District, at Marblehead Point.[^7]

On 15 October 1880 the steamer *B. F. Ferris*, of Put-in-Bay, bound for Sandusky, lost her reckoning in thick weather and stranded on rocks half a mile northwest of Marblehead light.[^7] The crew of Station No. 9 reached her half an hour after the accident, with both life and surf boats. Both boats were used to lighten her of part of her cargo of fruit and fish. When the rest of the cargo had been shifted, she backed off the rocks under her own engine. She had a crew of eight and eight passengers on board.[^7]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over in 1943, and the light was automated in 1958, when the tower was given a fresh coat of stucco.[^2][^3][^8]

During a renovation in 1969 the stucco came off. It revealed the original stone and brickwork, and the openings that William Kelly's crew had left in the tower to support their scaffolding.[^3] The lens was taken out that year and a 300-millimetre plastic optic replaced it. LighthouseFriends says the change came in 1969, but the Ohio marker dates it to 1972.[^3][^1] The lens was shipped to Detroit and returned to the town hall the next year. In the early 1980s it moved to the Coast Guard station, and in 2004 it went back to the keeper's dwelling.[^3] The tower has been on the National Register since 1969.[^2]

In January 1997 the Coast Guard declared the tower and its small plot surplus federal property.[^3] The Ohio Department of Natural Resources applied for ownership and took the lighthouse over in 1998.[^3][^2] On 1 September 2001 a $500,000 restoration began. It covered brickwork in the tower and the adjacent Victorian keeper's quarters. The keeper's dwelling was elevated on jacks so that rotting support columns and beams could be replaced.[^3]

## Today

The lighthouse and dwelling are now part of Marblehead Lighthouse State Park, and the Ohio Department of Natural Resources owns the lighthouse.[^3] LighthouseFriends says tours run daily from noon to 4 p.m. from Memorial Day weekend through Labor Day. The tower and dwelling are open in season, and the museum in the keeper's dwelling, run by the Marblehead Lighthouse Historical Society, is open during the same hours.[^3] As of 2018, an Ohio marker says, the light was an LED visible up to eleven nautical miles.[^2] The Keeper's House, Wolcott's stone dwelling a couple of miles from the light, is maintained by the Ottawa County Historical Society as a center for the preservation and interpretation of the heritage of Danbury Township and the Firelands.[^4][^3] Check the park's current hours before visiting.
