Punta Gorda Light stands on a remote stretch of beach on California's Lost Coast, in Humboldt County, about seven miles southwest of Petrolia.[^1][^2] It was first lit on 15 January 1912 and went out on 15 February 1951. Only the reinforced-concrete tower and the oil house still stand.[^1]

## The place

Punta Gorda is Spanish for "Massive Point," a rocky headland that juts into the Pacific about a mile to the northwest of the station.[^1] In its 1891 report the Light-House Board renewed a recommendation it had made in each of its two previous reports. Between Shelter Cove and Punta Gorda, it said, "several dangerous sunken rocks off the shore" added to the hazards of navigation, and on dark nights the overhanging mountains kept the shoreline in shadow.[^3] The Board estimated that a light and fog signal would cost $40,000.[^3]

The nomination says Pacific Coast mariners asked for a light here as early as 1892. It also says that from 1895 to 1907 ten ships sank or went aground near Punta Gorda.[^1]

The last of the ten was the passenger steamer Columbia. The nomination says she collided with a lumber ship on 12 July 1907 and that 77 people died.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the collision to 21 July and gives the toll as 87.[^2] A San Francisco newspaper of 23 July 1907 reported that the Columbia "sank Saturday night at midnight, 20 miles to the south of Point Gorda," after being rammed by the steam schooner San Pedro, a wooden steam schooner laden with lumber.[^9] Read as midnight at the end of that Saturday night, the newspaper's account falls on 21 July, the date LighthouseFriends gives. The nomination's 12 July does not fit it. The same newspaper's latest advices put the survivors at 139, and the final toll is not settled by the sources read here.[^9]

Congress appropriated $60,000 for the light in 1908, the year after the Columbia went down.[^1][^2]

## Building the light

Construction began in 1910. Supplies came ashore by a high line from a schooner moored off the mouth of Four Mile Creek, and horses dragged the materials on sleds down the beach to the site.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says three substantial dwellings with storage sheds and a blacksmith and carpenter shop went up within a year, with a frame fog-signal building.[^2]

The fog signal, compressed-air sirens driven by a gasoline motor, began on 22 June 1911.[^1][^2] The light followed on 15 January 1912. It was an incandescent oil-vapour lamp with a fourth-order lens and a focal plane 75 feet (23 m) above mean high tide, and the nomination says it was visible for 14 miles.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1912 gives the light about 96,000 candlepower.[^4] The tower is a 27-foot (8.2 m) reinforced-concrete structure under an iron lantern, and LighthouseFriends gives the total cost of the station as $59,814.51.[^1][^2]

For its first four years the station was served entirely by boat. In 1915 the balance of the right-of-way along the coast was acquired, and supplies could come by horse and wagon from Petrolia, ten and a half miles away. For seven months of the year, the nomination says, flooded creeks still cut the station off.[^1]

## The keepers

Frederick A. Harrington was the first head keeper, from 1911 to 1919. LighthouseFriends says that on 15 January 1912 the light was first exhibited by Harrington with his assistants Paschal Hunter and W. E. Greer, and that Harrington's father was then head keeper at Trinidad Head.[^2][^5]

Charles W. Lindley worked up through the station's ranks. He was second assistant from 1920, first assistant from 1925, and head keeper from 1928 to 1939.[^5]

The nomination says Punta Gorda was known as the "Alcatraz of Light Houses," and that Lighthouse Service employees were reportedly "exiled" there as punishment for misconduct.[^1] Up through 1945 the man on watch still had to crank a weight up every hour or so to keep the light turning. No power line ever connected the station with the outside world, so the light ran on its own plants, and during the war a shore patrol was stationed there.[^1]

Wayne R. Piland was a second assistant in 1935 and 1936.[^5] LighthouseFriends says he later served at three offshore stations, and that he rated the job of getting supplies to Punta Gorda as "the toughest job you ever saw."[^2] For several decades a horse called Old Bill was the station's link to the outside world. LighthouseFriends says he had been born at a light station, and a family member described him as "mean and ornery."[^2]

The nomination identifies John E. Evenden as officer in charge in 1943 to 1945. The archive roster lists him as head keeper for 1944 and 1945.[^1][^5] Samuel "Hank" Mostovoy was the last head keeper, from 1949 to 1951, and he was officer in charge when the station was decommissioned.[^5][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The steamer St. Paul ran aground in heavy fog in 1905, and pieces of her are still visible in the surf in front of the station, LighthouseFriends says.[^1][^2]

Earthquakes shook the station more than once. The Bulletin for 1923 reports a very severe earthquake at 1:08 a.m. that cracked the foundation of the keeper's quarters, shook the mercury out of the lamp and spilled about 30 gallons of mineral oil from the oil tanks. A landslide knocked down a telephone pole.[^6][^2] LighthouseFriends dates it to 22 January 1923.[^2] The Bulletin for 1924 records shocks on 9 January 1924 that shook the mercury out of the lamp's reservoir, broke the mantle and, in one shock, stopped the lens.[^7] The Bulletin for 1931 gives the keeper's report of two severe shocks on 11 December, and a third that same day. Apart from throwing the lens in the tower "out of time" and spilling some mercury, it says, no damage was caused at the station.[^8]

## After the keepers

The light was extinguished on 15 February 1951, according to the nomination.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard decommissioned the station in May 1951, removed the Fresnel lens, put a lighted buoy offshore and boarded up the buildings.[^2]

The two sources disagree on who held the property next. The nomination says the Coast Guard kept it until 16 January 1963, when it was transferred to the Bureau of Land Management.[^1] LighthouseFriends says control passed to the BLM when the Coast Guard left in 1951.[^2]

In the 1960s a counter-culture group, which LighthouseFriends describes as long-haired hippies, moved into the dwellings. The BLM burned the wooden buildings to the ground. The nomination puts this in the late 1960s, and LighthouseFriends puts it in 1970.[^1][^2]

The lighthouse and oil house went onto the National Register in 1976.[^2] Soon afterwards a volunteer fire department repaired cracks in the masonry and the staircase, and in 1989 a state conservation corps restored and painted the structures. Under the Great American Outdoors Act, the surviving structures were rehabilitated in 2022 and 2023, when a new circular staircase went into the lighthouse.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by the Bureau of Land Management and is part of the King Range National Conservation Area.[^2][^1]

Today the last stretch is a hike. Visitors drive Lighthouse Road to its end at Mattole Beach, then walk south along the beach for just over three miles. LighthouseFriends calls the hike strenuous, over loose sand or pea-sized rocks, with a headland called Wind Point that may not be possible right at high tide and creeks to cross in winter. It also warns of poison oak along the trail.[^2] The 1975 nomination gives the walk as 4.5 miles.[^1] The interior of the lighthouse is open to visitors, LighthouseFriends says. Check tides and trail conditions with the BLM office before setting out.[^2]
