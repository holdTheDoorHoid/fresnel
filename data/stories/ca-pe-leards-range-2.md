The Leards Range Front Light stands at the corner of Water Street and Russell Street in Victoria, on Prince Edward Island's south shore, where its square, shingled wooden tower overlooks the Northumberland Strait and Victoria Harbour.[^2][^3] In summer the tower houses the Victoria Seaport Museum.[^2][^3] It was the front light of a pair of range lights that guided mariners into the harbour, also known as Crapaud Harbour.[^2]

## The place

Victoria Harbour, also called Crapaud Harbour, opens onto the Northumberland Strait. Getting in meant following a dredged cut to the wharves.[^2][^3] The published instructions for the range lights advised strangers arriving at night to anchor in the road, because the cut was not safe to attempt in the dark.[^2] In the early 1900s the village was the outlet for a fertile district. Its pier was 148 m (486 ft) long, and a steamer came from Charlottetown once a week.[^2]

## Building the light

Before there were ranges, the harbour had a lamp.[^2] About a year after Prince Edward Island joined Confederation in 1873, the General Superintendent of Lighthouses visited and found the light at Victoria Harbour, then known as Crapaud, to be a lamp on a pole, tended by Donald W. Palmer.[^2] LighthouseFriends credits George Clark with erecting that light in 1869.[^2] The Register describes a number of years of "makeshift lights on poles or masts, sometimes attached to homes, or, from the window of a residence," before the range lights were set up.[^1]

The front tower was built in 1879, the PEI Lighthouse Society says, though some documents say 1878. It replaced an 1869 beacon that had been hoisted on a mast.[^3] Donald W. Palmer sold the lot for $75, and James Barclay was paid $535 to build the tower.[^3][^2]

The rear light was shown from a mast on the house of S. J. B. Leard, the first keeper of the rear range.[^2] In 1895 the mast was moved to the keeper's garden, because gales strained it and damaged the keeper's dwelling.[^2] On 23 October 1901 the light shown from Leard's dormer window was discontinued. A new tower about 61 m (200 ft) north, on the same line, replaced it.[^5][^2] The Department's report describes that tower as "46 feet high from its base to the top of the ventilator on the lantern," and it refers to the house as that of "the late Mr. Leard."[^5] LighthouseFriends dates the keeper's death to 1901.[^2]

In 1894 two more sets of range lights, Palmer's and Wright's, were set up to work with the Leards pair.[^2]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends gives two rosters, one for each light.[^2] The front light had Donald W. Palmer (1874 to 1875), Percy Palmer (1875 to 1889), James Johnson (1889 to 1893), Abner J. Howatt (1893 to 1940) and Emerson Malcom MacDonald (1940 to 1960).[^2] The rear light had S. J. B. Leard (1878 to 1901), James Inman (1901 to 1912), Kenneth C. Holm (1912 to 1935), Stewart Gordon Inman (1935 to 1936), Harry B. Clark (1936 to 1951) and Emerson Malcom MacDonald again (1951 to 1960).[^2]

Howatt's appointment was dated 22 July 1893, at $100 a year, and the Department's statement for the year ended 30 June 1902 lists him at the "Crapaud Outer Range Light" with that date.[^2][^4] On LighthouseFriends' roster dates, Howatt kept the front light for 47 years, from 1893 to 1940.[^2]

The rear light's record is less settled. LighthouseFriends gives 13 August 1901 as the appointment of James Inman to the rear light, at $100 a year.[^2] A later Department statement, for the year ended 30 June 1910, gives the same date for Inman at the inner light.[^8] The statement for the year ended 30 June 1902, however, lists the Crapaud Inner Range Light as vacant.[^4] The Department's station list for the year ended 30 June 1904 names the two lights "Leards Range, Outer Light, Crapaud" and "Leards Range, Inner Light, Crapaud."[^6]

When MacDonald retired in 1960, at a salary of $615 a year, Albert Trowsdale was appointed caretaker of the automated lights at $200 a year.[^2]

## After the keepers

By 1960 the lights were automated, with a caretaker in place of a keeper.[^2] The Victoria Seaport Museum has been housed in the front tower in summer since 1990.[^2] The Coast Guard discontinued the range in 2011.[^2] Rowlett's directory dates the front light's end to 2011, and Palmer's rear light, which shared the tower, to 2010.[^7] Ownership passed to the Village of Victoria in 2012.[^3][^7] Prince Edward Island recognized the tower as a heritage place on 3 October 2012.[^2][^3] Rowlett's directory dates a heritage designation to 2019.[^7]

## Today

The Village of Victoria owns the tower.[^3] The Victoria Seaport Museum is open in summer, from 10 a.m. to 5 p.m., Tuesday through Sunday, the PEI Lighthouse Society says.[^3] Rowlett's directory says the site and tower are open daily from 1 July to 15 September. There are no stairs, but visitors may climb a ladder to the lantern.[^7]

The light's current state is disputed. The PEI Lighthouse Society lists the front light as "Active - Open," shown at night in season only.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the front light remains active as Victoria Harbour Lighthouse.[^2] Rowlett's directory says the light was deactivated in February 2020.[^7] The Register says the lighthouse "continues to be an important cultural destination and visual landmark in its community."[^1]
