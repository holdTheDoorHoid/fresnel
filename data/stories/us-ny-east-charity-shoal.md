East Charity Shoal Light is an octagonal iron tower on a concrete superstructure over a stone-filled timber crib, in eastern Lake Ontario, marking a rocky shoal on the approach to the St. Lawrence River.[^1] The tower was first put up at Vermilion, Ohio, in 1877, and it came to this shoal in 1935.[^2][^3] Nobody was stationed here.[^1][^3] The 1935 Lighthouse Service Bulletin calls the station unattended, and LighthouseFriends says it was never staffed.[^1][^3]

## The place

The shoal was a hazard for ships bound for the St. Lawrence.[^3] LighthouseFriends describes East Charity as an outlying spur of Charity Shoal, about 3,000 feet (910 m) long and in places under only ten feet (3 m) of water, lying in the line that ships took for the river.[^3]

The main Charity Shoal already had a buoy of its own.[^5] The Light-House Board's annual report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1897 notes that, since 1 July 1897, that buoy had been replaced by a gas buoy.[^5] LighthouseFriends says that in the summer of 1900 John C. Churchill Jr. surveyed the spur, and that the Board then moved to mark its eastern edge.[^3] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 records the establishment of East Charity Shoal buoy No. 2, which it describes as "a red second-class nun."[^4]

## Building the light

In May 1934 newspapers in upstate New York advertised for sealed proposals for a timber-crib and concrete superstructure on the shoal, and LighthouseFriends says The Walls Company won the contract.[^3] The crib was fifty feet (15 m) square and between eleven and fourteen feet (3.4 to 4.3 m) high, shaped to fit the shoal's contour. LighthouseFriends says it was built on land upside down, then launched, righted, towed out and sunk in place, with its centre pockets filled with stone and its outer pockets with concrete.[^3]

On 24 November 1934 a temporary light atop a pole went up on the south side of the crib, and the lighted buoy was discontinued.[^3] The 1935 Bulletin describes the finished station: a stone-filled timber crib carrying a concrete superstructure and an octagonal cast-iron tower "with a fourth order lantern," in 14 feet (4.3 m) of water.[^1] The Bulletin gives its output as 1,300 candlepower.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the light burned acetylene, with its focal plane 52 feet (16 m) above low water. The whole project, riprap included, cost $95,125.[^3]

The tower had come from Vermilion, Ohio.[^3] LighthouseFriends says it was first installed there in 1877, at the end of a pier. After the beacon had been in service for over fifty years, the tower was found to lean after ice storm damage to the pier, and the Service replaced it with a lighter, automated tower.[^3] A 1937 article, "Olin Stevens Tends Beacon," as LighthouseFriends quotes it without naming a newspaper, says the tower had been a casualty of an ice shove. It was salvaged, taken to Buffalo and assigned to Charity.[^3] The Society's record says the tower was "moved from storage" for this site in 1935, and it gives 1929 as the year the station was established.[^2] The record gives no reason for the move from Vermilion, and its 1929 date does not match the November 1934 temporary light.[^2][^3]

## The keepers

The Society's roster for this station names no keepers.[^2] The one name the sources connect to the tower is Olin W. Stevens, a keeper. The 1937 article, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says the tower had once been under Keeper Stevens at Vermilion.[^3] It also says that although this was his first duty on Lake Ontario, the Charity light, visible from the Tibbetts Point headland, was "an old friend."[^3] The passage does not say that he tended the light on the shoal.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 5 December 1897 the steamer Rosedale grounded on East Charity Shoal in a northwest gale.[^3] A Kingston dispatch dated 10 December, printed in the New-York Tribune, says the wreckers at work on the Rosedale, stranded on East Charity Shoals, "have stripped the vessel and abandoned her."[^6] LighthouseFriends says a wrecking company later towed her off, and that after being rebuilt she returned to service.[^3]

In October 1912 the steamer Rock Ferry ran aground on the shoal, and tugs were sent out to try to free her.[^3]

On 5 August 1955 Dr. Joseph G. Reidel, a 37-year-old physician from Syracuse, was sailing on Lake Ontario with his wife and friends when winds estimated at seventy miles an hour struck their sailboat, and he was washed overboard.[^3] He saw the glint of a lighthouse and swam toward it, LighthouseFriends says. After more than eight hours in the water he reached the pier, fell asleep there, and was picked up at 5:30 the next morning by three fishermen.[^3]

## After the keepers

The Society's record gives 1939 as the year the light was automated.[^2] In July 2008 the Coast Guard declared the station surplus. It was then offered at no cost to eligible entities, such as government agencies and non-profit corporations, under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act.[^3] With no eligible taker, an online auction opened on 5 May 2009, and the lighthouse sold on 27 August 2009 for $25,501 to a private buyer.[^3]

## Today

The station is privately owned, and the tower is closed to visitors.[^2][^3] The Society's record lists it as an active aid to navigation but not as a private aid.[^2] On a clear day it can be seen with binoculars from Tibbetts Point, though a boat or plane ride is needed for a good view.[^3] The fourth-order lens used here is now displayed at the Coast Guard base in Buffalo, New York.[^3] The Society's record gives a fifth-order lens as the original optic, so the lens order remains open.[^2]
