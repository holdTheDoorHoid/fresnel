North Cape is the northwestern tip of Prince Edward Island, where the Gulf of St. Lawrence meets Northumberland Strait, and a long rocky reef runs out from the point. The wooden octagonal lighthouse here is one of the oldest wooden-frame towers still standing in the Maritimes, and the colony argued over a light on this point for decades before one was built.[^2][^1]

## The place

The reef off North Cape reaches seaward for roughly 2.4 km (1.5 miles). Lighthouse historian Kraig Anderson reports that an 1834 report judged a light on this corner of the Island "highly useful" to vessels forced down into the bight by northeast gales, and that ship owners and ship masters of the ports of Richmond Bay and New London petitioned the House of Assembly for one in 1840.[^1] The colony could not pay for it alone. In 1851 and again in 1860 the Island government asked its neighbours and the Imperial government to share the cost, arguing in 1860 that many vessels belonging to the adjoining colonies had been wrecked on these headlands.[^1] The Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society also records that funds did not come.[^2]

## Building the light

In 1861, according to Anderson, David Petrie put a light of his own on the edge of the cape, and in 1862 a petition asked the House for a small grant to repay him.[^1] A proper lighthouse followed in 1865: £500 was appropriated, a contract for £600 went to John McLellan, and Joseph Pope & Co. of Liverpool was paid £611 for the lighting apparatus.[^1] The Superintendent of Public Works, Thomas Alley, reported the tower finished in the fall of 1865 but unable to operate because the lantern had not arrived; he called the lantern "very superior construction" and said there was not one "to compare with it in the Lower Provinces."[^1] A £190 contract for a keeper's cottage and cedar fence went to John Chiasson in early 1866.[^1]

The tower stood finished in the fall of 1865 but waited for its lantern.[^1] The Lighthouse Society says it went into service in 1866, and the provincial heritage register dates the construction to 1865-1866.[^2][^3] The register says the plans were specified by the Hon. Alexander Anderson, and describes a four-storey octagonal tower of heavy timber, 62 feet (about 19 m) high, built for just over $5,000.[^3] The Society also gives 62 feet (18.8 m), but notes that the Notice to Mariners lists the tower at 19.5 m (63.9 feet).[^2] The Society adds that a fourth-order lens sits in a cast-iron lantern, and that the tower has 14-by-14-inch corner posts.[^2] A handwritten note quoted by the Society, from an unidentified source, says the first lighthouse was built by Robert MacRae with materials shipped from Halifax.[^2]

When the General Superintendent of Lighthouses visited in 1874, Anderson reports, he called North Cape "one of the most important light stations on the Island" but judged the fourth-order light "not of the power and character that its importance demands." The Lighthouse Society puts the same assessment of Island lights at 1873.[^1][^2] A catoptric revolving white light, showing a flash a minute, was placed in operation on 1 July 1875; the new lantern, revolving apparatus, lamps and reflectors cost $2,339.42.[^1]

## The keepers

Anderson's list of keepers begins with Stanislaus F. Perry in 1866, followed by Patrick Hogan, J. Stanislaus F. Perry, Lewis McDonald and Peter H. Perry, before Augustin Hackett held the post from 1883 to 1897.[^1] James Phee followed, appointed on 4 September 1897, and the Department of Marine's keeper list for the Island shows him at North Cape on a salary of $360.[^4] Later names on Anderson's list are Dougal P. McCarthy (1912 to 1928), James Mark Gaudet, Leo J. Shea, Wilfred Brennan, Percy Patrick Morrissey (1940 to 1956) and Basil W. Spears from 1956.[^1] The Lighthouse Society, whose own list differs in places, warns that its data is incomplete, with "conflicting dates and information on actual names and spellings."[^2]

The cliff was the station's long trouble. In 1945 it was noted that the tower and dwelling needed to move back from the eroding edge. By 1949 the move had not happened and the dwelling was unfit for habitation; in cold onshore weather, Anderson writes, spray froze on it and at times sealed the keeper inside, so that he had to chop his way out.[^1] A new dwelling was built in 1950.[^1] The Charlottetown *Guardian* of 18 October 1950, as reprinted by the *West Prince Graphic*, said the move of the lighthouse inland about four hundred feet was to start the following week, held up by a shortage of foundation materials, and named Percy Morrissey as keeper.[^5] The two accounts of the move differ: Anderson dates it to 1951 and says the tower, a woodshed and the old dwelling went roughly 122 m (400 ft) inland, by Neil McDonald and D. O'Leary for $4,785, while the Lighthouse Society dates it to 8 March 1951 and credits Neil MacDonald of O'Leary.[^1][^2] The heritage register says the tower was moved back from the shore six times in its history; Anderson and the Society document only the 1951 move.[^3]

## After the keepers

The light was electrified in 1962 and automated in 1967, and the 1950 dwelling and two sheds were sold as surplus in 1968.[^3][^1] The tower is still an active Canadian Coast Guard light; the List of Lights gives a focal height of 23.7 m and a nominal range of 18 nautical miles, and the Society describes a flashing yellow light, a one-second flash and four-second eclipse.[^6][^2]

## Today

The lighthouse stands just past the North Cape Interpretive Centre at the end of Route 12, and the Coast Guard owns it.[^1][^7] Lighthouse Friends lists the grounds and tower as closed, the Lighthouse Directory lists the site as open and the tower as closed, and the heritage register says the tower is fenced off for safety, so the tower is not open to visitors.[^1][^7][^3] Anderson reports that visitors can walk out on the reef at low tide.[^1] The Tignish Initiatives group petitioned for ownership under the Parks Canada Heritage Lighthouse Program, and the tower was awarded a Provincial Designated Heritage Place plaque on 25 September 2013.[^1]
