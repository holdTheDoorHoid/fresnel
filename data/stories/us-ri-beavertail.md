Beavertail Light stands on the rocky southern tip of Conanicut Island, where the two passages into Narragansett Bay meet the open ocean.[^1] A watch house is on record here in 1705, a lighthouse has stood on the point since 1749, and the granite tower that still shows a light was finished in 1856.[^1] It was the third lighthouse built in what became the United States, and over the years it has been a testing ground for fog signals and lamps.[^1]

## The place

Beavertail Point is a windswept promontory at the southern tip of Conanicut Island, which divides the East and West passages of Narragansett Bay. The National Register nomination of 1977 calls it a strategic position that has carried beacons and lighthouses since the early eighteenth century.[^1] The East Passage led to Newport, then one of colonial America's largest and busiest ports, and mariners of the period often called the lighthouse there the "Newport Light."[^1]

The point was watched before it was lit. Jamestown's colonial records mention a watch house in 1705, and an entry of 9 June 1712 orders a beacon built and a regular watch kept. The nomination thinks the purpose was probably military, to warn of hostile ships, though the beacon may sometimes have guided merchant vessels.[^1] A marker on the grounds, put up by the museum association, says the town council gave Gershom Remington the job of building a "Fire Beacon."[^2] New England Lighthouses adds that in 1719 the colony supplied a gun at Beavertail "to answer ships in a fog."[^3]

The colony's General Assembly voted for a lighthouse in 1738, but nothing was built until 1749.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of April 1939 printed the colony's later act of 1748, which says that a war with Spain had broken out before the first year's interest of a loan was paid, and that building the lighthouse was therefore put off.[^4] In February 1749, according to LighthouseFriends, a Newport town record appointed a committee to build it "as there appears a great necessity for a lighthouse as several misfortunes have happened lately for want of a light."[^5]

## Building the light

**The first tower.** The first tower, finished in 1749, was a wooden one designed by Peter Harrison of Newport, one of colonial America's best-known architects.[^1] Sources disagree on its size: the National Register nomination says 58 feet (18 m), the museum association's marker agrees (58 feet to the cornice, with an 11-foot lantern on top), and LighthouseFriends gives 68 feet (21 m).[^1][^2][^5] The same marker says it was thought to have been round until a ground-penetrating radar survey, dated 2007 on one marker and 2008 on another, showed that it was octagonal.[^2][^6] It burned in 1753; LighthouseFriends dates the fire to 23 July.[^1][^5]

**The second tower.** Harrison supervised a 64-foot (19.5 m) fieldstone replacement that the nomination says was finished in 1755.[^1] Other sources place the work earlier, and LighthouseFriends describes the replacement as a 58-foot (18 m) brick and stone tower built in 1754.[^5] The Jamestown Historical Society holds an agreement of 16 September 1754 under which the Newport treasurer was to pay John Stevens, a bricklayer, for building the second light, and a separate agreement a year later to pay Joseph Jacob of Newport; the society also quotes the General Assembly's grant, in August 1754, of "four thousand pounds for defraying the Charge of Building the Light House."[^7] The museum association's marker places the stone tower in 1753.[^2] British troops leaving Newport in 1779 set fire to this tower, and the nomination says it was repaired in 1783 and 1784 and used until 1856.[^1] The marker adds that they took some of the lighting equipment.[^2] The deed to the point passed to the United States government in 1793.[^2]

**Gas, and the lamps of 1838.** In 1817 and 1818 David Melville of Newport, a pioneer of gas lighting for homes and streets, fitted the lantern with a lamp burning gas he made by heating tar and rosin over a coal fire. The nomination says it burned much brighter than the oil lamp and was reportedly visible 25 miles away, probably the first use of gas as a lighthouse illuminant; the government doubted its cost and reliability, and the oil lamp came back.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives a different reason for the end of the trial, the lobbying of whale-oil interests.[^5] In 1838 Lieutenant Edward Carpender of the Navy surveyed the lights between Newport and Boston. LighthouseFriends quotes his report on Beavertail: 15 lamps with 9-inch reflectors on two circular tables, of which "5 of them are so much worn as to be of very little use."[^5]

**The tower of 1856.** An inspection in 1851 found the old stone tower in wretched condition, according to LighthouseFriends, and Congress appropriated $14,500 in August 1854 for a new tower, lighting apparatus and fog signal.[^5] The new tower is a ten-foot-square granite shaft; its blocks are laid in alternating lengths of eight and ten feet, which the nomination calls unlike the masonry of any other New England lighthouse.[^1] A third-order Fresnel lens was installed in 1856, the old tower was taken down, and a keeper's house was built, of brick according to the nomination.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends gives the first lighting as 20 October 1856, with a fixed white light.[^5] An assistant keeper's post was added in 1857, and a second dwelling for him followed in 1898.[^5][^1]

Heights given for the 1856 tower differ. LighthouseFriends calls it 52 feet (16 m) tall; the Senate press release on the 2024 transfer calls it both a 64-foot (19.5 m) and a 68-foot (20.7 m) tower; and the US Lighthouse Society's archive gives 64 feet (19.5 m) as the height of the light above the water.[^5][^8][^9]

In the summer of 1899 the lighthouse changed. The Light-House Board's report records that on 26 June 1899 the order of the light was changed from third to fourth and its characteristic altered.[^10] The color of the tower was changed on 15 August 1899, according to the Board's next report; the nomination, by contrast, says the upper half was painted white in 1900.[^11][^1] An electric lamp went in during 1931.[^1]

**Fog signals.** The nomination calls Beavertail "closely associated" with the development of new fog signals.[^1] A 600-pound bell mounted in a small brick tower was installed in 1829, according to New England Lighthouses, and was used only about four years because the surf drowned it out; the Lighthouse Service Bulletin confirms the bell was set up in March 1829.[^3][^12] In 1851 a trumpet and a whistle designed by C. A. Daboll were installed on trial, worked by compressed air from a horse-driven pump; the nomination says that these and the steam whistle that followed were each the first installation of their type in the United States.[^1] An 1851 report, as New England Lighthouses quotes it, estimated that it would "require about one-third of the time (during fogs) to work the horse."[^3] Johnson's *The Modern Light-House Service* (1889) puts the setting up of the Daboll trumpet at Beavertail in 1856, five years later than the nomination and the Bulletin do.[^13]

A steam whistle was installed and tested in 1857, according to the nomination; it worked less well than the compressed-air signals and was replaced by a reed trumpet about 1866.[^1] The museum association's marker says the first Ericsson hot-air engine went in for the fog signal in 1857, while the Bulletin dates the hot-air engine's arrival to 1868.[^2][^14] In 1881 a steam plant with boilers in duplicate and two 10-inch whistles replaced it, the Bulletin says, and in 1888 the Crosby automatic controller, which times the blasts, was adopted, one of the first being installed at Beavertail.[^14][^15] The Board's reports of the 1890s list a 10-inch steam whistle with a Crosby automatic signal at the station.[^16] The Board's report for fiscal 1894 puts the whistle at about 574 hours and 55 tons of coal for the year; the report for fiscal 1899 puts it at about 724 hours and 55 tons.[^16][^17] The whistle gave way to a compressed-air siren, which the Board's report dates to 10 November 1899 and the Bulletin to 1900.[^18][^15]

## The keepers

The roster printed by the US Lighthouse Society's archive, drawn from the National Archives' keeper registers, begins with Abel Franklin, keeper from 1749 to 1755, and then jumps to 1770. Josiah Arnold served from 1770 to 1783, and William Martin from 1783 to 1803.[^9] LighthouseFriends notes that Franklin was also on the committee that built the first tower.[^5] New England Lighthouses reports that Martin kept the lantern glass clear of salt and was troubled by seabirds that broke it.[^3]

Philip Caswell kept the light from 1803 to 1816.[^9] When the hurricane of September 1815 shattered 20 panes of lantern glass, New England Lighthouses says, he sent his wife to a nearby farmhouse and stayed through the storm in the keeper's house with an invalid uncle; LighthouseFriends says instead that the storm destroyed the dwelling and that a five-room replacement went up the next year.[^3][^5] Sylvester R. Hazzard held the post from 1829 to 1844.[^9] When Carpender called in 1838 he found, according to LighthouseFriends's quotation of his report, that the keeper "had left home without having guarded against accidental detention" and the reflectors looked as if they had not been cleaned for days, which Carpender thought might have been owing to the painting and repairs just done to the tower.[^5]

Robert H. Weeden took the post in 1844, and his widow Demaris A. Weeden succeeded him in 1848 and kept it until 1857.[^9] The museum's marker calls her Beavertail's first and only female keeper, though it spells the names Damaris and Wheeler.[^19] New England Lighthouses says her teenage son helped with the work, and quotes a local superintendent who called her a "respectable well disposed woman of good habits."[^3] The same page quotes an 1851 report on a keeper who "generally gets up at 1 a.m. to trim lamps," and which New England Lighthouses reads as describing her son.[^3]

Silas G. Shaw served from 1858 to 1862 and again from 1863 to 1869, with Peter J. Lee between.[^9] Lee, his wife and their six children reached the point by sailboat, New England Lighthouses says, bringing a horse and their supplies with them.[^3]

William W. Wales kept the light for twenty-two years, from 1873 to 1895.[^9] He came from Middletown, Rhode Island, and had earlier kept Dutch Island Light, says New England Lighthouses, which also reports that the family became known for clambakes at the station and that two of his sons, John and George, served as assistants.[^3] The archive's roster shows George B. Wales as first assistant from 1888 and as head keeper from 1895 to 1900, and John S. Wales as first assistant from 1885 to 1888 and again from 1895 to 1914, then head keeper from 1914 to 1918.[^9] When Wales died in June 1895, aged 76, the Newport Daily News, as quoted by New England Lighthouses, reported that the flag of the Beavertail Fishing Club and several others on the island were "at half mast in his honor."[^3]

Joshua A. Overton kept the light from 1900 to 1914, and George T. Manders served as first assistant from 1914 and head keeper from 1918 to 1937.[^9] The museum's marker dates Manders's service as "1913 to 1937."[^19] It quotes him on the weather: "I went out to set the fog signal running and the wind threw me right across the yard, into a wall."[^19] Edward A. Donahue was first assistant for 28 years, from 1920 to 1948, then head keeper until 1953.[^9] Carl S. Chellis kept the light from 1938 to 1948, spanning the transfer of the lighthouses from the Lighthouse Service to the Coast Guard in 1939.[^9][^19]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

**The Rhode Island, 1880.** On the night of 6 November 1880, in a dense fog with little if any wind, the steamer *Rhode Island* ran ashore on Bonnet Point, northwest of Beavertail and less than 2 miles from the fog signal. The Light-House Board's report for 1894 says that the newspapers stated that the officers and several passengers swore the Beavertail signal was not sounding.[^20] An investigation made for the Board by a naval officer showed that it was sounding and was heard several miles away in several directions.[^20] Johnson names him as Lieutenant Commander F. E. Chadwick, assistant inspector of the third lighthouse district.[^21] Arnold Burges Johnson, the Board's chief clerk, wrote in 1889 that the loss was put at a million dollars and that the Board's investigation showed the signal in full operation when the wreck took place.[^22] Johnson used Beavertail in his chapter on the odd behaviour of sound in fog. In a dense fog on 6 August 1881, Johnson wrote, his party heard no trace of the signal at Whale Rock, about 1.5 miles away, and then, abreast of the point, "the sound of the fog-signal broke on us suddenly and with its full power."[^23] The Board's 1894 report quotes Johnson's later cruise around the point, from which he concluded only that the sound at Beavertail was "often not heard at Bonnet Point, which is less than 2 miles away, and it is in plain sight."[^20]

**Shells, 1908.** According to LighthouseFriends, artillerymen at Fort Adams firing practice shells into the sea in December 1908 sent one close to the tower, one into the yard behind the keeper's house and one into the foundation of the tower, and the keepers ran for cover.[^5]

**The hurricane of 1938.** The hurricane that struck on 21 September 1938[^24] uncovered a circular stone foundation that had been buried for many years. The nomination says it is the base of the second tower, of 1755; the museum's markers call it the foundation of the first tower, of 1749.[^1][^6] One marker says the storm destroyed the fog signal building and that a new one was built next to the granite tower.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin listed Beavertail among the Rhode Island stations seriously damaged.[^25]

The storm also struck the keeper's family. The museum's marker says that Carl Chellis's son Clayton, his daughter Marion and six other children were riding a school bus home, that a huge storm surge hit as it crossed the causeway at Mackerel Cove, and that only the bus driver and Clayton survived.[^19] A 2018 retrospective in the Jamestown Press says the bus took the eight children who lived at Beavertail home last, that water on the causeway road had risen to the hubcaps when it started across, and that the driver, unable to go on, got the children out and had them hold hands; the surge broke the line, and seven of them died.[^24]

**The war.** During World War II the Rhode Island National Guard placed a searchlight, a mobile gun emplacement and range-finding equipment on the concrete cap over the old foundation.[^6]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1972, and the last keeper and assistant were transferred away.[^2] By 1977 both dwellings were unoccupied with the doors and windows boarded up, and vandalism had become a concern for the Coast Guard and local residents.[^1] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1977.[^1][^2]

In 1983 the Rhode Island Parks Association announced it would restore the assistant keeper's house as a lighthouse museum; the museum was announced in 1989 as a joint effort of the association, the state Department of Environmental Management, the Town of Jamestown and the Coast Guard.[^2] In 1991 the Fresnel lens was removed and replaced by a rotating aero beacon, and in 1993 the nonprofit Beavertail Lighthouse Museum Association was organized to run the museum.[^2] The first major restoration of the granite tower in 156 years was finished in 2009, and volunteers restored the 1905 oil house in 2010.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that the light's optic failed in 2019 and the Coast Guard replaced it, and that in February 2020 the perimeter road between the lighthouse and the shore was closed to vehicles because of erosion.[^5]

The property became available for transfer under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000, and in June 2024 it was transferred to the Rhode Island Department of Environmental Management, which runs Beavertail State Park; officials of the General Services Administration, the Coast Guard and the National Park Service took part in the ceremony.[^8][^5]

## Today

Beavertail Light is owned by the State of Rhode Island and sits in Beavertail State Park in Jamestown.[^8] The Coast Guard still maintains the light as an aid to navigation, and the museum association continues to run the museum.[^8][^5] At the 2024 transfer, the Senate press release described the museum as free and open to the public, with visitors offered the chance to climb the tower for views, and LighthouseFriends reports that the museum, in the keeper's and assistant keeper's quarters, opens seasonally, so it is worth checking the association's schedule before a visit.[^8][^5] The fog signal building houses an aquarium display run by the state, and the old stone foundation uncovered in 1938, restored in 2012 and capped with concrete the following year, has a museum association marker beside it.[^5][^6]
