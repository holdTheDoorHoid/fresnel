Big Tub Lighthouse stands on the west side of the entrance to Tobermory Harbour, on Georgian Bay at the tip of the Bruce Peninsula.[^1][^8] The Department of Marine had its hexagonal wooden tower built in 1885, and the light was automated in 1952.[^1] In its first two decades it had a keeper who vanished, another dismissed for absence, and a third sacked after the light suffered for want of a keeper.[^1]

## The place

The Commissioners of Public Works recommended a formal light at Tobermory in 1857, but the harbour stayed dark until 1881.[^1] Its entrance looked much like the rest of the shore, so strangers had trouble finding it, and steamers on the Georgian Bay route sheltered there in bad weather.[^1] The register says the tower marks the harbour entrance and is directly associated with the federal programme of navigational aids on the Upper Great Lakes.[^2]

Charles Earl started showing a light in 1881.[^1] He hung a lantern on a tree branch at night to guide vessels through the passages that led into the harbour, and the Department allowed him $100 a year for keeping it.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1885 the Department bought three lots on the west side of the harbour entrance for eighteen dollars.[^1] That year John George and David Currie of Port Elgin built a wooden tower for $675 on the point where Earl had hung his lantern.[^1]

The 1885 departmental description, as LighthouseFriends prints it, gave a hexagonal wooden tower with an iron lantern, 13 m (43 ft) from the ground to the lantern vane. Its fixed red dioptric light stood 12 m (40 ft) above the lake and could be seen eight miles from all points of approach.[^1]

## The keepers

Abraham Davis was appointed keeper in 1885, replacing Earl, who had been in temporary charge.[^1] He held the post for ten years, until he disappeared in 1895.[^1]

His widow, Flora Davis, applied for the post with a set of endorsements from local people. The endorsers said she had performed all the duties for five or six years, because her husband was well advanced in years and in poor health.[^1] The local member of Parliament urged the Department to appoint their son, Henry Bradley Davis, and it did so, noting that it was against regulations to place a woman in such a position.[^1]

In 1898 a local official complained that Keeper Davis was farming out the work of tending the light.[^1] Davis first denied it, then sent in his resignation, but the Department took no action until 1901.[^1] In 1901 the Superintendent of Lights, Patrick Harty, visited and found the keeper absent and a man named Henry Martin in charge. Davis was dismissed, and Daniel Butchart was hired in his place.[^1] The pay was $150 a year, and the keeper's only accommodation was an old cow shed.[^1] Butchart was sacked in 1903, when the light was suffering for want of a keeper.[^1]

Archibald Currie was hired in 1903 at $250 a year.[^1] The Department's 1904 report includes an alphabetical list of lightkeepers and their stations, which places "Currie, Archibald" at Tobermory.[^3] The report for the year to 30 June 1905 says that "a new dwelling for the lightkeeper was erected at this station."[^4] It was a wooden frame building, on masonry foundations, with its main part 5.1 m by 9.8 m (16 ft 9 in by 32 ft).[^4] A 1907 letter claimed that Currie had not lived in the new house but had slept at his farm.[^1] A local official defended him, saying he had to tend a farm, since "his family would starve on his meager salary."[^1] In 1910 Currie was given a hand fog horn, to be sounded in answer to signals from nearby vessels.[^1] The Department's 1912 report lists fog signals, and one line reads "Tobermory. — Hand fog horn."[^5]

Currie stayed until 1912, when John Henry Smith, a Baptist fisherman from St. Catharines, took over.[^1] An inspection in July 1914 found Smith actually present at the station, which LighthouseFriends says had rarely happened with earlier keepers. Smith resigned in 1926, aged eighty, because he was too ill to keep the light.[^1] The last keeper was Thomas Andrew Hopkins, a veteran of the First World War, who served from 1926 until the light was automated.[^1] In 1929 the apparatus was upgraded from a seventh-order lens to a fourth-order lens.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On a Wednesday night in November 1895, Abraham Davis set off in a small skiff toward the wrecked vessels *Owen Sound* and *Worts*. He told his wife he would be back about 3 a.m.[^1] The Kingston News of that month, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, reported that he had not been seen or heard from since and was feared drowned.[^1] The sources do not say where the two vessels lay.

On 26 November 1910 the steamer *Alaska* was totally destroyed by fire at Tobermory harbour. The cause of the fire was unknown.[^6] The report places the fire in the harbour and does not link it to the light.[^6]

On 24 August 1922 the steamer *Kearsarge* grounded on Yeo Island, in the vicinity of the Tobermory station, according to the Department's report for the year to 31 March 1923. She had no wireless, but a party went ashore in a small boat and reached the Tobermory station at 3 p.m. The *Kearsarge* was released at 4 p.m. on 27 August and went on to Depot Harbour under her own steam.[^7] The report does not say whether "the Tobermory station" was this light.

## After the keepers

The light was electrified and automated in 1952, when Hopkins's service ended.[^1] In 1985 a pathway and a viewing area were built around the lighthouse. LighthouseFriends credits Friends of Fathom Five, and the Lighthouse Directory credits the Friends of Bruce District Parks, which it says improved the grounds and put in a walkway.[^1][^8]

A winter storm in 1987 washed away part of the foundation and some of the tower's shingles, and repairs followed.[^1][^8] The Lighthouse Directory dates the federal heritage recognition to 1992. LighthouseFriends says it came in the early 1990s.[^8][^1]

In 2012 residents of Tobermory petitioned the federal government for ownership of the lighthouse, which was one of many made available under the Heritage Lighthouse Preservation Act.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard still owns the lighthouse. The Lighthouse Directory names the Municipality of Northern Bruce Peninsula as site manager and says the municipality has applied for ownership.[^8] No outcome for the petition is recorded in these sources.

Visitors drive Highway 6 to Tobermory and follow Big Tub Road around the harbour for about 2.2 km to its end. The grounds and the parking area are open, but the tower is closed to visitors.[^1][^8] A storm in November 2019 damaged the lighthouse, and repairs were under way in the summer of 2020.[^8]

LighthouseFriends still lists the light as fixed red, with a focal height of 13.2 m (43 ft).[^1]
