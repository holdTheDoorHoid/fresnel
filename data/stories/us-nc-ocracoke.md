Ocracoke Light stands in Ocracoke Village, at the southern end of Cape Hatteras National Seashore, in the state of North Carolina.[^1] It was built in 1823, and the Park Service calls it the oldest operating light in North Carolina. The National Park Service holds title to the tower and about two acres and uses the keepers' quarters, while the Coast Guard still owns and operates the light.[^1][^2]

## The place

The Park Service says Ocracoke Inlet was the only reasonably navigable waterway for ships bound for inland ports such as Elizabeth City, New Bern and Edenton.[^1] The village beside it, Pilot Town, grew up around the pilots hired to steer ships through the shifting channels, who settled there from the 1730s.[^1]

The first light for the inlet was not on Ocracoke. Construction began in 1794 on Shell Castle Island, a shell-covered island between Ocracoke and Portsmouth, and a wooden tower there was finished four years later, in 1798.[^1] By 1818 the main channel had moved nearly a mile away, and that year lightning destroyed the lighthouse and the keeper's house.[^1] The nomination says the North Carolina General Assembly authorized a lighthouse for Ocracoke in 1789, but construction did not happen, because lighthouses had passed to the federal government.[^2] In 1802 $14,000 was appropriated for a light vessel to replace the Shell Castle light, and the nomination says the vessel "did not prove satisfactory either."[^2]

## Building the light

On 7 May 1822 Congress authorized $20,000 for a lighthouse at the south end of Ocracoke Island.[^1] The Park Service says a two-acre plot was bought for $50 from Jacob Gaskill.[^1][^2] The contract was signed on 20 June 1823 and awarded to the builder Noah Porter.[^1] The nomination gives the cost as $11,359.35 and says the tower was built in 1823.[^2] The Park Service says the lighthouse and its keeper's quarters were finished by December of that year.[^1]

The first lighting is less certain. The Park Service says correspondence shows the light was first lit on 15 August 1824. It also says disputes between the government and the contractor over construction methods and final payment delayed the appointment of the first keeper.[^1]

The original keeper's dwelling beside the tower was a one-story brick house of three rooms, with walls 16 inches (41 cm) thick.[^2] A fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the reflector apparatus in 1854.[^1] The nomination says Franklin lamps replaced the old valve lamps in 1860.[^2]

When the Civil War came, the lens was taken down. The Park Service says Confederate troops dismantled it early in the war, and that Union forces reinstalled it in 1864.[^1] The nomination gives a different order of events: it says the lens had been removed by 1862 and that the light was re-exhibited by 1863.[^2] The two sources do not agree on the date.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists twelve lines of service at Ocracoke, from Anson Harker in 1824 to Clyde M. Farrow in 1954.[^7] Harker kept the light for twenty-three years. The Park Service says that by the summer of 1824 he had arrived at the station.[^1][^7] Enoch E. Howard kept the light from 1862 to 1897.[^7]

The Board's report for the fiscal year 1896 describes work on the dwelling: the roof was taken off and a full story added, which the report says provided three good rooms.[^3] The nomination dates the same work to 1897.[^2]

As duties grew, the Park Service says, an assistant keeper's position was created, and the quarters gained a second story in 1897 and another section in 1929.[^1] Wesley L. Austin was head keeper from 1912 to 1929.[^7] The Bulletin's index for 1918 to 1923 lists an efficiency flag awarded to Wesley Austin, keeper at Ocracoke, on page 275.[^8] The archive records a first assistant only from 1929, when Crawford R. Austin is listed in that post.[^7] Joseph M. Burrus took over the light in 1929 and kept it until 1946.[^2][^7]

A J. M. Burrus is listed as keeper at Bluff Shoal in the Bulletin's 1924–1929 index, which places him at the Philadelphia exposition.[^9] A 1927 Bulletin list of keepers from the fifth district gives "J. M. Burrus, Bluff Shoal, N. C."[^10] No source connects him with the Ocracoke keeper.

The nomination quotes Burrus, from a 1951 newspaper article, on the daily work: "the great event of each day used to be climbing the spiral stairs at sundown to trim the wick, fill the reservoir, polish the reflectors and light the lamp."[^2] Clyde M. Farrow was the last keeper. The light became fully automatic in 1954.[^1][^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's account of the hurricane of 2 and 3 September 1913 lists Ocracoke among the light stations damaged.[^4]

Keeper Wesley Austin gave shelter to residents of Ocracoke Island who had been driven from their homes by high tides, the Bulletin says.[^5] Putnam's history, published in 1917, gives a different account. He says twenty-seven people took refuge in the light-tower after their dwellings were destroyed in the storm.[^6] The two accounts give different reasons for the flight, and the Bulletin gives no count.

The nomination says that in bad storms townspeople gathered in the keepers' quarters on high ground until the water went down.[^2] The Park Service says that during hurricanes villagers sometimes came to the keepers' home by boat and waited out the storm there.[^1]

After the 1944 hurricane the tower's stairway was removed and replaced with ladders, and the coal shed collapsed.[^2] During the Second World War Coast Guardsmen stayed in the quarters and kept a round-the-clock watch from the top of the lighthouse.[^2] In 2019 Hurricane Dorian flooded the keepers' quarters with 18 inches (46 cm) of water.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Park Service and the nomination disagree on when the light was electrified. The Park Service says a generator electrified it in 1929.[^1] The nomination says that in 1938 the oil vapor lamps were replaced with electric bulbs.[^2]

The Coast Guard took over the lighthouse service in 1939, and the light became fully automatic in 1954, when the last keeper left.[^1] The nomination says the quarters went to the Park Service in 1957 under a special use permit.[^2] The Park Service says the Coast Guard agreed in 1955 to let the Park Service use and occupy the quarters.[^1] On 7 June 1999 the Coast Guard transferred title to the lighthouse and about two acres to the Park Service. The Coast Guard still owns and operates the light and its Fresnel lens, the Park Service says.[^1] The lighthouse was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1977.[^1][^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is not open for climbing, because of its narrow spaces, small doors and ladders.[^1] During the summer the base of the tower is open for limited hours. The Park Service says to check at the Ocracoke Discovery Center for more information.[^1]

The Coast Guard still owns and operates the light and lens, and the Park Service and Coast Guard maintain the station together as a historic site.[^1] After Hurricane Dorian the Park Service began a project to raise the keepers' quarters four feet (1.2 m). Its page gives work from 2023 to 2025 as expected, not as finished, so this story does not say the work is done.[^1]
