East Point Lighthouse is a white octagonal wooden tower on the easternmost tip of Prince Edward Island, where the Gulf of St. Lawrence, Northumberland Strait and the Atlantic meet. It was lit in 1867 and is still an active light. It was moved in 1885 because the charts put it nearer the point than it was, and the Island's lighthouse society and heritage file say it was moved again in 1908.[^1][^2][^4]

## The place

Heritage documents describe the waters off the eastern end of the Island as hard to navigate, because three bodies of water meet there.[^2] The Island colony was reluctant to carry the whole cost of lights on North Cape and East Point. In 1851 its House of Assembly asked the Lieutenant Governor to find out what share Canada, the Atlantic colonies and Britain would contribute to lighthouses at North Cape and East Point, since foreign shipping would benefit more than the colony's own.[^1] Petitions from ship owners and masters followed in 1860.[^1] The Island's lighthouse society adds that the Colonial Government could not afford the lights on its own.[^4]

## Building the light

Money for a North Cape tower was granted in 1865. The next year the Province of Canada provided $1,500 toward East Point, alongside the Island's own money and a contribution from New Brunswick.[^1] William MacDonald built the dwelling and the octagonal tower in 1866-67.[^1] The land is said to have been given by Alexander Beaton in exchange for the post of first keeper, and a survey of it was made in January 1866.[^5] The tower is 19.5 m (64 ft) tall, timber-framed and shingled, with its lantern painted red; the Island's lighthouse society counts 67 steps inside.[^3][^1][^4] It was the last lighthouse built on the Island before it joined Confederation in 1873.[^1]

In 1877 the Canadian superintendent of lighthouses visited and found the tower fit to carry a new lantern, though he disapproved of how the lights and premises were kept. The next year it was judged not strong enough for a new lantern and lighting apparatus, so improvements to the tower had to be made first. A new revolving white light, at its brightest every three minutes, went into service on 1 June 1878; the tower work, lantern and apparatus cost $3,474.66.[^1] In 1909 the signal became a white flash every fifteen seconds, burning petroleum vapour under an incandescent mantle.[^1] The tower's fourth-order lens was removed in 2000 and replaced with a modern optic.[^4] The light today is a flashing white light of about 11 nautical miles' range, 27.1 m (89 ft) above the water.[^4]

## The keepers

Alexander R. Beaton kept the light from 1867, with a break in 1871-72 when Angus MacDonald held it, until 1897.[^4] The station's own site says he had studied at a seminary in Montreal, that he and his wife Clementine raised an orphaned local girl, and that he was also the local postmaster.[^6] It repeats a story that when a Charlottetown official asked whether he was "the keeper of the light" he answered that "the light keeps me".[^6]

Lauchlin MacDonald took over in 1897. The site says he taught school, farmed, and sat in the Legislative Assembly, elected in a by-election in December 1875.[^6] Ronald J. MacDonald followed in 1908, by the lighthouse society's list; the station's own site has Lauchlin serving until 1911 and Angus MacIntyre succeeding him in 1912.[^4][^6] Angus C. MacIntyre kept from 1912 and handed over to his son, Wilbert Stewart MacIntyre, in 1926.[^4][^6] Harry James Harris was the last keeper and retired in 1989, when the station was de-staffed.[^4] The station's site says that in winter being "storm stayed for days" was a real possibility for Harris, and that the Harris family descend from survivors of a wreck off East Point in 1822.[^6]

The sources disagree on the dates after 1926. The Island's lighthouse society and Lighthouse Friends put the change from Wilbert MacIntyre to Harris at 1957 and 1963 respectively; the station's own site gives 1960 and 1961.[^4][^1][^6] Treat those years as uncertain.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the night of 12 September 1882 the British warship HMS Phoenix, bound from Quebec to Halifax, ran hard aground on the reef off East Point in a gale.[^1] Heavy seas kept her crew aboard until the morning of the 14th.[^1] Some officers were looked after at the keeper's house, and the ship's surgeon delivered a baby for a local family, who named her Mary Ellen Phoenix McDonald.[^1] The Island's heritage file says the ship could not be refloated even after many of her guns were removed, and that she capsized.[^2]

A court of inquiry blamed the ship's navigation, but many thought the real fault was the light's position, since charts showed it at the tip of the point when the tower stood about half a mile (0.8 km) inland.[^1][^2] The SS Quebec had grounded in nearly the same place three years earlier.[^1] In 1885 the tower and dwelling were moved 488 m (1,600 ft) east, to about 61 m (200 ft) from the point. Bernard Creamer was paid $2,836 and slid the buildings along greased timbers with horses and a capstan.[^1][^2]

## After the keepers

A fog alarm building went up in 1885, with two compressed-air horns sounding an eight-second blast every thirty seconds.[^1] The Department of Marine's report for the year ended 30 June 1906 records the signal changed to one five-second blast every minute, and an inspection on 24 January 1907 found the plant "in poor condition", with two new boilers being built to replace condemned ones.[^7][^8] Sources disagree on 1908: the Island's heritage file and lighthouse society say the tower was moved back again, about 200 ft (60 m), to clear room for a larger fog building, but Lighthouse Friends mentions only a new fog building at that date.[^2][^4][^1] A radiobeacon began operating on 21 November 1935.[^1]

A new two-storey dwelling was built in 1923. Two one-storey, three-bedroom houses followed in 1966. The two-storey house was damaged by fire in 1972, which also badly burned a keeper.[^1][^4] The fog alarm was discontinued in 1999.[^1] The Friends of Elmira, leasing the station from 2002, received ownership in 2010, and the Island recognised it as a heritage place in 2012.[^1]

## Today

The tower is an active aid to navigation. The fog alarm building was moved near the tower in 2007 (the lighthouse society says 2008) and now holds a craft and gift shop.[^1] The grounds are open in summer, mid-June to Labour Day, on Lighthouse Road off Route 16.[^4] Sources differ on whether the tower can be climbed: the Island's lighthouse society says visitors can climb to the top, while Lighthouse Friends lists the tower as closed.[^4][^1] Check with the Friends of Elmira before visiting.
