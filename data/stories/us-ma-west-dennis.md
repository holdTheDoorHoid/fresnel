West Dennis Light stands at the mouth of the Bass River on the south shore of Cape Cod, on Nantucket Sound. Built in 1855 as the Bass River Light, it has its lantern on the roof of the keeper's house, and today the house is the Lighthouse Inn.[^3][^4][^5] The federal light was shut down in 1880, relit in 1881, put out in 1914, and relit in 1989 by private owners.[^2][^4]

## The place

The Dennis Historical Society says the light guided an active fishing and packet fleet for about sixty years.[^4] A shoal had built up at the river mouth. In 1836 Congress appropriated $10,000 to study the shoals and remedy them.[^2] The plan was a stone breakwater, with a lighthouse on it, to hold back the building of the shoal. The breakwater was abandoned when sand drifted to its inner side.[^2]

Before any federal light, the booklet describes a tradition that a light was kept privately in the attic window of Warren Crowell's home near the West Dennis beach.[^2] The earliest published notice of a light near the river mouth is a *Register* notice of June 1841, signed by Judah Baker, announcing a light on the beach "for the convenience of those vessels passing through the Sound."[^2] The booklet says each master who used the harbor gave 25 cents for the oil, once a year in one passage and once a month in another. New England Lighthouses says monthly.[^2][^3]

In September 1850 Congress appropriated $4,000 for a light.[^2] A revenue officer reported that a light was unnecessary and the plan was dropped, but the owners of the vessels moored at Bass River protested. Congress reappropriated the money on 3 March 1853.[^2]

## Building the light

In February 1854 the government bought a lot on the West Dennis beach from a shoemaker and his wife for $75, according to the booklet.[^2] New England Lighthouses dates the purchase to March 1854 and names only George W. Richardson as the seller.[^3] Construction began on 1 May 1854, with several teams of oxen hauling materials over the salt marsh and through the dunes.[^2][^3]

The house with the tower on its roof was finished in the winter of 1855, and a brick oil house was built beside it.[^2] The light showed a fixed white light. New England Lighthouses and the booklet's 1914 entry give a fifth-order Fresnel lens, but the U.S. Lighthouse Society's place record gives a fourth-order lens installed in 1855.[^3][^2][^1] The booklet gives both 30 April and 1 May 1855 as the first lighting; New England Lighthouses gives 30 April.[^2][^3] James Chase was its first keeper.[^2][^1]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists six keepers, from James Chase in 1854 to Russell Eastman in 1914.[^1] The booklet says payroll records show Chase paid $350 a year beginning on 20 June 1854, and that some accounts name Warren Crowell as the first keeper.[^2]

Zelotes Wixon took over in July 1861.[^1][^2] The booklet, citing D'Entremont's history, says Wixon complained that Chase gave him no training and refused him access to the light, and that he accused Chase of adulterating the oil.[^2] The booklet gives Wixon $500 a year from payroll records; New England Lighthouses says he was paid $350, like Chase.[^2][^3]

Warren Crowell, the man of the attic-window tradition, served in the Union Army during the Civil War.[^2] The booklet says he was wounded and captured by the Confederate Army and returned home in 1869 to become keeper.[^2][^1] He moved into the lighthouse with his wife and their children, eventually nine, and kept the light until August 1880.[^2]

Samuel Adams Peak was appointed keeper on 1 July 1881 and served until his death in 1906.[^2][^1] New England Lighthouses says he had gone to sea as a boy and served as master of two barks before becoming a lighthouse keeper.[^3] The roster lists a Miron R. Peak for 1906, a single year.[^1]

Russell B. Eastman was the last keeper, from 1906 to 1914.[^1][^2] The booklet quotes a 1972 memoir describing him as "taciturn and unceasingly busy keeping the Lighthouse."[^2] When the light was put out he was transferred to Ned's Point Light in Mattapoisett.[^2][^3]

## After the keepers

The light was discontinued on 1 August 1880, after the new light at Stage Harbor in Chatham was built.[^2] The lighthouse and its land were sold at auction on 1 September 1880 for $400 to Hiram Loring of Dennis.[^2] Loring sold the light and land back to the United States for $400 on 11 June 1881.[^2]

The booklet tells a story of the 1881 relight. In early 1881 Captain William Garfield of West Dennis wrote to his cousin James Garfield, who had just been elected President, asking for the light to be restored.[^2] New England Lighthouses calls him a distant relative.[^2][^3] The booklet, quoting a 2010 newspaper article, says the President invited the Captain and his two daughters to the White House, and that on 1 July 1881 he told the Captain the light was being relit at that very moment. New England Lighthouses says they were invited to dinner that day.[^2][^3] The booklet's only citation for the letters and the visit is that 2010 article.

The light was put out on 15 June 1914, after the Cape Cod Canal opened, and its last fifth-order lens was removed.[^2][^3] The Lighthouse Inn's own history ties the light's end to the canal's opening in 1914.[^5] New England Lighthouses says a new automatic light was set on the Bass River west jetty.[^3] The Lighthouse Service's bulletin for 1914 lists a Bass River light with an acetylene lens lantern among the aids established that year,[^6] and the 1916 report lists a Bass River West Jetty acetylene light among its lights in commission.[^7]

After the light went out, William H. Brown of West Dennis bought the property, and a few years later sold it to Harry Noyes, of the Noyes Buick Company in Boston.[^5][^2] Noyes turned the property into a summer estate, expanding the main house and adding buildings.[^5] The booklet gives both 1915 and 1917 as the auction year.[^2]

Noyes died in 1933.[^5] The property was then on the market for five years, until developer Everett Stone and his wife Gladys bought it in 1938.[^5][^2] Gladys decided to take in overnight guests to help pay the mortgage, and many of those guests asked to come back. The Stones gave up their plan to develop the land, and the house became the Lighthouse Inn.[^5][^2][^3]

## Today

The light was relit on 7 August 1989, to mark the 200th anniversary of the Lighthouse Service.[^2][^5] It flashes white every six seconds.[^2] According to the booklet, the Coast Guard recognizes it as a privately run working light, and the booklet claims no other lighthouse in the country is run that way.[^2]

The light is part of the Lighthouse Inn, an inn and restaurant at 1 Lighthouse Inn Road.[^4][^8] The inn is now run by the third generation of the Stone family.[^5] The grounds are open to the public, according to New England Lighthouses.[^8] The tower belongs to the inn, not to a museum, so ask the inn before you go, and do not climb the tower without its permission.[^1]
