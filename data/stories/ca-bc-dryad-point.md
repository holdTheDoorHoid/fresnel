Dryad Point Lighthouse stands on the northeast corner of Campbell Island, in British Columbia's Central Coast, about three kilometres from the village of Bella Bella.[^9] It marks a tight turn where Lama Passage meets Seaforth Channel on the Inside Passage.[^1][^2]

## The place

The Canadian Register of Historic Places statement of significance describes Dryad Point as a coastal and range light at the northern entrance to Main Passage and Seaforth Channel, guiding mariners around "a potentially hazardous tight turn and low-lying lands."[^1] The point was first called Turn Point by Admiralty surveyors, for the sharp turn ships must make there. Lighthouse Friends says the name Dryad Point came in 1899, when the light was built, so that it would not be confused with Turn Point in the San Juan Islands. It adds that the name honours the Hudson's Bay Company brig *Dryad*, which carried materials and stores from Fort Vancouver for the founding of Fort McLoughlin, today's Bella Bella, in 1833.[^2] The register adds that the light supported the neighbouring villages after the 1898 Klondike Gold Rush by guiding ships safely at the junction of the two channels.[^1]

## Building the light

The first light was a small white wooden tower attached to the keeper's dwelling, with a red lantern, showing a fixed white light from a seventh-order lens. It was first lit on 7 November 1899.[^2] The Department of Marine's report for fiscal 1900 lists an appointment at "Dryad Point Light" on that date, and records an addition to the lighthouse "to afford better accommodation for the keeper," costing $441.46.[^3][^4]

Lighthouse Friends dates a hand-operated fog horn to 1901, and the Department's report of that period lists Dryad Point among stations with hand fog signals to answer vessels' signals.[^2][^5] In 1903 a red sector was added to the light. The report says mariners had complained that the light was so bright close aboard that they could not judge their distance in rounding the point.[^6]

The present tower is reinforced concrete. The annual report for 1919 says a concrete tower "is being built at Dryad point replacing the former wooden one," and the next year's report says it "was completed."[^7][^8] Lighthouse Friends and the federal heritage text both give 1919 as its date, while the reports show the work running into the following year.[^2][^9] The sources also differ on its size. The heritage text gives a height of 7.3 metres (24 feet), while Lighthouse Friends gives "twenty-five-foot (11.6 metres)", two figures that do not agree with each other.[^9][^2] The register calls it a square-tapered tower and an example of the Department's experiments with reinforced concrete at the turn of the century.[^1]

## The keepers

The first keeper was Captain Carpenter, who appears in the Department's list for the volume labelled 1902 as "Carpenter, C" with an appointment date of 7 November 1899 and a salary of $180 a year.[^10] The heritage register calls him a high-ranking leader of the Heiltsuk (Bella Bella) First Nation.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says he stayed until 1930, when a new dwelling and boathouse were built, and that a small foghorn plant replaced the hand horn in 1932.[^2] The report volume labelled 1911 still lists "C. Carpenter" at Dryad Point.[^11]

Lighthouse Friends says Carpenter recorded the story of a fur-trade ship attacked off Milbanke Sound, and that site links it to the 1805 attack on the Boston ship *Atahualpa*. That is the website's retelling; no primary record of Carpenter's account was consulted for this page.[^2]

Henry Bergen became keeper in 1964, according to Lighthouse Friends. He and his wife Angie lived at Dryad for nineteen years, raising their family there, and because the water near the point was calm the station had a dock from which the children went to school in Bella Bella, two miles away. His eldest son, Harvey, returned as keeper in 1996.[^2] The same site's list of head keepers gives Henry Bergen's years as 1964 to 1980, which does not match the nineteen years it describes in the text.[^2] Chris Mills was keeper in 1997, when a new foghorn and a prefabricated aluminium building topped with solar panels arrived, and the Coast Guard had announced that the station would be automated by March 1998.[^2]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says that the keepers' weather reports every three hours along the Inside Passage were largely responsible for the Coast Guard's decision to cancel the planned automation of several stations. It does not say whether Dryad Point was among them, but its list of head keepers continues past 1998, to 2019.[^2] The register describes the station as one that "has always been maintained by a lightkeeper" since 1899.[^1]

## Today

Lighthouse Friends says the lighthouse is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard.[^2] It has been a designated heritage lighthouse since 29 May 2015, with ten ancillary buildings that contribute to its heritage character, among them the 1962 senior dwelling, the 1981 junior dwelling and two keepers' greenhouses.[^1][^9] The Coast Guard's list of lights carries the station as the Dryad Point Sector Light, at the north end of Lama Passage.[^12] Lighthouse Friends reports the tower closed and the light best seen from the water, and the site has keepers' dwellings, so it should not be treated as open to visit. The register notes that kayakers, boaters and yachters pass it in the recreation season, so the light is best seen from the water.[^1]
