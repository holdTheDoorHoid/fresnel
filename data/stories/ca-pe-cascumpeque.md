Cascumpeque Light stands, or stood, at the entrance to Cascumpeque Harbour on Prince Edward Island's north coast.[^2] The official reports of the time spell it Cascumpec.[^2] The first light was lit there in 1854.[^1] The tower standing now is the one built in 1876, and it has been privately owned since 1968. The light that replaced it in 1968 was removed in 2005 and not replaced.[^1][^5]

## The place

LighthouseFriends traces the name to a Mi'kmaq word that it translates as a bold sandy shore.[^1] The harbour's entrance is a channel over a bar.[^3] The Department of Marine's report for 1901 refers to "the bar at the mouth of this harbour."[^3]

The first light was built on Sandy Island, on the north side of the entrance.[^1][^5] In 1848 a petition from John Le Cost, a Cascumpec mariner, asking for money to help him erect a lighthouse was presented to the Island's House of Assembly. The petition was tabled.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a petition by "divers inhabitants" in 1853 seems to have had the desired effect.[^1]

## Building the light

The light was built on Sandy Island and put into operation in 1854.[^1] Asa McCabe was paid for the construction, and Patrick (Richard) Keefe was compensated for building the keeper's dwelling. Prospere Gallant was appointed the first keeper.[^1]

The light did not serve well. In October 1855 Silas Barnard, Superintendent of Public Works, tried to improve it, and his report to the Assembly said the tower was too small: "the upper part of the Tower is so small that the man has no room to work at trimming the Lamp."[^1] John Smallwood replaced the tower in 1856.[^1] In 1874 a General Superintendent of Lighthouses found the tower and dwelling too decayed to repair. James Keefe built a new combination lighthouse and dwelling, and a range light, for $1,900 in 1876.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the 1876 tower as 12.5 m (41 ft) high. Rowlett gives it as about 15 m (49 ft).[^1][^5]

Erosion was the continuing problem. In 1889 and 1890 brush and stone protection went onto the island.[^1] In the winter of 1899 the light was moved about 695 m (2,280 ft) south across the harbour entrance, to a more protected location, at a cost of $576.72.[^1] Rowlett gives the move as 1899 and says the tower was relocated to the south side of the entrance.[^5] The Marine report for 1901 places the main light "when on Sandy island," in the past tense.[^3]

The channel over the bar was marked by range lights hoisted on masts.[^2] In the fiscal year ended June 1900, the masts were replaced by two towers on the sand hills south of the entrance.[^6] The front tower was 5.5 m (18 ft) from base to vane, and the back tower was 7 m (23 ft).[^6] The buildings were erected by the agent, under the supervision of Mr. Milton Walsh, at a cost of $183.[^6]

A gale on 29 October 1879 washed the front range light off its foundation and did considerable damage to the tower.[^1]

In the year to June 1901 the channel over the bar shifted. The Marine report blamed "last winter's storms" and said that in May 1901 the range lights on the south sand hills were discontinued. The buildings were later removed to Sandy Island, on the north side of the entrance, where the range lights were put in operation on 11 July 1901.[^3]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends gives the keepers in order. Prospere Gallant served from 1853 to 1863; LighthouseFriends dates his service from a year before the light was in operation.[^1] William Hubbard served from 1864 to 1867. Asa McCabe followed from 1867 to 1879, and LighthouseFriends lists "Mrs. Asa McCabe" as keeper from 1879 to 1880. John McCabe served from 1880 to 1894, and F. J. Cahill from 1894 to 1897.[^1]

James C. Tuplin was keeper from 1897 to 1912. A Marine list of light-keepers gives "Sandy Island, (Cascumpec)" as his station, with "Jas. C. Tuplin" appointed "May 5, 1897" and a salary of $360.[^4] The list names the station as Sandy Island and does not say whether that is the main light or its range lights. LighthouseFriends ties Tuplin to the main light.[^1][^4] After him came D. Fraser (1912 to 1913) and William H. Mallet (1913 to 1944). LighthouseFriends also lists Justin F. Mallett for 1943 and 1944, Maurice Perry for 1944, and Wilfred Richard Gaudin from 1944 to 1967, the last keeper on the list.[^1] Its years overlap for these names, and it does not explain why. This story does not treat Mallet and Mallett as one person.

## Today

In October 1968 a new light went into operation near the old tower. It was a square skeleton tower topped by a lantern.[^1] When the new tower went up, the old lighthouse was sold to a group of Charlottetown residents. Rowlett says they used it as a vacation retreat.[^5][^1] The new light was downgraded in 1999.[^1]

LighthouseFriends dates the deactivation to January 2004, after a winter storm had so damaged the sandy area that the light was taken out of service.[^1] Rowlett dates the storm that did the damage to December 2004, when erosion severely damaged the tower.[^5]

Rowlett says the new tower was demolished in June 2005, and that the light was not replaced.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the metal tower was removed from the site in 2005.[^1]

The 1876 tower is privately owned. LighthouseFriends says it is well maintained and, for now, appears safe from erosion.[^1] The island and the lighthouse are private. The grounds, dwelling and tower are closed.[^1][^5] Rowlett says the lighthouse is accessible only by boat, and that it is visible from the Northport Range Rear Light.[^5]
