The Michigan City Breakwater Light stands about half a mile offshore, at the northeast end of a breakwater that shelters the entrance to Michigan City harbor, on Lake Michigan in LaPorte County, Indiana.[^1] Its concrete tower is 23 feet (7 m) tall. Its red flash, every four seconds, is visible for six miles in clear weather.[^1][^2]

## The place

Michigan City lies at the southeastern end of Lake Michigan, about 30 miles (48 km) east of Chicago.[^3] Its harbor is at the mouth of Trail Creek, where sandy shoals limited the size of the vessels that could reach the port.[^3] In 1836 Congress appropriated $20,000 for harbor improvements and $5,000 to build and operate a lighthouse.[^3] The Corps of Engineers extended the piers in the 1840s.[^4] It then recommended a 1,000-foot (305 m) breakwater offshore, northwest of the entrance, and Congress rejected the proposal.[^4][^5] The breakwater that was finally built was 700 feet (213 m) long, and work on it began in the late 1890s.[^6]

## Building the light

A Congressional appropriation of 6 June 1900 provided for a light on the new breakwater. The Lighthouse Establishment then put up a simple square post with a fixed red light at the breakwater's northeast end.[^6] LighthouseFriends dates the breakwater light's establishment to 20 October 1904, the same day the new lights went into service.[^8]

In 1911 the Lighthouse Service replaced the lost light with a reinforced concrete tower.[^7] The tower is a pyramid on a concrete tank house, painted red at first.[^7] On top went a 200-millimeter American Gas Accumulator (AGA) buoy lantern, fueled by acetylene from tanks kept in the tank house. It showed a fixed red light visible for six miles.[^7] The nomination also calls the first lantern flashing in one passage, so the sources do not settle how it signaled.[^10] The first reinforced concrete light tower in the United States had been built at Point Arena, California, in 1908.[^3] The breakwater light followed three years later, and the nomination counts it among the earliest concrete aids of its kind.[^3][^7]

The lantern ran itself. A sun valve, invented by AGA in 1907, used a blackened metal rod. In the morning the rod absorbed sunlight and expanded, closing the gas line so the light went out. At dusk it cooled and contracted, and the valve opened again.[^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A violent storm in 1909 destroyed the pole-mounted light at the breakwater's end, and the breakwater went unmarked for two years.[^7] LighthouseFriends dates the loss to February 1909.[^8] A July 1909 item in the Lake County Times of Hammond, quoting the Michigan City Dispatch, reported that stone and timber had arrived to repair the detached breakwater, which had been "badly damaged by storm last winter."[^9] The sources record no wreck or rescue at the light itself.

## The keepers

The sources name no keeper of the breakwater light itself. After 1904 the 1858 lighthouse on the harbor housed the head keeper and two assistant keepers of the Michigan City Light Station.[^7] LighthouseFriends says those keepers were responsible for three lights and a fog signal. Its account of the October 1904 lights names three: the fixed white light at the outer end of the east pier, the fixed red lantern on the breakwater, and a fixed red light on the west pierhead.[^8] Reading that account, the breakwater light was one of the three.

Harriet E. Colfax was head keeper of the old lighthouse from 1861 to 1904.[^11] LighthouseFriends says she stepped down at 80 on 14 October 1904, six days before the old light she had kept for 43 years went dark.[^8] Thomas J. Armstrong, who succeeded her, was in charge of the station until 1918.[^8][^13]

How the breakwater light's acetylene tanks were changed is not recorded in these sources. The nomination says only that the light ran on its own, except that its tanks had to be replaced when they were used up.[^10]

## After the keepers

The acetylene lantern was replaced by an electric light on batteries.[^10] In the 1970s the light was upgraded with the existing 300-millimeter acrylic lens, and its batteries were recharged by a solar array.[^10]

The tower was painted red until 1969 and then white. A red horizontal band went on in 1999, and a red triangular dayboard was added around 2010.[^10] The Coast Guard's Aids to Navigation Team visits the light periodically for maintenance.[^12]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light. The Army Corps of Engineers owns the breakwater it stands on.[^1] The light is reached only by boat and is not open to the public.[^1] A sign on the tank house reads "Danger Restricted Area Keep Off."[^2] A date stamp on the nomination's cover page reads 9 June 2017.[^14]
