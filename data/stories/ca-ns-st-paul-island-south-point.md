St. Paul Island South Point Lighthouse marked the southwest end of St. Paul Island, in the Cabot Strait off the northern tip of Nova Scotia, from 1839 until the lights were discontinued in 2015. Its cast-iron tower, built in 1915, has since 2011 stood in front of a museum in Dingwall, on Cape Breton, far from the island.[^1][^2]

## The place

Parks Canada calls St. Paul Island the "Graveyard of the Gulf," and says it has been the site of numerous shipwrecks.[^1] Lighthouse Friends records an early wreck at the southwest end: the barque *Jessie* went ashore near the southwest tip of the island during a snowstorm on 1 January 1825. Its survivors reached the island, but heavy pack ice in the strait kept anyone from reaching them.[^2] In 1831 the province placed a frame house and provisions at a cove on the island's southeast side to help shipwreck victims, Lighthouse Friends says.[^2]

## Building the light

Parks Canada says the first lighthouses on the island, one at the northeast end and one at the southwest, were completed in 1839.[^1] Lighthouse Friends describes both as wooden octagonal towers forty feet high, with a fixed white light at the northeast and a revolving white light at the southwest.[^2] A fog bell had been used in thick weather at the southwest light since 1846, and a cannon fired every four hours was added a few years later.[^2]

A fire destroyed the first southwest tower in late 1914. Lighthouse Friends dates the fire to 17 November 1914, and Parks Canada to December.[^2][^1] The Dominion Lighthouse Depot staff designed its replacement, which was fabricated at the depot in Prescott, Ontario, and shipped by train and boat to the island. The tower is a prefabricated, cast-iron cylinder of 36 curved panels bolted together, with a 12-sided iron lantern, and it stands 8.2 m (27 ft) from base to vane.[^1] Parks Canada says it was completed in December 1915 and first lit early in 1916, and that it carried a fourth-order Fresnel dioptric lens. Its light was visible 18 nautical miles.[^1]

The lens was turned by weights on a cable that ran out of the tower and several feet down the cliff. When ice built up on the cable, Lighthouse Friends says, the keeper had to go out and beat on it with a large hammer.[^2]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends gives a roster of the southwest light's keepers from the 1840s to 1964. It lists John McKenzie for 1904 to 1910, Angus G. McNeil for 1913 to 1916, and Frank Huntley for 1916 to 1921, among others.[^2]

The government's list of rewards for saving life, printed in the 1906 Marine report, names "Roderick McLeod, keeper of the light on South West Point, St. Paul's Island, N.S., and his son Abraham McLeod."[^3] Lighthouse Friends does not list Roderick McLeod at all, and for 1904 to 1910 it names John McKenzie as keeper. The two sources do not settle who was keeper in May 1904.[^2][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Marine Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1904 says that the steamer *Turret Bay* "ran on the rocks near St. Pauls Island in May last and went down shortly after striking," and that "owing to prompt and courageous action on the part of the men at the station eight men were rescued."[^4] The report does not say which station it means, and it does not place the wreck at the southwest point.

A wreck inquiry printed in the same 1904 report, into the foundering of the *Turret Bay* at St. Paul Island on 20 May 1904, records "a loss of thirteen lives" from a crew the inquiry gives as 22.[^7] The 1906 reward list puts Roderick McLeod and his son Abraham against the rescue of the survivors of the *Turret Bay*, dated 20 May 1904, when the steamer foundered on Paddy Rock, St. Paul's Island.[^3]

## After the keepers

The southwest light was automated in 1964, when a round fibreglass tower replaced the cast-iron one, Lighthouse Friends says.[^2] Parks Canada places the cast-iron tower at the southwest corner of the island from 1915 to 1964, and at the Canadian Coast Guard base in Dartmouth from 1964 to 2011.[^1] At Dartmouth it stood for several years with a Fresnel lens in its lantern room.[^2]

The St. Paul Island Historical Society, formed in 2001, made various attempts to acquire the tower at Dartmouth.[^2] In October 2010 two members of the Nova Scotia legislature announced that it would move to the community museum at Dingwall. The tower was taken down in March 2011, sandblasted and painted in April, and trucked to Dingwall in May. In August 2011 its lantern room was set on a concrete base on the museum grounds. Lighthouse Friends puts the total cost at about $120,000, of which the federal government invested $108,000 through Enterprise Cape Breton Corporation.[^2]

Parks Canada's heritage lighthouse statement, report 2011-LR-043 dated August 2012, says the tower "is a heritage lighthouse because of its historical, architectural and community values."[^1] The island's two lights were discontinued in 2015. A centenary gathering for the tower was held at the Dingwall museum on 1 August 2015.[^2]

## Today

The tower stands in front of the St. Paul Island Museum, at 575 Dingwall Road in Dingwall. The museum is housed in a small nineteenth-century frame house.[^5][^2] Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^2] From the Cabot Trail, Dingwall Road runs 3.2 km to the museum.[^2] Parks Canada asks that the cylindrical cast-iron tower, its 12-sided lantern, its internal cast-iron stairs and its original fourth-order lens be respected.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends says a fibreglass tower replaced the cast-iron one on the island in 1964. Rowlett describes a steel tower that replaced a wood one burned in 1916, and says the island light has been removed. The sources do not agree on what, if anything, stands on the point today.[^2][^6]
