Pacific Reef Light is a steel tower on a coral reef in the Florida Keys, about three miles (5 km) southeast of Elliott Key.[^1] A temporary light was shown there in December 1921,[^2] and the automatic light that followed was built without keepers' quarters.[^3] The first tower was replaced in 2000, and its lantern room now stands in a park in Islamorada, near the southern end of Plantation Key.[^1]

## The place

The reef is one of the Florida Reefs, a chain of coral reefs and keys off Florida's southern coast.[^3] The Lighthouse Service's bulletin for 1923 says this light and the one on Molasses Reef were set roughly midway along two stretches of coast, 32 and 23 miles long, that had no lights. The stretches lay between the main lights at Alligator Reef, Carysfort Reef and Fowey Rocks.[^3] The bulletin says the new lights were meant to keep vessels off "these dangerous reefs." It gives their range as 12 miles, against 16 and 18 for the older lights, and does not say whether the miles are nautical.[^3]

By 1937 the Service's bulletin counted Pacific Reef among five unwatched lights built between 1921 and 1935 on reefs and shoals between the larger attended lights. It said they were of wrought iron and much smaller than the main lighthouses.[^4]

## Building the light

The reef lights went out to bid more than once. The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported in 1917 that two light towers, one for Molasses Reef and the other for Pacific Reef, had been approved, and that bids would be opened on 31 July 1917.[^5] The Service's bulletin for 1918 reported that another effort to get bids for the two towers had failed.[^6] By 1919 a contract for the metal work was under way.[^7] The Commissioner's report for 1920 gives the contract for the metal work and glass for the two towers as $62,864, with delivery within nine months.[^8]

In 1921 the metal work was delivered, and at Pacific Reef a construction platform was finished and the piles were being driven.[^9] The 1923 bulletin describes the method. A working platform about 32 by 42 feet (10 by 13 m) stood on 20 wooden piles driven into the reef, with its deck about 5 feet (1.5 m) above high water. Eight-inch wrought-iron piles were driven through openings in the platform and through cast-iron disks on the reef, and they carried the towers. The bulletin gives 50 days as the time spent erecting the Pacific Reef structure. On more than one occasion, the crews stayed on the job until seas and gales drove them off the platform.[^3] The Commissioner's report for 1922 gives the towers as 45-foot (14 m) iron skeleton towers erected on Pacific Reef and Molasses Reef.[^10]

A temporary light was installed on 15 December 1921. The bulletin for 1922 expected the permanent light to go into commission on 30 March 1922.[^2] The 1923 bulletin describes the two stations as recently completed and placed in commission, and the 1924 summary lists them among the year's completed works.[^3][^11] The sources do not give the date on which the permanent light first showed.

The Bulletin gives the cost of each station as about $36,500, at a time when materials and labor were very high after the war. It sets that against $185,000, $105,000 and $163,000 paid for the great lights between which the reefs lie.[^3] It also notes that the towers had no keepers' quarters "as they are automatic lights," and that they were "built exceptionally strong" to withstand the hurricanes that often visit the area.[^3]

## Today

The Lighthouse Directory says the original tower, which Rowlett describes as a square pyramidal steel tower on a screwpile foundation, was removed in 2000. The 1923 Bulletin describes the foundation differently: nine wrought-iron piles, driven through openings in the platform and through cast-iron disks on the reef.[^3] The sources do not reconcile the two descriptions. The replacement is a 44-foot (13.5 m) square steel skeletal tower with a gallery, which shows a white flash every four seconds.[^1] The first light's lantern room was moved to Founders Park in Islamorada, where it is displayed on a square stone pedestal off US 1 at mile marker 86.5.[^1]

The new tower stands on the reef about three miles out, inside Biscayne National Park. The Coast Guard owns it. Rowlett lists the site as open and the tower as closed.[^1] The reef is about three miles offshore, so reaching it means going by boat. The archive also records the station as not open to the public.[^12]
