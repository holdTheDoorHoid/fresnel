Apple River Lighthouse stands on Cape Capstan, at the entrance to the Apple River on the east side of Chignecto Bay, in Cumberland County, Nova Scotia.[^2][^3][^4] The white square tower there today is the third light on the point. The sources give different years for it, between 1965 and 1972.[^1][^2][^3] The first light on the point burned in 1869.[^1]

## The place

Apple River feeds into Chignecto Bay, which connects with the Bay of Fundy.[^1] Lighthouse Friends, quoting the provincial commissioners of lighthouses, reports that in 1838 they thought a light at the river mouth would help ships on that part of the bay. They did not think the legislature would be willing to build one, because the light at Cape Enrage was only about ten miles from the proposed site.[^1] A decade later, Lighthouse Friends says, the government changed its mind and built on Hetty Point, at the tip of Cape Capstan.[^1] The Preservation Society dates that first light to 1848.[^2] The sources do not name a particular wreck or hazard behind it.

## Building the light

The first light was a square white building that showed two fixed lights side by side. Lighthouse Friends gives its height as forty feet (12 m) above the water.[^1] The Preservation Society's record gives 64 feet (19.5 m) for the same light.[^2] The Preservation Society records a change in 1857, when the light was shown from two windows at the west end of the building and the lenses were catoptric, that is, mirror-based.[^2]

The tower that replaced the first one stood 30 m (100 ft) south-south-east of it.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says it went into service on 1 October 1870, after a temporary light had been shown, and that John Livingstone built it for $1,180.[^1] By the Preservation Society's account the light was changed to a fixed white light in 1874.[^2]

The fog signal came later. Lighthouse Friends says that in 1899 the fog alarm from St. Martin's Head, New Brunswick, was moved across Chignecto Bay to Apple River, and that George A. Briggs was transferred from there to look after it.[^1] The Preservation Society instead dates a fog signal here to 1889.[^2] The sources do not settle the year. The Department's 1906 repairs list shows $176.64 spent on the fog alarm at Apple River.[^9]

The optics changed twice in the Department's reports. In the report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901, the chief engineer said a dioptric apparatus would be fitted "making the light of more uniform intensity in all directions".[^5] The 1902 report says the catoptric apparatus "has been changed to dioptric of the seventh order".[^6] The Preservation Society dates a 5th-order dioptric lens with a steam fog signal to 1922–1941, and does not say which lens was in place between 1902 and 1922.[^2]

The present tower is dated variously. Lighthouse Friends dates it to 1968, when a three-metre-square tower went up against the corner of a one-storey fog building.[^1] The Preservation Society gives "c. 1965" and then "1966 - 1970" for the replacement.[^2] The Lighthouse Directory gives 1972.[^3]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends gives the keepers in order. Gaius Lewis, a former member of the Nova Scotia House of Assembly, kept the light from 1849 to 1858. Lighthouse Friends says complaints that his light was too faint to be seen well pushed Lewis out, yet in 1859 the government granted him twenty pounds for work he had done on its property.[^1] John Fowler followed from 1858 to 1863, and James Tate from at least 1867 to 1886.[^1]

Adam McCullough kept the light from 1887 to 1892. James E. Fowler, who was hired in 1894 to take charge of both the light and the fog signal, served until 1902, and Ernest E. Fowler from 1902 to 1905.[^1] Hill E. Elderkin came next, from 1905 to 1931.[^1] The Department's 1909 keepers' appointments give his start as 1905, and its 1910 table pairs him with the Apple River light and fog alarm.[^10][^13] Victor Elliott is listed from 1931 to 1965, and Marvin J. McNally from 1960 to 1972. Lighthouse Friends' dates for those two overlap between 1960 and 1965, and no source explains it.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The first tower burned on 9 August 1869, a Monday. Keeper James Tate, whose own account Lighthouse Friends prints, had cleaned the lamps and checked the house that morning, then left with his wife and children to cross the harbour on the bar, intending to come back on the tide. The wind was strong and the sea heavy, and he was held up. At about one o'clock he saw the house on fire. Eight men reached the point within half an hour, but the house and everything in it were lost.[^1] Tate wrote that he could not see how the house could have caught fire unless it had been set alight. Lighthouse Friends says he was not held responsible for the fire, but was reprimanded for leaving the light without anyone in charge.[^1] A temporary light was shown until the new tower was lit in October 1870.[^1]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the keepers' two dwellings were removed in 1973.[^1] The Preservation Society says the light was automated by 1973.[^2] In its report for the fiscal year 1922-23 the Department recorded a new type "B" diaphone at Apple River, with two 6-horsepower gasoline engines and two 8x6 compressors.[^12] Its report for 1923-24 describes another type "B" diaphone, this one with three pistons, installed "in place of old diaphone".[^11] The Preservation Society says the fog signal was discontinued by 1992.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the one-storey fog building was then torn down, leaving the tower, whose lantern room was later removed.[^1] In June 2019 the Coast Guard refurbished the tower and fitted a new lantern room on top.[^1][^3]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard's list gives the light a long flash of white light every twelve seconds (2 seconds on, 10 seconds dark), 20.8 m above the water, visible 9 nautical miles, from a white square tower 11.2 m high.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1] It adds that the light can be seen across the bay from Apple River Road, and that a logging road down the peninsula ends long before the light.[^1] The light stands on Coast Guard land.[^1] The sources do not say what the approach is like.
