A white brick tower with a flat, capped top stands on a grassy knoll in San Luis Obispo County, in sight of Highway 1 and visible from Hearst Castle.[^2] It was built in 1875 as a first-order seacoast light, and until 1949 it was taller and carried a glass lantern room on top.[^4][^2]

## The place

Piedras Blancas Light Station lies at the northern entrance to San Simeon Bay, 13 miles (21 km) north of Cambria.[^2] The National Register nomination says that before this light was built there was no lighthouse on the coast between Point Pinos at Monterey and Point Conception, a gap of some 145 miles (233 km).[^2] It ties the decision to build here to the wreck of the *Harlech Castle* on a jagged rock in 1868, and to the traffic of San Simeon Bay, which by the early 1870s was a whaling port that also shipped lumber, farm produce and supplies for the new cinnabar mines above Cambria.[^2] The Lighthouse Board's 1873 annual report, the nomination says, called Piedras Blancas one of the most important points left to light on the California coast.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $75,000 for the station.[^2] LighthouseFriends, which gives the date as 10 June 1872 and quotes a Lighthouse Board remark of 1871 that coasting steamers kept so far offshore that Point Pinos was never seen, says material for the tower was landed through the surf in April 1874, that a crew of about thirty worked on it, and that the rock of the point was "so hard as almost to resist the best tempered drills".[^3] The nomination names a Captain Ashley as builder.[^2] The tower is brick, with a Gothic portico, stone trim and a circular cast-iron stair inside; the nomination calls it the most ornately Gothic lighthouse built in California.[^2]

The Bureau of Land Management dates the first lighting to 15 February 1875; the tower was about 100 feet (30 m) tall and carried a first-order Fresnel lens; the BLM says its light flashed every 15 seconds.[^4] A Light-House Board report of the 1890s describes it differently: a brick tower 90 feet (27 m) high, the light 170 feet (52 m) above the sea, visible nearly 22 miles, a fixed white light varied by a white flash every 15 seconds, and a station cost of $90,000.[^5] The sources therefore disagree on the tower's original height (100 feet or 90 feet), and none of them explains the difference.

Funds ran short before the dwelling and fog signal were finished, and LighthouseFriends reports that the first keepers lived in the builders' shanties until a twelve-room, two-story dwelling was completed in 1876. The dwelling was meant for two keepers and housed three keepers' families.[^3] A fog signal followed at the start of the next century. A brick building 170 feet (52 m) west of the tower was commissioned on 10 April 1906 with an oil engine and a ten-inch whistle, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] The BLM instead says the fog-signal building was constructed in 1905 and first sounded in 1906.[^4] The nomination dates the fog-signal building and the oil house to 1906, while LighthouseFriends dates a concrete oil house to 1908;[^2][^3] the nomination also praises the fog-signal building's brickwork and varnished roof timbers, rare in such utilitarian buildings.[^2] In 1916 the light's characteristic was changed. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded the change from "fixed and flashing white to group flashing white",[^6] which the BLM describes as a lens modified to give a double flash every 15 seconds, and LighthouseFriends as a rearrangement of the lens panels.[^4][^3]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society archive, working from the National Archives keeper registers, lists Stephen H. Morse as first head keeper (1874 to 1879), followed by Lorin V. Thorndyke (1879 to 1906).[^1] LighthouseFriends adds that Morse was a Navy veteran transferred from the Farallon Islands, and that Thorndyke, born in Maine in 1839, had sailed around the world five times before taking the post. He married Elizabeth Jarmon soon after arriving, and two sons were born at the light.[^3] According to the same account, one of them, Lorin Jr., remembered a schooner from San Francisco anchoring near the white rocks in some summers of the 1880s to gather the guano, and the smell that drifted over the station.[^3] Thorndyke retired in 1906, at 67, giving the new fog signal's noise as his reason, though his son later admitted it did not really bother his father.[^3]

Later head keepers in the archive's list include Charles S. Kaneen (1906 to 1911), Andrew Czarnecke (1911 to 1930) and Norman Leo Francis (1934 to 1947; LighthouseFriends elsewhere gives 1934 to 1948).[^1][^3] The archive's list for the first year, 1875, includes a Zenobia A. Pico as second assistant; it gives that name and year and nothing more.[^1]

The assistants' housing was a problem. The Commissioner of Lighthouses' estimates for new works, in the reports of 1921 and 1922, said that the three assistants shared one two-story dwelling built for two, that the quarters were "dark and very poorly arranged", and that "great trouble has been experienced in keeping assistants on this station" because of crowding and quarrels between the assistants' families.[^7] The estimates asked for $5,400 and then $6,500 for improvements at the station.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Board's report for 1880, LighthouseFriends says, noted that on two occasions the year before large birds had crashed through the lantern panes, and falling glass had chipped the lens.[^3]

LighthouseFriends reports that on 23 December 1941 Keeper Francis wrote in the station log that a Japanese submarine had sunk the tanker *Montebello* off the station, and that everyone aboard escaped in lifeboats. On 1 April 1946 his log recorded "phenomenal changes of tide", which the account attributes to a tsunami from an earthquake in Alaska.[^3]

The tower's greatest damage came at the end of 1948. The BLM and LighthouseFriends date it to an earthquake on the last day of 1948, which left the upper tower structurally unsound; in 1949 the upper three levels, with the lantern room and its lens, were removed, leaving the tower about 70 feet (21 m) high.[^4][^3] The National Register nomination instead blames "a storm in 1949" that dislodged the lens and scattered the lantern room across the fields.[^2] The BLM and LighthouseFriends agree with each other and the nomination does not; no source here settles the matter.

## After the keepers

A rotating aerobeacon replaced the Fresnel light in 1949, and the BLM lists a 36-inch beacon from 1949, a 24-inch one from 1974, an emergency light from 1999 and a rotating marine beacon since 2002.[^4] The Coast Guard staffed the station until the tower was automated in 1975, according to LighthouseFriends; the USLHS archive's list of head keepers ends in 1975.[^3][^1] In 1960 the original Victorian triplex was razed and four ranch-style houses built, and the 1906 head keeper's dwelling was sold for $1 and moved to Cambria.[^3][^2] The Coast Guard transferred the station to the Bureau of Land Management in October 2001.[^3]

The lens had its own history. After removal, the Cambria Lions Club took it, and the BLM describes it as on loan to them and on display next to the Cambria Veterans Memorial Building.[^4] LighthouseFriends adds that a restoration effort begun in 1990 by Norman Francis, son of the head keeper of 1934 to 1948, returned the lens to a new lantern room, and that in 2021 control passed from the Lions Club back to the Coast Guard.[^3]

## Today

The station is managed by the Bureau of Land Management as a historic park and wildlife sanctuary. It is open only by guided tour, which starts at the Piedras Blancas Motel, 1.5 miles (2.4 km) north of the light; the BLM lists tours on set days of the week through the year, and visitors should check its page and reserve before going.[^8] The tower is listed on the National Register of Historic Places (reference 91001095).[^2] The grounds are not open to walk in on your own.[^8]
