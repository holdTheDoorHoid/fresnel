Gary Harbor Breakwater Light stands at the outer end of a timber-and-stone breakwater in Lake Michigan, off the steel works that U.S. Steel built at Gary, Indiana. The company lit it, not the federal government, and it is a steel tower on a private pier at the entrance to the harbor the works needed for its ore boats.[^1][^2]

## The place

Gary was built on lake-shore dunes, and its harbor was made rather than found.[^2] U.S. Steel bought the land in 1905 and formed the Indiana Steel Company to build and run the plant, and the work of leveling the ground was under way by the spring of 1906.[^2] A channel some 5,000 feet (about 1,500 m) long was dredged into the shore, and a Chicago firm, Great Lakes Dock and Dredge Company, was awarded the contract to build what would become known as Gary Harbor.[^2] An L-shaped breakwater ran out into the lake to shelter the entrance.[^1] Ships had to make a sharp turn around the end of the breakwater to line up for the channel, and Terry Pepper links the new light at the breakwater's end to that turn.[^2]

The federal government did not take the harbor on.[^2] Indiana's Congressman Crumpacker put wording into the 1910 River and Harbor Bill that asked for the harbor to be evaluated with a view to federal responsibility for its maintenance and lighting. The hearings found that the harbor served only the company and its subsidiaries, and the government was "not bound to accept any responsibility" for its upkeep.[^2]

## Building the light

The cross piers inside the harbor proved less than successful. Without federal help, "over 1910 and 1911 the company had no alternative but to build a substantial breakwater" from the west side of the harbor. It was built of "stone-filled timber cribs" with a pine-board deck.[^2]

The new light at the outer end stood on a concrete base.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives the tower as 30 feet (9 m) tall and of steel plates.[^1] Its lens lantern burned acetylene from tanks stored in the base, with its focal plane forty feet (12 m) above the water, and a sun valve lit and extinguished it automatically at dusk and dawn.[^1][^2] Both accounts date the first lighting to the night of 5 July 1911.[^2][^1]

The two accounts describe the 1911 signal differently. LighthouseFriends says it "exhibited two white flashes every four seconds," while Terry Pepper describes a repeated group of two flashes in a 15.7-second characteristic.[^1][^2] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive gives 1913 as the year the light was established. Because both accounts date the first lighting to 1911, the dates disagree.[^3][^1][^2]

The two pages also disagree about the fog signal. Terry Pepper says the 1911 structure "was also equipped with a six-inch air operated siren," which sounded a 2-second blast followed by 28 seconds of silence.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates a fog siren, which sounded a two-second blast every thirty seconds, to 3 October 1913, when it was established at the lighthouse.[^1] Its air came from an electric compressor on shore, and the air was "delivered 5,150 feet to the lighthouse through a two-inch pipe" (about 1,570 m).[^1]

No source used here names a keeper for the light. The company kept the light during the navigation season, and the sun valve did the nightly work.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 7 April 1914 a storm caught Sy Meredith, "an electrician for US Steel," at the end of the breakwater while he was making repairs to the light. LighthouseFriends says the company asked the South Chicago Lifesaving Station to bring him off, but the page's references do not cover the rescue.[^1] By that account, the South Chicago station's keeper and six surfmen arrived at 6 p.m. in their 36-foot (11 m) lifeboat, the Sea King. Ice was building up on their gear and heavy seas were washing over the breakwater, so they stayed by the light overnight and pulled Meredith aboard "on their third attempt the following morning."[^1] He was taken to the steel company's hospital.[^1] The sources used here tie no wreck to the light.

## Today

The pier and breakwater are on private property, and the lighthouse is owned by U.S. Steel. Its tower is closed, and LighthouseFriends advises seeing it from the water.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists it as not open to the public and not an active aid to navigation.[^3] Terry Pepper writes that the light is "virtually impossible to see from the land," because the breakwater and lighthouse are both on private property and large buildings stand between any public access areas and the light. He says a private boat, or a vessel chartered from a local fisherman, offers the only good view.[^2]

Terry Pepper's page, last revised in 2012, says the old 1911 light and its diaphragm horn take their power from an electrical conduit that runs out along the breakwater from shore, and that large ships still look for the light as they turn into the channel.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes a drum-style Fresnel lens still mounted in the lighthouse, and a horn that still sounds when needed.[^1] LighthouseFriends and Terry Pepper describe the light as still showing its signal, but the Society's listing says it is not an active aid to navigation.[^1][^2][^3]

The lighthouse was removed from the breakwater for a period in 2017 so it could be sandblasted, repaired and repainted.[^1]
