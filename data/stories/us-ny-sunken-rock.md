Sunken Rock Light is an iron-towered beacon on a rock in the St. Lawrence River, about half a mile from Alexandria Bay, New York.[^1][^3] The first light was shown there in 1847, although the archive's place record gives 1848.[^1][^2] The tower that stands now dates from 1882.[^2] It is still lit, and it is closed to visitors: the rock is seen from the shore at Alexandria Bay and from the tour boats.[^1][^2]

## The place

Sunken Rock is a mostly submerged obstacle near the eastern entrance to the narrows between Wellesley Island and the mainland, and the island it stands on is also called Bush Island.[^2][^3] On March 3, 1847, Congress appropriated $6,000 for three beacon lights in the Thousand Islands, one of them for this rock; the other two were for Crossover Island and Rock Island.[^2] The rock was bought from Chesterfield Pearson, by a deed dated April 12, 1847.[^2] The deed gave its area as 10,890 square feet (about 1,010 square metres), and only a fraction of that was above water.[^2]

## Building the light

That summer a brick beacon went up on a protective pier.[^2] Its tower stood 27 feet (8 m) from its base to the middle of the lantern room, where five lamps in 14-inch reflectors gave a fixed white light.[^2] In 1855 a sixth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps.[^2][^3]

The station had no dwelling for its keeper.[^2] By 1878 the keeper was renting a house in Alexandria Bay, half a mile away.[^2] That year the Light-House Board called the brick tower "an old and rough structure, in bad repair," and by 1880 it said the tower was past economical repair and should be replaced by an iron one.[^2][^3] On May 15, 1882, the old brick tower was torn down.[^2] A new round iron tower, built on the old foundation, showed its light on June 12.[^2][^3]

Later changes were to the colors. In 1899, at the opening of navigation, the light was changed from white to red, and the tower from brown to white.[^4]

## The keepers

The archive names fifteen principal keepers from 1848 to 1960.[^1] The first, James Merrill, served only in 1848.[^1] In December 1904 the Secretary of Commerce and Labor wrote to the Treasury about the station's living conditions.[^2] LighthouseFriends prints the letter from the Board's reports for 1905 and 1906.[^2] The letter gives the keeper's rent as about $120 a year out of a salary of $580, and the Board's opinion, as the letter prints it, was that "good men will not long remain in positions under such conditions."[^2] The Board proposed a dwelling and boathouse for the keeper on United States land on the river bank, but LighthouseFriends does not believe one was ever built.[^2]

Horace E. Walts came to Sunken Rock in 1912 from a post at Oswego and kept the light until 1937.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends tells two things about his time. His son Max recalled rowing to the light with him.[^2] And every morning, after the light was put out, Walts covered the lens with a white linen shroud to protect its prisms and their brass.[^2] Walts received the inspector's efficiency star for nine consecutive years, which LighthouseFriends records.[^2] Keepers were inspected each quarter, and a keeper commended at every quarterly inspection for a fiscal year earned the star for the succeeding year.[^2] Across the service, George Putnam's general history of 1917 says that keepers "with a high record of efficiency are entitled to wear an efficiency star."[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1912 keeper Frederick Lawson was commended for rescuing a boy from drowning.[^5] The Bulletin's 1914 volume records that on April 27 Walts gave food and shelter for the night to two men whose power boat had drifted.[^6] The same volume reports that on August 6 Walts rescued a man from a skiff, but it does not print a year.[^7] LighthouseFriends dates that rescue, and Walts's commendation for it, to August 6, 1916, and Thousand Islands Life also gives 1916, so the sources do not settle the year.[^2][^3]

On the night of November 22, 1974, the 640-foot (195 m) freighter *Roy A. Jodrey*, upbound with a load of iron ore pellets, passed Sunken Rock in visibility of about four miles.[^2] She struck Pullman Shoal as she approached the shoal light.[^2] Her captain beached her near the Coast Guard station on Wellesley Island, and she sank there before dawn, with her bow in about 140 feet (43 m) of water.[^2] She is now a dive site.[^2] The wreck was not at the light; LighthouseFriends tells it as a neighboring event.

## After the keepers

In 1931 the light changed from fixed red to fixed green.[^2] On November 21, 1934, it was electrified, and its intensity rose to 1,500 candlepower.[^2] Walts resigned in July 1937 because of poor health, and Edward J. Sweet took over Sunken Rock and several other automated lights in the area.[^2] The archive has Sweet as keeper until 1960,[^1] but Thousand Islands Life's keeper table gives 1958.[^3]

## Today

The light is owned by the St. Lawrence Seaway Development Corporation, and it was converted to solar power in 1988.[^2] It remains an active aid to navigation.[^1][^3] The tower and grounds are closed.[^2] The 1904 letter notes that there was no dwelling on the rock and no land owned by the United States on which one could be built.[^2] The island is fully occupied by the light and its boathouse.[^3] Visitors see it from the shore at Alexandria Bay, where the closest view is a grassy area near the Bonnie Castle marina, or from the tour boats.[^2]
