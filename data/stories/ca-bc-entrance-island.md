Entrance Island is a bare rock of about two acres in the Strait of Georgia, a short way off the north-east tip of Gabriola Island, where a light has marked the way into Nanaimo Harbour since the night of 8 June 1876.[^1] Three workmen drowned and two contractors left without paying their men before the first lamps were lit, and the tower that stands there now is the second on the site.

## The place

By the early 1870s Nanaimo and Departure Bay were busy coal ports. Accounts put the coal cleared from Departure Bay in 1872 alone at 50,000 tons.[^1][^2] When the lighthouse was being planned, the *Nanaimo Free Press* explained the choice of site: from Entrance Island a light would appear before the lights at Point Atkinson and the Fraser mouth dropped out of sight, so the three would form a triangle, and it would show the position of both Nanaimo harbour and Departure Bay.[^1] The paper preferred it to Lighthouse Island, where the other lights would have been hidden before the new one opened.[^1]

The island itself the paper described in August 1875 as made of rock, about two acres, and about 9 m (30 ft) above high water at its highest.[^1] It put the island about ten miles from the city, and half a mile off Gabriola's north-east end; in January it had said "about one mile".[^1] The agent of the Department of Marine and Fisheries, Captain Cooper, chose the north-east end for the lighthouse, on the spot where Imperial surveyors had once heaped stones as a mark for navigators.[^1] Canada's statement of significance adds that the harbour entrance was "notorious for both fog and the dangerous Gabriola Reef".[^4]

## Building the light

The contract went to Louis Baker of Montreal, who also built the light in Victoria Harbour; together the two were to cost $6,900, according to the Victoria *Colonist* as reprinted in the Free Press.[^1] (The Gabriola Museum's summary gives a separate figure of $4,450 for the Entrance Island contract alone.[^1]) Baker had been delayed by illness; when he reached Nanaimo in August 1875 he spoke "confidently" of having the light burning by 1 November.[^1] His workmen did not arrive on the island until late October.[^1]

On a Sunday afternoon at the end of October 1875 (the paper reported it on 3 November), three of those workmen, the carpenter Longden, and the sailors Sawyer and Furguson, set out for Nanaimo in an 18-foot boat. About 200 yards from shore the boat had to tack, and Longden, who weighed about 225 pounds, did not shift his weight. The boat filled and turned over. The paper reported that the "sea was smooth and the wind light", that the men on the island threw planks and sticks toward them, and that there was no other boat on the island.[^1] Sawyer slipped off the keel first; Longden dropped into the water after about a mile's drift; Furguson was still on the upturned boat when it went out of sight. In December the boat was found, broken up, on Lasqueti Island, with no trace of the men.[^1] Longden was about fifty, a native of Cheshire with a wife in Nanaimo; Sawyer was about thirty, from Brighton; Furguson about twenty-four, from London.[^1]

In February 1876 the lighthouse was about two-thirds built. Baker left Nanaimo on the steamer *Goliath* with the money he had drawn, owing his workmen about $800 and his storekeepers considerably more, the Free Press reported.[^1] His successor, James Gordon, left in the same way in April.[^1] On 29 April Arthur Finney reported the lighthouse complete.[^1] It was a square white tower, 20 m (65 ft) above high water according to the Gabriola Museum, joined to the keeper's dwelling.[^1] (The August 1875 plan had called for a tower 12 m (40 ft) high, putting the light 21 m (70 ft) above high water.[^1])

The light was first lit on Thursday 8 June 1876.[^1] The Free Press complained that mariners had been given no day's notice, "for the sake of humanity" hoping that no wreck would come of the Dominion Government's "cheese-paring policy".[^1] The light was made by six large lamps.[^1] In September an excursion steamer brought invited guests from Nanaimo; the keeper showed them over the lighthouse and explained how the six lamps were lit and regulated. The reporter noted that he was entirely alone on an almost barren rock.[^1]

LighthouseFriends records a fifth-order lens with a red sector to warn of the Gabriola reefs in 1891, a fourth-order lens in May 1905 and a revolving lens on a mercury bath in 1921.[^2] It dates a steam fog-signal engine room to 1894 and the change to gasoline-driven diaphones to 1915.[^2]

## The keepers

John Kenny, who had lived for some years at Newcastle, was named keeper in August 1875 and had charge of the light when it was first lit.[^1] In November 1876 the Free Press noted that he had resigned and that Robert Gray of Gabriola Island had taken his place.[^1] Gray stayed about twenty years.[^2] The Nanaimo Community Archives holds a journal from the station for 1879 to 1881 that notes weather and the ships entering the harbour.[^5] A modern article on the station reports that in the winter of 1884 Gray took a small boat out in a storm to fetch coal, and told the Free Press that frost on the lantern windows had to be melted with boiling water or the light could not be seen.[^6]

The department's list of light-keepers for 1902 shows M. G. Clarke at "Entrance Island Lighthouse and Fog Whistle", appointed 26 November 1897 at $900.[^7] LighthouseFriends, drawing on Donald Graham's *Keepers of the Light*, describes a keeper who lived mostly on a ranch on Gabriola and left his assistants to run the light and work the ranch. Assistants wrote to the marine agent that they had been set to feeding chickens, digging the garden and doing the keeper's housework, and an assistant who set out by rowboat at sunset one evening in November 1910 from the ranch for the island never arrived.[^2] These are LighthouseFriends' summaries, which draw on Donald Graham's *Keepers of the Light*, not records read directly.

A stranger episode from the Clark years was reported by *The Tyee* in 2026 from the *Nanaimo Free Press* and the *Province* of 1900. A young Japanese servant at the station, Tsunekichi Katsuyama, told Nanaimo reporters that figures in white had tormented him on the island for days. By the end of March the keeper's own son had confessed to dressing up as the ghost. The Nanaimo Museum, the article says, now judges that this "was not a harmless prank" and calls it workplace harassment.[^6]

The record is unclear about when Clark left. The Gabriola Museum and LighthouseFriends say he was let go in 1913, yet its own list of keepers shows J. Lewis at the station from 1911 to 1913, then A. L. W. Paul, then W. E. Morrisey.[^2] After Morrisey the list continues with John Silva (1914 to 1919), Albert Isaac Thomas, Daniel O'Brien (1922 to 1936) and Michael O'Brien (1924 to 1928), then Jonathon Allardice Fleming, David Charteris Milne, Robert Edwarde Pilcher, Frederick Arthur Mountain (1953 to 1959) and Kurt Felix Carl Cehak (1967 to 1985).[^1] The sources differ over Pilcher's years: the Gabriola Museum gives 1946 to 1953, LighthouseFriends 1941 to 1946, the same years it gives Milne.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On Monday 14 November 1881, during an easterly gale, a sloop drove ashore on the east side of the island. Gray ran down with a rope; the next wave struck the stern and turned the boat over, and he did not see the man he had seen on board again.[^1] In his letter to the newspaper he said he could not tell how many were on board "for the rain and sea flying in my face".[^1] The Free Press, working from a report by Captain Rogers of the steamer *Ada*, thought the sloop was a fishing boat from New Westminster with two men, two women and three children aboard, and feared seven lives lost. It added that it "would have been perfect madness to launch the small boat kept on the island".[^1]

The department's list of rewarded rescues for 1902 includes "Rescuing two Indians from drowning near Entrance Island, B.C." The entry names M. G. Clark, lightkeeper, and John Roberts, assistant, dates the rescue 10 July 1901 and gives the reward as a binocular glass to each.[^7] LighthouseFriends dates the rescue to July 1902 and says the two launched the station boat and rowed out to a capsized canoe.[^2] The department's own table, which falls within its fiscal year to June 1902, is the better guide to the date. LighthouseFriends also quotes the message Michael O'Brien radioed to Victoria in October 1928: "My wife drowned last night, rush a relief immediately."[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse standing now is a round reinforced-concrete "apple core" tower with an octagonal aluminium lantern. Canada's statement of significance gives its height as 12.8 m (42 ft) and its date as 1971, and calls it characteristic of the modernist concrete towers designed by Lorence E. Slaght.[^3][^4] LighthouseFriends and the Gabriola Museum put its building around 1970 and the height at 14 m (45 ft), with a light 19 m above the water flashing white every five seconds.[^1][^2] It was added to the list of heritage lighthouses under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act in July 2015.[^8] Five older buildings on the site also contribute to its heritage character: the 1939 double dwelling, the 1962 single dwelling, the winch house, the boat house and the 1955 engine room.[^4]

It is a Canadian Coast Guard station, and the grounds and tower are closed to visitors. LighthouseFriends suggests a distant view from the Gabriola ferry, from the Duke Point to Tsawwassen ferry, or from Berry Point Road and Orlebar Point on Gabriola.[^2] In 1995, when it was announced that the island might lose its keepers, more than a hundred kayakers formed a "human life preserver" around it, and it kept them.[^2] The Coast Guard told *The Tyee* in 2026 that light stations of this kind are typically staffed with a principal and an assistant keeper, who arrive and leave by helicopter from a pad near the centre of the island.[^6] The same article also calls the light "fully automated" since the late 1990s, alongside the Coast Guard statement that it is staffed.[^6]
