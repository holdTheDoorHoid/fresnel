Moose Peak Light stands on the eastern tip of Mistake Island, a rocky island five miles southeast of Jonesport, Maine, where it marks the southwest side of the entrance to Main Channel Way.[^1] In fiscal 1928 the Lighthouse Service recorded more fog at this station than at any other, and the light has been privately owned since a 2012 auction.[^2][^1]

## The place

In 1824 merchants and shipowners in Maine petitioned Congress for a light on one of the headlands of the Moose Peak islands. In March 1825 Congress approved $4,000 to build it.[^1]

## Building the light

The first tower was built of granite rubblestone and went into service on 9 October 1826. It sat directly on a stone ledge, and a wooden trestle carried the keeper across the rocks to his dwelling, 297 feet (91 m) away.[^1] The Society's record gives 1827 as the year the station was established, so the sources disagree about the first light.[^3]

The sea was the early problem. In 1839 a storm-driven wave burst into the keeper's dwelling and drove the household upstairs. In 1841 the trestle was washed away and the tower was nearly destroyed.[^1]

In 1851 the original tower was refitted and a new brick tower was built, raising the focal plane almost twelve feet (3.7 m), to 65 feet (20 m).[^1] The second-order Fresnel lens was installed in 1856.[^3] By 1885 the foundation had cracked and settled. LighthouseFriends quotes the Board's 1885 report as saying that "a new tower should be built, as near as practicable to the present one. It can be built for $10,000."[^1] Congress appropriated the requested amount on 4 August 1886.[^1] The work carried out in 1888 was an iron watchroom and a second-order lantern room on top of the old tower. A temporary fourth-order light ran until the big lens was relit, around 5 September 1888.[^1] An eight-inch (20 cm) brick covering reinforced the tower in 1901.[^1]

The keepers' houses came up again and again in the Board's reports. In 1893 the Board reported a brick cistern built in the cellar of the assistant keeper's house.[^4] By 1899 the Board said the dwelling was old and "past economical repair," and it proposed a new double dwelling.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the request was repeated for three more years before funds allowed the double dwelling to be built in 1903.[^1][^3]

## The keepers

Alexander Milliken was the first keeper, from 1826 until he retired on 17 October 1849. LighthouseFriends calls him the station's first and longest-serving keeper, and says he had been a farmer, a postmaster, a court justice and a privateer captain in the War of 1812.[^1] In an 1842 report, which LighthouseFriends quotes, he wrote: "The Government estate consists of three acres, and the whole land contains about 30 acres."[^1] As there was no well or spring on Mistake Island, rainwater was collected from the roof of the keeper's dwelling.[^1]

In 1850 Joshua Walker and an assistant had to turn the light by hand for ten nights, because a crack had leaned the tower and jammed its clockwork weights. Walker died in service in 1851.[^1]

Charles R. Dobbin rose from assistant keeper to head keeper in 1887.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that Inspector Oliver Batcheller, reporting in 1887, found that the wife and grown daughters of Keeper Thomas E. Dodge used "the vilest possible language" toward visitors, the Dobbin family and Dodge himself.[^1] Dodge was removed, and Dobbin held the head keeper's post until 1905.[^1] The Board's reports spell his name "C. R. Dobbins."[^6]

The Society's roster lists 96 stints at the station between 1826 and 1973, including keepers under a Coast Guard heading.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 28 September 1883 the crew of the Crumple Island life-saving station were on their way to Mistake Island to rig new signal halliards on their flagstaff near the light.[^7] On 30 September the lookout at the station saw a signal flying from the flagstaff on Mistake Island, four or five miles distant, and the crew went to find out what it meant. They learned that the schooner Mariel, of Machias, had been wrecked the evening before on nearby Man Island. Her captain asked to be taken to Jonesport. The Service reported that the old vessel became a total wreck, but her cargo was saved.[^7]

On 26 November 1896 Keeper Dobbin and his son rescued the crew of the Nova Scotian schooner Ashton, at risk to their own lives.[^1] The Canadian government gave Dobbin a gold watch, but he could not accept it until Congress passed an act on 28 March 1900.[^6]

In 1894 the Board's report recorded that the keeper here, ten miles southwest of the Libby Islands, could hear the Libby Islands whistle in every wind except a southeast gale.[^8] In July 1916 the fog signal ran for 181 hours without a break, from 2:15 a.m. on 20 July to 3:30 p.m. on 27 July.[^9] In fiscal 1928 the fog signal operated 1,934 hours, 22 per cent of the year, the most at any station in the service.[^2] The Bulletin's later tables give Moose Peak 1,562 hours for fiscal 1929 and 1,691 hours for the year ending 30 June 1930, which it called "slightly above the average for the past 16 years."[^10][^11] The 1928 entry words its figure as hours the fog signal operated, and the later ones as hours of fog, so the three figures are not averaged.

In 1920 the keeper, A. T. Faulkingham, and his assistant, Alvah Robinson, helped float and tow a grounded lobster smack to the light station.[^12]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1972, and the station's double dwelling was put up for sale. A private buyer was found, but complicated regulations made the sale impractical. In 1982 a training demolition of the house went wrong: instead of imploding, the house exploded and scattered timber and debris over a wide area.[^1]

A modern optic replaced the Fresnel lens in 1993. The second-order lens is in storage at the Coast Guard Exhibit Center in Forrestville, Maryland.[^1]

In 2010 the Coast Guard declared the station excess property. A non-profit group formed by the Town of Jonesport, Keepers of Moose Peak Light, sought to acquire it, but the National Park Service denied its application in 2012. The lighthouse, its four surrounding acres and the boathouse went to auction in June 2012, and the auction closed on 1 November 2012 with a winning bid of $93,500.[^1]

In 2014 a film planned for Jonesport and Moose Peak Island ran into the foghorn, which sounded around the clock. After the dispute became public, the Coast Guard agreed to a set-up that let the signal run only in forty-five-minute periods when mariners keyed their radios five times on a VHF channel.[^1] In 2017 the owner donated the light to a non-profit medical building society, which put it on the market for $249,000. In March 2019 a private couple bought it for $150,000.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is privately owned.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes the grounds as open and the tower as closed.[^1] The Coast Guard keeps the brick fog-signal building and a solar array on the property, and The Nature Conservancy owns the rest of Mistake Island.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the light is best seen from a boat, and that landing on the Nature Conservancy's part of the island is permitted if visitors stay on the boardwalk to the lighthouse.[^1] An estimate the Keepers group received put the repair of the tower's cracks, and its cleaning and repainting, at $207,000.[^1] Visitors should not land on the private property or climb the tower without the owner's permission.
