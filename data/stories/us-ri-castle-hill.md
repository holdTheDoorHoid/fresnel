Castle Hill Light stands on a rock ledge at the western end of Newport Neck, on the east side of the entrance to the East Passage of Narragansett Bay. Its tower is a rusticated granite cone, first lit on 1 May 1890 with a fifth-order Fresnel lens. The National Register nomination calls it one of perhaps only a few lighthouses in the country designed in the Richardson Romanesque style.[^1][^2]

## The place

The nomination says the Lighthouse Board recognized a need for a light and fog signal at Castle Hill as early as 1869, to guide ships through the entrance of Narragansett Bay and into Newport Harbor. High land prices delayed the work for more than twenty years.[^1]

The site came from Alexander Agassiz, "the noted Harvard zoologist" who had a large summer home on Castle Hill. In 1887 he deeded a parcel of his land to the government "for one dollar, so that a lighthouse could be built on the site." He first refused a right-of-way for the keepers' access, and agreed to land access only after learning that the government would take one by condemnation.[^1]

## Building the light

The tower was not built to the first design. The nomination describes an early design, drawn by H. H. Richardson, with an attached dwelling. Probably because of Agassiz's desire for privacy, the design was changed to a tower with no living quarters. A separate six-room keeper's dwelling, a wharf and a boathouse were built about 300 yards (274 m) east of the light, on Castle Hill Cove.[^1]

The nomination admits that the designer is uncertain: "Although the plans for the tower are unsigned, they may have been drawn by H. H. Richardson himself." It also says that "It is not known who designed the light that was built."[^1] The tower and fog bell were finished in 1890, and the original fifth-order lens was first lit on 1 May 1890.[^1]

The fog bell did not stay long, and the sources differ on when it went. The nomination says that "Two years later the bell was discontinued and removed because of complaints by Agassiz about its noise level." The Board's report of 1892 gives 30 November 1891 as the date the fog bell was discontinued and the bell and apparatus removed.[^1][^3]

Later the bell was changed. The Board's report of 1897 says that "The 1,286 pound fog bell was replaced by one of" 2,400 pounds, that a new fog-bell hammer was furnished and that the landing wharf was repaired.[^4]

## The keepers

The archive's roster names three principal keepers. Frank W. Parmele was the first, from 1890 to 1911, a span of twenty-one years on the roster.[^2] George L. Hoxsie followed in 1911 and is on the roster until at least 1946. Manuel Soares Macedo is listed from at least 1945.[^2]

Hoxsie appears in other records. The Commissioner of Lighthouses' table of life and property saved in the fiscal year 1916 lists "G. L. Hoxsie, keeper, Castle Hill Light Station," among the keepers and employees it credits.[^5] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1918 to 1923 lists "Hexsie, George, L., keeper Castle Hill, efficiency flag awarded," with the index spelling the name Hexsie.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The nomination's account of the 1938 hurricane is short: "The 1938 hurricane destroyed the three structures at Castle Hill Cove, leaving the light without its keeper's quarters."[^1]

The Board's report of 1896, under its new fog signals, records that "A bell struck by machinery, March 18, 1896" was established at the light, with no further detail.[^4]

## After the keepers

After the 1938 hurricane, the nomination says, operation of the light passed to "personnel at the nearby, newly constructed Castle Hill Coast Guard Station," which had been built to the east of the light on Ocean Avenue.[^1] The fog bell was replaced by an automatic electric horn, and the light was automated in 1957.[^1]

## Today

The archive lists the light as an active aid to navigation, and not open to the public.[^2] The nomination gives the owner as the United States Government, Department of Transportation, and its access as "yes, restricted."[^1] The nomination describes the red light as equal-interval, flashing every six seconds, with a range of 12 miles; it does not say whether statute or nautical miles are meant. In the same description the fog horn gives one blast every ten seconds.[^1]
