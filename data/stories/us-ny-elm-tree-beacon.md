Elm Tree Beacon stood on the south side of Staten Island, New York, near the shore, as the front light of a range that led ships through the Swash Channel into New York Bay.[^1] Its rear light was the New Dorp beacon, on a hill about a mile and a half inland.[^1] The range was discontinued in 1964.[^2][^3]

## The place

Lighthouse Friends says early sailors used a large elm tree off Staten Island as a landmark, and that the beacon was named for the tree it replaced.[^1] The Swash Channel was an alternate entry to New York Bay, used by smaller vessels.[^3]

Lighthouse Friends quotes the Lighthouse Board's 1855 report, which describes three sets of ranges in New York Bay, one of them "to guide vessels through the Swash channel." Lighthouse Friends identifies that set as Elm Tree and New Dorp.[^1]

## Building the light

The Board's 1855 report, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, records a contract dated 11 June 1855 for six range beacons, at $19,124 for the whole, with the work to be completed by 25 October 1855.[^1] The Elm Tree tower was a hexagonal wooden tower about 55 ft (17 m) tall. Its third-order range lens showed a fixed white light 62 ft (19 m) above sea level.[^1] The first keeper, William Hooper, lived in a five-room dwelling beside it.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends has the tower operating in 1855, while the USLHS record and Rowlett's directory date the station to 1856.[^1][^2][^3] The USLHS roster begins in 1855.[^2]

In 1891 the Board moved the tower about 265 feet (81 m) north-east, "to mark the axis of the Swash Channel," on 30 June 1891.[^4] Lighthouse Friends gives the distance as 250 feet.[^1]

## The keepers

The USLHS roster names seven principal keepers.[^2] William Hooper served from 1855 to 1858, and Sarah Ann Hooper followed from 1858 to 1867.[^2] John Wood (1867 to 1873) and Thomas Burke (1873 to 1876) came next.[^2]

Jacob C. Swaim held the post from 1876 to 1906, about thirty years, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1][^2] When he died on 14 February 1906, his widow, Sara Ann Swaim, applied for the post. The service instead appointed Edward B. Burdge, who had been keeper at West Bank, and who began at Elm Tree on 14 April 1906.[^1][^2] A petition was raised to ask the service to reconsider.[^1] The district inspector, Captain R. McKenzie, replied that the service owed more to working keepers than to the families of dead ones.[^1] The roster lists Sara Ann Swaim for 1906 only.[^2]

Burdge stayed until 1924.[^2] Lighthouse Friends quotes a 1924 interview in which he described the work: "waiting three hundred and sixty-four days, twenty-three hours and fifty-nine minutes, so as to be sure to be on the job in that other minute is hard enough."[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In April 1898, as the war with Spain began, the Lighthouse Board discontinued the light "as a war measure, on April 28, 1898." It was relighted on 1 August 1898.[^5] The New York Sun reported on 30 April an order issued the day before, which listed the "Elm Tree and New Dorp" range for the Swash Channel among lights extinguished "until further notice." The paper added that the only lights left burning in the lower bay were those on the Fire Island and Scotland lightships and the twin lights at Atlantic Highlands.[^6] The Board's report and the paper differ by one day on the date of the order.

On 16 December 1960 a TWA airliner and a United Airlines jet collided above Miller Field.[^1] The TWA plane came down at Miller Field, where the light stood, and the United jet crashed in Brooklyn.[^1][^3] Lighthouse Friends reports that 134 people aboard the two aircraft and six people at the Brooklyn crash site were killed.[^1] The sources read here do not say the light was involved in the collision.

## After the keepers

Miller Airfield was built next to the light in November 1919, on what had been a Vanderbilt farm.[^1] By 1924 the Bulletin lists the Elm Tree beacon among lights changed from incandescent oil vapor to electric incandescent.[^7]

In 1939 the old wooden tower was torn down and the light moved to a 65 ft (20 m) square concrete tower, which also carried an aviation beacon.[^1][^3] The USLHS record gives the new tower's height above water as 60 ft (18 m).[^2] The Bulletin's 1924 to 1929 index lists "Elm Tree lighthouse reservations donated to War Dept," on page 137.[^8] In 1964 the range was discontinued when its alignment was changed, and channel markers replaced it.[^1][^3]

Miller Field was abandoned in 1969. The land went to the National Park Service in 1972 and is now part of the Staten Island Unit of Gateway National Recreation Area.[^1]

## Today

The National Park Service owns the light, and it is part of Gateway National Recreation Area.[^1] Lighthouse Friends and Rowlett both list the grounds as open and the tower as closed.[^1][^3] Lighthouse Friends says the concrete tower and a vacant double seaplane hangar remain standing on what are now sports fields.[^1] Rowlett notes that the keeper's house has been demolished and the tower is unpainted.[^3]
