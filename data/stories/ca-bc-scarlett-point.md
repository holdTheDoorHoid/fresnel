Scarlett Point Lighthouse stands on the northwest point of Balaklava Island, at the mouth of Christie Passage, off the north coast of Vancouver Island in British Columbia.[^1] The light went into service in April 1905, and it still has a keeper.[^2][^1]

## The place

In 1864 Captain George Henry Richards, commander of the survey ships HMS Plumper and HMS Hecate, named Balaklava Island after a Crimean War battle.[^1] General James Yorke Scarlett led the Charge of the Heavy Brigade in that battle, and Richards named the island's northwest tip after him.[^1] Christie Passage runs between Balaklava Island and Hurst Island, and the Department of Marine chose this point to mark where the passage meets Gordon Channel.[^1]

## Building the light

The lighthouse was put in operation on 12 April 1905.[^2] A temporary fixed white light was shown nearby while the building went up.[^2] The Department's annual report for the year ended 30 June 1905 describes a rectangular wooden building with a hip roof and a square lantern, standing 30 m (100 ft) back from the water.[^2] Its fixed red light, of the seventh order, was 27 m (90 ft) above high water and was described as visible 10 miles, about 9 nautical miles.[^2] The building cost $7,642.66 to that date, having been erected by day labour under the foreman G. Blain.[^2]

Fog signals came in steps. In 1907 the Department supplied hand fog horns to the keepers at Pulteney Point and Scarlett Point, to be sounded in thick weather in answer to steamers' whistles.[^3] In 1909 a fog bell was hung in a small wooden tower, built by day labour for $269.75.[^4] The diaphone came in 1915.[^1] The report for the year ended 31 March 1916 lists the installation of a Class B diaphone plant at Scarlett Point,[^5] and the chartered steamer Leebro sailed in July 1915 with machinery and construction supplies for "the new fog-alarm station at Scarlet point."[^6] Lighthouse Friends gives the same year.[^1]

The original lighthouse was demolished in 1965, after it had been replaced by a skeletal steel tower, topped by an electric light, and a new dwelling.[^1] The present round steel tower, with an octagonal lantern room, shows a white flash every five seconds.[^1]

## The keepers

James William Davies was the first keeper, and he did not last a year.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says he had no fog signal, so in poor visibility he would go out and fire his shotgun.[^1] The Department's keeper list for the year ended 31 March 1907 names J. Wm. Davies at Scarlett Point.[^7]

Theodore Nelson, a bachelor, followed on a salary of $450 a year.[^1] He resigned in September 1908, after the Marine Agent could not win him a pay increase.[^1][^7]

William Hunt, who had briefly served as an assistant at Pine Island, took over in 1908.[^1] The Department's keeper list for the year ended 31 March 1912 names W. Hunt at Scarlett Point.[^8] Hunt moved his wife and two sons to the station, and the family grew to ten boys and a girl.[^1] He kept the light until the end of 1939, more than thirty-one years.[^1]

Vivian Hunt, one of the middle sons, was paid ten dollars a month to haul mail and supplies between Port Hardy, Scarlett Point and Pine Island.[^1] He recalled waiting for "what looked like a good spell of weather" before setting out.[^1]

In July 1940 the citizens of Port Hardy gathered to watch their mayor present Hunt with the Imperial Service Medal.[^1] Vivian Hunt took charge for a time, but the Department sent George Smith to replace him in 1941.[^1] Vivian later said that such jobs went to veterans, and he was not one.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A Vancouver dispatch printed in the Astoria *Morning Astorian* on 23 April 1908 says the gasoline fishing schooner Clara C blew up off Cape Scott on the Sunday before, and that her crew of 11 men had barely time to escape.[^9] The shipwrecked men rowed about 50 miles to the lighthouse at Scarlett Point for food and shelter, and the halibut schooner Celestial Empire picked them up the next day.[^9]

Lighthouse Friends dates the explosion to the evening of 20 April. It says the eleven men got away in two fishing dories and were fed and sheltered by Keeper Nelson until the Celestial Empire picked them up "a few days later".[^1]

In 1916 a Scandinavian ship ran aground near the station, though the new diaphone had been installed the year before.[^1] Keeper Hunt rowed out to the stricken ship.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says its captain, leaning over the rail, pointed to the rock that had holed his bow and said he knew all the rocks around there.[^1]

On 22 February 1930 a son of Keeper Hunt set out in a rowboat to take supplies to nearby settlers.[^1] Keeper Hunt reported to the marine agent that "a comber must have struck the boat and turned her right over", and that his son's body was found the next day.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard's list of lights describes the light as a white flash every five seconds, 23.8 m above the water and visible for 15 nautical miles, shown from a white cylindrical tower with a red band at its top.[^10]

Lighthouse Friends lists keepers through 2024, the most recent taking over that year.[^1] The lighthouse is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard. It is best seen from the water, and should be visible from the ferries that serve the Discovery Coast and Inside Passage from Port Hardy.[^1] The grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] Because this is a staffed station and the keepers' home, do not land uninvited.[^1]
