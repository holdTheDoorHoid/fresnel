The Michigan City East Pierhead Light stands at the outer end of the east pier, where Trail Creek meets Lake Michigan in La Porte County, Indiana. Its octagonal steel tower and the elevated walk that leads out to it were built in 1904, when the harbor was enlarged.[^1] The city calls it the only public operating lighthouse in Indiana.[^2]

## The place

Trail Creek gave Michigan City a mouth to the lake, but the harbor inside it was poor.[^4] In 1867 a Corps of Engineers captain reported that "the harbor at Michigan City is in so poor a condition that it can hardly be called a harbor."[^4] The shore lights came first. A lighthouse was built in 1837, and it was replaced in 1858 by the tower and house that now make up the Old Lighthouse Museum.[^1] The pierhead light belongs to the 1900 harbor work.[^4] The sundry civil appropriation act of 6 June 1900 provided $5,500 "for establishing a fog-signal at this station,"[^5] and on the same date Congress appropriated $195,000 to build a detached breakwater and extend the east pier to form an outer harbor.[^4]

## Building the light

The fog-signal building is 24 feet (7.3 m) square and steel lined with brick.[^1] An octagonal steel tower rises from its roof, and the 1984 National Register nomination puts the height from the base to the outer ventilator ball of the lantern at 49 feet six inches (15.1 m).[^1] The nomination names the builder as Robert Millen & Co., and it dates the structures to 1904.[^1] A work party arrived in June 1904, and on 20 October 1904 the fixed white light moved from the 1858 shore house to the pier end.[^4] The 1858 house was enlarged to give three keepers their quarters.[^4] The nomination says the building's two boilers operated a 10-inch Crosby automatic whistle.[^1]

The pier runs about 1,100 feet (335 m) from shore, with a bend of 37 degrees near the middle, and the walk runs about 600 feet (183 m) to that bend. The first pier was wood, and it was replaced in the 1930s.[^1] The nomination describes the keepers' routine on the walk: they went out "at sunset to light the light and at sunrise to extinguish the light," and extra trips were needed for cleaning, painting and starting the fog signal when conditions required.[^1]

## The keepers

The keeper of the shore light from 1861 until 1904 was a woman.[^4] LighthouseFriends says Harriet Colfax moved to Michigan City in 1853, turned to her cousin, Congressman Schuyler Colfax, for the appointment in 1861, and that her faithfulness earned the lighthouse the nickname "Old Faithful."[^4] She resigned on 14 October 1904, six days before the old light was put out. LighthouseFriends quotes her logbook from those last days: on 8 October she recorded that she had "sold household effects preparatory to vacating the dear old lighthouse," and her final entry notes "another call from Mr. Armstrong, my successor."[^4]

The first keeper of the new light was Thomas J. Armstrong, who took over in October 1904 and stayed until 1918.[^3][^4] The roster lists Philip Sheridan as principal keeper from 1918 to 1930 and Walter A. Donovan from 1930 to 1940.[^3] Thomas E. Martin served as first assistant from 1911 to 1934, and Frederick Dykeman was second assistant from 1909 to 1916. The table below gives each stint, with Colfax's from LighthouseFriends.[^3][^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The storm of 8, 9 and 10 November 1913 destroyed about 200 feet (61 m) of the elevated walk that led from the shore to the pierhead light and fog signal.[^6] The Bulletin says that in calm weather the keepers could reach the station by boat, but that it "would be extremely dangerous in rough weather."[^6] LighthouseFriends says the keepers then rigged an aerial trolley with a breeches buoy to cross the gap.[^4]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded several rescues by the station's keepers in these years. On 14 July 1915 Armstrong rescued two men who had been caught on the end of the breakwater near the light during a squall.[^7] On 21 January 1916 he and second assistant Fred Dykeman assisted in releasing the fish tug *Eagle* from the ice.[^7] On 13 August 1916 first assistant Thomas E. Martin rescued a man and a boy from probable drowning, and two days later Armstrong rescued a boy who had "gone overboard from the dock near the light station."[^8]

The storm of 18 and 19 December 1929 carried away the outer 312 feet (95 m) of the walk, according to LighthouseFriends.[^4] The 1984 nomination says a violent storm in 1929 damaged the pier structures and that the piers were rebuilt with new concrete superstructures in 1930 and 1931.[^1]

## After the keepers

The nomination and the city's page both say the light was electrified in 1933.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says it was automated that year, three years into Donovan's time as head keeper.[^4] The nomination says keepers went on tending the light, and living in the 1858 house, until 1940, and it dates Coast Guard jurisdiction from that year. The city's page gives 1939 as the year the Coast Guard took over the service.[^1][^2]

The nomination describes the light as operated remotely by electronic equipment installed in 1982 and 1983.[^1] It also records that in 1984 the Coast Guard had recently proposed removing the catwalks of Great Lakes lighthouses, including the one at this light.[^1] The catwalk was turned over to Michigan City in 1991, and the city's page says local people rallied to save it when it was no longer in use.[^4][^2] The Coast Guard declared the light excess in 2007, and the city was eventually awarded it, according to LighthouseFriends. The light was repainted and refurbished in 2014 in a project costing $160,000.[^4]

## Today

The city's page describes the pier as a favorite place for fishing and for watching sunsets.[^2] The tower is closed. LighthouseFriends' visitor notes say "Tower closed," and the Light Station record lists the light as not open to the public.[^4][^3] Check with the city before going out on the pier, and do not try to climb the tower. The 1858 house is a separate site, the Old Lighthouse Museum, which LighthouseFriends says opened to the public in 1973 after restoration.[^4]
