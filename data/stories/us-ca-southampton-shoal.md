Southampton Shoal Light stood from 1905 to 1960 on a two-mile shoal in San Francisco Bay, between Richmond and Angel Island.[^1][^2] It was a square two-story dwelling on eleven foundation cylinders, with a lantern on top and a bell that was struck by machinery in thick weather.[^2] Its top two stories were later moved to Tinsley Island in the delta, where the St. Francis Yacht Club owns them today.[^2]

## The place

Southampton Shoal lies along the east side of the shipping channel that runs between Richmond and Angel Island and the Tiburon Peninsula.[^2] Three buoys were anchored on it in 1850 to warn mariners.[^2] The Lighthouse Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1900, which discussed Quarry Point on Angel Island, called the quantity of shipping passing it "enormous," and said that many ships bound to and from the grain wharves at Port Costa were towed past it.[^2][^3]

The same report says that the passage between the eastern side of Angel Island and Southampton Shoal "is quite narrow," and that marine interests had petitioned for a fog signal at Quarry Point.[^3] It also records that a ship being towed to Port Costa ran aground on the shoal.[^3]

## Building the light

The Board asked for $30,000 to establish a light and fog signal on the shoal, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2][^4] Its report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1901 lists a $30,000 item for a "Southampton Shoal light and fog-signal, California."[^4]

LighthouseFriends says that Congress made the appropriation on 3 March 1903, and that the contract for the station was made in March 1905.[^2]

The Notice to Mariners issued in late 1905, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, announced "a fixed white lens lantern light, illuminating the entire horizon," to show from about 28 December 1905, 52 feet (16 m) above the water.[^2] The structure stood on eleven foundation cylinders carrying a platform, with a square two-story dwelling under a pyramidal roof and dormers.[^2]

The 1906 earthquake tilted the support cylinders as much as eleven degrees eastward. The Service re-leveled the station, braced the steel piles with iron cross-braces, and dumped more rock around them.[^2]

The archive lists a fifth-order Fresnel lens as the original optic, installed in 1905.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes a lens lantern at first, replaced by a fifth-order lens in March 1911.[^2] The two sources disagree about the first optic, and this story does not settle the question.

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists 53 stints at the station between 1905 and 1955.[^1] Samuel D. Hounsell was the first head keeper, from 1905 to 1910.[^1] Frank M. Schou was head keeper from 1924 to 1930.[^1] Albert H. Joost held the head keeper's post in four stints between 1921 and 1935, and was first assistant from 1930 to 1932.[^1]

In 1929 Ole Lunden, who had been working as a streetcar motorman in San Francisco, took an assistant's post at the station.[^2] He had been impressed when a friend came ashore from the Farallon Islands in his Lighthouse Service uniform.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that Bernice Lunden was the first woman the tug crews had ever seen at the station.[^2] The archive lists a John O. Lunden as first assistant from 1929 to 1930. The name and the years match, but the archive does not say they are the same man.[^1]

On 23 December 1935 Keeper Albert Joost was badly burned at the station. He was using a blowtorch to heat a soldering iron while he readied a radio aerial. The wind blew the torch out, and when he struck a match to relight it over gasoline spilled on the deck, the air around him burst into flame, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] With his wife's help, he put out the flames on his clothing and the fire in the woodwork, the Service's bulletin says.[^8] That evening, as darkness fell, Evelyn Joost lit the lamp.[^2] The Service's bulletin reported that he died on 25 December, from burns received on 23 December while repairing a radio.[^8]

Keeper James Simonson, returning to the station on 24 December, recorded, as LighthouseFriends quotes him, that he "found the station in a very disorderly condition."[^2]

John R. Chambers succeeded Joost as head keeper in 1936. He was transferred to Angel Island in 1939.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Service's 1925 bulletin records that on 21 November the keeper, A. H. Joost, assisted the occupants of a launch that had become disabled in a fog.[^6]

The 1928 bulletin reports that Max Schlederer, assistant keeper at the station, rescued two men in a disabled motor boat on 11 August.[^7]

The fire of 23 December 1935 is described above, under the keepers.[^2][^8]

## After the keepers

In 1918 the station's fog bell, struck by machinery, was changed to an air diaphone.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the diaphone was powered by a gasoline engine and a compressor.[^2]

The station remained in operation until 1960, when the Service decided a manned station was no longer needed.[^2] The St. Francis Yacht Club had bought Tinsley Island in 1959.[^2] In July 1966 giant cranes lifted the top two stories off the pilings, and they were barged up the delta to Tinsley Island.[^2] The page's text gives that date, but a photograph caption on the same page dates the lifting to 1960, so the year is disputed here.[^2]

## Today

At the shoal itself, LighthouseFriends reports that the pilings remain and that a red light on a pole now marks the site.[^2]

The house on Tinsley Island belongs to the St. Francis Yacht Club, and the grounds, dwelling and tower are closed. The club uses the house for its members and guests, and LighthouseFriends says access is generally limited to people staying in it.[^2] Landing on Tinsley Island requires permission, so do not land there without it.[^2] The station's lens is displayed in a museum at Angel Island State Park, and the pilings are visible from the Vallejo to San Francisco ferry.[^2]
