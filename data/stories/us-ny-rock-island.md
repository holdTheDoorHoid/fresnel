Rock Island Light stands on a small island in the St. Lawrence River, between Fishers Landing and the western end of Wellesley Island, in the Thousand Islands of Jefferson County, New York. Its first keeper was appointed in May 1848, and the first light was a lantern on a brick dwelling. Rowlett's directory describes the light as inactive from 1956 to 2013. It is now a New York State park, and it can be reached only by boat.[^2][^1][^6][^8]

## The place

Congress set about marking the Thousand Islands.[^2] On March 3, 1847, "Congress appropriated $6,000 for three lighthouses" for the area, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The next month the government bought Rock Island and two other islands, Crossover Island and Sunken Rock, from their owners for $250.[^2]

## Building the light

The first station was a "one-and-a-half-story brick dwelling with a lantern centered on its pitched roof," according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] Chesterfield Pearson, who had sold the island to the government, "was appointed first keeper of the light" in May 1848.[^2][^1] The lamp array was six oil lamps with reflectors.[^2] In 1855 the lantern was refitted with a sixth-order Fresnel lens.[^2]

In 1873 the Board noted that the station needed a new tower. LighthouseFriends quotes its report: "A new tower is imperatively necessary."[^2] The old tower was replaced in 1882 by a detached iron tower, set on a concrete pad on one of the island's rocks. LighthouseFriends dates a two-story Victorian dwelling to 1884.[^2] Rowlett dates two-story frame keeper's quarters to 1882, so the two sources disagree on the date of the keepers' quarters.[^6]

LighthouseFriends says the iron tower was not tall enough to make the light visible over the keeper's dwelling.[^2] Keeper Diepolder petitioned the Board for years to raise it.[^2] The Board's report for the year ended June 30, 1895, says the tower's lights "were obscured by the roof of the keeper's dwelling" in the direction of Thousand Island Park, and that a small lantern light with a reflector had been shown from the veranda "to complete the illumination of the navigable channel."[^3] The same report says the tower was "raised bodily 5 feet" (1.5 m), with "a solid octagonal wall of red granite" built under it.[^3] LighthouseFriends dates the work to the fall of 1894.[^2] The report says the small light was discontinued in 1894, at the close of navigation.[^3] In 1903 the old iron tower was set atop a new conical brick base, which LighthouseFriends describes as a fifty-foot (15 m) tower joined to the island by a walkway.[^2] On 10 April 1904 the light was changed from white to red, and about then the tower received a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists fourteen names for the station.[^1] Chesterfield Pearsons kept the light from 1848 to 1849, followed by John B. Collins. William Johnston followed from 1853 to 1861.[^1] LighthouseFriends tells Johnston's story at length. In 1838, during the Patriot War, he and thirteen men commandeered the British steamer *Sir Robert Peel* and set her afire. The government later offered a reward for him as an outlaw, yet, as LighthouseFriends quotes the historian Benson J. Lossing, it "was paying him $350 a year" to keep this light.[^2]

Willard L. Cook, keeper from 1870 to 1879, had lost an arm after a bullet shattered his elbow at Second Bull Run.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends quotes his logbook entry for 16 July 1872, which begins "Weather hot and dry."[^2] Foster M. Drake kept the light from 1879 to 1886, and Michael J. Diepolder from 1886 to 1901.[^1]

Diepolder died on July 16, 1901, after suffering a heart attack while going for an early-morning swim in the river.[^2] Emma Diepolder went back to the station after burying her husband and stayed until September.[^2] The archive lists her role as laborer; LighthouseFriends calls her "the first and only female keeper of Rock Island."[^2][^1]

After her, Eugene W. Butler kept the light from 1901 to 1912, and John C. Belden from 1912 to 1940. Frank K. Ward followed from 1940 to 1952, John W. Van Ingen from 1952 to 1955, and Dennis P. Carroll from 1955 to 1956, the last principal keeper on the roster.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 15 August 1889 the three-masted schooner *Vickery* ran aground near the lighthouse. LighthouseFriends says her crew spent the night at the lighthouse, "hosted by Keeper Michael Diepolder."[^2] On 23 August 1889 the *New-York Tribune* printed a dispatch from Thousand Island Park under the heading "A YACHT RUNS INTO A WRECK." It describes the side-wheel steamer *Sport* running into the "wrecked schooner Vickery" one night.[^4]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1915 volume reports that on May 9, "during a heavy gale, John C. Belden, keeper of Rock Island Light Station," rescued a large power boat with five men on board. The boat was disabled and drifting across the shoals some distance from the light station.[^5]

## After the keepers

Dennis P. Carroll, the last principal keeper on the roster, served until 1956.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he left the island in December 1956.[^2] Rowlett's directory describes the station as "inactive 1956-2013."[^6] The National Park Service's NPGallery record for the station gives a publication date of 14 November 1978.[^7]

In 2000 the Rock Island Lighthouse Historical and Memorial Association was formed to tell the station's history and to support its preservation.[^6] Rowlett records that restoration work began in June 2010, at a cost of $1.1 million, was completed in early 2013, and that the lighthouse opened for tours in June of that year.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the 2010 restoration used $1.5 million in federal and state funds, and that state parks officials unveiled the island to the public on June 4, 2013.[^2] The two sources differ on the cost. LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is owned by New York State Parks.[^2] The state's park page describes Rock Island Lighthouse State Park as part of the Thousand Islands region.[^8]

## Today

The park page says the island is "accessible by boat only."[^8] From 15 May to 19 September the lighthouse and keeper's quarters are open, from 10 a.m. to 5 p.m., and a museum and gift shop are available.[^8] The lantern room is open, and visitors can climb the circular staircase, a climb the park does not recommend for people with limited mobility.[^8] The park has partnered with Clayton Island Tours for a "Rock Island Tour," with a one-hour stop at the island.[^8] The same page says the lighthouse is closed for the season, so check the dates before you go.[^8] Distant views are possible from Thousand Island Park, Fishers Landing and the Thousand Islands Bridge.[^2]
