Butler Flats Light stands on an iron caisson with a brick tower in the channel at the mouth of New Bedford Harbor, Massachusetts.[^2][^3] It was first lit on 30 April 1898.[^1] It was automated in 1978,[^9] and two accounts say it has not been an active aid to navigation since 2014.[^3][^4]

## The place

The light marks the mouth of the harbor.[^5] In its 1894 report the Lighthouse Board described the entrance "near buoy No. 9, on the point of Butler Flat" as "narrow, obscure, and difficult to find in snowstorms, fogs, and dark nights."[^5] The Board had asked for a light there in earlier reports too; the recommendation "was made in the Board's last five annual reports, is renewed."[^5]

## Building the light

Congress authorized the light on 22 January 1894, but in the Board's words "no appropriation was made for its construction."[^5] The money came in the act of 2 March 1895, which provided $45,000 "to take the place of Clark Point and Fairhaven bridge lights."[^6]

The National Register nomination's text gives the designer as "F. Hopkinton Smith." Other accounts name the writer and engineer Francis Hopkinson Smith (1838–1915), and the nomination's spelling may be an error.[^2][^1] Eleven bids came in, "ranging from $32,700 to $63,000," and LighthouseFriends reports that "a contract was signed with William H. Flaherty, who had bid $33,800."[^3] New England Lighthouses gives the cost as $34,000.[^4] Flaherty prepared the cast-iron caisson on Crow Island and "floated it downriver to Butler Flats where five feet of mud had been dredged away," according to LighthouseFriends.[^3]

The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1897 said the light and fog signal "is to be established by the end of this year."[^7] Construction was completed on 30 April 1898, the date the light was first shown.[^2][^1] The lens was a fifth-order Fresnel.[^9] The fog bell was a 4,000-pound bell with a Shipman engine.[^8] The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1899 records that it was replaced by "a 1,000 pound bell with Stevens striking" machine.[^8]

The same report lists the tower's color change as "Color of tower changed November 8, 1898," and says "The red brick tower was painted white."[^8] LighthouseFriends dates the paint to 1899, so the two accounts differ on the year.[^3]

## The keepers

The first keeper was Captain Amos C. Baker Jr., who transferred from the Clark's Point light when Butler Flats opened.[^3] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1929 history of the family says his father "was keeper of Clark Point Light Station from 1872 to 1879," and that the son "who served at Clark Point till it was replaced by Butler Flats station in 1898" stayed on at Butler Flats "until his death in 1911."[^14]

Charles A. Baker, the captain's son, was the first assistant from 1898 and then took over as principal keeper.[^14][^9] The 1929 Bulletin says he succeeded "to the keeper's position on his father's death in 1911, and he is still light keeper at this station."[^14] He kept the light until 1941, when, according to New England Lighthouses, "Coast Guard keepers took over the operation of Butler Flats Light."[^4] LighthouseFriends says Joseph G. Hindley "started as an assistant at Butler Flats in 1931" and became head keeper when Baker retired.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded four occasions, between 1914 and 1923, when Charles A. Baker helped people in boats. On 8 May 1914 Charles A. Baker, "keeper of Butler Flats Light Station, Mass., towed a disabled motor boat with three men on board to wharf."[^10] In 1919 the Bulletin reported that he rescued men "whose boat had capsized" near the station.[^11] In 1921 the Bulletin reported that Baker had been commended for "assistance rendered" to a disabled sloop.[^12] In 1923 the Bulletin reports him helping "in towing a man in a rowboat who had lost an oar."[^13]

LighthouseFriends says Charles A. Baker "was alone at Butler Flats Lighthouse in 1938 when the great New England hurricane struck." It also quotes the captain's daughter, Amy, on the winter ice: "In the winter ice shakes the light a good deal at times."[^3] LighthouseFriends reports that on 7 April 1962 two people, trying to climb the ladder to the light after returning in the station's skiff, were swept away by rough seas, and that both drowned.[^3]

## After the keepers

In 1975 the Coast Guard announced plans to deactivate the light, move the beacon to the nearby hurricane barrier and dismantle the historic structure.[^1] New England Lighthouses dates a new automatic light and fog signal on the barrier to 1975.[^4] The National Register nomination says "The station was unmanned and" automated in 1978, and in 1979 a revocable license was "given to the city of New Bedford to operate the" light as a private aid.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says that after 1978 the city leased the light and restored it "through the efforts and financial support of local businesses," and that "Bristol County House of Correction inmates rewired the electrical system."[^3] More than 600 people came to the 100th anniversary on 30 April 1998, when a brighter 250-watt optic was installed.[^3] New England Lighthouses says the light "has not been an active aid to navigation since 2014."[^4] The 2015 auction ended with a high bid of $80,000,[^4] and the buyers formed a non-profit, Mass Light.[^3] In 2019 the city council approved a $250,000 grant to stabilize and repair the caisson foundation and concrete deck.[^3]

## Today

The light is privately owned and is not open to the public, according to the US Lighthouse Society's record.[^9] The nomination says "Access to the lighthouse is made only by boat."[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that "The lighthouse is privately owned. Tower closed." and that "This light is best seen from the water."[^3]

Sources differ on whether the light still shows. The US Lighthouse Society's record lists it as an active aid,[^9] and Cyberlights says it is operational.[^1] LighthouseFriends and New England Lighthouses say it has not been an active aid since 2014.[^3][^4] This story does not settle the present status.
