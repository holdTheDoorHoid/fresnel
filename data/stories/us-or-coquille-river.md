The Coquille River Light stands in Bullards Beach State Park, two miles north of Bandon, Oregon, where the Coquille River empties into the Pacific.[^22][^4] Its brick tower, stuccoed over, was finished in October 1895 and first lit on 29 February 1896.[^1][^9] It was the last of the eight surviving lighthouses on the Oregon coast to be built, and in 1939 an automated light on the south jetty took over its work.[^1]

## The place

The river mouth was a hazard. The National Register nomination says the old channel "exhibited dangerous, rock-filled conditions."[^1] Lighthouse Friends says that in 1880 Congress funded a jetty on the south side of the entrance, which created a deep channel and brought a rapid rise in the number of ships entering the river.[^4]

By an act of 3 March 1891 Congress appropriated $50,000 "for establishing a light and fog signal at the mouth of the Coquille River." The locality was visited in September 1891, and a preliminary survey was made on the north side of the river, at its mouth, in May 1892.[^5]

## Building the light

In the year ended 30 June 1893 the Board reported that negotiations were "in progress for the purchase of the land needed for this station."[^6] In the year ended 30 June 1894 a deed was obtained: "A deed of the land was obtained and a survey of the site was made," and drawings were still being prepared.[^7]

In April 1895 the contractors "shipped men, tools, and materials to the station."[^8] Lighthouse Friends says the first work included levelling the top of Rackliff Rock to give the tower a base. It describes the brick tower, under stucco, as a cylinder joined to a long, eight-sided room that housed the fog-signal machinery and a large Daboll trumpet.[^4] The archive records the tower as conical and attached to the signal building, and gives its height as 40 feet (12.2 m) to the centre of the lantern.[^3]

The Board's report for 1896 says the contractors finished their work on 7 October 1895, that the lighting apparatus and fog-signal machinery were put in place, and that "as everything was in readiness the station went into commission February 29, 1896."[^9] To complete the station, the Board said, "a good water supply is to be provided," and an oil house and a landing place for supplies were still to be built.[^9] The archive gives the lens as a fourth-order Fresnel, installed in 1896.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says it was first shown from the tower on 29 February 1896.[^4]

The Daboll trumpet's first season ran "some 99 hours" before the end of June 1896.[^10] In the year ended 30 June 1897 it "was in operation some 455 hours" and used about seven tons of coal.[^11] The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1899 gives 419 hours.[^12]

The keepers' duplex and barn stood apart from the light. Lighthouse Friends says a long wooden walkway connected the lighthouse to the duplex, 650 feet (198 m) away, and that the barn stood 150 feet (46 m) beyond the dwelling.[^4] The National Register nomination says the dwelling and the barn "have since been destroyed."[^1]

In 1901 the Board's report described the dwelling and barn as "built on a bleak sand spit," with sand piling up around them. It proposed a new boathouse on piling, planking the area around the buildings, and "sand fences in portable sections."[^14]

## The keepers

James F. Barker was the first head keeper, from 1896 to 1897, and John M. Cowan his first assistant, from 1896 to 1900.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says both had been "transferred to Coquille River from Heceta Head."[^4] Edward Durgan (1898 to 1899) and Adam J. Hartman (1899 to 1906) followed Barker as head keepers.[^3]

Oscar Wiren was first assistant from 1903 to 1906 and head keeper from 1906 to 1921.[^3] Oscar R. Langlois became an assistant in 1910, head keeper in 1921, and stayed until 1939.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says he was born at Cape Blanco Lighthouse, where his father was principal keeper for thirty-five years, and that his first posting, in 1905, was Cape Arago.[^4]

The archive lists a Marie Langlois as first assistant in 1925, and does not say how this Marie Langlois was related to Oscar Langlois.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says Oscar Langlois met his wife, Marie Amundsen, at Cape Arago, but does not say whether she is the Marie Langlois on this roster.[^4] Charles F. Walters was first assistant from 1931 to 1940.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the year ended 30 June 1900 the Board reported that "During a heavy storm the high water carried away the water tank and foundation of the oil house and about 75 feet of walk leading to the light and fog-signal building."[^13] Lighthouse Friends says a new water tank was then placed on a concrete foundation.[^4]

On 12 April 1892 Keeper Edward M. Kelson and Surfmen William H. Green and James K. Sumner, of the Coquille River life-saving station, "lost their lives" when the station's surf boat was launched for practice at the river mouth.[^21] That was the life-saving station, a separate station with its own record, not the light.

In 1915 maritime interests petitioned to move the light. The Commissioner's report said that "In its present location the light is of no great benefit to navigation," and that the point "is being encroached upon by the Coquille River."[^15] The report for 1917 records a $6,000 appropriation for aids to navigation at or near the entrance, and a plan that "an electric incandescent light in lens lantern and an electrically operated fog-signal plant will be established on the opposite side of the river from the present Coquille River Light Station."[^16]

The report for 1919 says: "Navigators and others having petitioned that no change be made in the establishment, no work has been done nor have any funds been expended during the year."[^17] In 1920 the Commissioner's report says that "Correspondence was carried on throughout the year with the port of Bandon and the local electric light company."[^18] Lighthouse Friends says the money was eventually used for an electrically operated fog bell on the south jetty, which went into operation in 1924.[^4]

For the fiscal year 1929 the fog signal recorded 1,806 hours of fog, "about 20 per cent of the whole time during the year." The Bulletin lists the Coquille River station as the one reporting the most fog for the seventeenth district.[^19]

In September 1936 a fire swept into Bandon. Lighthouse Friends says it consumed all but sixteen of the town's 500 buildings. The light, separated from the fire by a water barrier, was not damaged, though ash and soot got inside.[^4] The Bulletin says that on 26 September, while the tender *Rose* was tied up at Bandon, parties from her went ashore and "assisted the inhabitants in getting clear" of the rapidly burning buildings. For 48 hours a considerable number of people were sheltered on the tender, and the Secretary of Commerce commended her officers and crew for their "zeal, courage" and acts of heroism.[^20] Lighthouse Friends says Keeper Langlois and his assistant, Charles Walters, were commended for "helping refugees during the fire."[^4]

## After the keepers

The National Register nomination says the light operated "until 1939 when the lighthouse succumbed to an automated light on the south jetty."[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the Coast Guard decided in 1939 that the light was no longer needed, the dwelling was disassembled, and the lighthouse was abandoned. It stood neglected for twenty-four years.[^4]

In 1963 the lighthouse and 34 acres around it were leased by the federal government to the State of Oregon as a park.[^1] The National Register record gives 22 March 1974 as the date published.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says a joint restoration by the state and the Army Corps of Engineers began in 1976, and that the lighthouse opened to the public in 1979.[^4] As part of Bandon's centennial celebration in 1991, a solar-powered light was placed in the tower.[^4] The archive now lists the optic as "SOLAR POWERED (NOT ACTIVE)."[^3]

Lighthouse Friends says a restoration "costing over $600,000" was carried out during the summer of 2007. The colours used were reportedly found on some older layers of stucco, but some local people insist that white is the historically accurate colour.[^4]

The Light-House Board's reports for 1891 to 1896 describe the construction under the Board, while Oregon State Parks says the lighthouse was "built by the U.S. Coast Guard in 1896." The two accounts differ, and this story follows the Board's reports.[^9][^22]

## Today

Oregon State Parks owns the lighthouse and manages it within Bullards Beach State Park.[^3][^22] The signal room is open from 11 a.m. to 5 p.m. from mid-May through September and is staffed by volunteers who interpret the area's history. The tower is closed to the public for safety concerns.[^22]
