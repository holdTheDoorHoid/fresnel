Franklin Island Light stands on Franklin Island in Muscongus Bay, off the town of Friendship in Knox County, Maine. Its keepers watched over the approach to the St. George River, a bay full of ledges, from a brick tower that dates to 1855. The island had lights before that tower, and the sources disagree about when the first one was shown.[^1][^2]

## The place

Franklin Island is a twelve-acre island in the bay, and the light stands on it.[^2] I. W. P. Lewis, who reported on New England's lights in 1842, wrote that the light is "in the center of St. George's river bay," which he called "one of the most dangerous localities on the coast of Maine," as LighthouseFriends quotes his report.[^2] The light marks the entrance to the river and to several harbors among the islands, and the bay has "dry and sunken ledges that swarm in every direction."[^2] Massachusetts ceded jurisdiction over the island to the United States in 1806.[^2]

Distances disagree. LighthouseFriends puts the island five miles offshore from Friendship. The Fish and Wildlife Service says the refuge is six miles from the town.[^2][^4]

## Building the light

The first tower was wooden. Contractors Benjamin Beal and Duncan W. Thaxter began the wooden tower and a keeper's dwelling for $3,150, LighthouseFriends reports.[^2] When first exhibited in 1807, the tower's fixed white light had a focal plane of fifty feet (15 m) above the surrounding sea.[^2] The archive gives 1805 as the year the station was established.[^1] The archive's year and LighthouseFriends' first-exhibition year may mark different events, and the sources do not explain the gap.

The second tower went up in 1830. Ezekiel D. Demuth built it of rubblestone.[^2] In 1842 the keeper, James T. Hanna, described the result, as LighthouseFriends quotes him: "This light-house was erected in 1830 and my father kept it from that time until I succeeded him, a year ago." He added that the tower "now leaks in all directions."[^2]

Things were no better by 1853. An inspector, W. B. Franklin, reported, as LighthouseFriends quotes him, that "the towers of both are entirely worthless," and he asked for the towers on Franklin Island and Baker Island to be rebuilt.[^2]

Congress appropriated money on 3 August 1854.[^2] The circular brick tower that stands today was completed in 1855, with a fourth-order revolving Fresnel lens.[^1][^2] The archive gives the light's height as 57 feet (17 m) above mean high water.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive lists 21 principal keepers, from John Lowell in 1807 to Charles N. Robinson, who left in 1933.[^1] Lowell kept the light for twenty-three years, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] Thomas Hanna followed him in 1830, and his son James T. Hanna took over in 1841.[^1][^2]

James T. Hanna's 1842 statement, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, describes the dwelling: "My dwelling-house is, if anything, worse than the tower," he wrote, and after every rain storm the parlor could not be used.[^2]

George D. Pottle kept the light from 1888 to 1900.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends reports that he protected the nesting terns on three small islands nearby.[^2] In a 1901 report by an American Ornithologists' Union committee, printed in The Auk and quoted by LighthouseFriends, Pottle estimated that the three tern colonies had grown by one thousand pairs by the end of the season.[^2]

Albert J. Clinch kept the light from 1913 to 1918.[^1] The Commissioner of Lighthouses printed his name, as keeper of Franklin Island Light Station, in its fiscal 1917 table of lighthouse staff who saved life or property. The entry gives the act as towing a disabled boat, with its occupant, to shore.[^6]

Roscoe M. Chandler kept the light from 1919 to 1924, and George E. Woodward followed him, from 1924 to 1926.[^1] Charles N. Robinson was the last keeper before the station was automated.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends says a gale had destroyed the boatways "the previous February," preventing use of the station's boat.[^2] Hanna's 1842 statement says one of the chimneys had blown down on "the 4th October last."[^2]

On 9 January 1925 Keeper George E. Woodward heard a distress signal and went to help a party of fishermen who were marooned near the light.[^5][^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded that they were "marooned on an island near the light station."[^5] LighthouseFriends adds that Woodward gave them food and loaned them a boat so they could get ashore, and that his health was poor at the time.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Board's reports list several small works. In 1895 an oil house was built and a cistern provided for the keeper's use.[^3] In 1897 an ell was added to the dwelling.[^7][^2] In 1899 a boat winch was provided.[^8]

The light was automated around 1933. LighthouseFriends says it was converted to an automated acetylene apparatus, and the station was destaffed. All the buildings except the tower and the oil house were burned.[^1][^2] The fourth-order Fresnel lens was later taken out and placed on display at the Coast Guard station at Boothbay Harbor.[^2]

The island was transferred to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 1973.[^2] The Coast Guard licensed Franklin Light Preservation Inc. to care for the lighthouse in 1999.[^2]

In 2001 a pier and a helipad were added, with Coast Guard approval. Two years later the state's Department of Environmental Protection ordered them removed. They had cost $50,000, and the state said they "unreasonably harm significant wildlife habitats," LighthouseFriends reports.[^2]

## Today

Franklin Island is part of the Franklin Island National Wildlife Refuge.[^4] It is closed to the public during the seabird nesting season, from 1 April to 31 August.[^4] LighthouseFriends gives the end of the closure as 31 July in its history and 31 August in its visitor notes.[^2] Tour boats from Port Clyde and New Harbor show visitors the nesting birds and the lighthouse.[^4] The tower is closed.[^2] The archive names Franklin Light Preservation, Inc. as owner and manager.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard owns the lighthouse and leases it to the preservation group.[^2] Outside the nesting season visitors are welcome to explore the island.[^4]
