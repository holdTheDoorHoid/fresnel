Milwaukee Breakwater Light is a five-story steel house on a concrete pier in Lake Michigan, rising from the south end of the harbor's north breakwater about 0.7 mile (1.1 km) east of the mouth of the Milwaukee River.[^1] Built in the 1920s to mark the main entrance to the port, it is designed in an Art Deco style, and it is reached by boat.[^1]

## The place

The Milwaukee River meets Lake Michigan at a natural mouth that was curved rather than straight, with a sand bar offshore, and both limited the size of vessels that could use the port.[^1] Local commercial interests first petitioned Washington for help in 1843; Congress made an appropriation in 1852, and the Army Corps of Engineers began work in 1857 on a straight channel half a mile north of the old river mouth, with wooden piers on either side.[^1] The Lighthouse Establishment marked the north pier with a light that became known as Milwaukee Pierhead Light.[^1]

Shelter for ships came next. In 1881 Congress authorized a Harbor of Refuge at Milwaukee, a breakwater in the lake where vessels could ride out bad weather or wait to enter the port; the northern section was finished by 1896 and a further mile by 1904.[^1] The southern end of that breakwater was first marked by an unmanned light showing red above white, which had become a single flashing red light by 1912.[^1] The Lighthouse Friends history says the Lighthouse Board spent years asking for a major light on the breakwater, and that in 1912 a steam lightship, *Milwaukee*, took up station three miles offshore from it.[^2]

In the 1910s the Corps proposed a longer system of breakwaters, about four miles in all, with several openings for ships and a principal entrance directly east of the river mouth. Congress approved the work in 1922, and the whole project was finished in 1930 at a cost of $6,250,000.[^1] The lighthouse was part of this rearrangement: the Commissioner of Lighthouses asked in 1923 for $229,800 for new and improved aids at Milwaukee, remarking, as Lighthouse Friends summarizes the request, that the lightship cost $19,000 a year to maintain and could probably be discontinued once the work was done.[^2]

## Building the light

The Bureau of Lighthouses chose a crib-and-pier foundation under a steel superstructure for the new light at the south end of the north breakwater.[^1] The National Register nomination names the Office of the Superintendent of Lighthouses in Milwaukee as architect and builder.[^1] It says the lighthouse was built and established in 1926, but the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that the station "went into commission" on 26 February 1927, and Lighthouse Friends gives the same date, adding that a temporary light had marked the breakwater until then.[^3][^2] The sources do not say when in 1926 the structure was finished or when it was first lit.

The sources also differ on what lies under the water. The nomination describes a wood and concrete crib about 30 feet (9 m) tall, built of heavy timbers with steel fastenings and filled with rock ballast and concrete, under a concrete pier 60 by 54 feet (18 by 16 m).[^1] The Lighthouse Service's own description, in the Bulletin of February 1928, describes the subfoundation as three reinforced-concrete caissons side by side, filled with riprap and capped by a thick concrete slab, resting on rip rap in about 33 feet (10 m) of water.[^3] The Commissioner's report, quoted by Lighthouse Friends, puts the depth at 34 feet.[^2]

Above the reinforced-concrete basement the building was steel. The Bulletin described a two-story steel building with a tower rising two more stories from the middle of one side, topped by a fourth-order lantern.[^3] The basement held the fog-signal machinery, the radiobeacon apparatus, the heating plant, coal bins, an oil room and a workshop; above it were a large boat room for the launch and quarters for four keepers, with an air-powered derrick to handle the boat.[^3] The nomination records that the boat room had a rail track for the keepers' boat and that a steel crane stood on the pier deck until 1966.[^1] The station took its power from the city, by cable to the outer end of the north pierhead and from there by a 4,300-foot (1,300 m) submarine cable.[^3] The Commissioner's report gave the cost of the station as $150,939 and of the whole project as $184,950.[^2]

The light itself came partly second-hand. According to the nomination, the lantern and fourth-order Fresnel lens of the 1906 Milwaukee Pierhead Light were taken down and installed in the new breakwater light, and a decagonal lantern with a fifth-order lens went up on the pierhead in their place.[^1] The nomination gives the original signal as a red light on for 4.5 seconds and off for 1.5, at a focal plane 61 feet (19 m) above the water; the Bulletin says instead that a fixed lens was given its characteristic by a commercial sign flasher and puts the focal plane at 67 feet (20 m).[^1][^3] A diaphone fog signal ran on compressed air, and a 200-watt radiobeacon transmitter served with it.[^3] The nomination explains how the pair worked: the radio signal and a blast of the horn were sent together, so a pilot who counted the seconds between hearing one and the other could judge his distance from the light, each second being roughly a fifth of a mile.[^1]

The original color was red. The nomination says the superstructure was repainted buff in the 1930s and white in the 1960s, with a black lantern.[^1]

## The keepers

The station was run by a resident crew of four keepers of the Lighthouse Service.[^1] The keepers' roster in the US Lighthouse Society archive begins in 1928, when Reynold W. Johnson became head keeper, with George Rogan, Charles A. Linsmeier and Michael M. Rotta as his three assistants.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says that in that year the keeper of North Point Lighthouse and the three keepers of the pierhead light were joined into one four-man Milwaukee Harbor Station, responsible for the North Point and Pierhead lights, the breakwater light and the smaller lights on the breakwater.[^2] The keepers' work thus took them across the harbor as well as to the house at the harbor entrance.

Two families at the harbor lights suffered losses that Lighthouse Friends records. In August 1926, Keeper Rogan's five-year-old son George was watching workmen repair the pierhead fog signal when he fell into the water and drowned; nine years later the Coast Guard rescued two other Rogan sons and four more boys whose rowboat was caught in a squall near the breakwater lighthouse.[^2] Rogan left for Big Sable Point Lighthouse in 1936.[^2] Michael Rotta, who had come from North Manitou Lighthouse in 1928, was keeper of the pierhead light for several years and put in charge of all the Milwaukee lights in 1941.[^2] In April 1937 his six-year-old son Michael Jr., who is said to have wanted to be a lighthouse keeper, was killed by a truck on the way home from school.[^2]

Rotta himself died at the breakwater light. Lighthouse Friends gives the date as 21 May 1952, and Milwaukee Magazine also says 1952; an OnMilwaukee article gives 1953, and the archive roster closes his term as head keeper in 1952.[^2][^5][^6][^4] Rotta was painting the lighthouse from a staging when he fell to the concrete crib.[^2] Alfred Schreiber, an assistant keeper who was painting North Point Lighthouse that day, recalled that the left-hand tackle had come unhooked at the top railing, and that the upper block had not been moused ; of how it happened, he said, "No one knows exactly what happened."[^2]

After the Bureau of Lighthouses merged with the Coast Guard in 1939, Coast Guard personnel were assigned as keepers.[^1] Lighthouse Friends draws on a 2003 *Lighthouse Digest* account by Jack Eckert, who was assigned to the light in 1955, when four coastguardsmen served three days on and three days off, with two always at the lighthouse on twelve-hour watches. Their main duty was watching the timing of the radiobeacon and the distance-finding blast of the horn, and each day they cleaned cobwebs from the lantern room.[^2] A Milwaukee brewery had supplied a television, and the station followed the Milwaukee Braves' televised games that year; fishermen sometimes climbed the steel ladder up to the light and entered unannounced.[^2] The archive lists about two dozen Coast Guard men at the light between 1959 and 1966.[^4]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1966.[^1][^4] The nomination says that afterward the diesel engines, fuel tanks, compressors and generators, the four compressed-air tanks for the fog signal, the crane and the two smokestacks were removed.[^1] The radiobeacon, which the nomination says operated from 1926, was discontinued in 1995, and the fourth-order Fresnel lens was taken out in 1994 and replaced with a modern rotating beacon.[^1] The old lens went on loan to the Wisconsin Maritime Museum in Manitowoc, where it is on display.[^1][^2]

The National Register nomination, prepared by the Coast Guard and dated 1 June 2011, treats 1926 to 1966 as the period of significance.[^1] Lighthouse Friends reports that the Coast Guard declared the lighthouse excess in June 2011 and offered it to eligible organizations under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000.[^2] In September 2013 the deed passed to Optima Enrichment, a Brookfield nonprofit, which put the cost of restoration at about $2.5 million and said it would open the lighthouse to the public at least four times a year from 2016.[^7] The Journal Sentinel reported in 2016 that the group had raised less than $20,000, most of it spent on insurance.[^8]

## Today

The lighthouse is still an active aid to navigation.[^1] The 2004 Coast Guard Light List, as reproduced by the US Lighthouse Society, gives its light as a red flash every 10 seconds and its horn as two blasts every 20 seconds.[^9] The nomination says the horn runs from April to November and that the tower is floodlighted from sunset to sunrise.[^1]

It stands in the open lake, is not open to visitors and is reached by boat.[^1] In 2024 a National Park Service spokesperson told the Journal Sentinel that Optima Enrichment still owned it and that the Service knew the group had not raised enough money to meet the obligations of the 2000 Act; the newspaper reported that the building was untouched and inaccessible.[^8] Lighthouse Friends says a good view of it can be had from the parking lot at the end of Erie Street, from which the pierhead light can also be reached on foot.[^2]
