White Shoal Light rises from a concrete pier in northern Lake Michigan, about 20 miles west of the Mackinac Bridge, painted in red and white spirals.[^14][^2] The Lighthouse Service first lit it on 1 September 1910 to replace a steam lightship that could not stay on station through the ice.[^3][^2]

## The place

The shoal lies at the northern end of Lake Michigan, off the western approach to the Straits of Mackinac.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says it was covered by only about five feet of water and lay four and a half miles northwest of Waugoshance Lighthouse, which had been lit since 1851; Terry Pepper puts it 2.6 miles northwest of Waugoshance Island, running almost two miles east to west, with its western end breaking the surface.[^2][^4] Pepper adds that vessel traffic grew sharply in the late 1880s and that the Lighthouse Board singled out White Shoal, Simmons Reef and Gray's Reef as hazards needing marks at once.[^4]

Money for a permanent tower was not at hand. Congress had set aside $60,000 in 1889 for a light on Simmons Reef, but a survey found it could not be built for less than $150,000, and in March 1891 Congress let the Board spend the sum on three wooden steam lightships instead.[^2] The Board itself said it was trying inexpensive lightships in place of costly lighthouses "at the earnest request of the lake vessel-men."[^5] They were numbered 55, 56 and 57, and No. 56 went to White Shoal.[^5]

The Board's own list of new lights and whistles gives 22 October 1891 for her first signals; Lighthouse Friends and Pepper both say she reached her station on 24 October.[^6][^2][^4] Pepper reports that her first crew left the station without orders during a storm on 17 November and went into winter quarters at Cheboygan, and that the whole crew was discharged for it.[^4] In December 1892 the ship left her station and reached Cheboygan the following day, where she wintered.[^7] In the year covered by the Board's 1895 report her 6-inch steam whistle ran about 459 hours and burned some 23 tons of coal.[^8]

The weakness of a lightship was the calendar. The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported in 1912 that, because of ice, the vessel was "always withdrawn in the fall before navigation had fully closed and replaced in the spring after it had opened," leaving the shoal unmarked in the two seasons when traffic was heaviest.[^2] Pepper says the Board asked Congress for a permanent station in 1906.[^4] Congress appropriated $250,000 on 4 March 1907.[^3]

## Building the light

Lighthouse Friends reports that Gillen Construction Company of Milwaukee won the foundation contract at $93,785.60 and that work on the shoal began in May 1908.[^2] A timber crib 72 feet square (22 m) and 18 feet 6 inches high was built ashore, floated out and sunk on a bed of stone, and it carried a concrete pier whose deck stood 20 feet above the lake.[^2] Pepper says the crib was built at St. Ignace and filled with 4,000 tons of stone, and that an acetylene lantern on a temporary steel frame was lit on 5 December 1908 before the winter storms ended the season.[^4]

The tower was raised in 1909 and 1910. It is steel, backed with brick and faced with terra cotta, with an aluminum lantern, and the 1912 description counts nine stories, counting the cellar inside the pier that held the oil tanks.[^2] The light was placed in commission on 1 September 1910 and the fog signal on 15 September.[^3] The lens was a second-order flashing lens of two panels, rotating on a mercury float and driven by clockwork; the Commissioner's 1912 report estimated 1,200,000 candles and a range of 20 miles in clear weather, while Pepper gives 28 miles.[^2][^4] The tower cost $224,563.83 against the $250,000 appropriated.[^2] A submarine bell was established on 20 September 1911.[^3] Pepper says it was discontinued after the 1914 season and Lighthouse Friends says after 1916; the Commissioner's list of submarine signals discontinued in the fiscal year ended June 1917 includes White Shoal, which fits 1916.[^3][^4][^2][^9]

## The keepers

The station had four keepers: a principal keeper and three assistants.[^2] The archive's lists open with James M. Marshall as principal keeper in 1910, followed by Ingvald Olsen from 1910 to 1914; Lighthouse Friends calls Olsen the station's first keeper, so the two lists differ on who held the post on opening day.[^1][^2] Under Olsen the station won the district's efficiency pennant, and in late 1914 he was transferred to Sheboygan, Wisconsin.[^10][^2] Marshall, who had served as first assistant from 1912, came back as principal keeper from 1914 to 1919.[^1]

William Barnum held the post from 1919 to 1934 and Frank E. Frederickson from 1934 to 1946.[^1] George Keller's name runs through more than two decades of the archive's lists: it shows him as second assistant from 1923, first assistant from 1930 to 1946, and principal keeper in 1946.[^1] Coast Guard names fill the archive's lists from about 1960 to 1970.[^1] After the Coast Guard took over the nation's aids to navigation in 1939, Pepper says, the station had four-man crews on a schedule of 14 days on and 7 off, with three men on board at all times.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Commissioner's table of life-saving acts for the fiscal year ended June 1915 credits Keeper Marshall and Third Assistant Sullivan, together with the first assistant keeper of Old Mackinac Point, in a single entry.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says the two men from White Shoal were credited with saving the launch *Ida L* during a gale.[^2] In August 1916 the Bulletin reported that second assistant keeper John Sullivan towed a large gasoline motor boat to harbor after it had drifted for 12 hours without fuel.[^11] In June 1920 assistant keeper L. R. O'Neal helped four people in a disabled motor boat.[^12]

The tower's keepers did not stay through the whole of navigation. In 1914 the Bulletin announced that unwatched acetylene lights, running for about two weeks, would be put in commission at White Shoal and two other Michigan stations "when the keepers are taken off the stations for the winter about December 10."[^13] The Commissioner records the auxiliary flashing winter light as installed in December 1914.[^3]

## After the keepers

The station's appearance and equipment changed several times. The air whistle was enlarged from 6 to 10 inches on 22 April 1914 and replaced in 1938 by a diaphragm horn.[^2] In 1930 the light was made electric and its intensity raised from 360,000 to 3,000,000 candlepower.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the tower, white with a narrow black band when built, was painted black in 1927, white again in 1937, and given red and white spiral bands in 1954; Pepper says the spiral scheme dates from 1990, so the sources disagree.[^2][^4]

White Shoal was automated in 1976.[^2][^4] Pepper puts the removal of the second-order lens in 1983 and Lighthouse Friends in 1987; both say it is displayed at the Great Lakes Shipwreck Museum at Whitefish Point.[^4][^2] The Coast Guard declared the lighthouse excess in 2014 under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, and when a new custodian was not found the General Services Administration auctioned it online.[^2] The owners say the winning bid was $110,009, placed in September 2016.[^14]

## Today

White Shoal Light is an active aid to navigation, privately owned and not open to the public; it stands offshore, and Lighthouse Friends notes that a lengthy boat ride is needed to see it up close.[^1][^2][^14] Its owners, Brent Tompkins and Mike Lynch, run the White Shoal Light Historical Preservation Society, whose stated aim is to restore the light to a mid-1950s appearance and offer day tours and volunteer opportunities.[^14] In 2018 they told a local news outlet they also planned to open it as a bed-and-breakfast; the article describes a plan, and no source found reports that the work was finished.[^15]
