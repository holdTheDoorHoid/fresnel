Two brick towers stand side by side on a marsh island in South Carolina's Cape Romain National Wildlife Refuge: a short one from 1827 and a tall, octagonal, visibly leaning one from 1857. Together they warned ships off a shoal nine miles to the southeast between 1827 and 1947, and the island has had no resident keepers since 1937.[^1][^3]

## The place

The station sits on Lighthouse Island, which the National Register nomination says was originally called Racoon Key, in Charleston County.[^1] LighthouseFriends puts it about six miles offshore from McClellanville, and no major port lies nearby; the light's job was to warn mariners of a dangerous shoal nine miles to the southeast.[^2] The 1931 *Lighthouse Service Bulletin* described the island as low-lying, some 10 miles from McClellanville by tortuous channels through the marsh.[^6]

Before there was a lighthouse there was a tower of another kind. The Cape Romain Lighthouse Preservation Society says a wind-powered sawmill was built nearby in 1792 and left a 60-foot tower after its machinery was moved away by 1820; sailors, it says, confused that tower with the Charleston harbour light.[^13] LighthouseFriends tells a related story in its own words: the first plan was to reuse an old stone windmill that mariners had been mistaking for the Charleston light, but the ground around it flooded and had no fresh water, so a site on another island was bought after long negotiations.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress's 1823 act included $10,000 for a lighthouse at Cape Romain, according to the National Register nomination.[^1] LighthouseFriends adds that the money lapsed into the surplus fund while the land was being negotiated, so work could not start until Congress appropriated $17,000 on 18 May 1826.[^2] (The Preservation Society dates the funding to 1825, a year the other two sources do not use.[^13])

The first tower was a 65-foot (20 m) conical brick tower, built in 1827 under the contractor Winslow Lewis, who fitted it with his patent lamps and reflectors.[^1][^2] The sources disagree on cost. The nomination gives $7,475 for the tower and a dwelling, and $950 for the lighting apparatus; LighthouseFriends gives $12,001 for the whole.[^1][^2] The nomination records that the wooden lintel of the single doorway is carved with the word "Light" and the date 1827.[^1] The 1931 Bulletin says the light was placed in commission in 1828, so the first lighting date is not settled between the sources.[^6]

The light did not do its job well. LighthouseFriends says the weak signal failed at its main task, even after the apparatus was upgraded in 1847 to a fixed light of eleven lamps with 21-inch reflectors.[^1][^2] In 1852 the new Lighthouse Board asked for money to replace what it called the "present small and useless light at Cape Romain" with a first-class seacoast light, as LighthouseFriends quotes the request.[^2] Congress appropriated $20,000 in March 1853 and $30,000 in August 1854.[^2]

The new tower, 150 feet (46 m) high and octagonal, went up beside the old one, with a focal plane 161 feet (49 m) above the sea and a first-order Fresnel lens.[^1] It was first lit on 1 January 1858, according to the nomination and LighthouseFriends; the 1931 Bulletin says the new tower was first lighted in 1857, and gives the old tower as only about 80 feet high against the 65 feet given above.[^1][^2][^6] The nomination says it was "reputedly" built with slave labour; LighthouseFriends states it as fact.[^1][^2] The nomination also records a local tradition about the lean the tower is known for: that halfway up it was discovered the walls were not sloping at the right angles, and that despite orders to correct the error the tower was finished leaning.[^1] LighthouseFriends instead attributes the lean to the foundation settling, and says the tower leaned 23 and a fraction inches in 1873, more the next year, and then steadied.[^2] By 1931 the Lighthouse Service described it as "about 3 feet out of plumb, just enough for one of the octagonal sides of the tower to be perpendicular."[^6]

Because the new light revolved to give a flash every minute, it needed more work than the fixed light, and two assistants were hired to help the head keeper.[^2] A Light-House Board report of the early 1890s, discussing the Cape Fear light, noted that there was no first-order light-house between Cape Lookout, North Carolina, and Cape Romain, a distance of about 170 nautical miles.[^12]

## The keepers

The first keeper, Thomas Skrine, was recommended by his congressman from a field of eleven candidates; his appointment at $400 a year was submitted for approval on 1 June 1827, and President John Quincy Adams endorsed it three days later.[^2] The archive's roster lists Thomas Skrine as head keeper from 1827 to 1839 and a Thadeus C. Skrine from 1839 to 1857, so a Skrine kept the light for thirty years.[^3] From 1857 the roster lists a head keeper, a first assistant and a second assistant.[^3]

**The Civil War gap.** The roster has no keeper between 1861 and 1866.[^3] LighthouseFriends says Confederate forces put the light out soon after the war began and destroyed the lens and lantern so the Union could not use the tower; it was relit in 1866.[^2] The nomination, which calls this tradition, says the condition of the lights was reviewed in 1867 and the tower found in good condition, though the keepers' houses leaked, probably from wartime neglect.[^1]

**Andrew Johnson and the death of his wife.** One episode from the keepers' years is a death that the sources still describe as unsettled. On the evening of 8 April 1873 the wife of Keeper Andrew Johnson was found dead in the keeper's house. The *Daily Phoenix* of Columbia, South Carolina, reported a week later that "Captain Andrew Johnson is the keeper of the light-house at Cape Romain, where he resided with his wife," and that he went up to the tower about 5 o'clock to light the lamp.[^4] The paper says assistant keeper Julius L. Lee went to relieve him at about half past eight; LighthouseFriends and *Lighthouse Digest* both say 9 p.m.[^4][^2][^5] Lee saw Johnson running toward the assistants' dwelling crying that his wife was dead.[^4] She was found in the middle of the floor, and the paper reports that her throat had been cut with a razor.[^4] Her jewelry and a sum of money were missing, though some of her husband's jewelry was left.[^4] The newspaper adds that she had called on her neighbour Mrs. Lee earlier "in good health and oheerful spirits" (as its OCR text reads), and that Johnson said she had once told him she had tried to take her own life.[^4] *Lighthouse Digest* gives the missing money as $1,400, withdrawn from a bank days earlier, says the death was ruled a suicide, and notes that questions remain.[^5] Johnson stayed at Cape Romain until 1875, when the roster ends his term, and *Lighthouse Digest* says he was then transferred to Georgetown.[^3][^5] *Lighthouse Digest* calls the mystery unsolved.[^5]

*Lighthouse Digest* says Johnson was a native of Norway who arrived in 1867 as an assistant keeper and was later promoted; the archive roster lists him as head keeper from 1867.[^5][^3]

**John Robertson, who stayed.** The roster shows John Robertson at the station from 1873: second assistant, then first assistant from 1874, then head keeper from 1880 until 1906.[^3] That is more than three decades on one island.

**August F. Wichmann.** Wichmann appears first as a second assistant in 1906 to 1908, and returned as head keeper from 1913 until 1934.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he earned commendations several times for helping mariners and that the station won the lighthouse efficiency flag at least once; the Bulletin index for 1918 to 1923 lists an efficiency flag for the station.[^2][^9] LighthouseFriends also tells a story of the birth of his son in about 1930, when a midwife held up the newborn at a window just as he came home with a big bass; it is a family anecdote from a single source.[^2]

**Lemuel G. Owens**, first assistant from 1927 and the last head keeper, was injured on 6 April 1937 when acid exploded as he poured it into an old bottle, according to LighthouseFriends; it says he lost his right eye.[^2][^3] The station was automated that year.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A dated account of the station survives from the Charleston earthquake of 31 August 1886. LighthouseFriends reproduces the Lighthouse Board's account of that night: "a low rumbling noise was heard in a west-southwest direction," like "a battery of artillery or a troop of cavalry crossing a long bridge," followed by shocks that threw things off shelves, stopped all the clocks and brought down a chimney and cracked others and the plaster in the dwellings, though the tower and lantern were not damaged.[^2] LighthouseFriends adds that the head keeper was in bed with malarial fever, so the assistants' families sheltered in his house, and that the station's cattle and poultry ran about in fright.[^2]

In 1886 the old tower was also fitted with a concrete floor, a fireproof door and shelving, so that it could be used as an oil house for 450 cans of oil.[^2]

In 1916 (the *Bulletin* volume for that year carries the report) Keeper Wichmann went to the aid of the crew of the schooner barge *Northwest*, which had broken adrift from its tow and wrecked near the station. On 16 July two of her men came to the station and reported that they had floated ashore about 6 miles away on wreckage and left their captain and two others on the beach too weak to reach the station. The station's motor boat had been damaged by the storm, so the keeper went out in a rowboat; the Bulletin says that on the way back he carried the captain on his back part of the way, found it hard going along the beach after dark, "was in danger of being drowned," and brought the men back after 14 hours.[^7] LighthouseFriends says Wichmann and the barge's captain exchanged Christmas cards for many years afterwards.[^2]

Smaller services followed. A later Bulletin records Wichmann and assistant Ludie R. Munn lodging four people from the power boat *Wallie A. Silsbie* at the station for eight days.[^8] The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported Wichmann and Munn helping to float the yacht *Runaway* after it grounded about 2 miles from the station, and a Bulletin index for 1924 to 1929 lists Wichmann and first assistant Theodore T. Gaillard for assisting a vessel.[^11][^10]

## After the keepers

In 1931 the station was modernized. Two gasoline-engine generators were installed in a new power and store house and both tower and dwellings were wired for electricity.[^6] A 500-watt bulb went into the first-order lens; the Bulletin says the flash grew to 390,000 candlepower, and LighthouseFriends says 430,000 (from 280,000).[^6][^2] The characteristic changed from one flash a minute to a group of three flashes every 30 seconds, and an electric motor replaced the clockwork.[^6] The same account says the two assistants' brick dwellings were believed to be 100 years old, and that termites had damaged the second assistant's house so badly that it was unsafe; the brick walls were kept in the remodelling.[^6]

The nomination says a 1000 candlepower stationary light fitted around 1937 needed attention only every three months, which ended the need for resident keepers.[^1] The roster's last names are dated 1937.[^3] The light was discontinued in 1947, and LighthouseFriends says lighted buoys were placed offshore to mark the shoals.[^2] The nomination says the keepers' houses and other support buildings were destroyed in 1947; LighthouseFriends says only the foundations of the dwellings remain.[^1][^2] The island was then conveyed to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and became part of the Cape Romain National Wildlife Refuge.[^13] The state's Historic Preservation Officer nominated the towers to the National Register of Historic Places in September 1981, naming the Fish and Wildlife Service as owner.[^1]

## Today

The island is part of a federal wildlife refuge, and the towers are owned by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.[^1][^2] The only way to see the lighthouses is by boat, and the refuge allows landing only on a tour led by Coastal Expeditions from the McClellanville boat landing, which runs only a handful of times a year; there is no dock, so a muddy marsh walk is needed to reach the high ground, and weather or government limits can cancel the landing.[^14][^2] The towers themselves are closed to the public.[^2] LighthouseFriends records that new doors were fitted to both around 2010 and that a McClellanville contractor made emergency repairs to secure the 1857 lantern room in 2011.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the Sewee Visitor Center, working with Coastal Expeditions, offers six trips a year and is the only service permitted to take visitors onto the refuge islands.[^2]
