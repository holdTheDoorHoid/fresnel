Point Fermin Light stands on a bluff at the southern tip of Los Angeles, on the western edge of the harbor at San Pedro.[^3][^1] Its wooden tower was first lit on 15 December 1874, and the Los Angeles Times calls it the city's first lighthouse.[^2][^1][^3] The light was put out in December 1941, and the NRHP nomination says it stayed off after the war.[^1][^2]

## The place

The point was named in 1793 by George Vancouver, after Father Fermin Francisco de Lasuen, who had succeeded Father Junipero Serra as president of the California missions.[^1] The harbor at San Pedro grew in the late 1840s as Los Angeles did. A lighthouse site there was chosen in 1855, but the government had no approved title to it, and the project stalled.[^1] The Los Angeles Times credits Phineas Banning, who earned the title "Father of Los Angeles Harbor," with about twenty years of lobbying for the light.[^3]

The nomination says the light was needed by vessels entering Los Angeles Harbor, and that off the cliffs where it stands some 35 ships have been lost, mostly in dense fog.[^2] The Los Angeles Times says a wind that whipped along the bluff in storm season chilled the first keepers to the bone.[^3]

## Building the light

The parcel had several owners, some of them minor heirs, so buying it proved impossible and the government went to court instead.[^1] Congress had appropriated $20,000 for a lighthouse and fog signal in June 1872.[^1] The government won a condemnation suit on 4 November 1873, and work began on 21 February 1874.[^1] The redwood and fir were delivered by ship.[^1] The station also had two large cisterns and outbuildings, all enclosed by a substantial fence.[^1]

The Los Angeles Times describes a multiprism lens mounted in brass, shipped from France at a cost of about $12,000.[^3] The light flashed red and white at first, LighthouseFriends says. It was changed to fixed white on 1 April 1889 and back to flashing in 1912.[^1][^8]

## The keepers

The first keepers were two sisters, Mary L. Smith and Ella T. Smith, who came from Ediz Hook Lighthouse in Washington.[^1][^10][^11] They lit the lamp on 15 December 1874.[^1] Mary Smith is the head keeper on the Lighthouse Service roster for 1874 to 1882, and Ella the first assistant.[^10] After eight years the sisters resigned. LighthouseFriends says they found life too lonely.[^1] The Los Angeles Times names the second sister Helen, not Ella, and gives Mary's pay as $700 a year.[^3] The sources disagree on that name.

George N. Shaw was head keeper from 1882 to 1904, and the Times says he held the post for more than two decades, living at the lighthouse with his wife and daughter.[^3][^10] Irby H. Engels followed (1904 to 1916), then Ralph H. Jordan (1916 to 1917).[^10]

Willie Austin took the post in 1917 and moved in with his wife, Martha, and their children.[^1][^3] The roster lists Martha M. Austin as a laborer from 1917 to at least 1921.[^10] In 1925 Martha died. The Times says her husband died three months later, and that their daughter Thelma, then 24, applied for the job and got it.[^3] LighthouseFriends gives two months for his death.[^1] The roster ends Thelma Austin's service in 1927. LighthouseFriends says that year the Los Angeles Park Department made an agreement with the Lighthouse Service, letting a park superintendent live in the dwelling in exchange for looking after the electrified light.[^1][^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1888 the British ship Respigadera came toward the shore.[^3] The nomination says it struck a rock and sank within 20 minutes after it cut too close to the Point.[^2] The Times says the Shaws watched it strike a reef and stop, after which it was forced into deeper water and destroyed with dynamite.[^3] The two accounts do not agree on how the ship ended.

On 1 March 1921 the Austin family watched the Coast Guard tow a German U-boat out to sea off the point. A destroyer fired on it before it sank.[^3]

In 1926 the steamer David C. Meyer went aground. Thelma Austin radioed for help, and the Times says no lives were lost.[^3] The nomination lists the David C. Meyer among the ships lost, so the two sources may disagree about the outcome, or about the event.[^2]

When the Long Beach earthquake struck on 10 March 1933, the Lighthouse Service reported that the tower at Los Angeles Harbor and the old combined dwelling and tower at Point Fermin, then used by the Los Angeles Park Commission, were not damaged.[^9]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Board's report for 1900 says the dwelling, outhouses and fence were painted, and the keeper was given lumber for minor repairs.[^4] The Board's report for 1896 to 1897 lists Point Fermin among the stations where repairs and renovations were made that fiscal year.[^5]

In 1915 the Commissioner's report proposed a new light at Point Vicente. It said the Point Fermin light would be discontinued once that station was completed.[^6] George Putnam's 1917 history of the lights repeats that plan.[^7]

The nomination says that in 1925 the oil lamps were replaced by an electric light that reached 18 miles (29 km) out to sea.[^2] The Times dates the final changeover to electricity about three years after the 1926 grounding of the David C. Meyer, and says it ended Thelma Austin's work.[^3]

After Pearl Harbor the beacon was blacked out. LighthouseFriends says the light was last active on 9 December 1941, two days after the attack. The Times says blackout warnings forced it out within hours of the attack.[^1][^3] The nomination says the Coast Guard turned the light off for security and painted the gleaming white building "wartime green." Its wartime service included conversion to a radar lookout station, the first of its kind in the harbor area.[^2] The Times says the Navy used the lighthouse as an experimental radar station.[^3] Shortly after the light went out, LighthouseFriends says, the lantern room was removed and the top became a box-shaped lookout known as the "chicken coop."[^1] After the war a beacon on a plain pole stood nearby.[^3]

In 1948 the city took the lighthouse back and made it a residence for a park maintenance supervisor.[^3]

The Coast Guard considered tearing the lighthouse down in 1972. Bill Olesen and John Olguin led a campaign to save it, starting with its listing on the National Register of Historic Places in June 1972. Restoration followed, and the centennial was held on 2 November 1974.[^1] In preparation for the centennial, the pair tracked the Fresnel lens, removed before the war, to a Malibu real estate office, LighthouseFriends says. Whether it had come from this lighthouse was unresolved for several years. On 13 November 2006 the lens was brought back and placed on the ground floor.[^1] On 20 January 2015 the lighthouse was awarded to the City of Los Angeles, and the formal transfer took place in December 2018.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse stands in Point Fermin Park. The City of Los Angeles owns it, and the Point Fermin Lighthouse Society and the Point Fermin Historical Society will help oversee it.[^1] A 2002 Times article described the light as Coast Guard-owned, before the 2015 award.[^3]

LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is open for tours Tuesday through Sunday, and that the lens is on display on the ground floor.[^1] A 2002 Times article said the grounds were open and the interior was not, so visiting hours may have changed since.[^3]
