Mabou Lighthouse stands at McKeens Point, at the mouth of Mabou Harbour on the west coast of Cape Breton, Nova Scotia. Two range lights first marked the harbour entrance in 1884. The square wooden tower there now dates from 1908, when towers replaced the mast lights.[^1][^2][^5]

## The place

The register calls Mabou Harbour the only protected harbour on the western side of Cape Breton.[^2] Its natural entrance was a channel obstructed by a bar that left only 1.2 m (4 ft) of water over it at low tide.[^1] Work began in 1872 on a 229 m (753 ft) pier on the south side of a new, straight channel that was dredged into the harbour. The work took four years, and the old channel was then closed.[^1] Vessels called there for gypsum and coal.[^1] The register says the light supported the development of the harbour's two local industries, fishing and gypsum mining.[^2]

## Building the light

The Department of Marine put the two range lights into operation on 15 July 1884.[^1] Each was shown from a small dioptric lantern hoisted on a mast, with a shed painted white at its base.[^1] The outer light was fixed white. It stood at the end of the breakwater pier, 7.6 m (25 ft) above high water, and a notice of the time, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, said it should be visible 9 miles from all points seaward. The inner light was fixed red, 914 m (3,000 ft) away on the shore at McFayden's Wharf, and stood 9 m (30 ft) above the water.[^1] Lined up one behind the other, the two lights led vessels through the dredged channel and past the pier.[^1]

In 1892 the outer light was taken off the pier at the close of navigation, because the pier had fallen into poor repair, and it went back up the next spring.[^1]

In 1908 E. C. Embree of Port Hawkesbury was awarded a $2,450 contract to replace the mast lights with enclosed towers.[^1][^5] The Department's report for 1909 describes the finished work: two square wooden towers with sloping sides and square wooden lanterns. The front tower was 10 m (33 ft) high and the rear tower 14 m (47 ft), measured to the top of the lantern ventilator.[^5]

## The keepers

Allan McLean was hired as the first keeper of the rear light, and John McDonald as the first keeper of the front light. Both earned $50 a year at first. McDonald soon received $20 more a year, because he had to cross the harbour entrance to reach his light on the south pier.[^1]

Edward Doyle kept the front light from 1897 to 1912. The Department's keeper list for 1906 places him at the "Mabou Front Range Light."[^1][^6]

Roderick McLean kept the rear light from 1906 until 1934. William Cummings kept it from 1935 until 1966, the year the light was made unwatched.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the night of 14 November 1904 a gale carried away the mast and shed that held the front light on the breakwater.[^1][^4] The Department's report for 1904 records that the light "was carried away by a gale on the night of the 14th November, 1904," and that a temporary pole light was kept in its place.[^4]

In December 1964 a storm tore most of one wall off the front tower.[^1]

## After the keepers

In the spring of 1965 the wooden front tower was demolished, and a red steel skeleton tower was put up to show the front light.[^1] The rear light was electrified in August 1966 and made unwatched.[^1] The front light was discontinued on 9 November 1987. From then on the 1908 tower at the wharf was the harbour's only light, guiding boats toward the wharf.[^1][^2]

## Today

The 1908 tower is a two-storey wooden tower that tapers from a square base to a short, four-sided lantern.[^2] The register recognized it as a Federal Heritage Building on 18 March 2004, although LighthouseFriends gives 2006.[^2][^1] The register cites the tower's place in the history of aids to navigation, and says its design is consistent with the Department's standard plan for harbour and minor coastal lights.[^2]

The Coast Guard's List of Lights gives the light a fixed green characteristic, a focal height of 13.9 m and a range of 6 nautical miles. It describes the tower as white with a red upper portion, 13.7 m high.[^3] The same list calls the light "Seasonal" and a "Private aid." LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard owns it.[^1][^3] None of these sources explains the difference.

LighthouseFriends says the grounds and the tower are open.[^1] None of the sources says visitors may climb the tower, so this story does not suggest it.
