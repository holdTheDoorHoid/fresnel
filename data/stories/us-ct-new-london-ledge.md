A square brick house, painted red, with a mansard roof and a lantern on top stands in the water at the mouth of New London Harbor, Connecticut, on a concrete pier built up from a foundation sunk on the ledge. First lit in 1909, it was put there to mark a sharp ledge and a shoal that the older harbor light, farther inside, could not warn ships about in thick weather.[^1][^2]

## The place

New London Ledge Light rises from Long Island Sound on the east side of the entrance to New London Harbor, and the National Register nomination says it marks two hazards: a sharp ledge of rock and a shoal 200 feet (61 m) long on which it rests.[^1] Before it, the harbor's light was the New London Harbor Light, whose 1801 tower the nomination calls long considered inadequate.[^1]

The Lighthouse Board made its case in its own words. In its report for 1900 it said that the harbor's outlying shoals and ledges "make the approach to it dangerous in thick weather," that the old light stood "so far inside the obstructions as to be partially ineffective," and that the port's commerce had grown since that light was built. It added that a naval station now operated on the Thames River.[^2] The Board noted that the recommendation had already appeared in eight recent annual reports, and asked for a light and fog-signal station "on the southwest ledge on the eastern side of the entrance," at an estimate of $45,000.[^2] The nomination dates the lobbying to 1890; LighthouseFriends traces a request for a light on Black Ledge back to an 1853 petition from the superintendent of lights at New London, and says the Board repeated its funding request eleven times before Congress acted.[^1][^3]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $60,000 on 28 April 1904 for a light and fog signal "at or near Black Ledge," LighthouseFriends reports. The Board then chose Southwest Ledge, about a quarter mile west, so that the channel to the west of the light would be left free of obstructions, and a March 1907 act let it contract for the building so long as the total stayed within $115,000.[^3] The nomination agrees that the first site was Black Ledge and that it was dropped for Southwest Ledge, and that the contracts were awarded in 1908.[^1] Bids were opened on 4 June 1908 and the contract went to the T. A. Scott Company of New London for $73,000.[^3]

The foundation came first. The nomination says the timber crib, 52 feet (16 m) square and 31 feet (9.4 m) high, was built by the Scott company on the Thames River bank in Groton, towed over the ledge, filled with concrete, riprap and gravel, and sunk in 28 feet (8.5 m) of water.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the tow to 18 August 1908, eight hours behind four tugboats.[^3] The house on top was put up by the Hamilton R. Douglas Company of New London, and LighthouseFriends gives the total cost of the lighthouse as $93,968.96.[^1][^3]

By mid-September 1909 the Norwich Bulletin reported the station complete except for the light and the fog signal, a brick building set on a block of cement that rests on the ledge.[^4] The light was lit on 10 November 1909 with a fourth-order Fresnel lens made in Paris by Henry-Lepaute, showing three white flashes and one red every thirty seconds.[^3][^1] The Norwich Bulletin of 11 November said the light "began to shine regularly" the previous Wednesday night, which was 10 November.[^5] Sources disagree about the fog signal. The nomination says the signal came in 1911 and replaced the one at the old harbor light; LighthouseFriends says a first-class air siren sounded from the start.[^1][^3]

The station was called Southwest Ledge Light at first, and the newspaper still used that name in 1909. LighthouseFriends says the Board renamed it New London Ledge just before it went into operation, to avoid confusion with Southwest Ledge Light off New Haven; the New London Maritime Society dates the renaming to 1910.[^3][^4][^10]

## The keepers

The house had a center hall with four rooms on the first floor, four on the second and three on the third, and a cast-iron stair up through the middle.[^1] The Norwich Bulletin named Captain Bloom, formerly of a Gull Island light, as the new station's keeper, with two assistants.[^5] The Lighthouse Society's archive lists Wilford B. Petty as head keeper for 1909 to 1910, then George E. Hansen from 1910 to 1918, so the two records do not match on the first keeper.[^6] LighthouseFriends, citing a 1916 newspaper, says Hansen once netted an eagle with a six-foot wingspan that had fallen into the sound, tethered it and nursed it back to health, and had to cut the rope when it turned vicious.[^3]

Israel H. Southworth was assistant in 1923 to 1924 and head keeper from 1924 to the end of 1939.[^6] Howard B. Beebe appears in the archive as first assistant from 1926 to 1938, while New England Lighthouses calls him principal keeper for those years.[^6][^7] William I. Clark is listed as head keeper from 1956 to 1959, though New England Lighthouses gives 1954 to 1959 and calls him the last civilian keeper.[^6][^7] New England Lighthouses says Coast Guard crews lived on the rock from then until 1987.[^7] In its final years a four-man crew served eighteen-month tours, standing twelve-hour watches that included sending weather reports to ships and shore.[^3]

The station's best-known story is a ghost. Local lore holds that a keeper nicknamed Ernie haunts the lighthouse, and crews before automation reported knocking at bedroom doors, doors opening and closing, and a television turning itself on and off.[^3] One telling has a keeper falling or jumping from the roof in the 1920s or 1930s; New England Lighthouses says that if the legend has any truth in it, the truth is elusive, and the archive roster does not identify an Ernie.[^7][^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Howard Beebe was in the lighthouse during the hurricane of 21 September 1938, with a second assistant and a tinsmith, while his family was ashore, according to New England Lighthouses, which draws on a Providence Journal interview. Around 3:15 the engines stopped though the light kept going, and the men moved up to the lantern. "Waves were coming through the second floor," Beebe is reported to have said, and the surge boiled eleven tons of coal out of the cellar.[^7] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin listed New London Ledge among the Connecticut stations seriously damaged in that storm.[^8] The nomination records that in 1938 the timber crib was removed and a new boarding ledge created, though it does not link the two.[^1]

On 2 October 1981 a contractor installing safety railing slipped about twenty-five feet into the water and was knocked unconscious. A coastguardsman, Richard J. Mumenthaler, who had just stepped outside, jumped in and brought him out, with Vernon L. Smith helping and radioing for help.[^3]

## After the keepers

The Fresnel lens came out in 1984 and a smaller automated light replaced it.[^1][^3] The station was destaffed in 1987, making it the last manned lighthouse on Long Island Sound.[^3] Peter Polotnianka is the archive's last head keeper, serving 1986 to 1987.[^6] On the last day the log carried an entry that began "Rock of slow torture. Ernie's domain. Hell on earth," and went on to wish that the light would shine on forever "because I'm through."[^3] LighthouseFriends names Bill Grace as the coastguardsman who wrote it, and says he lowered the flag on 1 May 1987 when the last crew left; the Maritime Society's page calls the writer an unknown Coast Guard officer.[^3][^10] One museum page dates automation to 1978; the other sources agree on 1987.[^10][^3]

By 1989 the house was unoccupied and run automatically, its windows blocked with plywood.[^1] The lighthouse was leased in 1988 to the New London Ledge Lighthouse Foundation, and was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 29 May 1990.[^3][^1] In 2011 the foundation cleaned, repaired and painted it and installed an interpretive center.[^3] On 1 May 2013 the General Services Administration declared it excess to Coast Guard needs; the New London Maritime Society was announced as new owner in 2014, and the Society itself says it took ownership in 2015.[^3][^10]

## Today

New London Ledge Light stands offshore and is owned by the New London Maritime Society; the Society says the Coast Guard still owns and maintains the lamp and lantern.[^9][^10] It is out in the water, so it is best seen from a boat. The Thames River Heritage Park says tours inside run from June through September and are booked through the Society, so check its current availability before planning a trip.[^9] The station's fourth-order Fresnel lens is on display at the Custom House Maritime Museum in New London.[^3][^7]
