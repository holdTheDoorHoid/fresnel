Boston Light stands on Little Brewster, a small rock island at the entrance to Boston Harbor, Massachusetts. A tower was first lit here on 14 September 1716, the first lighthouse in what became the United States, and the National Historic Landmark nomination says that no other lighthouse site in the country has been used for that purpose for longer.[^2] The tower seen today dates from 1783, built on the remains of the one that British forces blew up in 1776.[^2]

## The place

Little Brewster is a small island about 800 feet (240 m) long, east to west, and at most 250 feet (76 m) wide. Its highest point is 18 feet (5.5 m) above the sea.[^2] The light was the result of a campaign begun in 1713 by Boston merchants; the Province of Massachusetts Bay then authorised the building of the lighthouse.[^2]

A history of the light printed by Major D. P. Heap in 1889, which reprints an account from the *Boston Evening Transcript* of 26 August 1880, gives the steps: the General Court ordered a lighthouse in June 1715, and on 1 August 1715 the proprietors of Hull voted to grant "Brewster Island" to the Province "for the use of a Light house forever".[^4] The light was to be paid for by the shipping that used it. Arnold Burges Johnson's *The Modern Light-House Service* (1889) says it was supported by light dues of one penny a ton on incoming and outgoing vessels, coasters excepted, collected by the collector of imports at Boston.[^5]

## Building the light

The nomination gives the cost of the first lighthouse as £2,385 17s 8d and the opening night as 14 September 1716.[^2] Johnson gives £2,285 for the same building, which is a hundred pounds less.[^5] The National Park Service describes the first tower as a round, slightly tapering rubblestone tower about 60 feet (18 m) high, with a keeper's house, a barn and a wharf.[^3] Johnson says its light came from tallow candles.[^5]

In 1719 the colony supplied a cannon to answer ships in fog. The nomination calls it the first fog signal in the country and says it was not replaced until 1851.[^2] The nomination credits the request to "the first lighthouse keeper", but George Worthylake, the first keeper, had drowned in 1718, and Lighthouse Friends says the request came from a later keeper, John Hayes, whom it calls the third.[^2][^12] Fires started by the lamp damaged the interior in 1720 and 1751, and each time the tower was quickly repaired.[^2] The National Park Service adds a severe storm in 1723 and repair or rebuilding work in 1734-37.[^3]

The Revolution took the tower down. The sources agree on the outline and differ on the details. After the British took over the harbour, American raiding parties set the tower on fire in July 1775.[^2] The Park Service dates the first burning to 20 July and says it was followed by a second on 31 July and a third in September, while the nomination describes a 300-man party sent on 31 July under orders from General Washington.[^2][^3] Heap's reprinted account says the British admiral had it repaired after the first burning and the Americans destroyed it again on 31 July.[^4] When the British left Boston, they destroyed what was left. The nomination puts the explosion in March 1776 and the Park Service in June 1776; Heap's account credits a Captain Bangs of the *Renown* with placing a train of powder under the tower.[^2][^3][^4]

Massachusetts rebuilt the light in 1783. The nomination says the builders apparently followed the old plans and used the surviving parts of the 1716 tower.[^2] The Park Service gives a tower 75 feet (23 m) high of mortared rubblestone, lit by four fish-oil lamps; Heap's account gives a height of 68 feet (21 m) with four lamps.[^3][^4] Heap dates the cession of the island to the United States to 1790; the nomination says control passed to the federal government in 1790, and by 1789 the tower held sixteen lamps.[^2][^4]

Later changes are mostly in the nomination and the Park Service's history. Large cracks in 1809 led to six iron bands being put round the tower.[^2] In 1811 Argand lamps on a revolving base replaced the older lamps.[^2] In 1844 an iron spiral stair replaced the wooden steps, and the Park Service lists new iron window frames, a balcony and a large iron door for the same year.[^2][^3] In 1859 the tower was raised from 75 to 89 feet (27 m) and fitted with a second-order Fresnel lens, and a frame duplex house for the assistant keepers was built the same year.[^2][^3] Heap's reprinted account dates the heightening to 1860.[^4] The present equipment, installed in 1962, is a 1,500-watt bulb at the centre of a twelve-sided Fresnel lens, rated at two million candlepower.[^2]

The fog signal was rebuilt again and again. The Park Service lists a wind-up bell in 1851, a striking apparatus in 1869, a whistle in 1871, a fog trumpet in 1872 and a steam siren in 1887, with a brick signal house put up in 1876 that still stands.[^3] In the Lighthouse Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1892 the Boston siren ran for 767 hours, using about 52 tons of coal.[^6] The Board's report for 1894 describes the island as the site of "a sort of experimental fog-signal station", because vessels bound into Boston sometimes heard the siren from a distance and lost it on coming closer. The report says the silence had been noticed at and about Harding Ledge, on the path of vessels from Cape Cod, and that many a ship had fallen victim to that rock for failing to hear the signal.[^7] The Board's report for the year ended June 1900 records about 800 tons of riprap provided and 58 running feet of sea wall built "to protect the island and site of the double dwelling from the sea".[^8] A Bulletin note of 1919 records that the station's fog signal had been changed from a steam siren to an air diaphone.[^9]

## The keepers

Worthylake, a 43-year-old husbandman, was chosen on 25 June 1716 at £50 a year.[^4][^5] In his second year his salary was raised to £70 because, in the *Transcript*'s words as Heap reprints them, "he lost 59 sheep by drowning in a severe storm, his attendance on the Light House preventing him from saving them."[^4] On 3 November 1718 he drowned with his wife and daughter while going to Noddle's Island.[^4] Lighthouse Friends adds that the party was returning from Boston, that a canoe capsized as an enslaved man named Shadwell paddled out to bring them ashore, and that Worthylake's younger daughter Ann watched from the island; it also says the young Benjamin Franklin wrote a poem about it. Heap says only that Franklin issued a ballad and hawked it about Boston.[^4][^12]

Accounts of who followed him differ. The US Lighthouse Society archive lists Robert Saunders for 1718 and John Hayes from 1718 to 1733.[^1] Heap's account names John Haynes as the second keeper, chosen on 18 November 1718, and says he resigned on 23 August 1733 and was succeeded by Capt. Robert Ball, an Englishman.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says Sanders (the spelling it uses) drowned days after taking the post.[^12] The archive lists Ball (as "Robert Balls") until 1774 and William Minns from 1774 to 1776.[^1] Thomas Knox, a pilot, was appointed keeper on 28 November 1783 by Governor Hancock, and his parents, Adam and Martha Knox, lived on the island with him. Heap's account says Martha died there in January 1790 and Adam in December of that year, aged eighty-one.[^4] The archive gives Knox's service as 1783 to 1811 and Jonathan Bruce as keeper from 1811 to 1833.[^1] Heap says that in 1829 Bruce, a pilot, was keeper on the recommendation of the Boston Marine Society.[^4]

For the later nineteenth century the archive's roster is the main record, and it shows how the work was passed up the ranks. Thomas Bates was principal keeper from 1864 to 1893, twenty-nine years.[^1] Henry L. Pingree is listed as second assistant from 1886, first assistant from 1888 and principal keeper from 1894 to 1909.[^1] Ralph C. Norwood went from second assistant in 1929 to first assistant in 1937 and principal keeper in 1941.[^1] Maurice A. Babcock held the principal keeper's place from 1926 to 1941.[^1] In the late 1940s and early 1950s the archive's list of principal keepers changes almost every year, with several names listed for a single year.[^1] A station that had been run by families became a male-billeted Coast Guard post in 1959.[^3]

The last keeper was a civilian. Sally Snowman was hired by the Coast Guard in 2003.[^12] The Park Service calls her the first civilian keeper since 1941 and the first woman to keep Boston Light.[^3] She served until the end of 2023, twenty years.[^3][^12][^13]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The work was not only the lamp. On 24 February 1921, according to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin, the keeper, James L. Hart, and the first assistant, William J. Howard, helped rescue the second assistant keeper, whose boat had capsized. The same notice records Arthur A. Small, keeper of the Narrows Light, assisting the second assistant and recovering the boat.[^10] Lighthouse Friends gives the man's name as Tom Small; the archive lists a Thomas Small as second assistant from 1920 to 1922.[^1][^12] On 1 April 1924, the Bulletin says, Hart and his two assistants rescued three men whose boat struck the ledges near Outer Brewster Island; the men were looked after at the station until the Coast Guard came to take them to the mainland. The Bulletin prints the first assistant's name as W. J. Howland, where the archive's roster has William J. Howard.[^11][^1]

Lighthouse Friends records that in July 1938 the fog signal sounded for sixty-one consecutive hours, a record for the station.[^12] The light was dimmed during the War of 1812 and again in the First World War, and it was extinguished entirely from 1941 until July 1945.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over the station in 1939.[^2] The light was electrified in 1948, and in 1957 an underwater cable from Windmill Point in Hull replaced the island's generator and batteries as the power supply, with the generator kept as backup.[^3] In 1960 the Coast Guard burned the decayed duplex and finally put indoor plumbing in the keeper's house.[^3] Boston Light became a National Historic Landmark in 1964.[^3] The Park Service dates its listing on the National Register of Historic Places to 1987.[^3]

In November 1989, as the Coast Guard prepared to automate the light, the Senate passed a law sponsored by Senator Kennedy that required Boston Light to stay permanently manned.[^3] The light was automated in 1998 and the law kept a Coast Guard crew on the island.[^3][^12] Little Brewster became part of the Boston Harbor Islands national recreation area in 1996 and was opened to the public in 1999, according to the Park Service.[^3][^12] To mark the tricentennial in 2016 the Coast Guard completed a $1.5 million repainting and refurbishment of the tower and the other station buildings.[^12]

## Today

Boston Light is federal property, and the General Services Administration describes it as the oldest continuously operating lighthouse in the United States.[^14] The Park Service says that, for safety and access reasons, neither the lighthouse nor Little Brewster Island is open to the public; in summer, visitors can take a lighthouse cruise to see the tower from the water.[^3] Do not land on the island on your own.

On 3 August 2026 the General Services Administration announced that eligible agencies and organisations could apply to acquire the station at no cost. The property is the island and six structures: the tower, a boathouse, a keeper's house, a sound signal building, a cistern building and a small oil house.[^14] In 2023 WBUR had reported that the lighthouse would be sold to a private owner who would be required to preserve it.[^13] No new owner was named in the sources available on 22 October 2026.
