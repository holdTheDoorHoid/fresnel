Two white octagonal towers stand on Arrowsic Island, on the Kennebec River downriver from Bath, Maine. The river turns twice within half a mile at this point, and a ship that keeps the two lights in line is in the middle of the channel.[^1][^2] The towers went up in 1898 and are the only surviving pair of range lights in Maine.[^1]

## The place

Before 1898 the river's aids were private. The National Register form says the Kennebec's navigation aids "had, prior to 1898, been maintained by private companies whose ships utilized the waterway."[^1] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1892, recommended federal lights, including one at Doubling Point: "At Doubling Point a red lens-lantern light, showing up and down the river," with a fog bell, and "one-half mile east from the point, white range lights."[^4]

The bend is the hazard. LighthouseFriends notes that ships over 600 feet (183 m) long can find it very difficult to navigate, and that newly commissioned ships leaving Bath Iron Works often fall into that class.[^2] A separate Doubling Point light stood on the point, listed by the Board on its own. In the fiscal year 1900 its tower was moved onto a new stone pier at the low-water line.[^10] That light is not part of this station.

## Building the light

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1895, records an appropriation "made by the act approved on March 2" for "range lights, and fog signal at or near Doubling Point."[^5] A year later the Board reported that "the title to the site is nearly ready" and that plans for the buildings were under way.[^6]

The towers were erected in 1898. The National Register form says the southern tower stands seventeen feet (5.2 m) from its granite base to the middle of the lens, and the rear one four feet (1.2 m) shorter.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the two towers as twenty-one and eighteen feet (6.4 and 5.5 m) tall, and Maine Lights Today gives seventeen and thirteen feet (5.2 and 4.0 m).[^2][^7] The towers are joined by walkways to a two-story keeper's house.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives the original optic as a fifth-order Fresnel lens installed in 1898.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the characteristic of both lights was at first fixed white.[^2]

Work continued after the towers went up. In the year to June 1899 the Board recorded "a boat slip and about 1,000 running feet of plank walks" and a concrete floor laid in the cellar of the dwelling.[^8] The same report says the appropriations were insufficient to build boat houses at the four Kennebec stations, whose keepers use boats almost exclusively.[^8] The US Lighthouse Society's record dates the boathouse to 1901 and the oil house to 1902, and the National Register form also dates the brick oil house to 1902.[^3][^1]

The fog bell came later. LighthouseFriends says a passenger steamer ran aground in fog just west of the range in 1912, and that a 1,200-pound bell went into a pyramid-shaped wooden tower at Fiddler's Reach the following year.[^2] The Bulletin's 1914 issue lists "Fiddlers Reach, Kennebec" among the fog signals established, and the 1912-1917 index gives the entry as page 114.[^9][^15] The US Lighthouse Society's record dates the fog-signal building to 1913.[^3]

## The keepers

Edward H. Pierce was the first keeper, from 1898 to 1921.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he had served at Halfway Rock and at Cuckolds before he came here.[^2] The Bulletin of 1912 printed a commendation for Pierce, keeper of the Doubling Point Range light station, who "has been commended for rendering assistance to a party" in a disabled power boat.[^12] LighthouseFriends dates his assistance to a pair in a disabled power boat to 1913.[^2] The 1912-1917 index lists two rescues by Pierce, at pages 40 and 240 of that volume.[^11] LighthouseFriends describes a rescue on October 6, 1916, when Pierce pulled a man from the water after the man had jumped from a wharf at Bath. The index does not give dates, so it does not show whether that rescue is the one at page 240.[^2][^11]

Harry L. Nye kept the light from 1921 to 1937.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he was a sea captain who retired from the sea in 1914 to keep lights, first at Seguin and then here.[^2] The Bulletin's index records an efficiency flag for him in the 1924-1929 volume, and its 1926 issue lists the station among those entitled to fly a flag that year.[^17][^16] In 1931 the Bulletin named "Harry L. Nye, keeper" as the first-district station with the highest general efficiency.[^14]

William H. Woodward kept the light from 1937 to 1945, and Harold L. Kilton from 1945 to 1949.[^3] A 1948 magazine profile, quoted by LighthouseFriends, describes Kilton as "being a civilian keeper, and thoroughly enjoys it."[^2]

Between 1949 and 1970 the roster lists Clyde T. Whittaker, Clarence G. Dunn, Edgar M. Wallace, Lester W. Davis, Robert E. Harrington, Jr., John R. Nardone, Donald H. Dixon, Junnie C. Dail, Arvin H. Young, Richard D. Brown, Julius D. Graham, Richard A. DeMayo and Michael G. Weare, whose entry is left open after 1970.[^3] Joseph Robicheau kept the light from 1981 to 1982.[^3] Karen McLean served from 1982 to 1987. LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard offered her the station, "where she would live and oversee the range lights."[^2][^3] Dan McLean followed from 1987 to 1990.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On December 28, 1927, Nye got his boat out into the river and intercepted a floe on which four young men had been carried downriver, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The Bulletin's commendation records four persons rescued from a large block of ice that had broken adrift from the Sasanoa River's mouth.[^13] On August 12 that year he helped rescue three people from "a disabled motor boat which had drifted on" the rocks near the lights.[^13][^2]

## After the keepers

The 1987 nomination says the Coast Guard still maintained the complex and calls it "one of the handful of manned stations in Maine."[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record and Maine Lights Today both give 1990 as the year the station was automated.[^3][^7]

In 1998, under the Maine Lights Program, the range lights became the property of The Range Light Keepers, a nonprofit group.[^2] The group restored the fog-bell tower at Fiddler's Reach and fitted it with a bell and clockwork striking mechanism. It mounted the bell there in May 2016.[^2] The original bell from Fiddler's Reach went to the Coast Guard Academy and is now on display at the Maine Maritime Museum.[^2] The group restored the rear tower in 2017 and the front tower in 2019.[^2]

## Today

The Range Light Keepers own the lights. Maine Lights Today lists the keeper's house and fuel house of 1898, the boathouse of 1901 and the oil house of 1902 as the structures still standing, and names ANT South Portland as the Coast Guard unit responsible for the aid.[^7]

To reach the lights by land, LighthouseFriends gives a route from Route 127 along Whitmore's Landing Road and Doubling Point Road, ending at a path. It reports "Grounds open, dwelling/towers closed," and says visitors are urged to stay on the path. Public cruises that pass the lights leave from Bath and Boothbay Harbor.[^2] Coast Guard personnel still live in the dwelling, LighthouseFriends says, so the dwelling and towers are closed to visitors. Do not go into the towers or the dwelling. Check the Range Light Keepers' website before you go.[^7]
