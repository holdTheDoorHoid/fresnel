North Point Light stands on a bluff above the north end of Milwaukee Bay, in Lake Park on Milwaukee's East Side.[^3][^4] Its octagonal cast-iron tower dates from 1888. In 1912 and 1913 a new steel superstructure was built beside it, and the old tower became its upper part.[^2] The light was discontinued in 1994. The keeper's quarters stand beside it. The station is now a museum, open on weekend afternoons.[^1][^2][^3][^5]

## The place

The first light at North Point was a brick tower built in 1855 "to mark the entrance into the Milwaukee River at Milwaukee."[^2] The station stood on a high bluff over the lake.[^7] In its 1892 report the Lighthouse Board, under an entry headed only "Milwaukee" but describing this reservation, wrote: "There is no right of way to the light-house reservation." Supplies could hardly be landed on the lake shore, and from the land side the Board says it was necessary to cross private property to reach the light.[^8]

A proposed park improvement was expected to change that.[^8] The Board's report says the park commissioner was allowed to do the proposed work on the lighthouse grounds, and that "the proposed improvement will bring a street to the door of the light-station."[^8] Lake Park's development began in 1892, when arrangements were made to obtain the services of the Olmsted firm.[^12] The museum dates Olmsted's design for the park to 1893.[^3]

## Building the light

The first tower was a brick structure, which the museum describes as 28 feet (8.5 m) high.[^4] A few years after Georgia Stebbins arrived, the bluff on which it stood began to erode, and the Milwaukee Notebook says the original light was then threatened.[^7] In 1888, when erosion threatened the brick tower, a new octagonal cast-iron tower replaced it about 100 feet (30 m) inland, according to the Historic American Engineering Record (HAER) inventory.[^2] The new tower was 14 feet (4.3 m) across at the base and 39 feet (12 m) high to the top of the ventilator ball.[^2] Its Queen Anne keeper's quarters, a frame house, were built in the same year.[^1][^4]

The lens was a fourth-order Fresnel made by Barbier, Benard & Turenne of Paris, according to the Milwaukee Notebook.[^7]

By the early twentieth century, trees growing in Lake Park were blocking the light from ships on the lake.[^2] Congress appropriated $10,000 in 1909 to raise it.[^2] Work began in July 1912 and was completed in April 1913 at a cost of $9,455.[^2] A new steel superstructure went up beside the old lighthouse, and the old structure was used as the upper portion of the taller tower.[^2] The Service's 1912 bulletin records that the contract for raising the light had been approved.[^10] Its 1913 bulletin says the light was displayed from 15 December 1912.[^11]

## The keepers

The Society's keeper roster begins in 1838 with Eli Bates.[^1] Its principal keepers after him included Josiah Sherwood (1849 to 1853), Andrew Sullivan (1853 to 1856), Michael Burke (1856 to 1861) and William Bruin (1861 to 1871).[^1] The roster starts seventeen years before the first light was built in 1855.[^1] The sources read for this story do not explain those early keepers, and the story does not describe them.

The museum says Daniel Kellogg Green had recently been appointed keeper when his daughter came to the station in 1873.[^4] The Wisconsin Maritime Historical Society (WMHS) says he kept the title of head keeper for some years, while his daughter did much of the everyday work.[^6] The roster gives his stint as 1871 to 1881, and the museum's keeper list gives 1871 to 1874.[^1][^4][^5]

Georgia Anna Green was born in July 1846 in Jordan, New York, near Syracuse.[^6] At twenty she married Lemuel Dibble Stebbins, a watchmaker and jeweler from Connecticut, and they lived in New York City.[^6] In 1873 doctors diagnosed her with consumption, as tuberculosis was then called.[^4][^6] They told her to leave the unhealthy air of New York City, so she went to Milwaukee to live with her parents at the lighthouse. Her husband joined her there some months later and opened a jewelry shop on East Mason Street.[^4][^6]

In an 1897 interview with the *Milwaukee Journal*, as the Milwaukee Notebook quotes it, she said: "Since January 1874, I have never been away from the premises overnight."[^7] The museum dates her arrival to 1873, and the Milwaukee Notebook dates her start to January 1874.[^4][^7] WMHS says that by 1881 she had been the unofficial keeper for nearly eight years.[^6]

In 1881 her mother died, and federal officials named Georgia the head keeper.[^6] The roster has her as keeper from 1881 to 1907.[^1] The museum counts 26 official years, and WMHS adds eight years of assisting her father before that, so that she served more than three decades.[^4][^6] The Milwaukee Notebook gives 33 years of service.[^7]

Her 1897 account of the work, as the Milwaukee Notebook prints it, has the lamps burning from sunset to sunrise, and the flash machinery wound up every four hours through the night.[^7] The museum describes the same routine, with the lamp lit at sunset and the clockwork wound during the night.[^4]

Her son, Albert Kellogg Stebbins, was born in the lighthouse in June 1875. He later became a Milwaukee attorney.[^6] Her log for the night of 28 January 1888 records a total eclipse of the moon and a clear night.[^6]

On 30 June 1907 she made the last entry in the station's log, that the light was discontinued. WMHS prints the entry as: "Light discontinued this 30th day of June 1907. Correct Statement. Georgia A. Stebbins, Keeper."[^6] She died on 11 July 1920 and is buried at Forest Home Cemetery in Milwaukee.[^6]

After her came Thomas J. Bailey (1909 to 1918), Martin N. Knudsen (1918 to 1924) and Reynold W. Johnson, whom the roster gives as keeper from 1924 to 1928. The museum gives Johnson's stint as 1924 to 1940.[^1][^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 29 July 1880 the propeller *James Fisk, Jr.*, of Buffalo, with twenty-two men aboard, stranded about half a mile north of North Point, five miles from Station No. 15, the Milwaukee station. The Service's report says the fog was so dense that the crew needed the utmost care in steering, that they reached the vessel safely within an hour, and that they found the captain expecting a tug. The life-saving crew helped run lines, and the vessel was got off with little damage.[^9]

The Wisconsin Maritime Historical Society says that shipwrecks occasionally happened off North Point, and that on at least one occasion Mrs. Stebbins helped sailors get off a wrecked sailboat.[^6]

On 20 April 1893 a gale washed away the house on the crib of the new city waterworks tunnel, off Milwaukee. The Service's annual report says the workmen had taken refuge in the air lock during the night of 19 April, and that on the morning of the 20th all but one of them were drowned. The survivor was rescued from the crib by the Milwaukee station's crew, who were towed out to the crib by the tug *Welcome*.[^13] The report counts fifteen men in all.[^14]

The Service's gold medal list gives the medal of 24 May 1893 to Ingar Olsen, a surfman of a life-saving station, for rescuing "the sole survivor of fifteen men, from the waterworks crib."[^14] WMHS gives a different count. It says the storm trapped sixteen men below the lake, and that fifteen died. It also says Mrs. Stebbins saw the disaster from the lighthouse and ran to a telephone to alert the life-saving crew.[^6] The Service's count and the WMHS count differ, and the telephone detail rests on the WMHS article alone.

## After the keepers

The light was discontinued on 30 June 1907.[^6] Congress appropriated money to raise the light in 1909, and the work was done in 1912 and 1913.[^2] The light was displayed again in the raised structure from 15 December 1912.[^10][^11]

The Coast Guard took over the lighthouses in 1939.[^5] The light was automated in 1943.[^1]

The light was decommissioned in 1994.[^3] The North Point Lighthouse Friends, Inc., formed in 2002 to preserve and restore the station, and restoration was completed in the fall of 2007.[^3]

## Today

The museum is open to the public for tours and visits on Saturdays and Sundays from 1 to 4 p.m., with closed dates listed on its website.[^3] The museum's About page gives the tower as 74 feet (23 m) tall, but its Georgia Stebbins page gives the 1888 tower as 39 feet (12 m), as HAER does to the top of the ventilator ball.[^3][^4][^2] The USLHS record gives 36.25 feet (11 m) to the lantern centre and the light as 122 feet (37 m) above mean high water.[^1]

The keeper's quarters are a Queen Anne frame house, and the park around the light is Olmsted's Lake Park.[^1][^12]
