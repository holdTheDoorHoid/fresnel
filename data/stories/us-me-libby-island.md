Libby Island Light stands on the southwestern part of the Libby Islands, a treeless pair of islands off Machiasport, Maine, where it marks the entrance to Machias Bay.[^1][^2][^7][^3] It is a short granite tower, 42 feet (13 m) from base to the centre of the lantern, and the station was established in 1822; LighthouseFriends calls the place one of the foggiest spots on the Maine coast.[^1][^3]

## The place

The Libby Islands are two islands, Little Libby and Big Libby, joined by a sandbar. LighthouseFriends says they were once covered in trees, and that today only stumps in scrubby grass remain.[^3] In 1899 the Light-House Board wrote that vessels entering the Bay of Fundy first made Libby Islands and then tried to make Little River light.[^8]

The Board's reports count the fog in hours of fog-signal running: in the year covered by its 1893 report the steam whistle sounded 1,554 hours and burned about 85 tons of coal, and in 1895 some 1,521 hours and about 66 tons.[^6][^7] When the Lighthouse Service tabulated hours of fog for the fiscal year 1918, its Bulletin named Libby Islands as the highest record on the Atlantic coast: 1,906 hours, about 22 per cent of the year.[^11]

## Building the light

Benjamin Beal and Quincy Bicknell won the building contract with a bid of $2,850. According to LighthouseFriends they put up a tower of beach stone and a small brick dwelling on a rocky ledge near the south end of the island during the summer and fall of 1822, and Keeper John McKellar lit the lamps for the first time that November.[^3] The tower cracked over the winter and fell in April 1823. Isaac Isley, the Collector and Superintendent of Lighthouses, put the blame on poor workmanship and on lime mortar that had not set hard before frost came.[^3] Noah Humphrey rebuilt it for $1,449, and New England Lighthouses dates the present tower to 1823.[^3][^4]

The National Register nomination, prepared in 1975, tells it differently: it says the station was ordered built by President James Monroe in 1822 and "has never been rebuilt".[^2] The two accounts do not agree, and the sources used here include no contemporary record that settles it. The USLHS archive lists the tower as granite blocks and the original optic as a fourth-order Fresnel lens, installed in 1855.[^1] Before that the lantern held ten lamps in fourteen-inch reflectors.[^3]

According to LighthouseFriends, an inspection in 1842 found the tower cracked from top to bottom, its mortar largely sand, and the keeper's house in poor shape; the dwelling stood 250 feet from the tower and was reached by a long wooden trestle.[^3] Keeper Isaac Stearns, who took charge in February 1841 according to that account (the USLHS roster gives 1842), found the station in ruins, had no well or cistern, and had to go twelve miles to Machias for supplies with a family of seven.[^3][^1] A new dwelling followed in 1854.[^3]

Fog signals came next. A bell tower was blown down in 1867 and rebuilt, and a third-class Daboll trumpet was installed in August 1884 in a new brick building, which brought the station its first assistant keeper, Alexander M. Drisko.[^3][^1] The National Register nomination dates the fog signal building to 1856; the USLHS archive and the lighthouse histories say 1884.[^2][^1][^3] In the early 1890s the Light-House Board reported the signal changed from hot air to steam, and the caloric engines taken from Libby Islands were nearly rebuilt in the Board's machine shop at Boston and set up at another station.[^5]

## The keepers

John McKellar kept the light for twenty years, from 1822 to 1842, and the USLHS roster lists thirty-eight terms as principal keeper down to 1973.[^1] From 1884 there were assistants, and until 1891 the assistant was a relative of the head keeper, a brother-in-law or son-in-law, so that the keepers could share one house; Alexander Drisko, the first assistant, became head keeper in 1885 and resigned in 1891.[^3][^1] The steam whistle of 1891 needed a second assistant, and the family arrangement ended.[^3]

Housing remained a problem. In 1895 the Light-House Board pointed out that there was only one set of quarters for three keepers, and asked for a double dwelling at an estimated $6,000.[^7] Its 1899 report said the dwelling occupied by the two assistant keepers was too small and so badly arranged that the two families had little privacy; it asked for $2,500 to rearrange and extend it.[^8] According to LighthouseFriends, the old dwelling was enlarged in 1896, part of the rain shed was made over as a head keeper's house in 1898, and the buildings were joined by telephone in 1902.[^3]

On 16 June 1918 Samuel Holbrook, first assistant keeper, drowned near the station when the boat he and the second assistant keeper, Justin Foss, were returning in capsized. The Daily Kennebec Journal of 17 June carried a short report from Machias under a headline announcing the drowning of the Libby Island keeper.[^14] LighthouseFriends, drawing on the inspector's report, says the weather was fine and the sea smooth, that Holbrook was an excellent boatman but could not swim, and that he went over the side and sank while Foss righted the boat.[^3] The same account says his body was found on 1 September after drifting roughly seventy miles, a few miles from Swans Island, where he was born.[^3]

Hervey H. Wass became head keeper in 1919 and stayed twenty years. The tender Hibiscus delivered the family: Wass, his wife Mabel and four children, with their household goods, which included a piano belonging to their daughter Hazel.[^3][^1] The head keeper's house was entered through a shed about 100 feet long that fed rainwater to underground cisterns, and eighty tons of soft coal were delivered each year for the fog signal.[^3] The head keeper kept the log; on Libby Island, LighthouseFriends notes, many of the entries concern the fog-signal equipment, fuel used and hours sounded.[^3] LighthouseFriends describes the night watches as split into three shifts: lighting-up to 10 p.m., 10 p.m. to 2 a.m., and 2 a.m. to dawn.[^3] In 1920 George Woodward, a widowed assistant, arrived, and in 1924 he married Wass's daughter Hazel.[^3][^1] In the 1930s Gleason Colbeth was assistant and in 1943 became head keeper; Jasper Cheney followed in 1945.[^1]

The war brought a small Coast Guard detail. LighthouseFriends records six coastguardsmen posted to watch for enemy ships and planes, and Keeper Colbeth remembering the difficulty of landing them from a boat.[^3] LighthouseFriends quotes Keeper Gene Watts, writing to a magazine in October 1949, as saying that kerosene lamps in the dwelling had given way to "110-volt electricity all over the place".[^3] The roster shows Coast Guard crews alongside civilian keepers from 1949 until 1973.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The National Register nomination, drawing on the station's old journals, says thirty-five wrecks occurred from 1856 to 1902, with fifteen lives lost.[^2] It names a few: the schooner *Caledonia* of Windsor, Nova Scotia, in 1878, the bark *Fame*, and the *Princeport* in 1892.[^2] The nomination calls the *Princeport* a ship wrecked on the bar between the islands and says the keepers rescued her crew. The Life-Saving Service's report lists the *Princeport* as a British schooner, wrecked on 15 September 1892 near the Cross Island Station, and credits that station's crew with the service.[^9] The two accounts may describe the same event differently, but the sources do not settle it.

The barkentine *Africa*, the last square-rigger to go ashore at Libby Island according to the nomination, struck in 1902, and the crew reached the island in a small boat.[^2] The Life-Saving Service's report for the year ended June 1903 lists the British barkentine *Africa*, stranded on Libby Island on 19 October 1902, six miles from the Cross Island Station. Its crew found the whole ship's company ashore and safe, took them to the station, and carried them on to Machias on the 21st; the master sold ship and cargo.[^10] The nomination says the two anchors the crew threw overboard, weighing over 5,000 pounds, were still in excellent condition in 1975.[^2] In September 1902 a Portland paper reported that a Boston steam yacht, the *Annie S.*, had hit wreckage ten miles south-east of Libby Island on a Sunday night and sunk in fifty fathoms, and that her crew of five and one passenger reached the island by boat and were taken to Machias by a lighthouse keeper on Monday.[^15]

LighthouseFriends dates the loss of the *Ella G. Eels* to 1906: the three-masted Rockport schooner ran aground in thick fog, and the keepers could rescue only her captain, W. A. Breen, while four of the crew drowned.[^3]

In 1922 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin noted that Keeper Wass and Assistant Justin A. Foss assisted the occupants of the fishing boat *Bessie May*, wrecked on Ram Island.[^12] In March 1923 it recorded their assistance to the schooner *F. C. Lockhart*, ashore near the station.[^13] LighthouseFriends reports that the pair were recognised for helping the crews of three vessels that wrecked near the station in 1923, naming the *Lockhart*, the *Bessie May* and the schooner *Hiram D. MacLean*; the Bulletin's *Bessie May* entry, however, is in its 1922 volume and dates the event 19 September.[^3][^12] The nomination's account of the *Lockhart*, with all sail set, lodged in a natural cradle of rocks, fits the 1923 Bulletin entry.[^2][^13]

## After the keepers

In 1923 the Lighthouse Service listed Libby Islands among the fog signals improved, changed from a steam whistle to an air diaphone.[^16] The station was automated in 1974; the Fresnel lens was taken out and the dwelling, rain shed and barn were demolished, leaving the tower and fog-signal building.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends adds a report that the last keepers' house was destroyed in a training exercise by an amphibious army unit.[^3] The National Register nomination describes the buildings differently: it says the enlarged duplex dwelling was destroyed before 1949, that a later house was still standing in 1975, and that the rain shed survived until 1944.[^2] It recommended that only the tower be nominated, having concluded that the other structures had no historical significance.[^2] The light was transferred to the Maine Coastal Islands National Wildlife Refuge in 1998.[^3]

## Today

The tower is an active aid to navigation, now solar-powered, in the care of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service as part of the Maine Coastal Islands National Wildlife Refuge.[^1][^4] The island is not open to the public. The lighthouse is best seen from a boat.[^4][^1]
