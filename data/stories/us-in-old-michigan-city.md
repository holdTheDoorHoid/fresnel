The Old Michigan City Light stands in Washington Park, at the mouth of Trail Creek on the Indiana shore of Lake Michigan.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates its first light to 1838, while the National Register nomination says the first tower was built in 1837.[^1][^2] The building that stands there now dates from 1858. Harriet Colfax kept the light for forty-three years, from 1861 to 1904, and in 1904 the light moved out to the end of the east pier. The building is now a museum run by the Michigan City Historical Society.[^1][^2][^7]

## The place

Michigan City grew up at the mouth of Trail Creek, where a real estate speculator, Isaac C. Elston, had bought land in 1830 with the idea of making Indiana's first harbour on Lake Michigan.[^1] In the 1830s the harbour was "not a safe or good port."[^2] The National Register nomination says that after requests from the Indiana General Assembly in 1832 and 1834, "the United States Congress finally appropriated $20,000 to improve the harbor at Trail Creek" on 4 July 1836.[^2] A survey had already shown the need. In the nomination's words, "The water was only a foot deep over the sand bar and Lake Michigan was already known for its sudden and violent storms."[^2]

The land for the light also came from Elston. LighthouseFriends says that in 1835 he deeded the federal government a tract of land for a lighthouse.[^1] The nomination's 1990 additional documentation calls the surviving parcel "the largest intact portion of the original land grant made expressly for the purpose of establishing a U.S. government lighthouse on June 23, 1835."[^2]

The harbour was still poor more than thirty years later. In 1867 Captain A. Mackenzie of the United States Engineers reported that "The harbor at Michigan City is in so poor a condition that it can hardly be called a harbor."[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $5,000 for a light at Michigan City on 30 June 1834. Both bids came in above that sum, and on 9 May 1836 another $3,000 was added. A contract went that August to Jeremey Hixon, Sr. and his son, according to LighthouseFriends, which spells the name that way.[^1]

The first tower is described two ways. LighthouseFriends describes a conical brick tower 39 feet (12 m) high, with a brick keeper's dwelling beside it.[^1] The nomination's text says the 1858 light was built to replace "a building and wooden conical tower" that dated from 1837.[^2] The two accounts disagree about the material, and this story cannot settle it.

LighthouseFriends says the light was ready in December 1837, when Edmund B. Harrison was appointed the first keeper, but was not shown until the summer of 1838.[^1] The nomination says the first lighthouse was built in 1837, which agrees with the account of the work being ready that year.[^2]

A sixth-order Fresnel lens was installed in 1857.[^1] A new tower followed in 1858. LighthouseFriends describes a short, square wooden tower built atop the northern end of a new seven-room, one-and-a-half-storey dwelling, with a Joliet stone foundation and Milwaukee brick above it.[^1] Its fifth-order Fresnel lens gave a fixed white light from a focal plane of 52 feet (16 m).[^1]

In 1871 a wooden tower and an elevated walk were built on the east pier, and in 1875 they were moved to the longer west pier.[^1] The NPS biography gives 1874 for the move.[^5] In the Lighthouse Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900, the station entry records "Some 6,000 bricks for the construction of an oil house," and says that the sundry civil act of 6 June 1900 "appropriated $5,500 for establishing a fog-signal at this station."[^8]

On 20 October 1904 the light moved. The fixed white light was put out at the 1858 tower and re-established at the outer end of the east pier.[^1] The 1858 house was remodelled to hold the keepers. LighthouseFriends says it was enlarged to quarter three keepers, and the nomination says the building was converted into apartments.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

The roster in the U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists the early keepers for this place. Edmund B. Harrison served from 1837 to 1841, followed by James Towner (1841 to 1844), Harriet C. Towner (1844 to 1853) and John N. Clarkson (1853 to 1861).[^3] Abigail Coit was first assistant from 1844 to 1853.[^3]

Harriet Colfax came to Michigan City from New York in 1853 to help her brother run a daily newspaper.[^1] The National Park Service's biography says her brother died in 1856.[^5] LighthouseFriends says she asked her cousin, Congressman Schuyler Colfax, for the appointment in 1861.[^1] The NPS says that later reporters "assumed she achieved this with the influence of her legislator cousin," an assumption the NPS page does not document.[^5] Her own account, as LighthouseFriends prints it, gives her "continuous service dating from April 1, 1861."[^1] She was keeper from then until 1904, the roster's "Harriet E. Colfax (1861-1904)."[^3]

Her companion, Ann C. Hartwell, lived with her in the lighthouse.[^4][^5] The NPS says the two women "moved into the lighthouse together when she assumed her role."[^5] The Indiana State Library's blog says the two women worked side by side to keep the lanterns lit.[^4] The roster does not list Hartwell as a keeper.

Colfax's work was physical. The lamps burned lard oil, and in cold weather the oil had to be heated.[^1] In a 1904 interview with the Chicago Tribune, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, she said: "I have a helper to carry up the lamps, but always trim and light them myself."[^1] She also told the reporter that "The lard oil would get hard before I could get the lamp lighted, but once lit it never went out."[^1] Her faithfulness earned the light the nickname "Old Faithful."[^1] An assistant was hired when the pier light was added in 1871. The roster lists Solomon G. Hayward, Julia L. Ebart, Katy Reilly and Talcott Miller as first assistants in 1871 to 1873, and Horatio James from 1875 to 1882.[^3]

Colfax resigned at eighty. LighthouseFriends says she left her post on 14 October 1904, six days before the light she had tended for forty-three years was put out.[^1] The NPS biography disagrees: it says she "was forced into retirement with the decision to modernize and electrify the old lighthouse."[^5] Her last log entries, as LighthouseFriends quotes them, read "Sold household effects preparatory to vacating the dear old lighthouse," and "Received another call from Mr. Armstrong, my successor."[^1] Thomas J. Armstrong's own entry, on 13 October, reads: "The retiring keeper left the station this 4 p.m. and all her personal effects."[^1]

Her companion's health failed after the move. The Daily Palladium of Richmond, Indiana, printed on 31 October 1904 a Michigan City dispatch, dated 30 October, about Ann, Colfax's companion. It called her "the sole companion of Miss Harriet Colfax, the keeper," and said she had become "insane as a result of being removed from it."[^6] The Indiana State Library gives 22 January 1905 as Hartwell's death date, and the NPS gives 27 January.[^4][^5] Colfax died on 16 April 1905, according to LighthouseFriends and the Indiana State Library; the NPS text gives 17 April.[^1][^4][^5] The two women are buried next to each other in Greenwood Cemetery.[^4]

After 1904 the keepers were Thomas J. Armstrong (1904 to 1918), Philip Sheridan (1918 to 1930) and Walter A. Donovan (1930 to 1940), as LighthouseFriends lists them.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Colfax's own log, as the Indiana State Library's blog quotes it, records a storm on 28 May 1873: "A terrible hurricane to-night at about the time of lighting up. Narrowly escaped being swept into the lake."[^4]

In December 1885 a severe storm damaged the elevated walk and the pier, and the keeper took the light apparatus out and stopped the light until repairs could be made.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a severe gale on 14 October 1886 swept away the pierhead beacon.[^1] The Indiana State Library's blog says the West Pier beacon collapsed in a windstorm in 1886.[^4]

The later storms that LighthouseFriends records mostly struck the pier lights and their walks. The cylindrical tower on the west pier was washed away in 1906, a breakwater light was destroyed in February 1909, and a storm of 8 to 10 November 1913 took 200 feet (61 m) of the elevated walk.[^1] Keepers rigged an aerial trolley with a breeches buoy to cross the gap.[^1] A storm of 18 and 19 December 1929 carried away the outer 312 feet (95 m) of the east pier's elevated walkway.[^1]

Armstrong also made rescues. LighthouseFriends says he was recognised in 1916 for rescuing two men from drowning, and that his rescue of a boy who had fallen from the pier was noted in Lighthouse Service records the next year.[^1]

The NPS page quotes Colfax, within its account of an 1881 newspaper interview, as saying: "It is fearful to hear the groaning storm for hours before it breaks upon us."[^5]

None of the sources cited here ties a shipwreck to the 1858 light itself.

## After the keepers

The keepers used the 1858 house until 1940. The nomination says "The lighthouse remained as the keepers quarters until 1940."[^2] LighthouseFriends adds that the Coast Guard had taken over the nation's lights the year before, and had a station nearby, so the house was no longer needed. After a period of rental and use by the Coast Guard Auxiliary, the building was declared surplus in 1960.[^1]

The General Services Administration sold it to Michigan City in 1963 for $18,500, and two years later the Michigan City Historical Society signed a twenty-year lease.[^1] After eight years of work the restored lighthouse opened to the public on 9 June 1973, with a new tower and lantern room paid for with money from the Marquette Chapter of Questers.[^1] The nomination's additional documentation says "The lighthouse was listed 11-5-74."[^2]

Later changes included a spiral staircase, installed by the Hoosier Lighthousing Club in 2003 to let visitors climb into the lantern room, and a new lantern room in 2014 to replace the replica built in the 1970s.[^1]

## Today

The Old Lighthouse Museum is run by the Michigan City Historical Society. The Society says it is open from April through 31 October 2026, on Wednesday to Sunday from 1:00 to 4:00 p.m. Central time.[^7] The city owns the building, and the Society manages it under its lease.[^1][^2]

The fifth-order Sautter Fresnel lens, which LighthouseFriends says was used in both the old light and the pierhead light, is on display.[^1] In 1990 the nomination's additional documentation listed a flagpole beside the main entrance, "composed of portions of the rudder of the J.D. MARSHALL shipwreck," among temporary displays on the grounds.[^2]
