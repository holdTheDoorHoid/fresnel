Rock Harbor Light stands on a wooded bluff at the southwest entrance to Rock Harbor, on Isle Royale in Lake Superior. It is the oldest lighthouse in Isle Royale National Park. It was put out in 1859, relit in 1874, and closed for good in 1879. The tower still stands, and the keeper's house beside it is a museum inside the national park.[^1][^2][^4][^6]

## The place

The copper rush brought the light. The National Park Service says that after the Treaty of LaPointe in 1843, "Isle Royale was opened to copper exploration and mining."[^2] Terry Pepper says "by 1847 more than a dozen mining companies were working the island."[^3] In 1852 Henry B. Miller, superintendent of lights on the upper lakes, recommended $5,000 for a light at the entrance to the harbor.[^3][^4]

Congress came through on 3 March 1853.[^3][^4] The National Register nomination calls the station "the earliest lighthouse erected at Isle Royale," built "in 1855 with $5,000 appropriated by the United States Congress."[^1]

## Building the light

The 50-foot (15 m) tower is attached to a one-and-a-half-story dwelling 29 feet (9 m) square.[^1] Terry Pepper says construction "appears to have began the following year," that is 1855, and that the work "was not completed until 1856," for "as yet undetermined reasons."[^3] The Park Service says the light was "completed and lit in 1855."[^2]

The lens was a fourth-order Fresnel.[^2][^3][^4] Terry Pepper gives its focal plane as "70 feet above lake level" (about 21 m). LighthouseFriends gives "sixty-five feet above Lake Superior" (about 20 m).[^3][^4] The Park Service says the lens "once sent out a beacon of light for 15 miles across the lake," although its history section gives the light as "visible for 14 miles."[^2]

## The keepers

Mark Petty was the first keeper, and his brother Michael was his assistant. Terry Pepper says both "arrived at the station on October 24, 1856."[^3] Michael died on 16 July 1857, and Mark resigned from the service after that.[^3][^5]

Francis and William Bomassa replaced the Pettys. Terry Pepper spells the name Bomassa, and the roster spells it Bomasa. William resigned on 8 September 1858, and Francis "bade farewell to both the light and to lighthouse service on August 1, 1859."[^3][^5] The Park Service says the light was put out that year because shipping had fallen off with the mining.[^2]

The light stayed dark from 1859 until 1874.[^2] In December 1872 John T. Whiting, an agent for the Western Transit Company, asked for the light to be relit because mining was about to resume. LighthouseFriends says the engineer agreed and recommended $10,000.[^4] Congress voted $5,000 for repairs. The National Register nomination dates the appropriation to 1873; LighthouseFriends gives 23 June 1874.[^1][^4] Terry Pepper says the station was examined in 1873, with plans for renovation at a total cost of $5,000.[^3]

The light came back in 1874 with Anthony O. Kruger as acting keeper. Terry Pepper says Kruger relit the lens on the evening of 5 August. LighthouseFriends gives 25 July.[^3][^4] Terry Pepper says Kruger "would tend the light alone for over a year" before Ferdinand Dumont, whom the roster spells Dumant, was appointed assistant in October 1875.[^3][^5] LighthouseFriends says Kruger "had to travel twenty-five miles by boat to obtain supplies" from the Island Mining Company at Siskiwit, and that an inspector recommended an assistant.[^4]

Kruger's own words survive in a letter that LighthouseFriends prints. He asked to be repaid for two docks, a barn and a hay shed he had built. He wrote that the barn and shed were "to keep cow, pigs, & fowl to live on through the winter," and that he "should probably remain there yet" if not for "malice and revenge of scoundrels."[^4] LighthouseFriends says he was removed as head keeper in October 1877 and replaced by Martin R. Benson.[^4][^5]

Benson was the last keeper. Terry Pepper says he "exhibited the Rock Harbor light for the last time on October 4, 1879," and then went to the Portage Lake Ship Canal light.[^3] LighthouseFriends says an 1879 inspection noted that Benson had "done more to improve the appearance of the grounds" than any other keeper in the district.[^4]

## After the keepers

The National Register nomination says the station was "again abandoned until the turn of the century," when camping parties chose it for their shelter.[^1] Terry Pepper says that, apart from fishermen's use in the 1930s, "the old station sat empty, and slowly deteriorating."[^3]

An inspector who visited about a decade after the station had been abandoned found the dwelling "a substantial structure in a good state of preservation." LighthouseFriends prints his report, which says the locks were off the doors and "the plastering was disfigured with charcoal sketches." The report adds that "The remoteness of the lighthouse had apparently limited vandalism."[^4]

Campers used the station around the turn of the century, the Park Service says.[^2] Two fishermen worked from it later. The National Register nomination says "From 1928 to 1939, commercial fishermen Arnold and Milford Johnson ... resided and fished from the lighthouse."[^1] LighthouseFriends dates their stay from 1927.[^4] Terry Pepper places the fishermen's use in "the 1930's when two dormers were added to the roof." The nomination says the dormers "may have been added circa 1910."[^3][^1]

The Lighthouse Board asked for a new light in 1902, after mining reopened. LighthouseFriends says the Board "repeated its requests annually until 1907," but no light was built.[^4]

The national park was established in 1939.[^2][^3] The Park Service says the lighthouse "was again abandoned in 1939 when the National Park was established."[^2] LighthouseFriends gives 1940.[^4] Terry Pepper says that after the park was set up, "the old structure became a part of the National Park," and that "no steps were taken toward either rehabilitation or restoration."[^3]

The tower leaned. Terry Pepper says "In the late 1950's the tower began to tilt," and an emergency stabilization followed.[^3] The nomination says the Park Service stabilized the tower in 1962 by pressure-grouting its hollow walls, and that in 1969 the lean was "stable at two degrees from vertical."[^1]

The nomination was prepared in 1976. It says the Park Service "is planning restoration of this early Lake Superior lighthouse for use as a museum of Isle Royale and Lake Superior shipping."[^1] The Park Service says all four Isle Royale lighthouses are on the National Register.[^6]

## Today

The Park Service calls Rock Harbor "the most viewed and visited lighthouse" in the park. Its keeper's house "now serves as a museum."[^2] LighthouseFriends says the museum includes a fourth-order Fresnel lens, and that the grounds are open with the tower open in season.[^4]

The sources differ on access. LighthouseFriends says the site is "six miles from the Rock Harbor campground by boat," and that "Access to the site is by boat from Rock Harbor Lodge."[^4] Terry Pepper, whose page was last modified in 2007, says "an improved trail system throughout the island has now made the lighthouse easily accessible."[^3] Check the park's current boat and trail information before a visit.
