Goat Island Light stands on a small island at the entrance to Cape Porpoise Harbor, in Kennebunkport, York County, Maine. The first light there dates from the 1830s, and the brick tower that stands today from 1859.[^1][^2] The island belongs to the Kennebunkport Conservation Trust, which restored the station in 2011 and invites visitors who come by boat in summer.[^4]

## The place

LighthouseFriends says the reefs and islets at the harbor's entrance made the approach to Cape Porpoise Harbor dangerous.[^2] The National Register nomination calls the light "a warning beacon to the numerous small islands and rock shoals that populate this short stretch of coast northeast of Kennebunkport," and says the station was established "as a guide to the harbor of refuge at Cape Porpoise."[^1] LighthouseFriends adds that Samuel de Champlain named the harbor Le Port aux Isles for its small islands when he sailed the coast in 1605, and that John Smith called it "Cape Porpus" in 1614.[^2]

The light also served as a mark for a speed trial. In May 1893 the Portland Daily Press reported that the cruiser *New York*'s course would run from a signal on the shore, over Goat Island light, to a buoy some four miles out at sea. The range from that signal over the light was to be the eastern goal of the route.[^5] The Press's issue of 23 May reported that the cruiser "steamed across the line" on the course. The course began "off the Salvage Ledges," the paper said, and its start was measured from "Goat Island Light on the water." The *Kearsarge* was placed "at the upper end of the course at Cape Porpoise."[^6]

## Building the light

The sources give different dates for the start of the work. LighthouseFriends says Congress appropriated $6,000 on 3 March 1831 for a light "at or near Cape Porpoise," and that customs collector John Chandler then called for proposals to build a tower and dwelling on Goat Island.[^2] The Trust's history gives 1834 for the same $6,000, and 1835 for completion.[^4] The nomination gives 1833 as the year the station was established.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the first light was shown in August 1833.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1835 as the year the station was established.[^3] The sources do not settle the date, and this story does not choose between them.

The first tower was conical and rubblestone, with an octagonal wrought-iron lantern. LighthouseFriends gives its height as twenty feet (6.1 m), with the focal plane 38 feet (11.6 m) above mean high water, and says only $2,711.80 of the original appropriation went into the buildings.[^2] The nomination says the 1848 light list shows the tower at about twenty feet (6.1 m).[^1] The Trust says the light burned whale oil for its first few decades and lard oil from the 1860s.[^4]

Inspector I. W. P. Lewis described the station in 1842. LighthouseFriends quotes him as finding the tower "laid up in lime mortar of bad quality, with a loose and leaky roof and decaying woodwork."[^2] Keeper Thatcher Hutchins added that leaks and condensation in the lantern meant he had to wipe the lantern glass three times each night.[^2]

A request for a new light came in 1853, passed on by Customs Collector Enoch Cousens.[^2] By 1857 the order was to rebuild the station as quickly as the means would allow.[^2] The new cylindrical brick tower, 25 feet (7.6 m) from the ground to the base of its lantern, was finished in 1859.[^2] The nomination says the tower and dwelling were pulled down that year and replaced, and it describes the L-shaped keeper's house as dating from 1859.[^1] The Trust gives 1860 for a remodel and a new residence.[^4] LighthouseFriends says a fifth-order Fresnel lens was installed in 1859, and that in 1867 a set of fifth-order Franklin lamps was placed inside it.[^2]

The nomination dates the frame boathouse to 1905.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record lists a brick oil house from 1907.[^3] The Lighthouse Board's reports list repairs made at the station in the fiscal years ended 30 June 1899 and 1900.[^8][^9] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892, the Board's survey list includes Goat Island, Maine, among the stations where the lighthouse lands were surveyed and "their boundaries were marked with stone posts or copper bolts."[^7]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists the principal keepers from 1833 until 1991, with one relief keeper in the 1960s.[^3]

John Lord was the first keeper, from 1833 to 1839.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he came from a local family and earned $350 a year. The Trust calls him Captain John Lord of Kennebunk.[^2][^4]

Inspection reports on the station's keepers went both ways. LighthouseFriends quotes an August 1850 inspection, under keeper George Fletcher, as finding the tower and lantern "in good order" and concluding, "The keeper is a good one."[^2] A July 1887 inspection under keeper John Emerson found the dwelling "being dirty from top to bottom," as LighthouseFriends quotes the report. A return inspection found the whole station dirty, and Emerson was dismissed.[^2]

George W. Wakefield kept the light from 1888 until 1921.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he also worked as a harbor pilot and fisherman, and that he died on the island in 1921.[^2]

The most recent principal keeper on the roster is Bradley Culp, who served from 1985 to 1991.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the station stayed staffed until the fall of 1991.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends gives totals for wrecks near the station without naming a source for them. Between 1865 and 1920, it says, forty-six vessels were wrecked nearby, and twenty-eight of them were total losses. It adds that not a single person aboard the 229 who were on those vessels died, and it credits the keepers' vigilance in part.[^2]

In a gale in 1947, while Coast Guardsman Joseph Bakken and his family were on the island, waves crashed across it, damaged the walkway and boat slip, and swept away a fence.[^2]

The blizzard of 1978 carried away the walkway that linked the keeper's quarters to the tower.[^4] LighthouseFriends describes keeper Martin Cain as having just left the covered walkway when the wind-driven sea lifted it and carried it off.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1959 the fog bell was replaced by a horn that sounded every twenty seconds.[^2] In 1976 local people petitioned the Coast Guard to delay its plans to automate the station. LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard listened, and the station stayed staffed until the fall of 1991.[^2]

The sources do not agree on when the light became automatic. The US Lighthouse Society's record gives "Year Automated: 1990."[^3] Its own roster runs Culp's service to 1991, and LighthouseFriends dates the end of staffing to that autumn.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends says a modern 300-millimeter optic replaced the historic Fresnel lens when the light was automated.[^2] The Society's record gives the current optic as an LED solar-powered unit installed in 2008.[^3]

The Trust leased the station in 1992. In 1998 it acquired the island's 7.6 acres and the light from the United States, under the Maine Lights Program.[^2][^4] A solar array with a battery bank went in in 2008 to power the light and the horn.[^4]

The Trust's restoration was completed in 2011. It rebuilt the walkway lost in 1978, reconstructed the bell tower, and refurbished the keeper's quarters to look as they did in the 1950s.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the original bell, which had been on display at the Kennebunkport Historical Society, was mounted on the new bell tower, and that a dedication ceremony marked the end of a $1 million renovation project in October 2011.[^2]

In November 2022 the submarine power cable from the mainland failed. Generators supplied some power until solar panels went up in June 2024.[^4][^2]

## Today

The Kennebunkport Conservation Trust owns the island and the light. The station has been on the National Register of Historic Places since March 1988.[^4][^1]

The keepers live on the island in the keepers' quarters, which the Trust describes as a private residence. The Trust says the keepers will give tours of the tower when they are home, and asks visitors to remember that the quarters are private.[^4] LighthouseFriends lists the grounds as open and the dwelling and tower as closed.[^2]

The Trust invites visitors by boat during the summer months. A small dock is usable at high tide and for one to two hours on either side of it.[^4] From land, the Trust suggests the landing at the end of Pier Road in Cape Porpoise, from which the light can be seen.[^4] A passport stamp is available at the Trust's headquarters in Kennebunkport.[^4] The Trust's page is the place to check hours and access before a visit.[^4]
