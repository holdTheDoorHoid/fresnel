Fort Niagara Light stands inside Fort Niagara State Park, beside the old French fort at the mouth of the Niagara River, where the river meets Lake Ontario near Youngstown, New York.[^1] Its octagonal tower went into service in 1872 and was deactivated in 1993.[^2] The British had placed a beacon on the roof of the fort in 1781, which LighthouseFriends calls the first navigational light on the Great Lakes.[^1]

## The place

The point was a portage and a harbor long before there was a lighthouse.[^1] French traders carried furs around Niagara Falls, and the forts built here date from 1679 and 1687.[^1] LighthouseFriends also lists the stone French Castle, built in 1726.[^1] The British captured the fort in 1759. When it was handed to the United States in 1796, the roof light was still in use, and it stayed in use until 1803.[^1] The British put up a second light across the river at Mississauga Point in 1804. It was pulled down in 1814 to clear a site for another fort.[^1]

The William Morgan affair touched the fort in 1826. Morgan, a former Freemason who threatened to publish Masonic rituals, was held at the fort shortly before he disappeared.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the keeper, Edward Giddings, was suspected of involvement but was never arrested.[^1] The roster does not list Giddings as keeper in 1826. It lists Ezekiel Jewett for 1826 to 1841, and Giddings only for 1861 to 1862.[^2] The keeper's part in the affair is unresolved here.

Vessel traffic in the area declined after the Erie Canal opened in 1825, and the Welland Canal followed in 1829.[^1] Enough sailing and steam vessels remained on the Niagara River to justify a light at its mouth.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress provided $1,000 on 7 May 1822 for a lamp on the mess-house inside the fort, and $1,500 on 3 March 1823 to finish a tower for it.[^1] Putnam's 1917 history of the lights lists Fort Niagara among the lights first established in 1823.[^4] The 1823 light was a lamp on a squat wooden tower atop the mess-house.[^14] In 1838 Naval Lieutenant Charles T. Platt described it as standing 70 feet (21 m) above the water, with nine lamps and an equal number of reflectors.[^1] He also credited the keeper with keeping it in good order.[^1]

In 1855 a tornado damaged the keeper's dwelling and outbuildings. In 1857 the tower received a fourth-order Fresnel lens and a new lantern, but USLHS gives 1859 as the year the original lens was installed.[^1][^2] By 1868 the Lighthouse Board wanted a new light. The wooden tower was old and let in snow and rain in storms, a chimney spark had once started a roof fire that could have destroyed the lens, and the light's supplies had to pass through the officers' quarters.[^1]

Congress provided $16,000 on 3 March 1871 for a new lighthouse. The plan was a fifty-foot (15 m) octagonal limestone tower with an attached oil room, placed outside the fort's walls near the stone keeper's dwelling.[^1][^14] Work started in July 1871 and stopped on 30 November because cold weather came early. It resumed on 15 April 1872, and the fourth-order lens, moved from the old tower, was first shown on 10 June 1872.[^1]

## The keepers

The roster lists 21 names for the station, from J. Heileman (1823) to Edward Wallingford Moon (1934 to at least 1939).[^2] Ezekiel Jewett kept the light for fifteen years, from 1826 to 1841.[^2] George F. Ferguson's stint of 1914 to 1934, by the roster's dates, is the longest principal term on the list, at twenty years.[^2]

The roster lists Elizabeth Powley as principal keeper from 1868 to 1869.[^2] LighthouseFriends calls her "Mrs. Francis Powley," and the roster lists a Francis Powley as principal keeper from 1862 to 1868.[^1][^2] The sources do not say why she took the post.

Other principal keepers on the roster include Ebenezer D. Barker (1869 to 1885), John F. Taylor (1885 to 1903) and Orlo J. Mason (1908 to 1914). LighthouseFriends gives Mason's last year as 1913.[^2][^1]

George F. Ferguson's career at the light included several episodes that LighthouseFriends records.[^1] In June 1926 he was captaining a seized speedboat that was carrying 400 gallons of alcohol to the Coast Guard station at Fort Niagara when the boat caught fire.[^1] He and a deputy sheriff, the only others aboard, got into a small dory being towed behind and watched the speedboat burn to the waterline.[^1] In May 1929 he was at the Cleveland Clinic when a fire there killed 123 people.[^1]

Edward Wallingford Moon kept the light from 1934 and was keeper in 1939, when, according to LighthouseFriends, he opened the tower to visitors during a week that marked the 150th anniversary of the Lighthouse Service. He showed visitors the power plant on the ground floor and took them up the spiral stairway to the balcony outside the lantern.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A Lighthouse Service Bulletin notice from 1917 names Keeper George F. Ferguson of Fort Niagara and gives 3 February 1917 for a rescue of three fishermen on the ice in the vicinity of the light station.[^11] LighthouseFriends describes the citation Ferguson received for that rescue differently: a young man whose boat was caught in an ice jam at the mouth of the river.[^1] The two accounts disagree on the number of people and the kind of trouble, and both are recorded here.

The 1855 tornado that damaged the keeper's dwelling is recorded only by LighthouseFriends.[^1] No wreck tied to the station is recorded in these sources.

## After the keepers

In its 1894 report the Board called the keeper's dwelling "old, in bad condition, and unsuitable," and estimated that a suitable one could be built for not exceeding $4,000.[^6][^5] The Board's report for fiscal 1896–97 records a contract for the new dwelling, dated 25 June 1896, and says the keeper's dwelling was rebuilt under it.[^7] LighthouseFriends says the new house was six rooms in two stories, placed 140 feet (43 m) west of the tower, using the plans of the Dunkirk and Oswego dwellings.[^1]

In the same 1894 report, a steel water tank erected on the military reservation was found to obscure the light from lake points. The Board reports that the tank was moved to a place where it no longer blocks the light.[^6] In 1899 the Board reported iron handrail put up in the tower stairway, 289 running feet of sidewalk built, and various other repairs.[^8] It also asked for $2,000 for a small light at the mouth of the river. LighthouseFriends says the request was repeated for six years and appears to have gone unfulfilled.[^9][^1]

In 1900 the stone tower was built up with brick 11 feet and 4 inches (3.5 m), and a watch room was fitted below the lantern.[^10] LighthouseFriends says the focal plane was raised by the same amount.[^1] SeaTheLights adds that the higher light could be seen 25 miles out on the lake.[^14] An iron oil house with room for 540 gallons went up about 25 feet (7.6 m) south of the tower. LighthouseFriends dates it to 1905, SeaTheLights to 1900, and the USLHS record to about 1900–1905.[^1][^14][^2]

The light was electrified in 1919. LighthouseFriends dates it to 28 August 1919, and it says the light's characteristic could now be changed to four seconds of light and a two-second eclipse.[^1] A Commissioner of Lighthouses list of electric incandescent lights includes Fort Niagara.[^12]

The Coast Guard decommissioned the historic light in 1993, rather than remove trees that were starting to obscure it. It was replaced by a light on a nearby radio tower.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that the Fresnel lens was taken out of the tower in 1995 and is now on display at the visitor center at Old Fort Niagara.[^1][^2] Discover Upstate says the light was automated in 1983 and that the lens stayed in the lantern until 1983.[^13] The two accounts give different dates, and the sources do not settle them.

The National Register record gives 19 July 1984 as the date published.[^3] Its PDF, at 22 KB, appears to be a placeholder rather than the nomination text.[^3] In 2013 the windows were replaced with a $10,000 gift from the East Hill Foundation.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse stands in Fort Niagara State Park, near the French Castle, and the Old Fort Niagara Association manages it.[^1][^13] The two sources differ on ownership. LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard owns the tower; Discover Upstate says New York State owns it.[^1][^13]

Opening hours also differ. LighthouseFriends says the tower is typically open on weekends from mid-July through August.[^1] Discover Upstate lists free self-guided tours from noon to 3 p.m. from mid-July to mid-October.[^13] Check with the fort before you go. The fourth-order lens is displayed in the fort's visitor center.[^1][^13]
