Turkey Point Light stands on a 100-foot (30 m) bluff at the tip of Elk Neck, near North East in Cecil County, Maryland, at the head of Chesapeake Bay.[^1][^3] The light was first lit in 1833, though the US Lighthouse Society's archive dates the station to 1832.[^3][^2] Women kept the light for more years than men did.[^3]

## The place

The bluffs have been a landmark for a long time. The 2002 National Register nomination says they "have served as a landmark and aid to navigation since colonial times."[^1] The nomination puts the light's focal plane at 129 feet (39 m) above the water, the highest on the Chesapeake apart from Cape Henry and Cape Charles, and says the light is visible for 13 miles. It does not say whether those are statute or nautical miles.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 65 feet (20 m) above the water instead, and gives 1832 as the year the station was established, so the sources do not agree on these figures.[^2]

After the Chesapeake and Delaware Canal opened in October 1829, the Lighthouse Board asked for a light at Turkey Point. The light was to mark where ships changed course from the bay to the Elk River, which leads to the canal.[^1] Congress first authorized $5,000 for the light on March 3, 1831, according to the nomination. LighthouseFriends gives the date as March 31.[^1][^3] The site was a four-acre tract, sold for $564 in December 1832 with access from the water.[^1]

## Building the light

John Donahoo built the tower and keeper's quarters in 1833 for $4,355.[^1] James Geddes supplied the eleven lamps, each with a 15-inch (38 cm) reflector, for $419.[^1] The brick tower is 31 1/2 feet (9.6 m) from its base to the parapet and 16 feet (4.9 m) across at the base.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it was built from the plans for Concord Point Light, and that although many sources give it cast-iron stairs, a 1930s photograph and the blueprints at the National Archives show wood.[^1][^3]

In 1855 a Fresnel lens was installed.[^1] The nomination later calls it a fourth-order lens.[^1][^2] In 1864 the Lighthouse Board asked for money to replace "lanterns of an old and exceedingly defective character" at six lighthouses, Turkey Point among them. Congress authorized $6,000 in April 1866, and the work was finished in 1867.[^1] The next year a Franklin lamp replaced the constant-level lamp.[^1]

In April 1888 a fog bell room, prefabricated at the Lazaretto depot in Baltimore, was erected over a dry well.[^1] The nomination ties the well to the bluff: because the bluff was so high, the bell was to sit as low as possible. The keeper had to hand-wind the weights back up during use in foggy weather.[^1] In 1889 the roof of the keeper's quarters was raised one story.[^1] In the fiscal year ended June 30, 1895, the Lighthouse Board's report records that wire rope was supplied for the fog-bell machine.[^1][^6]

## The keepers

The first keeper was Robert C. Lusby, who served from August 10, 1833, to August 18, 1841.[^1] John C. Waters took over for just under two years, and Robert Lusby returned on June 3, 1843.[^1][^2] The nomination calls his wife, Elizabeth Lusby, "the first of many women keepers." She replaced him on his death and served from May 8, 1844, to at least 1861. The archive's roster ends her service in 1862.[^1][^2]

A Cecil Whig death notice of March 1912 names Mrs. Elizabeth Lusby as "keeper of the light-house at Turkey Point" when she died.[^12] The notice says she had been keeper for many years, but gives no dates. If it is right that she was still keeper when she died in 1912, it conflicts with the roster, which ends her service in 1862 and has Edward Cloman keeper from March 1862.[^1][^2] The notice's remark that "We believe he was appointed by General Jackson" does not fit her appointment in 1844, because Jackson left office in 1837.[^12][^1]

John Crouch died on July 3, 1873. His widow, Rebecca L. Crouch, assumed his duties on October 2, 1873, and kept the light until she died on July 11, 1895.[^1][^2] The Midland Journal of October 10, 1919, says that Mrs. Crouch, the mother of Georgiana Brumfield, was appointed "after the death of Mrs. Lusby."[^9] The roster does not fit that account. It has Edward Cloman following Elizabeth Lusby in 1862 and John Crouch following Cloman in 1865.[^2]

Their daughter, Georgiana S. Brumfield, became keeper on July 26, 1895, according to the nomination; the Midland Journal gives July 25. She served until 1919 and retired at 70.[^1][^9][^10] The nomination says she had lived at the station for 54 years.[^1] A Maryland paper that reported her retirement in October 1919 called her "Mrs. Georgia S. Brumfield" and gave her nearly 25 years as keeper.[^9] A Prince George's County paper, in November 1919, said the station had been her home for 64 years.[^11] The two figures do not agree.

Caleb Stowe, from North Carolina, served from 1919 to 1922.[^1] The nomination says the date he began service varies from 1919 to 1920, according to the source.[^1] The 1919 paper calls him "B. C. Stowe."[^11] Clarence W. "Harry" Salter followed in 1922 and died in 1925.[^1][^2][^3] His wife, Fannie May Salter, took over in 1925. The Civil Service had told her she could not succeed her husband, because of her age. She appealed to her senator, and the nomination says President Calvin Coolidge personally authorized her appointment.[^1][^3]

She kept the light until August 1947, when she retired at 65, with 22 years as keeper and 23 earlier years assisting her husband at other stations, the nomination says.[^1] The roster gives her service to 1948.[^2] Of the work she said, "Oh, it was an easy-like chore, but my feet got tired, and climbing the tower has given me fallen arches."[^1] Before the station was electrified she filled and lit one of two lamps at dusk, climbed the tower to place it in the lens, checked it again about an hour later, and checked it once more at 10 p.m. before she went to bed.[^1] In 1928 the Lighthouse Board authorized $25 a month for a laborer to wind the fog bell's striking mechanism during the months when fog was prevalent. The fee was cut to $15 a month in 1932.[^1]

The nomination says Turkey Point had more women keepers than any other lighthouse on the Chesapeake Bay.[^1] A subheading over the Midland Journal's report put it another way: "Turkey Point Light In Charge Of Women For Three Generations."[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources name a few dated rescues and repairs. For the fiscal year 1921 the Commissioner of Lighthouses reported that keeper Caleb B. Stowe "towed disabled power boat to Town Point Wharf, Md., with seven men aboard."[^7]

In May 1923 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that Salter had helped a barge whose towing motorboat had broken down.[^8] The nomination adds that the barge was a "Floating Department Store Loaded with Bankrupt Merchandise," valued at $25,000, and that a strong northeast wind was about to catch it. Salter took the station's boat and towed the barge to safety under the point, out of the wind.[^1]

The nomination also records an undated fog episode. When the fog bell suddenly failed as a steamer was heading for the Chesapeake and Delaware Canal in fog, Fannie Salter struck it by hand, four times a minute for 55 minutes, until the steamer had safely passed.[^1] The nomination notes that Joseph R. Grant's history says she rang it by hand all night, but that most accounts give the 55 minutes.[^1] In 1929 the station needed unspecified repairs after hurricane damage.[^1]

## After the keepers

The nomination dates automation to 1948, shortly after Fannie Salter's retirement. It also says the light changed to flashing white in 1947 "when the station was automated," so the nomination gives both years.[^1][^2] The Fresnel lens was stolen after automation.[^1][^3] After vandalism, LighthouseFriends says, a large portion of the interior stairs was removed to protect the lighthouse.[^3]

The keeper's quarters and outbuildings were demolished. The nomination dates this to about 1971; LighthouseFriends says the state's parks service tore the house down in 1972.[^1][^3]

The light was decommissioned in April 2000.[^1] In 2001 it was leased to Turkey Point Light Station, Inc., a non-profit.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the group had been formed in 1995 and that the light was reactivated as a private aid to navigation on November 30, 2002.[^3][^2] In 2006 the property was transferred to the State of Maryland, which leased the light to the group.[^3] The group built a new interior staircase in 2007 and a flagpole in 2008. A replica fog bell tower was completed in 2023, with a bell cast in 1891 that had been used in a New Jersey church.[^3] Access was closed from March 2024 while a $3.8 million trail and parking lot were built, and it reopened in September 2024.[^3]

## Today

The Maryland Department of Natural Resources lists the Turkey Point area as open daily from 8 a.m. to sunset, and says the lighthouse's interior is opened by the volunteer group.[^4] The same page also says the area is open from sunrise to sunset, so its hours are not consistent.[^4] The group's own website carries a copyright notice for 2009 to 2022. It gives weekend climbing hours of 10 a.m. to 2 p.m., from May 1 to the end of October, weather permitting, and warns that its hours depend on the weather and on volunteers.[^5] The page does not say which year those hours are for, so they may be out of date. Parking is limited, and the light stands within Elk Neck State Park.[^4][^1] The state owns the light, and the volunteer group manages it.[^3]
