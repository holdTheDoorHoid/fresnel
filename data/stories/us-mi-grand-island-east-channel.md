Grand Island East Channel Light stood on the Thumb, the eastern lobe of Grand Island, where the channel into Munising Harbor on Lake Superior runs between the island and the mainland shore. Its wooden tower was first lit in 1868, and it was last lit on the night of 29 October 1908. The keeper's house still stands on private land. Volunteers and students restored it after 1999, and it can be seen from the water.[^1][^6]

## The place

Mariners on the south shore of Lake Superior had a light at the north tip of Grand Island, but the harbor at the island's south end needed more lights to be reached safely.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Senator Zachariah Chandler presented a petition from Lake Superior masters, pilots and vessel owners to Congress in February 1860, and that Congress appropriated $6,000 that June for "one or two beacon lights." The Forest Service gives the appropriations as June 1864 and June 1866.[^1][^6] The two accounts differ on the dates; this story follows LighthouseFriends for the sums it quotes.

A committee that reported on Great Lakes lights in 1863 said, in the words LighthouseFriends quotes, that the coast from White Fish Point to Copper Harbor had no harbor of refuge except Marquette. It recommended either a screw-pile light on the Sand Point shoal, or a light on the southeast point of the Thumb.[^1] Congress added $10,000 on 28 July 1866, and the Lighthouse Board chose the Thumb for the eastern entrance after asking shipmasters who knew the harbor.[^1]

## Building the light

The work was planned for 1867 with a crew from Gull Rock Lighthouse. The drowning of the foreman, William Tunbridge, pushed the start back to 1868.[^1]

In the summer of 1868 the Board built a wooden lighthouse on a stone foundation on the Thumb. It was a square tower joined to the gable end of a one-and-a-half-story keeper's dwelling.[^1] The fixed white light stood 49 feet (15 m) above the lake and was first exhibited on 15 August 1868. It first used a steamer's lens, which was upgraded to a fifth-order lens in 1869.[^1] The archive gives 1870 as the year the station was established and the tower and quarters were built.[^2] The Forest Service says the Board spent its money on the light and on a pair of range lights in 1868 to 1870.[^6]

The shore on the Thumb was eroding. LighthouseFriends says storms in 1898 cut so far back that the Board planned to move the light 200 feet (61 m) back from the lake, and that the plan was then reconsidered.[^1] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 describes a crib 8 by 24 feet (2.4 by 7.3 m) built in front of the light, a second crib 45 feet (14 m) to the west of the house, and 12 cords of stone placed in the cribs.[^4] The next year's report records that the crib work was finished in December 1899.[^5]

## The keepers

The archive lists six principal keepers, one after another, from 1868 to 1908: Thomas Wilson (1868 to 1870), Napoleon Beedon (1870 to 1873), Frank Beland (1873 to 1880), Joseph Bridges (1880 to 1886), George McGregor (1886 to 1893) and George Prior (1893 to 1908).[^2] The archive lists no assistants and no women keepers for the station.

Napoleon Beedon had kept Copper Harbor light for fourteen years before coming to the Thumb in 1870. In 1873 he moved to the mainland to take charge of the Grand Island Harbor range lights, which marked the western entrance.[^1]

George Prior began his lighthouse career at Stannard Rock in 1883, as assistant to his brother James. He became head keeper there in 1888, after James was transferred to Duluth, and was posted to Grand Island in 1893.[^1] LighthouseFriends says his wife, Theresa, and their children lived on the island. Three children were there when the family arrived, and two more were born there. In storms the family stayed dressed and awake all night, in case they had to flee inland; the lighthouse, they feared, might pitch over into the lake.[^1] Theresa Prior is not listed as a keeper, and the sources do not give her a role at the light.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1892 records that lightning struck the tower "on August 5, 1891," and damaged the tower and the dwelling. The Board had repairs made.[^3] LighthouseFriends dates the strike to 15 August 1891.[^1] This story keeps the Board's date, since its report is the primary source.

On 24 October 1903 the 252-foot (77 m) wooden steamer *Manhattan*, on her way from Duluth to Buffalo, took shelter in Grand Island Harbor from northerly gales. When she got under way the next day and came abreast of the light, her steering chain broke and she grounded on a reef. The collision knocked over an oil lamp, and fire burned the ship to the waterline. LighthouseFriends says Keeper Prior was reportedly the first to reach the wreck and give help.[^1]

LighthouseFriends also says that Prior once saved nine people from a boat that foundered in a violent storm. It does not date the rescue.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1905 the Board said the light no longer served its purpose. Mariners in the East Channel could not see it until they were close, and a wreck lay just north of the narrowest part of the channel. The Board asked for $13,200 for a pair of range lights on the mainland, and repeated the request the next year.[^1] Congress provided $15,000 for the range lights on 4 March 1907. Two metal towers and a keeper's dwelling went up at Munising in 1908.[^1] The light was last lit on the night of 29 October 1908. The next evening Prior exhibited the red range lights at Munising for the first time.[^1]

The archive records the station as discontinued in 1913.[^2] That year the 44-acre lighthouse reservation was sold to twenty members of the Munising Moose Lodge, each holding a one-twentieth share. LighthouseFriends says little was done to keep the structure up after that.[^1] A Munising newspaper in the 1990s warned that its rotting timbers and the lake's erosion both needed attention very soon.[^1] In 1999 the Alger County Historical Society formed the East Channel Lighthouse Rescue Committee. The committee raised $4,000 by raffle, and the American Lighthouse Foundation contributed $5,000.[^1] In 2000 volunteers drove around 600 cedar posts into the sand in front of the light, and they set wire cages of rock behind them.[^1] In 2002 the building was lifted off its stone foundation, one side at a time, so that the rotted sill and floor joists could be replaced. In 2003 the lantern room was taken apart, and Northern Michigan University students restored it. It went back on the tower in 2004.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is privately owned and closed to visitors. The Forest Service says Grand Island is a National Recreation Area, but that the lighthouse "remains on private property," and that it cannot be reached by land.[^6] The Munising Visitor's Bureau describes the structure as private.[^7] Visitors see the light from the water, on boat tours from Munising Harbor, or from a private boat.[^1][^6][^7] Do not land at the light or on the Thumb: it is private land, and its grounds and tower are closed.[^1]
