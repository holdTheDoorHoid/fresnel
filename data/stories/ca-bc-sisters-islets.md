Sisters Islets Light stands on the easterly and largest of a group of rocks in the Strait of Georgia, off the west end of Lasqueti Island.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends places it off the northwest end.[^2] The first lighthouse there was built in 1898. The present concrete tower dates from 1967, and the light has been unstaffed since 1996.[^2][^3]

## The place

Sisters Islets are a close grouping of rocks in the Strait of Georgia.[^2] In 1897 James Gaudin, the Department of Marine's agent in Victoria, notified the chief engineer that more lights were needed for the traffic heading north to the Alaskan gold fields, and named Sisters Islets "the first place that should be lit."[^2] The site chosen was the most easterly and largest of the three main rocks, where a day beacon had marked the hazard.[^2] The Coast Guard list places the light "on easterly and largest rock."[^1] Rowlett says it guides vessels through a passage between Lasqueti Island and Hornby Island.[^3]

## Building the light

The lighthouse was built during 1898 and placed in commission that December. George H. Frost, the lowest bidder, built it for $3,257.[^2] It was a rectangular wooden dwelling with a square tower above the northwest corner of the roof. The tower stood 11 m (36 ft) from its masonry foundation to the ventilator ball on its octagonal wooden lantern.[^2] A seventh-order lens showed a fixed white light from a focal plane of 14 m (46 ft). A bell in the dwelling's northeast gable was struck every thirty seconds in low visibility.[^2]

A fog signal came later. The Department's report for the nine months ended 31 March 1907 records a fog-alarm building for a diaphone under construction by day labour, under the direction of George H. Frost.[^4] The report for the fiscal year ending 31 March 1908 records the building finished and the diaphone plant installed.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the diaphone came from the Canadian Fog Signal Company of Toronto for $1,900, and that the new alarm gave two three-second blasts every ninety seconds when needed.[^2]

## The keepers

Ronald McNeill was the first keeper. LighthouseFriends says he spent one long winter at the station and resigned the following summer.[^2] Harry Higgins brought his wife to keep him company, but she left after a few months, unable to bear the fog bell, whose tolling cracked the plaster in the dwelling.[^2] Alfred Jeffries is listed against "Sister's Rock, Vancouver" in the Department's keepers' list for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1902.[^10] LighthouseFriends says Sisters Islets had five keepers in its first six years.[^2]

Benjamin Blanchard served in two stints, from 1902 to 1904 and again from 1904 to 1910, with W.C. Farnehough briefly between them.[^2] When the fog signal started, his salary rose from $500 to $800 a year, but he had to pay an assistant. LighthouseFriends says his wife was up to that work, so the family kept the extra money. He resigned in 1910 to obtain educational advantages for his young family, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The Department's keepers' list for the fiscal year ending 31 March 1908 prints "Sisters B. Blanchard", and the list for the fiscal year ending 31 March 1911 still shows "B. Blanchard" against the station, which does not fit the 1910 end date.[^6][^7]

Walter Buss followed, from 1910 to 1912 by LighthouseFriends' list, and the Department's list for the fiscal year 1911-12 prints "Sisters W. Buss."[^2][^8] Thomas Charles Lindsay Hayllar is listed for 1912 to 1918. The Department's list for the fiscal year 1914-15 has a Hayllar against the Sisters light, matched by column position in the scan.[^2][^9] Gordon Halkett visited in March 1915 and found the station in a disgraceful state. The marine agent, George Robertson, warned Hayllar to give the place a thorough cleaning.[^2]

In 1914 a fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed, and LighthouseFriends says it changed the light to a group of two flashes every ten seconds.[^2]

Charles Clark's short stay in 1924 was spoiled by earwigs, which overran the lighthouse. The Department sent a mixture of flour and plaster of Paris, which had worked at other stations. Clark sent his wife and two-year-old son ashore, and he soon followed them.[^2]

Charles Lundgren was keeper from 1944 to 1945. He had an assistant and fifteen dogs at the station, and LighthouseFriends says that when he left, the station had become a "reeking kennel."[^2] The next keeper, Art Tolpitt, and his wife Elsie had to clean the place. Elsie wrote to the marine agent, Stamford, about the dirt, fleas and old furniture she found, and said she was contented with the life of a light keeper's wife.[^2] Keeper Tolpitt resigned in 1947 when Elsie became seriously ill, and Charles Lundgren was reassigned to the station.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1932 a severe southeast gale struck over Christmas. Keeper Jonathon Fleming reported that the boathouse was wrecked and most of it washed away with its equipment, the boatways were gone, and seas were breaking over the platforms.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1967 a slender, cylindrical concrete tower replaced the 1898 light. The tower flares at the top to carry the lantern room and gallery. A four-bedroom dwelling, a fog-signal building and a helicopter pad, used to shuttle keepers to and from the mainland, were built with it.[^2][^3]

The station was de-staffed in June 1996.[^2] Rowlett says it was automated that year, and that the keeper's house is boarded up and probably endangered.[^3]

## Today

The light is an active Coast Guard aid, showing two white flashes every fifteen seconds.[^1][^3] The Coast Guard list gives the focal height as 24.3 m, with a nominal range of 16 nautical miles, and describes the tower as white with a red top.[^1] Rowlett gives the focal plane as 21 m (70 ft).[^3]

The Coast Guard owns the lighthouse. LighthouseFriends notes that the grounds and tower are closed.[^2] Rowlett says the island is reached only by boat, and that the site and tower are closed.[^3] Visitors should not land on the island or try to climb the tower.
