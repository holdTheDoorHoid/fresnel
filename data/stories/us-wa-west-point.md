West Point Light stands at the tip of a low sandy spit that reaches into Puget Sound from the foot of Magnolia Bluff, in Seattle. Its square brick tower, first lit in 1881, still flashes alternating red and white over the entrance to Elliott Bay.[^1][^2]

## The place

The spit is the first prominent point north of Seattle on the east side of Puget Sound. The National Register nomination calls it the northern turning point for shipping from the Sound into Elliott Bay, and says that with the construction of the Lake Washington Ship Canal in 1915 the light gained significance, as the turning mark for Shilshole Bay.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the canal was completed in 1917; the two sources differ on the year.[^2]

In 1879 the Lighthouse Board argued that Puget Sound had no aids to navigation south of Point No Point and recommended a 10-inch fog whistle at West Point, at $10,000.[^2] Congress voted the money for a fog signal on 14 June 1880, and an act of 3 March 1881 let it be spent on a lighthouse as well.[^2]

## Building the light

Six acres at the end of the point were bought from John Leary, and building began on 6 July 1881.[^2] The station had a low square brick tower with a watch room and oil room, a frame tower for a fog bell, and a five-room cottage for the keeper.[^2] The 1,600-pound bell had come from Cape Disappointment, where it had served since 1856.[^2] The fourth-order Fresnel lens, with twelve bull's-eye panels, was made in Paris by Sautter, Lemonnier et Cie and bears their mark.[^1][^2] The light was first shown on 15 November 1881.[^2]

The sources do not agree on the tower's size. The nomination describes a tower 10 feet (3 m) on a side with a catwalk 19 feet (5.8 m) above grade; LighthouseFriends gives 11 feet (3.4 m) square and 21 feet (6.4 m) to the top of the ventilator ball.[^1][^2]

In 1886 the Board replaced the bell with a Daboll trumpet driven by a caloric engine, and added a second dwelling; the trumpet went into service on 7 February 1887.[^2] By 1901 the Board reported the fog-signal machinery worn out and the frame fog-signal building "in a dilapidated condition and almost beyond repair", and planned two oil engines and a new building of brick or concrete in front of the tower.[^3] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended June 1917 records three-way horns installed at the station.[^7] LighthouseFriends says the new machinery began sounding on 29 December 1901; the National Register nomination dates the fog-signal building to 1900, and the Lighthouse Society archive to 1906.[^2][^1][^4]

## The keepers

Alfred W. Martin, an assistant at Cape Flattery for eight years, was the first head keeper at $800 a year, with Archibald W. Prushaw as assistant at $600. They shared the one dwelling until the assistant's post was cut in October 1882.[^2] George T. Fonda came from Cape Flattery in 1883 and stayed until 1907, the longest tenure at the station; the Society's roster gives him 1884-1907, after acting service from 1883.[^2][^5] The first keepers were isolated enough that the station was given a boat and boathouse for the seven-mile row to Seattle; a half-mile horse trail up the bluff came in 1883.[^2]

Louis A. Pettersen served as assistant from 1909 and as head keeper from 1913 to 1932.[^5] The January 1916 issue of the Lighthouse Service Bulletin records that on 18 December he rescued a man whose sailboat had capsized in a squall, bringing him to the station and giving him dry clothing; the issue gives no year for the date, so it was December 1915, though LighthouseFriends places the rescue in 1916.[^6][^2] LighthouseFriends also says he was awarded the efficiency flag at least twice for keeping the model station of the district.[^2]

On 3 December 1913 Assistant Keeper James E. Shaw, returning from Ballard with his family in an open power boat, fell overboard and drowned; his body was found in January 1914.[^2][^5]

Roswell Heins, whose father Otto Heins was an assistant around 1920, remembered the marsh behind the station as a mosquito breeding ground and the beach fouled by Seattle's sewage.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Light-House Board paid more than once to protect the spit from the sea. Its report for the fiscal year ended June 1892 records about 275 tons of rock delivered beside the bulkhead near the tower to keep the spit and grounds from being washed away by high seas, and the reports for fiscal 1895 and 1897 record further deliveries of stone along the bulkhead.[^8][^9][^10] The fog signal ran about 892 hours and burned about 7 tons of coal in fiscal 1897, against 195 hours in fiscal 1894.[^10][^11]

## After the keepers

A diaphragm air horn replaced the Daboll trumpet in June 1944.[^2] The Coast Guard took over the station and its crews are listed in the archive roster into the 1970s.[^5] The 1977 nomination said the station was manned and due to be automated shortly.[^1] Keeper Marvin Gebers, at the station in 1979, saw automation postponed past the centennial, and on 15 November 1981 he climbed the tower and poured champagne over it.[^2] The station was automated in 1985.[^2]

Declared surplus in 2002 under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, the light passed to the City of Seattle, which received the deed in 2006. The exterior was restored in 2009-10 at about $600,000, and the dwellings in 2011.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is in Discovery Park, owned by the City of Seattle. Grounds are open; the tower and dwellings are closed. Parking at the beach is limited to permit and ADA use, and the park describes it as visible from the South Beach Trail.[^2][^12] The fourth-order lens is still in the lantern, though a modern beacon does the work.[^2]
