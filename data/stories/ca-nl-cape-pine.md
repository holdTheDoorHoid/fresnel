Cape Pine Light stands on a barren headland just east of the southernmost point of Newfoundland, a red-and-white banded cast-iron tower that was first lit on 1 January 1851.[^1] Its parts were made in England, shipped across the Atlantic and hauled up a cliff to build it.

## The place

Early Portuguese charts marked the cape "C de pena", which Kraig Anderson's history of the light translates as cape of punishment or sorrow, and the name proved apt: ships were lost around it through the centuries.[^1] In 1840 the Commissioners of Lighthouses urged a light on the headland, and in November of that year Newfoundland's governor, Henry Prescott, wrote to London citing the season's wrecks along the island's south shore and the narrow escape of HM Sloop Snake in the strong set of water near the cape.[^1] Paying for it took years: the Newfoundland legislature voted £500 a year for upkeep on 29 April 1844, and in May 1847 the British Parliament granted £2,000 toward construction, with further sums of £3,000 in 1848 and again in 1850.[^1] Captain Henry Bayfield of the Royal Navy was asked to fix the height and position, and he also recommended a fog alarm, noting that in summer there was often not more than one day in a week free from fog.[^1]

## Building the light

The tower was designed by the British engineer Alexander Gordon, made by C. Robinson and Son of Pimlico, and fitted out with a lantern and lighting apparatus by Deville and Company.[^1] Its curved plates were landed at Arnold Cove, hoisted up a cliff and carted to the site, and the work ran through 1849 and 1850.[^1] The Canadian heritage record describes a tapered cylinder of overlapping cast-iron plates topped by a twelve-sided lantern with a shallow conical cap, with an iron gallery and six horizontal bands of red and white.[^2] It was the first of a series of prefabricated iron lighthouses put up in Newfoundland in the second half of the nineteenth century.[^3]

The first lighting apparatus revolved and carried sixteen lamps with reflectors; the tower is given as about 17 m (56 ft) high, with the light at about 92 m (302 ft) above the sea because of the headland.[^1] Keeper Henry Hearder lit it for the first time on 1 January 1851.[^1]

## The keepers

Within weeks Hearder wrote to the Commissioners that the tower was "by no means adapted" as a home. Wind came up the spiral stairs and disturbed the revolving machine, and he reported that "we have not had a dry bed since our arrival here."[^1] A frame dwelling joined to the tower by a covered passage was built in summer 1851, together with a 22 km road to Trepassey, which ended the risky landing of supplies at the cape.[^1] To save wear on the machinery, the revolution was slowed on 1 July 1852 so that the light flashed every thirty seconds instead of every twenty.[^1]

Hearder died in September 1866 and George Hewitt, an assistant at Cape Race, was sent to replace him, bringing his wife and eight-month-old son George J. Hewitt. Over the next century three generations of Hewitts kept the light.[^1] In 1875 assistant Thomas Doran was dismissed for being away without leave and William Costello came from Ferryland; in 1879 Costello was hurt in an accident while building an addition to the assistants' quarters, one that "narrowly escaped being fatal".[^1] Anderson's keeper list runs Hearder (1851 to 1866), George Hewitt (1866 to 1898), George J. Hewitt (1898 to 1935), George T. Hewitt (1935 to 1959), Valentine Hewitt (1959 to 1973), Michael Myrick, Tom Finlay and Peter Myrick (1990 to 1996).[^1] The same account separately names a Michael Myrick as keeper of the fog alarm until 1935.[^1]

Theresa Molloy married George T. Hewitt in 1936 and arrived at the station in a horse and carriage. She raised nine children there over the next twenty-three years; the family kept cows, sheep and hens, grew hay and picked berries, and the older children boarded in St. Shott's during the school week.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Marine department's annual report for 1923-24 lists the Canadian Pacific steamer Marvale as having "struck and sunk off cape Pine", and its ship went to help recover mail, baggage and valuables.[^4] A separate entry records that the Cape Race direction-finding station received a distress message from the Marvale at 19.25 on 21 May 1923.[^5] An earlier Marine Branch report, published in 1918, lists a collision off Cape Pine on 18 June between the Lunenburg, Nova Scotia, vessel Lucile M. Schnare and a second ship registered at London. The year is not stated in the entry. A formal investigation held at Quebec on 30 June found that no one was to blame and the collision was unavoidable.[^6]

## After the keepers

The light's character changed several times. It was made fixed in 1866 and its old apparatus went to Harbour Grace in 1869 and to Cape Bonavista in 1895; in 1880 the lamps were rearranged to light the entrance to Trepassey; an acetylene light of 1928 gave two white flashes every nine seconds.[^1] A diaphone fog horn began at nearby Cape Freels in 1910 and moved close to the tower in 1935.[^1] The original attached dwelling was demolished in the 1950s and replaced by two single-storey houses.[^1] The station was declared a National Historic Site of Canada in 1974 and a recognized federal heritage building in 1989; Anderson gives 1996 as the year it lost its last resident keeper.[^1]

## Today

The station is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard; Anderson reports the grounds as open and the tower closed.[^1] To reach it, the directions given are to leave Route 10 about 20 km west of Trepassey on the road to St. Shott's, then after 3.1 km bear onto an 8.3 km gravel road.[^1]
