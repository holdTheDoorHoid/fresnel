Lighthouse Point Lighthouse, until recent years known as Drew's Head, is a white tower on the south side of Beaver Harbour, on the Bay of Fundy shore of New Brunswick, where it marks the entrance to a harbour that vessels often used for shelter.[^1][^2]

## The place

Beaver Harbour was settled in 1783 by Loyalists who had fled the United States at the close of the Revolutionary War. A great fire in 1790 destroyed all but one house in the town, and the settlement was slowly rebuilt.[^1] The light stands on Drews Head, just south of the harbour. Its purpose was to guide vessels into the harbour, which, in the words of the Lighthouse Friends history, was "frequently resorted to as a harbour of refuge."[^1]

Parliament appropriated money for a lighthouse at Beaver Harbour in 1874. A site on Drews Head was chosen that autumn and bought from Lewis Holmes for $100.[^1]

## Building the light

W. B. Deacon and John Ward of Shediac built the lighthouse under a contract for $1,650. It was a square, wooden tower attached to a dwelling.[^1] Lighthouse Friends does not give the year the work was finished. It describes the tower as 11 m (36 ft) high from base to vane, with its fixed white light shown 13.7 m (45 ft) above high water.[^1]

The sources give different years for the first light. Lighthouse Friends says the first keeper, Ezra Munro, made the inaugural lighting of the lamps on 15 January 1876.[^1] Rowlett's directory gives 1875 as the year the station was established.[^4] This story keeps both dates, because the sources do not say whether the station and the light began in the same year.

In August 1876 John H. Harding, the Department of Marine's agent at Saint John, visited Drews Head and reported favourably on the work done since the light was built.[^1] Munro and his family cleared the site, and he put up a small wharf where supplies could be landed.[^1]

The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 lists Drews head among the stations where hand fog horns were established, "which will be used whenever the fog signals of vessels in the vicinity of the stations are heard."[^3]

The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1905 records that the light was improved by "a seventh order lens and lamp" in place of the reflectors and lamps it had used before. The report calls it a fixed white light, "and visible 10 miles," burning petroleum vapour under an incandescent mantle.[^7] Lighthouse Friends dates the change to 1905 and gives 1915 as the year a fourth-order lamp went into the lantern room.[^1]

## The keepers

Ezra Munro was the first keeper, at an annual salary of $250, and he had a large family to support. In 1877 he asked for a raise of $100. Lighthouse Friends reports that even with the strictest economy he could not support his large family.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says Munro was described as a man of weak constitution, but was credited with being very attentive to his work.[^1]

In 1882 Munro exchanged posts with Edward Dukes Snell, who had been keeping the Southwest Wolf Island light, and Munro's salary rose to $500.[^1] Snell had been born in England and had served aboard a British warship. He later settled in New Brunswick and piloted ships out of St. Andrews. He kept Drews Head for nearly ten years, until his sudden death in February 1892.[^1]

John C. Conley followed. Lighthouse Friends says a hand foghorn was supplied to him in 1900 and dates his service from 1892 to 1904.[^1] The Department's list of lightkeepers for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1903 places him at Drews Head.[^5]

John "Melvin" Eldridge kept the light from 1904 to 1926, the longest stint in the Lighthouse Friends roster. The same page's text says only that he "served as keeper for several years in the early 1900s," so the 1926 end date rests on the roster.[^1] The Department's list for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1904 also places "Eldridge, John M" at Drews Head.[^6] Melvin was the grandson of Lewis Holmes, who sold the site. Holmes's daughter Amelia had married David Eldridge, and Melvin was their son.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends gives no keeper for 1926 to 1928. From 1928 it lists Roy A. Sparks, at least until 1939. Later names are Hazen Holmes (1952 to 1957), Garnett William Eldridge (1957 to 1967), Edward N. Wilson (1967 to 1970), C. A. Stuart (1970 to 1983) and R. C. Stuart (1983 to 1984).[^1]

## After the keepers

The original tower and dwelling served until the late 1960s, when a skeletal tower replaced them.[^1][^4] The present fiberglass tower dates from 1984.[^1][^4] The keepers' list in Lighthouse Friends ends with R. C. Stuart in 1984. The sources used here do not say when the light stopped having a resident keeper.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light and its site.[^1][^4] The Coast Guard's List of Lights gives a white cylindrical tower with a red top, 8.6 m high above ground, showing an isophase white light with a six-second period, with a focal height of 14.2 m and a nominal range of 13 nautical miles. The light operates day and night.[^2] Rowlett's directory gives the tower as 8 m (28 ft) and the focal plane as 14.5 m (47 ft), so the two sources disagree slightly on the height.[^4]

The grounds are open and the tower is closed to visitors.[^1][^4] Rowlett's directory places the light beyond the end of Lighthouse Road, with limited parking and a walk of about 800 m (1/2 mile) round trip.[^4]
