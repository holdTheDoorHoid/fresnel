Chance Harbour Light stands on Reef Point, on the western side of the entrance to Chance Harbour, on the Bay of Fundy coast of New Brunswick.[^1][^2][^3] The station dates from 1915.[^1][^2] The cylindrical tower that stands there now dates from around 1969.[^1][^2] The Canadian Coast Guard still lists the light as working year round.[^3]

## The place

The light sits in Belding's Reef Nature Preserve, which the Nature Trust of New Brunswick describes as 5.7 ha (14 acres) of rugged coastline along the Bay of Fundy, with reddish shale and sandstone cliffs, ledges and reefs running out into the ocean.[^4]

The harbour had a marker before it had a light. In the year ended 31 March 1913 the Department of Marine placed a bell buoy off the harbour.[^5]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says that day labourers working under D. H. Mawhinney completed a square wooden tower on Reef Point in 1915. It also says Wallace Belding reportedly helped build it.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says that around 1969 a cylindrical tower with red and white horizontal bands replaced the original, and was still active in 2020.[^1] Rowlett dates the replacement to 1969, with a question mark, and describes a 7.5 m (25 ft) round fiberglass post painted red with a white band.[^2] The Coast Guard list gives a white cylindrical tower with a red band, 7.4 m (24 ft) high.[^3]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends names Nathan C. Belding as the first keeper, from 1916 to 1950.[^1]

Ronald Thompson followed him, after service in the Second World War.[^1] His wife, Frances Thompson, took over responsibility for the light and served until it was automated, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] No date is given for the automation.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In early February 1922 a Saint John dispatch printed in the New York Herald of 3 February said that the two-masted Boston schooner Senator, bound for Saint John, had sprung a leak off Dipper Harbour and keeled over. She had been towed on her side to the mouth of Chance Harbor.[^6]

By 9 February a Saint John dispatch printed in the Daily Kennebec Journal of Augusta, Maine, said there was "little hope" of saving the schooner, which had capsized in the Bay of Fundy the week before and was lying off Chance Harbor.[^7]

The Department's report for the year ended 31 March 1923 records the wreck of the schooner Senator as removed by a departmental diver, and the masts of the schooner, sunk off Chance Harbour, as removed.[^8] The dispatches call her the Boston schooner Senator, and the reports name a schooner Senator at the same place. No source gives a registry number that would show they are one vessel. On 9 July 1923 a green can buoy was placed to mark the wreck at the western entrance to the harbour, in St. John county.[^9]

On 15 December 1952 Ronald Thompson drowned at the wharf in Chance Harbour, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends gives Frances Thompson's service as ending with automation, with no date.[^1] The sources also disagree on the flash. The Coast Guard list gives a long flash every six seconds, two seconds lit and four dark.[^3] Rowlett gives a white flash every 12 seconds.[^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light.[^2] Its list shows it operating year round.[^3] Rowlett names the Nature Trust of New Brunswick as site manager for the preserve.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the light can be reached by a loop trail in the preserve.[^1] The Nature Trust describes the preserve's one walking trail as ending at the navigation light, formerly a lighthouse, at the end of the peninsula.[^4] Its wording calls the light a navigation light, formerly a lighthouse, while the Coast Guard list still gives a working light there.[^3] Rowlett gives the site as open and the tower closed.[^2]
