Aloha Tower stands on Pier 9 at the edge of Honolulu Harbor, in Honolulu County, Hawaii. It is a concrete tower with the word ALOHA cut into its parapet below the observation balcony.[^1][^4] From 1926 a light on its top, run by automatic equipment, served the harbor.[^4][^5] By the late 1960s the beacon had moved to a television tower, and the tower now stands in a waterfront marketplace without a light.[^4]

## The place

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin says the first light in the Hawaiian Islands was established near this site in 1858, but the early light was not regularly maintained until 1869.[^3] LighthouseFriends does not mention an 1858 light. It says an 1851 act for lights at Honolulu Harbor was never carried out, and that the Hawaiian government bought a small island near the harbor entrance in 1869. Its keeper first exhibited a light there on August 2, 1869.[^4] The 1858 date comes from the Bulletin alone, and this story does not settle it.

The harbor light later moved to Sand Island. LighthouseFriends says that light was first lit on 15 February 1910.[^4] The Bulletin says the old light, erected in 1908, marked the entrance from 1909 until 1926, and that it had insufficient geographic range.[^3] The sources differ by a year on when the light began to serve, and this story does not choose between them. LighthouseFriends quotes the Honolulu Advertiser of 30 May 1926 as saying the Sand Island light was to be done away with once the tower light was installed.[^4]

## Building the light

In 1925 the unfinished tower was the center of an argument. Some people wanted no tower at all, and some wanted a ten-story structure.[^2] After months of debate the Territorial Legislature chose the ten-story plan.[^2] The 1975 nomination names Arthur Reynolds as architect and the National Construction Company as builder, and says the tower was turned over to the Board of Harbor Commissioners in June 1926.[^2]

The sources disagree on the height and on the number of floors. The nomination gives 184 feet (56 m) and ten stories, with the harbor pilots' lookout on the tenth floor. The Bulletin of 1926 gives 190 feet (58 m), and the Bulletin of 1934 gives 198 feet (60 m).[^1][^5][^3] LighthouseFriends counts twelve stories, puts the pilots' lookout on the eleventh, and gives the cost as $190,000.[^4]

The Bulletin of 1926 reports that the Territory had arranged for automatic electric equipment, with an acetylene gas auxiliary, to work a new light on top of the tower. It describes the light's characteristic as two seconds of light and three seconds of eclipse.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the light was put in commission on 1 July 1926.[^4]

The tower also carried a time ball, which was lowered down a forty-foot (12 m) mast at noon each day. LighthouseFriends says a siren sounded at 7 a.m., noon and 4 p.m.[^4] The novelty of the siren soon wore off for nearby residents, LighthouseFriends says, and its use was then limited to welcoming ships and to warning of dangers such as tsunamis and fires.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 29 March 1925 E. E. Robins, keeper of Honolulu Harbor Lighthouse, rendered assistance to five persons adrift in a small yacht off the harbor entrance and towed them back to the boathouse, according to the Bulletin.[^7] LighthouseFriends lists Robins as head keeper from 1917 to 1925.[^4] The tower's light was not lit until 1 July 1926, so in 1925 the Honolulu Harbor Lighthouse was the light on Sand Island, not the tower.[^4][^7]

Immediately after the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor, the United States Navy took over control of Aloha Tower. Access was denied to all but authorized persons, and armed sentries patrolled the tower.[^6] The tower had been a control facility for military convoy shipping.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the light was extinguished for the rest of the war and the tower was camouflaged in brown and green paint.[^4] The nomination says the Coast Guard, which used the tower under Navy authority, gave up control on 20 December 1947.[^6]

## After the keepers

The light on the tower was automatic.[^5] LighthouseFriends says running the light cost about $50 a year, and that the government saved about $1,500 compared with keeping a keeper on Sand Island.[^4]

In 1947 the green camouflage was sandblasted off and the tower was painted white again.[^6] LighthouseFriends says a reopening celebration was held in 1948.[^4]

The Bulletin of 1934 reported that the old harbor light, replaced in 1926, was then being demolished.[^3] LighthouseFriends says that by the late 1960s taller hotels and business buildings surrounded the tower, and the Coast Guard had moved its beacon to a 220-foot (67 m) television tower.[^4] That beacon served until 1975, when the present Honolulu Harbor Light was set up on a metal pole at the end of Pier 2.[^4]

## Today

The State of Hawaii owns the tower.[^4] The 1975 nomination lists the Department of Transportation, Harbors Division, as owner.[^8] LighthouseFriends says the developer of the adjacent Aloha Tower Marketplace renovated the tower in 1994, at no cost to taxpayers, and lists the grounds and tower as open.[^4] The tower no longer carries a light.[^4] One visitor's account on LighthouseFriends describes an elevator that did not reach the top. The sources do not say whether the upper floors are open today, so ask the marketplace before a visit.[^4]
