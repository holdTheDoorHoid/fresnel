Whaleback Light is a granite tower on a tide-washed ledge at the outer entrance to Portsmouth Harbor, built in 1872 after the first tower on the same rock had shaken for four decades. It is in Maine waters, though it guides ships to a New Hampshire port.[^1][^2]

## The place

The ledge lies on the northeast side of the entrance to the Piscataqua River, about half a mile south of Gerrish Island in Kittery, York County, Maine. It is covered at high tide and is a continuation of the island's southern point. Jeremy D'Entremont, writing for the Friends of Portsmouth Harbor Lighthouses, puts the light in Maine by about 1,500 feet, and notes that it is often called a New Hampshire lighthouse; the Light-House Board's own reports of the 1890s file it under New Hampshire.[^3][^5]

The ledge was known for wrecks before there was a light. D'Entremont cites a February 1733 wreck, in which two of five men aboard a schooner drowned, and the schooner *President*, lost in April 1821 when boats of soldiers from Fort Constitution came out and seven people were taken from the water. In February 1828 the Portsmouth *Journal*, as he quotes it, asked how many more wrecks must be made before Congress made an appropriation. He credits New Hampshire's Representative Ichabod Bartlett with getting the money through; three appropriations by 1829 totalled $20,000.[^3]

## Building the light

The first tower went up in 1829-30. Sources disagree on the year it was lit: the National Register nomination says the station was established in 1829, LighthouseFriends says the tower was erected in 1829 and 1830, D'Entremont gives a lighting date of 16 September 1830 (and later calls it the 1831 tower), and the American Lighthouse Foundation says 1831.[^1][^3][^4][^10] The contractors were Daniel Haselton and William Palmer. D'Entremont says the work could be done only for a few hours around low tide, with nearby Wood Island as the base.[^3]

Accounts of the first tower's size also differ. LighthouseFriends describes a pier 48 feet (14.6 m) across and 22 feet (6.7 m) high under a 32-foot (9.8 m) tower; D'Entremont gives a pier 42 feet (12.8 m) wide at the bottom and a 38-foot (11.6 m) tower. Both agree it was poorly built: the ledge had not been levelled and small stones filled the gaps at the bottom.[^3][^4] The light was two fixed white lights, one 10 feet above the other, to tell it from its neighbours.[^3]

By 1838 a naval officer surveying the district wrote of "Whale's-back light" that "it appears to be exceedingly unsafe for the keeper in a gale of wind," as LighthouseFriends quotes him. The engineers Alexander Parris and Sylvanus Thayer advised pulling it down rather than building the breakwater Congress had paid for; nothing was done for more than thirty years beyond repairs.[^3][^4] Iron clamps put around the foundation snapped, and storms in March 1869 cracked the pier. LighthouseFriends says Congress gave $70,000 on 15 July 1870; D'Entremont gives the cost of the new tower as $75,000.[^3][^4]

The new tower is granite blocks dovetailed together, designed with the involvement of General James Chatham Duane and modelled on Minot's Ledge and Eddystone. D'Entremont says the granite came from Biddeford. Levelling the ledge began in summer 1870; the last stone was laid in August 1871. The National Register nomination, which calls the Army Corps of Engineers its designer, gives 1872 for completion and describes a conical granite tower joined to a brick fog signal building, with the light about 59 feet (18 m) above mean high water.[^1][^3] D'Entremont notes the focal plane was reported as 68 feet (21 m) when built.[^3]

The original tower stayed standing alongside it. A Daboll fog trumpet went into the old tower in 1877 by D'Entremont's account; in 1878 an iron fog-signal tower was built beside the new light, and the old tower came down in 1880.[^3] A fourth-order Fresnel lens had been installed in 1855; a revolving fourth-order lens followed in 1898 and a new bivalve lens made in France replaced it in 1912.[^3][^4] The 1912 Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists Whaleback among the stations whose lamp was changed to incandescent oil vapor that year.[^9]

## The keepers

The first keeper, Samuel E. Hascall, served from 1830 to 1839 at $500 a year.[^2][^3] Keeper Eliphalet Grover told the engineer I. W. P. Lewis in 1842 that "the vibration was so great as to move the chairs and tables about the floor."[^4] D'Entremont says a station that later became men-only began with keepers' families living in the tower. In September 1849 keeper Jedediah Rand's 15-year-old daughter Elizabeth Jane was visiting when the station boat was upset by the waves. The crew of a passing schooner heard the keeper's cries, a boat was sent out, and the two were taken to New Castle; she was revived before they reached land.[^3]

In March 1871 head keeper Ferdinand Barr, a Civil War veteran said to have been Prussian by birth, drowned while tending his lobster traps; his children, alone in the tower, signalled for help, and the rescuers arrived too late.[^3] The archive lists an Augustus Ferdinand Barr as first assistant from 1868 and head keeper in 1871, and an Emily F. Barr as second assistant in 1869-71. It also lists Mary M. Amee as first assistant from 1864 to 1867, under head keeper Gilbert D. Amee. The sources read do not say how any of these people were related.[^2]

William H. Caswell was in charge when the new tower was finished; his son Frank was an assistant. In November 1871 Caswell called it "perfectly safe," D'Entremont says, and noted it did not tremble in a storm.[^2][^3] In July 1882 assistant John Lewis fell while painting the fog-signal tower and died a few days later.[^3]

Walter S. Amee kept the light from 1893 to 1921, with John W. Wetzel as first assistant to 1924 (the archive lists him as second assistant from 1897 and first assistant from 1899; D'Entremont has him as first assistant from 1897) and John P. Brooks as second assistant from 1899 to 1915.[^2][^3] A 1911 Boston *Globe* article, as D'Entremont reports it, said the three had turned down posts at other Maine stations, worked four days on and two ashore, and that Amee had spent eleven days out of sight of the light in eighteen years. Amee was succeeded in 1921 by Arnold B. White, son of the earlier keeper Leander White, who stayed until 1941 or 1942 (D'Entremont and the archive differ), and was the last civilian head keeper.[^2][^3] D'Entremont records an efficiency pennant for the crew in 1938, and a 1939 newspaper visit that found a kitchen and brass kept bright; White's wife, who had to live ashore, sometimes stayed for days.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the early hours of 15 February 1863 the British schooner *Rouser* was wrecked close to the ledge. D'Entremont reports that the keeper saw three men on the wreck at 5 a.m. and threw a line, but the seas were too rough, and all seven aboard died.[^3]

In 1886 a wave broke a window of the new tower and flooded the living quarters; keeper Leander White flew a blanket as a distress signal, and LighthouseFriends says the wind shredded it and that granite replaced the window.[^3][^4] Two Kittery men, Walter Amee and Samuel Blake, reached the light as the storm eased.[^3] The March 1888 blizzard destroyed much of what remained of the old foundation, and a November gale left a reported 2,000 tons of stone around the new tower.[^3]

The Board's own report on the January 1892 storm is drier: the stones the sea had washed onto the slip and in front of the tower's ladder were cleared, and boatways 10 by 100 feet were laid on the ledge.[^5] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 puts the station's third-class Daboll trumpet at 832 hours of operation for the year, burning 4 tons of coal.[^6] In September 1893 observers from the tender *Myrtle* tested its audibility at sea and lost the trumpet at about 6 miles.[^7] The trumpet's characteristic was changed on 29 May 1899.[^8]

Keeper Amee's boat rescues are told in D'Entremont: two men whose small boat capsized in August 1899, while an assistant sounded the horn for the nearby life-saving station, and two York men whose powerboat hit a rock in July 1911.[^3]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over the Lighthouse Service in 1939, and D'Entremont reports crews of two or three in 1957, working eight days on and two off, and 24 days on and six off by 1959. The horn is said to have run 18 straight days in 1961. In December 1956 a crewman lost his boat's power on his way ashore and drifted for six hours before a cutter found him.[^3] The last Coast Guard crew left at 4 p.m. on 4 March 1963 and aerobeacons replaced the lens.[^4] The USLHS archive and the National Register nomination both give 1963 for automation.[^1][^2]

The fourth-order lens from the station is now at the Peabody Essex Museum, Salem.[^3][^4] In 1991, D'Entremont says, the Coast Guard found the fog signal was damaging the tower and lowered its volume. A VRB-25 optic arrived in 2002 and a solar LED beacon in October 2009.[^3][^4]

## Today

Whaleback is an active aid to navigation owned by the American Lighthouse Foundation. LighthouseFriends says the Foundation was licensed to care for it in October 2005; D'Entremont reports that the Interior Secretary accepted the award of ownership to the Foundation in November 2008 under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act; its Friends of Portsmouth Harbor Lighthouses chapter is raising money to restore it.[^3][^4][^10] It sits on an offshore ledge, so it is reached only by boat, and the tower is closed to the public.[^4] It can be seen from Fort Foster in Kittery and other shore points, or from tour boats leaving Portsmouth Harbor.[^3]
