Ten Pound Island Light stands on a small island at the eastern end of Gloucester Harbor, Massachusetts.[^1] A light has been shown there since 1821. The cast-iron tower on the island today is believed to date from 1881.[^1][^3] The Lighthouse Service kept a keeper on the island until the station was discontinued in 1956.[^2][^3]

## The place

The Massachusetts Historical Commission's 1981 survey form describes the light as standing on "a small island at the eastern end of Gloucester Harbor," with shoals lying "between" the island and the mainland to the east.[^1] The form says the light was "used as guide by fishermen and vessels entering the harbor."[^1]

The state and the selectmen of Gloucester gave the island to the federal government in July 1820.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Congress had allocated $9,000 that May to build two lights, this one and one on Baker's Island near Salem.[^3]

## Building the light

The first tower was a stone tower 40 feet (12 m) high. The 1981 form lists it under 1821 with a covered passageway, the tower and passage together costing $15,000.[^1] The same chronology lists a stone and slate oil house for $1,000 and a wood-framed dwelling for $8,000.[^1]

An inspector's report of 1842, as LighthouseFriends prints it, says the tower and dwelling were "built of rough-split stone" and that the lantern leaked.[^3] The same report says "there were formerly ten lamps, but four have been discontinued."[^3]

LighthouseFriends dates a sixth-order Fresnel lens to 1856, when it replaced the lamps and reflectors in the lantern.[^3] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record gives the original optic as a fifth-order lens.[^2] The two accounts disagree, and the sources do not settle which is right.

The tower that stands now replaced the stone one in 1881. The form describes the tower as "a conical cast iron structure, 30' tall," about 9 m high, with an interior that is "brick lined."[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends says that on 1 March 1889 the light's characteristic changed from fixed white to fixed white with a flash every five seconds.[^3] The 1981 form's light history gives a different date: a flash that went from six seconds to five in 1919.[^1] The sources do not agree on the date.

The oil house is the one early building whose date the sources do not share. The form says the oil house "remains in ruins only; granite block walls do stand," and dates it to 1821.[^1] LighthouseFriends says an oil house was added to the station in 1906.[^3]

In its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894, the Light-House Board recorded that the westerly side of the dwelling "was rebuilt, and various repairs were made."[^5]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster lists seventeen principal keepers between 1820 and 1956, and one contractor.[^2]

James Sayward was the first keeper, from 1820 to 1833.[^2][^3] He was followed by Amos Story, "a local mariner from Gloucester," who kept the light from 1833 to 1847.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends says Story was paid $350 a year.[^3] Before he took the post, Story gave a sworn account of "a strange marine animal" seen off the island in August 1817.[^3]

George W. Davis, "a former sailor who developed deafness at sea," kept the light from 1849 until at least 1851.[^2][^3] In the summer of 1880 the painter Winslow Homer lodged with the lighthouse keeper on the island, and LighthouseFriends names the keeper as Octavius A. Merrill.[^4][^3] LighthouseFriends says Homer painted about fifty views of the harbor that summer.[^3]

The roster then names two more Hoopers, George P. and Winfield S. S., and George W. Bailey, who kept the light until 1922.[^2] Edward H. Hopkins transferred to the island that year from Cape Poge Lighthouse.[^3]

LighthouseFriends calls Hopkins "the last keeper employed by the Lighthouse Service" on the island.[^3] The roster runs on, though, to Andrew M. McLaughlin for 1955 and 1956, so the two sources do not agree.[^2]

In the Christmas season of 1940 Hopkins's wife, Evelyn, spread newspapers on the ground and nailed them in place to spell out MERRY CHRISTMAS, for Edward Snow, the "Flying Santa" who dropped presents for lighthouse children from a plane. "A picture was taken from the plane and sent to the Associated Press," LighthouseFriends says.[^3] A keeper's son, who lived on the island in 1951, later recalled rowing to the mainland for school.[^3]

Ernest A. Sampson (1942), Howard A. Ball (at least 1947 to 1950) and Thomas L. Keene (at least 1951) complete the other names on the roster.[^2]

## After the keepers

The station closed in 1956.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the fifth-order lens was then replaced by a modern optic, "mounted first on the old fog bell tower" and later on a skeleton tower. The old lens is "now on display at the Maine Lighthouse Museum in Rockland, Maine."[^3] That makes the fifth-order lens here a different account from the sixth-order lens LighthouseFriends dates to 1856.[^3]

The keeper's dwelling came down in the 1960s. LighthouseFriends says it was razed in 1965.[^3] The Lighthouse Preservation Society says the city had burned it down over concerns about vandalism.[^7] The historical marker says the station "continued to serve the maritime community until 1965."[^6]

The city made a start on cleaning up the island. The Lighthouse Preservation Society writes that within a few years the island and the lighthouse were "again abandoned," a victim of the city's financial troubles.[^7]

In the late 1980s the Lighthouse Preservation Society led the restoration of the lighthouse.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the light was relit on 7 August 1989; the historical marker gives 5 August, when the tower was "restored and relit" "in commemoration of America's Lighthouse Bicentennial."[^3][^6] The oil house was restored in 1995.[^2][^3]

The National Register received the nomination in 1988, as part of a group of Massachusetts lighthouses.[^1]

## Today

The island and the light belong to the City of Gloucester.[^1][^3][^7] The 1981 form also records a 0.1-acre Coast Guard parcel, and the U.S. Lighthouse Society's record names the Coast Guard as owner or manager.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends lists the grounds as open and the tower as closed, but the Society's record says the station is not open to the public.[^3][^2]

The Lighthouse Preservation Society wants the island's old pier rebuilt, "making the island accessible and safe," and hopes the island can become a seaside park with seasonal water taxi service.[^7]

The light is clearly visible from many points along the Gloucester waterfront, and the historical marker stands in Stacy Boulevard Waterfront Park.[^3][^6]
