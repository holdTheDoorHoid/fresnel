Table Bluff Light stood on a bluff about four miles (6.4 km) south of the entrance to Humboldt Bay, in Humboldt County, California, from 1892. Its wooden tower was cut in two and moved to Eureka's Woodley Island Marina in 1987, where it stands today.[^1][^10]

## The place

The first light on Humboldt Bay stood on the sandy north spit near the entrance.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the spit was prone to flooding and often obscured by fog, and that in 1867 the Light-House Board recommended moving the light to Table Bluff, which rises 165 feet (50 m).[^1] Earthquakes and an extraordinary high tide had made the old Humboldt Harbor tower uninhabitable by 1885, the same source says.[^1] The Board's report for 1891 says Congress, at its last session, had appropriated $25,000 for a new station on a new site, and that the Board chose "a point about 4 miles south of the present light."[^3] The Board's 1893 list of station moves gives the distance as "about 5 miles S. by W.", so the two reports disagree.[^5]

## Building the light

The Board's 1891 report says the inspector and engineer of the district visited the place on 8 May 1891 and selected the site.[^3] Lighthouse Friends gives the visit as 1892.[^1] The owner, Eureka councilman W. S. Clark, refused to sell for less than $5,000 for about ten acres and a road, and the government began condemnation proceedings.[^3][^1]

The suit was withdrawn and a compromise was reached with the owner. The United States secured about ten acres, and the right to take water from an adjacent spring, for $2,226.[^4][^1]

Bids were opened on 21 March 1892.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says the tower and keeper's dwelling went to W. M. Wickersham of San Francisco for $16,949, and a separate $845 contract supplied the fourth-order lantern.[^1]

The Board's 1893 report describes the finished station: "a keeper's dwelling and tower combined," a double dwelling for the assistant keepers, a fog-signal building with two boilers and engines, and a coal shed.[^5] Water came from a spring near the station, forced into the tanks by a windmill and pump, with a hydraulic ram to help in calm weather, and about ten acres were enclosed by a fence.[^5]

The lens and one fog-signal boiler stayed at the old station until 31 October 1892. On that day the new light was first exhibited and the old station was abandoned.[^5] Lighthouse Friends says the station was patterned on San Luis Obispo's of 1890, and that because the lens and fog equipment could be moved from Humboldt Harbor, the new station cost half as much.[^1] Head keeper Tony Schmoll lit the fixed fourth-order Fresnel lens that evening.[^1][^2]

By 1899 the station had a brick foundation under a 50,000-gallon water tank.[^6] In 1911 a revolving lens replaced the fixed one, so that the light flashed every five seconds, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1912 lists Table Bluff among the lights changed from fixed to flashing, marked as an incandescent oil-vapor light.[^7]

## The keepers

Tony Schmoll was head keeper from 1892 to 1913. Lighthouse Friends says he had served at Humboldt Harbor and came to Table Bluff with his two assistants, Charles F. Allen and Emil C. Parch.[^1][^2]

The archive's roster lists 39 keeper stints, from 1892 into the 1950s. The head keepers after Schmoll were John E. Lind, Charles C. Bruehl, Bernard H. Linne, Wallace Evans and Harry W. Miller, who is listed from at least 1950.[^2]

Stephen Pozanac came to Table Bluff as first assistant in 1922 and stayed until 1938, when he was transferred to Ballast Point.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends says the Pozanacs raised chickens and vegetables on the reservation and supplied eggs and produce to grocery stores in Eureka.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 31 January 1922, at 5:15 in the morning, two heavy earthquake shocks put the Table Bluff light out, broke its mantle and knocked covers off the lamp. The keeper was on watch and had the light in operation again at 5:20 a.m.[^8]

On 22 January 1923 severe shocks damaged several light stations in California. Table Bluff's light was put out at 1:05 a.m., its mantle was broken, and the keeper on watch had the light in operation at 1:15 a.m. The Bulletin says no other damage was done.[^9]

Lighthouse Friends also tells of a night when Pozanac was walking to his post in the fog-signal building and a strong tremor struck. The tremor toppled the chimney of that building through its roof. The account gives no date.[^1]

## After the keepers

Electricity reached the station in 1935. The tower and dwellings were wired, and the fog signal was changed from a siren to a type F diaphone.[^1] A wireless station had been added in 1906, and during the Second World War the site held a coastal lookout, a radio station and horseback patrols along the coast.[^1] Afterwards the Coast Guard decided to demolish the historic dwelling portion of the lighthouse and the keeper's duplex. The wooden tower, separated from its dwelling, had to be steadied with cables against high winds.[^1]

In 1953 the station was one of the first to be automated. A modern optic went into the tower, the Fresnel lens was shipped to Old Point Loma Lighthouse, and the fog signal was discontinued.[^1][^10]

The end date of the light is disputed. The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive gives 1961 as the year it was discontinued. Lighthouse Friends and Point Cabrillo say it stayed active until 1971.[^2][^1][^10]

In 1969 the pastor of a Christian youth ministry in Oregon bought the station and renamed it Lighthouse Ranch.[^1] Jim Durkin bought the ranch in 1971, and a commune grew up there, with the fog-signal building converted into a chapel.[^1] Gospel Outreach sold the land to the state in 2005 for $1.5 million.[^1] The state donated the ranch to the Bureau of Land Management, which in 2012 contracted for the demolition of the remaining structures at a cost of $307,125.[^1][^10]

In 1987 the abandoned tower was cut in two and moved to Eureka's Woodley Island Marina, under the guidance of Ray Glavich, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1][^10]

## Today

The tower stands at Woodley Island Marina in Eureka.[^10] The Fresnel lens, retrieved from Old Point Loma, is on display at the Humboldt Bay Maritime Museum in Samoa, just outside Eureka.[^1]

The bluff site is Bureau of Land Management land. Lighthouse Friends says visitors can enjoy the views from the bluff, and that all of the station's historic structures are gone.[^1][^10] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists the light as not open to the public.[^2]
