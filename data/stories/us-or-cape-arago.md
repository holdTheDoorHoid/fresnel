Cape Arago Lighthouse stands on a small island just south of the entrance to Coos Bay, Oregon. It is the third light on the island. The first was lit in 1866, and the present concrete tower dates from 1934.[^2][^1] The light was deactivated in 2006.[^2] Since 2013 the island has belonged to the Confederated Tribes of the Coos, Lower Umpqua and Siuslaw Indians, and the lighthouse is not open to the public.[^2]

## The place

The Coos Bay bar was the hazard, and numbers of vessels were lost in the nineteenth century.[^1] In 1864 Congress passed an act "for erecting a lighthouse on Cape Arago" and gave $15,000 for it.[^1] As coal and timber shipments grew, the pressure for jetties at the bar grew too: "By 1893 the mounting coal and timber shipping over the Coos Bay bar had led to increased pressure for adequate jetties."[^1]

The lighthouse stands on Chiefs Island, "named by the Coos, whose homelands include the headland across from the island."[^2] According to tradition, the small island tip became the home of a village chief.[^1] From the mid-1850s until 1886, Gregory Point and nearby Sunset Bay "had been the area's most popular beach, camp ground, and picnic area."[^1]

## Building the light

The first tower went up in 1866. It was an octagonal tower "about 25 feet high" (7.6 m), and the light was first lit on 1 November 1866.[^1] Leonard Hall was the first head keeper, and the Oregon Encyclopedia says he "lit the light on November 1, 1866."[^2][^5] By 1893 the Light-House Board judged the 1866 keepers' quarters "poorly built and ill adapted to accommodate the two keepers with their families."[^1]

Getting to the island was hard. A low bridge went up in 1876.[^1] The Oregon Encyclopedia says it "lasted only until a November gale in 1877 destroyed it and the boathouse."[^2] A cable tramway followed in 1891: a 400-foot (122 m) cable that "ran between framed towers or tram houses on the island and the mainland."[^1][^4]

In 1896 the Service enclosed the iron tower in brick and built a brick fog-signal room. The nomination calls these "the most significant" changes since 1866.[^1] In the fiscal year ended June 30, 1900, the station's Daboll trumpet "was in operation some 539 hours and consumed about 6 tons of coal."[^3]

The first tower was abandoned in 1903.[^1] The wooden lighthouse that replaced it was, in the nomination's words, "erected in 1908 to replace the lighthouse erected in 1866." The Oregon Encyclopedia says it began operating on 1 July 1909, with a fourth-order Fresnel lens "manufactured in Paris by Barbier, Benard and Turenne."[^1][^2]

The third lighthouse was built of concrete in 1934.[^1] The nomination says it "was completed as per the plans drawn the Lighthouse Bureau," and that the Coos Bay contractor Jake Hillstrom completed the building.[^1] The Oregon Encyclopedia names the contractor as R.J. Hillstrom.[^2] The nomination calls it "the only tower constructed of reinforced concrete on the Oregon coast."[^1]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster starts with Leonard C. Hall as head keeper in 1866 and 1867, and it runs into the 1960s.[^5] The nomination says Thomas Wyman "served for more than fifteen years as the first assistant at the station in the 1880s and 1890s."[^1] The archive's roster gives Wyman as first assistant from 1891 to 1906.[^5] The sources disagree on the dates.

The keepers lived with the island's hazards. In late October 1881 keeper C. F. Smart, after a visit to Empire City, went out in his small boat to reach the station, and he "was swept an estimated ninety miles north and out to sea along the coast before he was at last able to return to his station."[^1] LighthouseFriends describes a similar drift in 1881 for Thomas Brown, keeper of the life-saving station, who was blown roughly ninety miles north of his intended destination.[^4] In 1886 the Corps of Engineers decided to fence the lighthouse reserve. The nomination quotes the Coos Bay News of 25 August 1886 on the keeper: "Smart is left no discretion in the matter."[^1]

The nomination says William Denning was keeper "in the 1910s and early 1920s," and that Ralph Barker was keeper "from 1923 to 1939."[^1] The USLHS roster gives William S. Denning from 1909 to 1912, Joseph Dunson from 1912 to 1922, and Ralph D. Barker as first assistant from 1925 to 1939.[^5] LighthouseFriends says Joseph A. Harris was keeper in 1938, with Barker as his assistant.[^4] The sources disagree on those dates.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The high tides of November 1885 "destroyed the bridge between the island and the mainland," the nomination says.[^1]

The steamer Arago "wrecked on the south side of the harbor entrance on October 3, 1891," the nomination says. Among the last official life-saving activities of the men at the lighthouse was the rescue of "fifteen passengers and part of the crew."[^1] The Salem Evening Capital Journal reported the wreck on 5 October. Its latest word from Empire City was that the crew and passengers were "not yet" saved.[^6] Two days later the paper reported that the steamer had gone ashore while lying just inside the bar, and that her captain remained with the ship. The paper credited his judgment and persistence with saving the vessel.[^7]

On 4 June 1898 the tramway cable broke under a load of four people. The nomination says Thomas Wyman, his daughter, a Life-Saving Service crewman and another man were in the cage "hanging on the cable tramway" when "the cable broke, plunging the four onto the rocks." Wyman later had one leg amputated.[^1] LighthouseFriends places the accident "just over a month before" the high bridge was completed in July 1898.[^4] The Oregon Encyclopedia dates the accident to July 1898, and says the high bridge was completed "a few weeks later."[^2]

In November 1938 the crab boat New River of Umpqua "hove to in the lee of the island" in a moderate southwest gale and asked the keepers for help. LighthouseFriends says the keepers were "commended for the assistance they rendered in the emergency."[^4]

## After the keepers

The nomination gives 1939 as the end of its period of significance, the year "the U.S. Lighthouse Bureau was superseded by the United States Coast Guard."[^1] The keepers' duplex was razed in 1957.[^2] The wooden lighthouse had become the keeper's office after 1934, and it was razed in 1960.[^1]

The Coast Guard automated the light in 1966, according to the Oregon Encyclopedia.[^2] The nomination says the station was "placed on automatic beacon in the early 1960s."[^1] The two sources disagree on the date. The Coast Guard put the lens on display at its air station at North Bend in 1993.[^2] LighthouseFriends says it was later placed on display at the Coos History Museum in 2016.[^4]

The light was switched off on 1 January 2006. The Oregon Encyclopedia says it was "deactivated on January 1, 2006, after 140 years of service."[^2] LighthouseFriends says that in mid-October 2005 the Coast Guard sent out a notice of deactivation, and that when "no comments to speak of" were received, the light was switched off.[^4]

In 2007 Senator Gordon Smith submitted a provision for the transfer, as part of the Coast Guard Authorization Act for fiscal 2008.[^4] LighthouseFriends says that provision was not approved. In 2008 Representative Peter DeFazio sponsored a bill covering only the transfer, which LighthouseFriends says passed both houses in September and was signed by President George W. Bush on 8 October 2008.[^4] The Oregon Encyclopedia names President Barack Obama as the signer of a bill "transferring the Cape Arago Lighthouse to the Tribes."[^2] The two sources disagree on who signed it. The Coast Guard renovated and repainted the lighthouse and turned it over, with twenty-four acres including Chiefs Island, to the tribes on 3 August 2013.[^2] The high bridge to the island was removed to prevent unauthorized access.[^2]

## Today

The island is tribal land, and the lighthouse "is not currently open to the public."[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's record lists the station as "Open to Public: No."[^5] The nomination notes that the site is "located immediately adjacent to popular Sunset Bay State Park."[^1]
