Braddock Point Light is an octagonal red-brick tower on Bogus Point, on the south shore of Lake Ontario in Hilton, New York, just west of Braddock Bay. It was first lit on 17 August 1896 and shut down on 1 January 1954. The house is now privately owned and closed to visitors.[^1][^2][^7]

## The place

Lighthouse Friends, summarizing the Light-House Board's recommendation, says there was "no light of any importance" along about 100 miles of Lake Ontario's south shore between Thirty-Mile Point and Oswego, and that the Board wanted a light near Braddock's Point that navigators could use as a point of departure.[^1] Congress approved $20,000 for a light-station there on 3 March 1891.[^4]

The site was bought on Bogus Point, just west of Braddock Bay. Lighthouse Friends gives the price as $500 for about five and a half acres from one owner.[^1] The Board's report for 1891 to 1892 records that the deed was approved by the Attorney General and recorded in Monroe County, and that the purchase money was paid. That report also says the State of New York had not yet granted jurisdiction over the reservation.[^4] The Board's report for 1893 says jurisdiction was obtained under a general law enacted in May 1892.[^5]

## Building the light

The Board's annual report for 1893 says the boundaries were marked by stone monuments and that "the designs for the tower and dwelling are being prepared."[^5] A contract for the keeper's dwelling, tower and wood shed was approved on 25 June 1895, with completion due within nine months.[^6] Lighthouse Friends names the contractor as A. J. Serius & Son of Youngstown, New York.[^1]

The tower was modeled on the Cleveland light, which was scheduled to be torn down in 1895.[^1] When the Cleveland station was discontinued, its lantern, iron stairway, window frames and lens went to Bogus Point.[^7][^1] Lighthouse Friends says the octagonal tower was 97 feet (30 m) tall, and the Lighthouse Society record gives 93 feet (28 m) above mean high water and 86 feet (26 m) to the lantern.[^1][^2] The sources give different heights, and this story gives each with its source. The lens was a third-and-a-half-order Fresnel, and the light was first exhibited on the night of 17 August 1896.[^7][^2]

The light was changed several times. In 1899 the Board replaced the 180-degree lens with a 270-degree lens, so that "the light can be seen from all points of approach from the lake."[^8] In 1902 the main lens light was discontinued from 8 May to 30 June while the tower was repaired, and a lens-lantern light was shown from the top of the tower instead.[^1] On 16 June 1915 the illuminant was changed from oil to incandescent oil vapor, and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists the change.[^1][^9]

## The keepers

Frank T. Coleman was the first keeper. He served from 1896 until 1929.[^3] The Lighthouse Society's archive gives his birth year as 1857 and death year as 1940, and his salary went from $520 to $900.[^3] In May 1899 he fell while cleaning the lighthouse windows and broke a leg, according to Lighthouse Friends, which says a newspaper later estimated that before the light was electrified he had walked about 700 miles up and down the tower.[^1] Coleman recalled his first inspector as Captain Gridley of the Navy, "a real man for you."[^1]

Michael A. Fitzpatrick kept the light from 1929 to 1935, after transferring in from Fair Haven.[^2][^1] Claude Jacox was keeper from 1935 to 1947. Lighthouse Friends says he ran a jewelry business from the keeper's house in the early 1940s and opened a shop in Hilton after he retired.[^1][^2] Robert Bruce Millar, a Coast Guardsman, was the last keeper, from 1947 to 1950.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources cited here describe no individual wreck or rescue at this station. The Life-Saving Service's annual reports list a place called "Braddocks Point" in their Lake Ontario table in each year from 1892 to 1900, with figures for each year. The OCR scrambles the table columns, so this story gives no counts, and does not say whether the place was a station or a stranding location.[^10]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the light was automated near the end of Jacox's time, and the Coast Guard deactivated it on 1 January 1954.[^1][^2] The Coast Guard replaced it with a fifty-foot (15 m) skeletal steel tower, and the upper half of the brick tower was removed because of structural damage.[^1]

After Millar left in 1950 the empty house was damaged by trespassers.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the dwelling, the thirty-foot (9 m) tower, about 4.7 acres and the carriage house were sold in April 1957 for $14,500 to a private couple, who began a full-scale restoration.[^1] The tower was rebuilt to 65 feet (20 m) in 1995, and Lighthouse Friends says it was relit on 28 February 1996 with the Coast Guard's approval.[^1]

From 2010 to 2020 the keeper's house was run as a bed and breakfast. Lighthouse Friends says it became private again when that ended, and that the property went back on the market in 2025 at an asking price of $1.495 million.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is privately owned, and its grounds, dwelling and tower are closed to the public.[^1][^2] A small public park, Bogus Point Park, lies just west of the lighthouse. Lighthouse Friends says its grassy area offers decent pictures of the lighthouse.[^1] The original lens was removed, and the Lighthouse Society record gives the current optic as "REMOVED."[^2]
