Saugerties Light stands on a granite crib in the Hudson River, where Esopus Creek runs into the river, in Ulster County, New York.[^1][^2] The brick tower that stands there dates from 1869, when it replaced the wooden lighthouse on the pier.[^1][^2] One of its keepers was a woman, Katie Crowley, who was made keeper in 1873. She often went out alone in a skiff, and LighthouseFriends credits her with rescues in the 1870s.[^1][^2] The light was automated in 1954.[^1] Today the lighthouse is a museum and a bed-and-breakfast, run by a conservancy that bought it for $1 in 1986.[^1][^3]

## The place

The name Saugerties comes from a Dutch phrase meaning a sawmill on a creek. The mill on Esopus Creek became the cornerstone of a paper industry.[^1] On June 30, 1834, Congress appropriated $5,000 "for the construction of a lighthouse at the mouth of the creek," to guide mariners past nearby shallows into Esopus Creek and the port.[^1]

## Building the light

Charles Hooster built the first lighthouse on a timber-framed pier 40 by 50 feet (12 by 15 m). Its light, from five whale-oil lamps in parabolic reflectors, "commenced operation in 1835 with Abraham Persons as its first keeper," LighthouseFriends says.[^1] The Digital Archive gives 1836 as the year the station was established.[^2] A fire in 1848 destroyed the first lighthouse, and it was rebuilt on the old site by 1850. A sixth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps and reflectors in 1854.[^1]

Ice floes and tidal currents took their toll on the pier. On March 2, 1867, Congress appropriated $25,000 for a replacement a few yards closer to shore, built on a "circular granite crib."[^1] The Digital Archive gives 1869 as the year the tower was established, with keeper's quarters in an Italianate brick style of the same year.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes the two-story building's walls as twenty inches (51 cm) thick, and the old wooden lighthouse was sold.[^1]

The Light-House Board reported in 1899 that the boathouse was moved from the rear to the front of the pier, and that a new fence and two gates were built.[^4] LighthouseFriends says that around the turn of the century the boathouse, which had stood on the foundation of the first lighthouse, was moved to the circular pier.[^1][^1] In 1888 jetties were built to enlarge the harbor, and a small road was built along the north jetty to the lighthouse. In 1910 a wooden platform was extended from the top of the tower to support a fog bell.[^1]

## The keepers

The Digital Archive roster has twenty-one lines for principal keepers from 1835 to 1954. Abraham Persons was first, from 1835 to 1838.[^2] The roster also lists Dorcas Schoonmaker as principal keeper from 1846 to 1849, and LighthouseFriends names her in its list of head keepers with no further detail.[^2][^1]

Dennis Crowley was removed from service after three months in 1865. His son Daniel took charge, and was keeper when the brick tower was built.[^1] Daniel's sister Katie "grew up at the lighthouse" and "was made the official keeper of the light in 1873," according to LighthouseFriends.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says her "lack of fear of the water" led to rescues, and she is listed on the roster from 1873 to 1885.[^1][^2]

When a reporter asked about her rescues, she gave a short summary, which LighthouseFriends prints: "We are simply two girls trying to do our duty here in this quiet place, taking care as best we can of our blind father and aged mother." Her father, Dennis, was seventy-five at the time and had lost his vision to cataracts.[^1]

Her nephew James Crowley replaced her in 1885 and "became the fourth member of the family to be in charge of the light."[^1][^2] The roster then lists Theodore DeShong, William F. Aichele and Julius B. Young before Conrad Hawk.[^2]

Conrad Hawk's term was the longest on the roster, from 1914 to 1940. LighthouseFriends says it "was far longer than that of any other keeper of Saugerties Lighthouse."[^1][^2] He won the efficiency flag for 1920, awarded to the light station with the highest general efficiency in its district.[^5][^6] In 1916 the keeper's son and daughter brought a small battery into contact with the large battery for the fog bell; LighthouseFriends says the short circuit that followed caused an explosion that injured both children. Hawk died on January 8, 1940.[^1]

The last keeper on the roster was Edward T. Pastorini, from 1948 to 1954.[^2] In February 1954, LighthouseFriends says, he was told "his light would be automated come spring."[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1875 "a young couple fell into the river while attempting to cross on the ice." Katie Crowley rigged her boat with runners and reached them. She "pulled the limp and lifeless body out on the ice," and the woman "eventually regained consciousness," LighthouseFriends reports.[^1]

On another occasion, a sloop loaded with bluestone overturned near the lighthouse during a storm. Katie and her sister Ellen launched the station's boat and set off to help. LighthouseFriends quotes a river captain's account, which it says was printed in 1878. The captain wrote that "it seemed impossible for the women ever to reach the men."[^1] The date of this rescue is not given beyond that printing.

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the crew of the tender "tore out the plumbing, furnace, and fixtures" when the light was automated, and the building was sealed up and left to deteriorate.[^1] The Digital Archive gives the discontinued period as 1954 to 1990.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that a decade later the Coast Guard "planned to demolish the vandalized and dilapidated lighthouse." Ruth Reynolds Glunt, whose husband had been a Coast Guard light attendant, "mounted a campaign to halt the demolition."[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the lighthouse's National Register listing to 1978.[^1]

The Saugerties Lighthouse Conservancy was established in 1985 and bought the lighthouse and surrounding wetlands in 1986 for $1. LighthouseFriends says "roughly 10,000 old bricks" were replaced, and the lantern room was removed and refurbished.[^1] A solar-powered light installed by the Coast Guard "was activated on August 4, 1990."[^1][^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by the Saugerties Lighthouse Conservancy, and LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open, with the dwelling and tower open during tours and to overnight guests.[^1] The conservancy offers tours of the interior and tower on summer Sundays, June through August.[^3] LighthouseFriends gives Sundays from Memorial Day through Labor Day, from noon to 3 p.m., or by appointment.[^1] Overnight guests can "sleep in a river-view room."[^3]

The walk to the lighthouse is a half-mile trail through the Ruth Reynolds Glunt Nature Preserve, and LighthouseFriends warns that the trail floods at high tide. The parking lot is just beyond the Coast Guard station.[^1] The museum, on the second floor, shows the sixth-order Fresnel lens that the light used. LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard took back a fourth-order lens that had been on display and replaced it with that one.[^1]
