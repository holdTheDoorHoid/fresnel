A red-brick duplex with a square tower rising from its lake side, Big Bay Point Light was built in 1896 on a bluff of Lake Superior in Marquette County, Michigan, to fill a gap in the south shore's lights of 15 to 18 miles in each direction.[^1] It is best remembered for its first keeper, who walked into the woods in June 1901 and was not found for seventeen months.[^9]

## The place

The Light-House Board described the point as a projection of the coast about halfway between Marquette and Portage Entry, with anchorage on either side and a bend where coasting steamers changed course.[^1] Its case for a light was the traffic: the Lake Superior passenger steamers running between Duluth, Buffalo and Chicago, which also carried freight and called at the south-shore ports, including Marquette and the copper ports on Portage Lake. Granite Island and Huron Islands, the nearest lights either way, could not see each other, and the Board wrote that "Quite a number of vessels have in past years been wrecked on Big Bay Point."[^1]

## Building the light

Congress authorised a light and fog signal here, at a cost not to exceed $25,000, by the act of 15 February 1893. The Board's report of that year noted that no money had yet been appropriated.[^2] The $25,000 came in the act of 18 August 1894; an offer to sell a site was accepted, and the Board asked for the title papers.[^3] The Lighthouse Friends history says about five acres were acquired in 1895 and that building started in May 1896, beginning with a landing crib and a barn that housed the work crew.[^8]

The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1896 describes the first work on the site. The crew built camp buildings and a crib landing, laid 600 feet (183 m) of tramway track and cut a road through woodland, about 2,500 feet (760 m) in all, diagonally uphill to the tower site.[^4] The next year's report says the brickwork of the tower was finished in July 1896, and that in September the boilers and engines went into the fog-signal building.[^5] It says the light was shown on the night of 20 October 1896.[^5] Its own table of new lights and fog signals gives 22 October for both the third-order light and the 10-inch steam whistle, while its fog-signal table says the whistle was established on 20 October, so the report does not settle the exact night.[^5]

The station was a two-story brick duplex dwelling, the head keeper's family on one side and the assistant's on the other, with the tower on the lake side and a separate brick fog-signal building.[^8] Sources disagree on how high the light stood. Lighthouse Friends gives a tower and lantern of nearly 64 feet (19.5 m) and a focal plane of 89 feet (27 m); Terry Pepper's page gives 105 feet (32 m) above the lake; the US Lighthouse Society archive gives 108 feet (33 m).[^8][^7][^10] Lighthouse Friends says the lens was a third-order Fresnel made by Henry-Lepaute, with panels that produced a flash every twenty seconds, and that its revolving weights had to be wound every five hours.[^8] In 1899 the Board reported a hoisting engine installed and a tramway built from the landing pier up the bank.[^6]

## The keepers

William H. Prior, transferred from Stannard Rock, wrote in the log on 15 August 1896 that he had arrived at the station the night before.[^9] He had trouble with his assistants. After walking to Marquette in November 1897 for his sister's funeral, he returned and wrote that "I can not see that the assistant has done any work around the station since I left." The commentary on the transcription names that assistant as Heater and says he was moved to Granite Island, though the transcribed log gives the letter of transfer both after the November entry and on 7 March 1898.[^9] In February of that year Prior noted that Heater had walked 33 miles in 12 hours, "pretty good gait for a lame man."[^9]

When George Beamer left in May 1898 to volunteer in the war, his wife Jennie Beamer was appointed acting assistant until August.[^9] The archive lists her as a temporary keeper for 1898.[^10] Prior then hired his son George E. Prior as a laborer, and George became the assistant in January 1900.[^9]

In April 1901 the log records that George was hurt falling on the steps of the crib and had to stay in hospital at Marquette. On 13 June the keeper was called to Marquette to bury him.[^9] Prior vanished into the woods on 28 June, and a long search found nothing.[^9] His family left the station on 22 October 1901.[^9] On 14 November 1902 the log records a hunter who found a skeleton "hanging in a tree" a mile and a half south of the station, and that the clothing matched the missing keeper's.[^9] The Mining Journal of 27 November 1902, as transcribed on Pepper's page, calls him "Harry W. Prior," where the archive has William H.[^9][^10] James H. Bergan became head keeper in 1901 and served until 1915.[^10]

The archive's roster lists seven head keepers between 1896 and 1941, and dozens of assistants, many of whom stayed a year or less.[^10] Lighthouse Friends says a second assistant was added in 1903 as the country moved toward the eight-hour day, with a frame dwelling built near the fog signal at the foot of the hill.[^8] Pepper calls that post third assistant, but the archive roster shows second assistants from 1903.[^7][^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The station's steam whistle ran 284 hours in the Board's reports for the fiscal years ended 30 June 1899, 176 hours in 1900 and 326 hours in 1901, using about 6 tons of coal in 1900 and about 17 tons in 1901.[^11][^12][^13] Pepper says the steam whistles were replaced by diaphones in 1928.[^7]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists Big Bay Point among the stations damaged in the Great Lakes storm of 8 to 10 November 1913, at about $4,000 for a group of stations that also included Whitefish Point and Fort Gratiot.[^14] No source consulted says what was damaged here.

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the station was electrified and the fog signal discontinued in 1940, and that the last keeper left in 1941, after which the light was unwatched.[^8] Pepper puts the automation in 1941.[^7] The assistant's half of the house was rented out, and in 1951 and 1952 the army leased the land for anti-aircraft training.[^7][^8] Both sources say a soldier stationed there committed the 1952 murder at the Lumberjack Tavern in Big Bay on which the book and film *Anatomy of a Murder* are based.[^7][^8]

The government sold the decommissioned light and 33 acres in 1961 to a Chicago surgeon, Dr. Pick, for $40,000. The roof was mostly gone, the windows broken and the plaster falling.[^8] Pick spent most of his years of ownership (Lighthouse Friends says seventeen) repairing it into a summer home, and it passed in 1979 to Dan Hitchens, who ran it as an executive retreat.[^7][^8] Norman "Buck" Gotschall and partners opened it as a bed-and-breakfast in 1986. He restored the fog-signal building and brought the third-order lens back from a Traverse City hotel to display in it.[^7][^8] John Gale and Linda and Jeff Gamble bought it in 1992 and ran it as an inn, Lighthouse Friends says.[^8]

## Today

The Big Bay Point Light Station is privately owned and operates as a bed-and-breakfast. The inn's own page says the light is an active aid to navigation monitored by the Coast Guard, a 1940s rotating beacon with an emergency backup. Since 2023 the public may enter the property only as guests or on a paid, guided tour (by appointment, $10 a person, five people at a time) and the owners ask people not to trespass on the grounds or the private drive.[^15] Lighthouse Friends reports the present owner bought it in 2018.[^8]
