Huron Harbor Light stands at the end of a long stone pier on Lake Erie, where the Huron River reaches the lake in Erie County, Ohio. Pier lights have marked this harbor mouth since 1835, and the present steel tower at the end of the west pier was commissioned in 1936.

## The place

Huron grew up at the mouth of the Huron River, one of the earliest settled river ports on the Ohio shore of the lake. By 1824 it had a boarding house and several log cabins, and shipbuilders soon followed.[^1] As commerce increased, so did the need for a navigational aid, and the first Huron Harbor lighthouse was built in 1835 on the west pier.[^1]

In 1838 Lieutenant Charles T. Platt of the U.S. Navy described the harbor as he found it. In his account the pier ran 900 feet (274 m) into the lake and the entrance between the piers was 175 feet (53 m) wide. Only 11 feet (3.4 m) of water lay between the piers, although the river held 14 to 18 feet for more than six miles upstream.[^1] (This is Platt's description as quoted by Lighthouse Friends; the report itself was not read.) The light then burned eight lamps with as many reflectors, and Platt estimated that a keeper's dwelling with outbuildings and a lot would cost $1,950.[^1]

In 1915 the Commissioner of Lighthouses described the harbor's commerce as mainly ore and fish coming in and coal going out, and reported over 300 vessels, with a registered tonnage of over 640,000, entering it every year.[^8]

## Building the light

The first tower, a wooden pyramid, did not last. A violent storm in May 1854 destroyed it, and an iron tower, 27 feet (8 m) high, replaced it three years later, fitted with a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^1] Its lower part was open, with a circular stair rising to the watchroom and lantern, and an elevated walkway let the keeper reach it when waves swept the pier.[^1] An earlier keeper's house had been washed away. Congress voted money for a new dwelling on 3 March 1871, and it was occupied on 1 February 1873 after delays over the title to the lot and a sick construction crew.[^1]

The pier was lengthened in the early 1890s. In June 1892 the Light-House Board moved the iron beacon about 120 feet (37 m) northeast, to within 20 feet (6 m) of the new end of the west pier, and gave it a new timber foundation.[^3] The elevated walk needed constant work: the annual reports record 400 running feet rebuilt in fiscal 1894, about 200 feet in fiscal 1896-97 and 110 feet in fiscal 1899.[^4][^5][^6] The Board also noted that the appearance of the tower was changed on 20 October 1900.[^7] Lighthouse Friends adds that the lower part was then covered with sheathing and metal shingles and painted white.[^1]

In his 1915 report (and again in 1916) the Commissioner asked for $4,500 to establish aids to navigation at Huron, explaining that a rear range light to work with the existing light would meet the purpose of an act of 17 June 1910, which had authorised range lights at a cost not to exceed $3,800 but had received no appropriation. He also wanted a fog bell at the front light.[^8] The money came on 12 June 1917.[^9][^1] According to Lighthouse Friends the rear light, a skeleton tower on a bend in the pier about 400 yards from the front light, began work on 29 August 1919, and a fog bell was established on 9 April 1923.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1919 lists a Huron Harbor light among the lights established, described as fixed white, electric incandescent, which does not match Lighthouse Friends' fixed red rear light, so it is unclear which structure that entry means.[^10] The Bulletin's 1920 summary mentions a light tower at Huron Harbor.[^11] In 1928, Lighthouse Friends says, electricity became the illuminant of the front light and an air diaphone replaced the fog bell, sounding two blasts every thirty seconds.[^1]

In May 1934 work began on a 1,200-foot (366 m) rubble-mound extension of the west pier, finished the next year, with a 50-foot-square crib at its end to carry a new steel lighthouse.[^1] The Art Deco tower, fabricated by F. P. Dillon and W. G. Will, rises 72 feet (22 m) over a fog-signal building, and was put in commission on 8 April 1936, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's archive also dates the tower and its fog-signal building to 1936.[^2]

## The keepers

Huron had many keepers, most for a year or two.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's archive, drawing on the federal keeper registers, lists Morris Jackson as the first principal keeper, from 1835 to 1837.[^2]

One early dismissal drew a crowd. Emanuel Fisher, described as a one-armed man, took over in 1843 and was removed in early 1844 by the Secretary of the Treasury after a local petition. In reply, a list of 300 names went to Washington saying the light was in excellent order, and an inspector wrote that Fisher had made a great reformation despite his one arm. Fisher, defending himself, said he found the dwelling run down, and Alexander P. Chesley was appointed in his place.[^1] An earlier keeper, Joseph Barnes, complained of the townspeople's meddling but added that "the sailors are satisfied as to the lights."[^1]

Richard L. Mansell held the post longest. The archive gives his years as 1870 to 1909, a span of almost forty years.[^2] Lighthouse Friends calls him a Civil War veteran.[^1] It also quotes the Sandusky Daily Register of 1890 on a gale in which Mansell went out along the narrow walk to light the lamp: a wave washed over the end of the pier just as he reached the iron stairway, and its spray rose over the lighthouse.[^12] His successors were Joseph F. Crawford (1910-1923), Richard Tonge (1923-1933) and Daniel D. Hill (1933-1942).[^2] The Bulletin index for 1918 to 1923 has an entry under Crawford's name, "Huron light station, efficiency flag awarded", though the entry does not say which Huron.[^13]

Later head keepers included Robert E. Jones (1947-1954) and Robert J. Siggens, whose term the archive dates 1955-1961 and Lighthouse Friends 1954-1961.[^2][^1] The archive also lists two assistants, Orville W. Bauman (1939-1941) and John E. Brophy (1944-1950), and a run of Coast Guard men from 1958 to at least 1971.[^2]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1972, according to Lighthouse Friends. The lantern room was removed and a solar-powered 375 mm lens installed.[^1] Around 2015 the Coast Guard converted the light to solar power and removed the power cable, which switched off four lamps that had lit the tower's exterior. After nearly two years of talks, the City of Huron got leave to relight the tower, and in 2019 four LED lights were mounted around its base.[^1]

## Today

The light is listed on the National Register of Historic Places (reference 91000971, published 19 December 1991) as part of the Light Stations of Ohio multiple listing.[^14] The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists the station as not open to the public, and records it as an active aid to navigation.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the breakwater is often used as a fishing pier, and the old square foundation of the pierhead light is still visible on it.[^1] The light is now red and flashes in a six-second cycle.[^1]
