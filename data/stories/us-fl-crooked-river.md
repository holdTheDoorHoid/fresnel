An iron skeleton tower beside U.S. Highway 98, a little over a mile west of Carrabelle Beach in Franklin County, Florida, the Crooked River Light was first lit on 28 October 1895.[^2][^11] Paired with a front light, it marked the channel between St. George Island and Dog Island.[^11] It was the rear light of a pair; the Lighthouse Board listed it as the Crooked River Range.[^3][^4]

## The place

The Lighthouse Board's reason for the light was timber. The National Register nomination says that growing lumber shipments from the Apalachicola River to Crooked River, southwest of Carrabelle, led the Board in 1888 to ask for a light on the mainland to replace the Dog Island Lighthouse, which had been destroyed in 1873.[^2] The same nomination notes that the river entrance had 18 feet (5.5 m) of water on the bar, and that the Board expected a fairly large port to grow there.[^2] By the Board's 1894 report Carrabelle was "a town of about 1,500 inhabitants" whose business was lumber and canning oysters, newly the end of a railroad from Tallahassee.[^5]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $40,000 on 31 July 1888 for a light-house "on the mainland to the westward of Crooked River."[^6] The National Register nomination dates the authorization to 1889; the Board's own reports give 1888.[^2][^6] Getting the land took years. A deed for about 12 acres was sent to the United States attorney in September 1889 and judged insufficient, so condemnation proceedings began; in July 1891 the court-house burned with all the papers, which were duplicated, and the Attorney General approved the title on 22 August 1892.[^6][^7] Locating the section lines and taking borings of the foundation soil held things up further.[^6][^7] Work began under contract in January 1895.[^8]

The contractors finished on 12 August 1895 and the Board reported the light as a fourth-order lens showing two flashes every 10 seconds, 115 feet 2 inches (35 m) above mean high water.[^9] It was first exhibited on 28 October 1895, together with a lens-lantern light for the front of the range.[^9][^10] Lighthouse Friends adds that the lens was made in 1894 by the Paris firm Henry-Lepaute and that the front light stood 645 feet (197 m) toward the shore.[^11] The tower is variously given as 100 feet (National Register nomination) and 103 feet (31 m; the station's own museum).[^2][^12] An oil house followed, completed on 21 February 1896.[^9]

The first keeper, James A. Williams, wrote in the log of the first night: "Everything worked well, weather was clear and fine, Keeper stood watch to 12 p.m. Light was good throughout the watch."[^11]

In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 the Board built a 414-foot (126 m) cypress wharf with a T-head, rebuilt the boathouse, repaired the washhouse and laid plank walks across the grounds.[^13] The fiscal-year 1900 report records that a storm had destroyed the boathouse and knocked down the wharf, both rebuilt that year, the wharf now 417 feet long.[^14]

## The keepers

James A. Williams, the first head keeper, served until 1905, and his son Albert A. Williams was first assistant from 1898 and head keeper from 1906 to 1912.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says James Williams was sixty-seven when he came and had served almost twenty years at Cape St. George, and that several of his sons also became keepers, one of them, Charles H. Williams, assisting his brother at Crooked River from 1906 to 1909.[^11]

On 5 June 1896 Williams wrote a note into the station's watch book for his assistant, Tip Warren: he asked for an open passage at the steps of Warren's house, so that he could reach the assistant quickly at night, and he entered it in the book so that, as he put it, the blame would go where it belonged.[^11] Thorwald Hansen came first as an assistant in 1911 and resigned after just over a month, writing that he did not like the station or the keeper.[^11] He returned in 1921 as head keeper and stayed until 1945, the longest head-keeper tenure on the roster.[^1][^11]

The archive lists keepers into the Coast Guard period, among them the head keepers William A. Davis, Peter DeBoer and James L. Pippin in the late 1940s and early 1950s.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Service's bulletin noted that on 30 March 1919 Keeper Milton E. Wheelock helped two men, three women and three children in a disabled launch, towing them to the light station and giving them food and lodging.[^15] On 21 July 1921 Keeper T. Hansen and Assistant Keeper U. M. Gunn were commended for helping two men in an open launch that had been disabled in a squall about a mile from the station.[^16]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends gives 1933 for electrification and 1952 for automation; a marker at the site agrees on both.[^11][^17] The two keepers' houses and the outbuildings were sold in 1964 and removed from the site.[^17] The Coast Guard removed the original lens in 1976 because mercury in its float was leaking, and fitted a modern optic that burned until the light was decommissioned in 1995.[^17] The Carrabelle Lighthouse Association formed in 1999 to restore the tower.[^17]

## Today

The tower was restored and relit on 8 December 2007 with an acrylic replica of the original lens.[^11][^18] The tower is owned by the City of Carrabelle and managed by the Carrabelle Lighthouse Association.[^11] The site has a Keeper's House Museum, a park and a gift shop, and the association says the tower can be climbed in daytime hours, weather permitting, for a fee, up 128 steps.[^12] The original lens came back from the Coast Guard's New Orleans office in early 2020 on a long-term loan and is displayed in the museum.[^18][^12] Hours change, so check the association's website before going.
