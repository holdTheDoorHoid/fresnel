Little Traverse Light stands on the end of Harbor Point, the long spit that shelters the harbor at Harbor Springs, Michigan. Its red-brick tower and attached keeper's house were first lit in September 1884. The first keeper was a woman, Elizabeth Whitney Williams, who served for twenty-nine years. A steel light replaced the brick tower in 1963, and the point is now a private, gated community.[^1][^2]

## The place

Harbor Point forms the harbor, and in 1871 the Michigan Legislature asked Congress for a light and fog bell there. The district engineer, Orlando M. Poe, wrote that the harbor was "excellent in every respect," with good anchorage and complete shelter from all winds, as Lighthouse Friends quotes his report. He recommended a fifth-order light and a 600-pound fog bell at the end of the point.[^1]

Congress took more than a decade to act. Lighthouse Friends says it finally appropriated $15,000 on 7 August 1882.[^1] The point has grown since. Lighthouse Friends says accretion built the point out until the light stood "a significant distance from the entrance to the harbor."[^1]

## Building the light

Workers and materials reached the site on 14 May 1884. By the end of June the stone foundation walls were in place. The red-brick tower, eight and a half feet (2.6 m) square, was attached to the south end of a one-and-a-half-story dwelling and was finished on 18 September.[^1]

The light was first shown on 25 September 1884. Williams, who had come over from Beaver Island, wrote: "The light-house just finished, the lamp being lighted the first time September 25th, 1884."[^2] The lens was a fourth-order Fresnel lens in a decagonal cast-iron lantern, which Lighthouse Friends says showed a fixed red light at a focal plane of 41 feet (12.5 m) above the bay.[^1] Williams wrote that the lens "seemed to be appealing to me for care and polishing."[^3]

Two later additions are recorded in the Board's reports. A summer kitchen was added in the fiscal year ended June 30, 1894, according to the Board's report.[^4] A fog bell was set up in 1896, on 1 June: the Board's report describes "a square pyramidal frame structure, of two stories, resting on five brick piers."[^5] Lighthouse Friends says the bell was struck a double blow every thirty seconds when needed, and that from 26 June 1914 it gave a single blow every fifteen seconds.[^1]

## The keepers

Elizabeth Whitney Williams was born on Mackinac Island in 1844, and married Captain Clement Van Riper when she was sixteen. Lighthouse Friends says Van Riper was appointed keeper of the Beaver Island Harbor light in 1869 and drowned on 29 November 1872 while trying to help a ship in distress.[^1] Williams wrote that he was lost with a companion, and that her own appointment followed "a few weeks after."[^6] She kept the Beaver Island light after his death, and kept the position after she married Daniel Williams in 1876.[^1]

She asked for a mainland post. Her memoir says she made "the request to be changed to a mainland light station," and she was transferred to Little Traverse.[^2] From then on, her official title was "Mrs. Daniel Williams."[^2] The USLHS roster gives her as principal keeper from 1884 to 1913.[^7] She left the station on 1 November 1913, to live in Charlevoix.[^1] Her memoir, *A Child of the Sea; and Life Among the Mormons*, was published in 1905, and its dedication, to sailors, refers to "the loss of many of my people by drowning."[^8]

She wrote in general terms about the keeper's life: "Our light keepers many times live in isolated places, out on rocks and shoals far away from land and neighbors."[^9] That is her own view, not a record of the station's routine.

Her successor was Alfred Curtis Erickson, who had been head keeper at Chicago Harbor and Calumet Harbor.[^1] The roster gives him as principal keeper from 1913 to 1942.[^7] The Commissioner's list for 1916 names "A. C. Erickson, keeper, Little Traverse Light Station, Mich."[^10] Lighthouse Friends says he died in November 1942, and that he built model yachts. Every boat in the 1926 model-yacht races competing for the A.C. Erickson trophy had been built by him.[^1]

After Erickson, the roster lists Angus H. Phillips (1942 to 1946), Ray H. Buttars (1946 to 1948), Henry J. Rocheleau (1949 to 1954, after a first-assistant term from at least 1946), Herman N. Barr (1954 to 1955) and Frank A. Wollack (1956 to 1962).[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Service's index to its 1912 to 1917 bulletins lists the keeper of the Little Traverse light station as having towed a disabled launch to safety.[^11] In September 1917 the bulletin for that year reports that Erickson "rescued a woman who fell out of a" boat near the station.[^12] Lighthouse Friends dates a rescue of a woman from a boat to 1918, and does not say whether it is the same event.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends says that in 1923 Erickson picked up six men from the launch *Evelyn*, which had caught fire, and then helped put out the blaze.[^1] No other record of the fire is cited here.

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard held a public hearing on 28 July 1959 "to receive feedback on the plan to discontinue Little Traverse Lighthouse," and to set up an automated light on a skeletal tower near the end of the point.[^1] The property was exchanged with the Harbor Point Association for a fifty-foot-square (15 m) parcel, and the association paid the difference in cash.[^1]

The steel light went up in 1963. Rowlett gives it as a 72-foot (22 m) square steel tower with a green flash every six seconds, and a focal plane of 73 feet (22 m).[^13] Lighthouse Friends gives the tower as "a forty-one-foot-tall skeletal tower."[^1] The two sources disagree on its height. Rowlett also says the original fourth-order lens is still mounted in the old tower.[^13]

## Today

The point is a gated community, and the Harbor Point Association owns the old light and runs the site.[^13][^1] Lighthouse Friends says the best public views of the lighthouse are from the water.[^1] Rowlett says the site and tower are generally closed, with guided tours "once or twice each year" in recent years. Lighthouse Friends says the Harbor Springs Area Historical Society gives tours "roughly once every three years."[^13][^1] The sources do not agree on how often that happens. The lighthouse is private property; it is not open to casual visitors.

Williams's own account of her arrival evening is the only description of the light's first night cited here. As her steamer came into the bay in 1884 the "red light" was "flashing its rays over the waters of Little Traverse Bay for the first time," and a passenger told her, "Here is your home."[^14]
