Rondout Light stands on a concrete pier at the mouth of Rondout Creek, where the creek meets the Hudson River at Kingston, in Ulster County, New York.[^1][^3] It is the last of three lighthouses the port has had at the creek's mouth.[^2] The brick tower went into service in 1915, and it is reached only by boat.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Rondout became a busy port after the Delaware and Hudson Canal opened in 1828.[^1] The last several miles of the canal followed the creek to the Hudson, linking coal mines in northeastern Pennsylvania to the river.[^1] Steamboats, sloops, schooners and barges loaded with passengers and cargo regularly left the port for New York City.[^1]

The mouth of the creek was shallow. A government survey in 1869 found only seven feet (2.1 m) of water there, and recommended two parallel dikes run out into the Hudson to make a channel fourteen feet (4.3 m) deep.[^1] The dikes were built from 1872 to 1880 at a cost of $90,000, and three stake lights were set on them in 1880.[^1] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1900 lists the light at the end of the north dike, whose intensity was raised that year "by substituting a lantern for the post lantern."[^8]

## Building the light

The first lighthouse went up in 1838 on the south side of the creek: a wooden house with a tower, on a pier that cost nearly $5,000.[^1] Its lamps had parabolic reflectors, and by 1850 there were four of them.[^1] The USLHS record and LighthouseFriends give 1838 for the first light. The Lighthouse Directory and the Hudson River Lighthouses site give 1837.[^3][^1][^4][^5]

Congress appropriated $22,000 for a new light on July 28, 1866, and a bluestone tower, known as Rondout I, was completed in 1867.[^1] The Directory and the Hudson River Lighthouses site also give 1867.[^4][^5]

Congress acted again in 1911. The act of March 4, 1911 appropriated $40,000 for "a light and fog-signal station at or near the mouth of Rondout Creek."[^7] Work started in March 1914, and the light was put in commission on August 25, 1915.[^7][^1] The Hudson River Lighthouses site says construction began in 1913.[^5] The Commissioner of Lighthouses explained that the shoals had extended out so far that the old light was, in the report's words, "of little use to mariners."[^6]

The Commissioner's report for 1916 describes the building. It stands on a reinforced concrete pier inside a steel sheet cofferdam, resting on wooden piles, and a boring near the site showed mud overlying sand to a depth of 55 feet (17 m).[^6] The tower and two-story dwelling are faced with vitrified brick. The light is a fourth-order lens showing a fixed red light, with its focal plane 52 feet (16 m) above mean high water,[^6] visible nine miles in clear weather (the report does not say nautical or statute miles). The USLHS record gives the height above mean high water as 40.5 feet (12.3 m).[^3] The fog signal was a 1,000-pound bell operated by clockwork.[^6] The dwelling had quarters for one keeper and his family, a modern plumbing and heating plant, and a landing platform for supplies brought by tender.[^6] LighthouseFriends gives the cost as $33,575.81.[^1]

## The keepers

The Murdock family's service began in 1856, when George Murdock came to the first light with his wife Catherine and two small children.[^1] He drowned about a year later, on his way home from town with supplies.[^1] Catherine kept the light. LighthouseFriends, drawing on her later recollections, says she was told at first that the Board did not employ women, and that she was made the regular keeper before the navigation season began.[^1] The USLHS roster lists her as head keeper from 1857 to 1907, the longest service on the station's list.[^3]

Her son James B. Murdock served as assistant keeper from 1880 to 1895 on the USLHS roster.[^3] In August 1907 Catherine, then eighty, wrote to the district inspector to resign, and asked that her son be named her successor. He was.[^1][^3] James Murdock was keeper of both the 1867 light and the 1915 light, according to LighthouseFriends, and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1914 calls him keeper of the Rondout light station.[^1][^10] He retired in 1923, which ended the family's service.[^1]

After Murdock, the principal keepers changed more often. Ernest Bloom kept the light from 1923 to 1935, and Robert L. Howard from 1935 to 1945.[^3][^1] LighthouseFriends says Howard slipped and fell while walking across the ice with a few other men to see a Coast Guard icebreaker in January 1945, and died in Kingston Hospital a few weeks later.[^3][^1] Albert G. Possell, Anthony George Zuius and Herman Lange followed in turn. Lange was the last keeper, and LighthouseFriends says he locked the lighthouse up when it was automated in 1954.[^3][^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends describes a night in December 1878, when an unusually heavy snowstorm turned to rain.[^1] The Eddyville dam, upstream, burst, and its flood carried away houses, barns, barges and boats.[^1] Catherine stayed at her post and kept the light burning.[^1] The next morning she found the lighthouse had sustained little injury.[^1]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1912 records that James Murdock, keeper of Rondout light, was commended for rescuing two men from drowning.[^9] LighthouseFriends describes a larger rescue that year, of seven men from the wreck of the *Geisha* on May 12, 1912.[^1] The Bulletin dates a rescue of an exhausted swimmer, carried three miles out of his course by the tide, to October 23, 1914.[^10] LighthouseFriends dates the swimmer's rescue to 1915. The story gives the Bulletin's year.

LighthouseFriends also records a Canadian seaplane that had engine trouble and landed near the lighthouse in 1921.[^1] The next year, it says, Murdock rescued a man who had slipped off the dike into the icy creek.[^1] The Bulletin confirms the second rescue. On January 2, 1922, Murdock, by then keeper of the Rondout North Dike light, rescued from drowning a man who had slipped off the dike into the creek.[^11] The Bulletin's cumulative index lists efficiency awards for Murdock at Rondout.[^12]

## After the keepers

In 1916 the lantern and deck of the 1867 lighthouse were removed and shipped to Rhode Island, where they went onto the Bristol Ferry Lighthouse.[^1] The old building was put up for auction but never sold, because the buyer would have had to remove it from the pier.[^1] After the roof collapsed, the Coast Guard feared that children playing around the old building would be hurt, so Joseph Stephano, a local contractor, was hired to demolish it.[^4][^1] Two charges of dynamite leveled the remains on May 19, 1953, but the circular stone base can still be seen.[^1][^4]

The light was automated in 1954.[^1] Warren Spinnenweber, named by LighthouseFriends, monitored the light from land from 1954 until the 1990s.[^1] In 1984 the Hudson River Maritime Museum took a thirty-year lease from the Coast Guard, and the city and the museum restored the interior and exterior between 1984 and 1988. The interior décor is representative of the 1930s.[^1][^4] Under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000, the City of Kingston received ownership of the lighthouse on June 19, 2002.[^1][^4] The Directory records a 2019 federal extension of time for the city to spend $401,736 of Hurricane Sandy relief money on repairs.[^4]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by the City of Kingston and operated by the Hudson River Maritime Museum, which says it is still fully operational as a navigational light.[^2][^1] It is accessible only by boat. The museum runs guided tours on its solar-powered boat *Solaris* from May through October.[^2] Its options include a sightseeing cruise that passes both Rondout and Esopus Meadows lights, and a tour that goes inside Rondout.[^2] The Hudson River Lighthouses site gives a Thursday-to-Sunday schedule for tours, which differs from the museum's listing, so check the museum's cruise schedule before going.[^5][^2]

The museum says the lighthouse is not handicapped accessible.[^2] It has many stairs, a ladder to the light tower, open-grate decking, and no restrooms, so visitors should use the museum's facilities before they leave.[^2] Children under 8 are not permitted, and the museum asks for one adult for every child under 12.[^2]
