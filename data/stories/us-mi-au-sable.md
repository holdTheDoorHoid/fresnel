A white-and-black brick tower on a bluff of Lake Superior's south shore, twelve miles (19 km) west of Grand Marais, Michigan, first lit in August 1874 to mark a stretch of coast that sailors dreaded. It was called Big Sable until 1910, and it is now the centrepiece of a restored light station inside Pictured Rocks National Lakeshore.[^1][^2]

## The place

Au Sable Point sits on the south shore of Lake Superior in Alger County. The National Park Service says an offshore reef of Jacobsville Sandstone lies only a few feet below the surface in places, that thick fog builds quickly there, and that vessels bound for Marquette or Munising, or blown onto the lee shore by a north wind, were easily caught on this coast.[^2] The Mining Journal of Marquette said on 29 July 1871 that "in all navigation of Lake Superior, there is none more dreaded by the mariner than that from Whitefish Point to Grand Island."[^2] The National Register nomination adds that the coast between Munising and Grand Marais was known as the "Graveyard Coast", and that the steamer track lay two and a quarter miles (4 km) off the station.[^1]

## Building the light

According to the Park Service, the Eleventh Lighthouse District reported in 1871 that a light was needed at Au Sable Point more than at any other unprotected place in the district. Congress appropriated $40,000 in 1872, and the State of Michigan sold 326 acres (132 ha) to the federal government for $407.[^2] Work began the following year, 1873, and the nomination records the buildings finished by August 1874.[^1][^2]

The brick tower stands on rubble masonry that the nomination says runs down to bedrock; its base is 16 feet 6 inches (5.0 m) across, tapering to 12 feet 8 inches (3.9 m) at the top. The Park Service puts the tower at 86 feet (26 m) from base to ventilator ball; the nomination gives 87 feet (27 m) above ground and 107 feet (33 m) above the lake.[^1][^2] The lantern held a third-order Fresnel lens made by L. Sautter & Cie of Paris, which showed a fixed white light over 270 degrees of the horizon; the nomination says the remaining 90 degrees were blacked out, and gives its reach as seventeen and three-quarter miles, while the Park Service says 17 miles.[^1][^2] The original dwelling was attached to the tower by a passage, and the station was built to the same plan as the Outer Island light in Wisconsin's Apostle Islands.[^1][^2]

A notice printed in the *New York Herald* of 12 August 1874 told mariners that a fixed white light would be shown from the new lighthouse at "Big sable Point", about 70 miles west of Whitefish Point.[^13] The nomination and the Park Service both give 19 August 1874 as the night the lamp was first lit.[^1][^2] The nomination says the station's name changed from Big Sable to Au Sable on 10 June 1910; the Park Service says May 1910.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

The first keeper was Casper Kuhn, followed in 1876 by Napoleon Beedon; the US Lighthouse Society's archive, drawn from the keepers' registers, lists Kuhn for 1874-1876 and Beedon for 1876-1879.[^3] The Park Service draws on the keepers' journals, and quotes Beedon's entry for 8 December 1876, when a "frightful storm" blew down "50 trees or more close by the lighthouse".[^2] Mary Beedon appears as first assistant in 1876-1879; the archive does not say how she was related to the head keeper.[^3]

Frederick W. Boesler kept the light from 1879 to 1884 with his son Frederick Boesler Jr. as first assistant for part of that time.[^3] On 25 September 1883, the Park Service says, Boesler wrote of the weather "clear, blowing hard from the northwest" while the steamer *Mary Jarecki*, stranded since 4 July, broke up on the beach.[^2]

Gus Gigandet arrived on 21 May 1884 and wrote in the journal that he felt "contented and satisfied with the station".[^2] He stayed until 1896, and Mary Gigandet is listed as first assistant from 1892 to 1897; again the archive does not state a relationship.[^3] A modern history of the light reports that a thunderstorm bolt struck the tower on 24 July 1893, and that Gigandet died at the station on 29 October 1896.[^15]

The journals also record ordinary life. The Park Service says the assistant killed a bear on 4 November 1901 that took two men to drag home, that a keeper had caught 144 brook trout the previous summer, and that Boesler grafted 24 fruit trees in 1881. The nearest village, Grand Marais, lay 12 miles away, and supplies and people normally came by boat.[^2] The nomination agrees on how remote the place was: as recently as 1905 it could be reached only by boat, or by wagon road to within three miles and then a foot trail, and the sandy soil allowed no gardening.[^1]

John F. Brooks, who had served as assistant under earlier keepers, was head keeper from 1915 to 1923.[^3] The 1921 *Lighthouse Service Bulletin* lists "Au Sable, Mich." and John Brooks among the stations and keepers entitled to fly the efficiency flag that year. Separately, the Park Service has refurnished the first floor of the assistant keepers' quarters to reflect the time when a Keeper John and his wife Martha Brooks lived at Au Sable, without giving the years, so the two cannot be tied together from these sources.[^11][^2] Klaas L. Hamringa followed him, from 1923 to 1930.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In thick fog a light cannot be seen, so the station needed a sound signal as well. A steam fog signal was authorised at up to $5,500 by an act of 15 February 1893 but the Board's report for 1895 says no money had yet been appropriated.[^4] An act of 11 June 1896 finally provided $5,600 for a steam fog signal at Big Sable.[^5] The nomination dates the red-brick fog-signal building to 1897, and a modern history gives 1 September 1897 for the whistle's first use.[^1][^15] The Park Service says the first apparatus installed did not work and a replacement took another year.[^2] The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1899 records a hoisting engine installed and a turntable finished, and that the ten-inch steam whistle ran some 164 hours and burned about 15 tons of coal.[^6][^7]

The fog signal was damaged and discontinued on 15 November 1900, and re-established at the opening of navigation in 1901.[^8] The next report records material delivered to repair the fog-signal water-supply crib, among other repairs.[^9]

On the morning of 9 April 1902 the steamer *Crescent City*, towing a whaleback barge, stranded at Point au Sable; the *Minneapolis Journal* of that day carried the news from Sault Ste. Marie.[^12] The Park Service tells the story from the station's side: the keeper had gone out at 6 a.m. to light the boiler, and the steam whistle could not be sounded in the 45 minutes that raising steam took, so it was silent when the ship ran aground at 6:15.[^2]

The best-documented rescue came at the end of November 1929. According to Keeper Hamringa's report, printed in the *Lighthouse Service Bulletin*, the steamer *Kiowa* drifted six miles west of the station in a northwest gale, and her crew's lifeboat capsized, drowning five men. A Mr. Chilson, returning from a hunting trip in a small gas boat, took three men off the ship and made for the lighthouse. Hamringa sounded the whistle for an hour and then launched the station's gas boat; in the dark the rescuers took off the remaining crew in the gas boat, a rowboat and Chilson's boat, and passed 16 of them to a Coast Guard boat, which carried them to Grand Marais.[^10] The Bulletin reported that Hamringa, first assistant John R. Hamann and second assistant William Campbell were officially commended.[^10]

## After the keepers

The nomination says the old steam apparatus was replaced by a diesel-driven fog signal in 1928, and the Park Service describes a compressed-air diaphone of the same year.[^1][^2] The Park Service says the Coast Guard took over from civilian keepers in 1945, and that in 1958 it made the light automatic and unattended and discontinued the fog signal, after 84 years of continuous staffing.[^2] The archive's roster continues to 1958.[^3] The National Park Service received the station from the Coast Guard in January 1968; the Coast Guard still maintains the light.[^2]

Restoration began in earnest in 1988. The Park Service lists historic investigation and a Historic American Buildings Survey record in 1988, the black upper tower repainted in summer 1992, and the third-order lens, which had been replaced by a small acrylic lens in 1972, returned to the lantern room in 1996.[^2]

## Today

The Au Sable Light Station is owned by the National Park Service and lies inside Pictured Rocks National Lakeshore; the tower is still a navigation aid, now shown by a small solar-powered LED on the catwalk railing.[^2] It is reached on foot: a 1.5-mile (2.4 km) walk each way from the Hurricane River Campground along the old Coast Guard access road.[^14] The park offered free guided climbs from 7 June to 15 October in 2026, limited in numbers and subject to weather, and takes volunteers who live and work at the station for a month each summer.[^14] Outside the guided hours, treat the tower as closed.
