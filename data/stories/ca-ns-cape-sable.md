Cape Sable Lighthouse stands on a sandy islet just off Cape Sable Island, at the southern tip of Nova Scotia, where ships turning for the Bay of Fundy round the cape.[^1][^4] Its octagonal concrete tower is about 30 m (101 ft) tall, although the Lighthouse Directory gives 31 m. LighthouseFriends and the Lighthouse Directory both call it the tallest lighthouse in Nova Scotia.[^1][^3][^4]

## The place

Parks Canada says the cape marks the entrance to the Bay of Fundy for ships coming from the east, and that many New England vessels charting courses to Europe saw it.[^2] The lighthouse committee of the House of Assembly proposed a light for the cape in 1841, but only if New Brunswick would help pay for it.[^1] Money for a light at Cape Sable or at Baccaro Point was granted in 1850, and a light was built at Baccaro Point later that year.[^1]

A petition was read in the House of Assembly in 1856, and others followed.[^1] The wreck of the steamship Hungarian on the cape in February 1860 finally prompted the House of Assembly to act.[^1] The wreck is told below.

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says it appears New Brunswick declined to help, which left Nova Scotia to pay for the light alone.[^1] The light was erected in the summer of 1861 and first shown on 12 November that year, a fixed red light from nineteen lamps in reflectors.[^1]

LighthouseFriends quotes John H. Kendrick's 1863 report on the province's lighthouses, which called the light "a very poor one and not to be depended upon."[^1] In 1869 ruby-red chimneys went into the lamps and clear glass into the lantern room, which LighthouseFriends says let the light be seen four to five miles farther.[^1] From 1 September 1870 the light showed a flashing white light.[^1] A fog alarm was established on the cape in 1876.[^1]

On 1 November 1902 the light was changed to a third-order dioptric white light, flashing for about one-third of a second at intervals of five seconds.[^6] On 11 July 1903 the light at Baccaro Point was changed to a revolving white light, using apparatus that had been in use at Cape Sable.[^7] A new fog alarm building and its installation were under way at Cape Sable in 1906.[^8] In 1907 a steam-operated diaphone replaced the steam whistle.[^10]

The concrete tower came later. The Department's report for fiscal 1923 to 1924 lists the erection of a 26 m (86 ft) reinforced concrete tower and a 3 m (10 ft) cast-iron lantern at Cape Sable.[^11] LighthouseFriends says Boone and McDonald of St. John built the octagonal tower under a $10,800 contract, and that it was activated on 4 September 1924.[^1] The Parks Canada register describes it as a classically designed octagon in thin-shell reinforced concrete.[^2]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends names Harvey Doane as the first keeper. He died in 1871, and his son Isaac K. Doane was appointed in his place.[^1] Isaac Doane kept the light for thirty-one years, the longest term of any of the station's keepers.[^1] After him came Arthur H. Cunningham, from 1902 to 1916, and Albert Wise, from 1916 to 1927.[^1]

Benjamin F. Smith kept the light from 1931 to 1945 and again from 1952 to 1970.[^1] Two of his sons were children at the station in July 1942, when the rescue described below took place.[^1] Sidney F. Smith and then James R. Smith followed him.[^1] Francis Casey and Andy Dugandzic were among the last keepers, and the station lost its last keepers on 26 May 1986.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Hungarian was an iron-hulled passenger steamer, and she had made her first transatlantic voyage in 1859.[^1] LighthouseFriends says she left Liverpool on 8 February 1860 for Portland, Maine, under Captain Thomas Jones, and struck the cape on the night of 19 February.[^1] Residents of Cape Sable Island saw flares about three in the morning, and at daybreak they could see people clinging to her masts. The sea was too heavy for anyone to reach them.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives 205 people aboard, all of whom were lost.[^1]

A dispatch from Barrington, datelined 20 February and printed in a Michigan paper, reported that the steamer's lights had been seen at four o'clock that morning. At daylight she was found ashore half a mile from the island, and the sea was too rough for boats to reach her.[^12]

Two Halifax dispatches in the same paper disagree about where she sailed from. One says she had left Liverpool on the 8th, and the other says she sailed from Queenstown on the 9th.[^12]

A message from Cape Sable to Cunard gives the lights at three o'clock on the Monday morning. It says that at daybreak the spars and pipe were seen standing, and that at 10 a.m. all had gone by the board in a heavy sea breaking mast-high over the ship.[^12]

The keeper of the lighthouse at Barrington, writing to the Provincial Secretary of Nova Scotia, described squalls through the night and a very heavy sea during the day. He wrote that a ship like the Hungarian ought to have been fifteen miles off the land when rounding Cape Sable in that weather, and he called her loss the most mysterious wreck almost on record.[^13] He was the keeper at Barrington, not at Cape Sable. His letter was printed in the Halifax Sun and reprinted in Biddeford, Maine.[^13]

The life-boat station at Cape Sable was abandoned and moved to Clark's Harbour by 1900. The new station was given a boat and a coxswain with a crew of six.[^5]

In June 1906 the Clark's Harbour life-saving crew went to the vessel Etolia at Cape Sable. They stood by her for sixty hours and landed men from her.[^9]

On 25 July 1942 the German submarine U-89 sank the schooner Lucille M, of Lockeport, Nova Scotia.[^14][^1] LighthouseFriends, drawing on the recollections of the Smith boys, says the submarine shelled her without warning about eighty nautical miles due south of Cape Sable, and that her captain and crew got away in two dories.[^1]

On Sunday 26 July, the keeper's sons saw two boats rowing toward the station.[^1] The keeper went down to the beach and asked who the men were.[^1] They were helped up to the keeper's cottage, where the household made them a quick meal. Before they ate, the captain led a short prayer of thanks.[^1] The keeper's brother-in-law then went out to pick up the men and tow their dories to Clark's Harbour.[^1]

## After the keepers

The last keepers left on 26 May 1986.[^1] Apart from the concrete tower, the station's structures have since been removed or destroyed.[^1]

The old wooden tower was cut down to two storeys and used for storage.[^1][^4] The Lighthouse Directory says it was relocated to Yarmouth in 1991, and its fate there is unknown.[^4] LighthouseFriends says a community group had meant to use it in a Yarmouth waterfront project, but the plan came to nothing.[^1]

In the fall of 2013 a contractor spent six weeks washing, patching and coating the concrete tower.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the Friends of the Cape Sable Lighthouse raised more than $90,000 for the work, including $50,000 from the province.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory gives the campaign total as $95,000.[^4] In April 2015 Fisheries and Oceans announced a major restoration project.[^4] The work ran from late 2016 into early 2017 and included concrete repairs, rehabilitation of the lantern room, a heating system and repainting.[^4][^1]

In 2018 the Fresnel lens was found in boxes in a Coast Guard building in Saint John, New Brunswick, which was being prepared for demolition.[^1][^4] The clockwork and the fog horn trumpet were found with it.[^1] The lens went to the Maritime Museum of the Atlantic in Halifax for restoration.[^1][^4] The Friends plan a lantern room on a concrete base at the end of Fish Plant Road on Cape Sable Island to hold it.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the station. The grounds are open, and the tower is closed.[^1][^4] The Coast Guard list gives the light as a white flash every five seconds, 29.7 m above the water, with a nominal range of 18 nautical miles. The tower is white with a red top. The fog horn gives a four-second blast with 56 seconds of silence.[^3]

The best views come from the water, LighthouseFriends says. From land, a distant view is had from the end of Hawk Point Road.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory places a view of the light from the beach at the end of Hawk Road, off NS 330.[^4] Sea-kayak tours pass the lighthouse.[^1]
