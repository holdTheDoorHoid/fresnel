Two Rivers Light was a wooden pierhead lighthouse at the end of the north pier at the mouth of the Twin Rivers, in Two Rivers, Wisconsin, on the shore of Lake Michigan.[^1] It was first lit in 1886 and was deactivated in 1969.[^1] Since 1975 its tower has stood on the grounds of the Rogers Street Fishing Village museum, where visitors can climb it.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The city takes its name from the East and West Twin Rivers, which run together just before they reach the lake.[^1] In 1870 Army engineers reported that using the river as a harbor would require widening and deepening its mouth and extending piers into the lake.[^1] The town then had two tanneries, a chair factory, two sawmills and a tub factory, and leather was the most lucrative export through the private piers.[^1] The engineers estimated $265,589 for extending parallel piers and dredging a channel.[^1] Work on the piers began in 1871.[^1] By 1878 they had been extended 1,200 feet (366 m) at a cost of $110,000.[^1] Most of that length was piles, and sand sifted through and filled the dredged channel until a sand-tight lining was fitted in 1879.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $3,500 in 1850 for a lighthouse at the mouth of the Twin Rivers.[^1] Work began in 1852 on a one-and-a-half-story brick dwelling with an octagonal lantern on top, after a deed for the land was obtained that April.[^1] Its fixed white light came from a single lamp and a fourteen-inch reflector, with a focal plane 52 feet (16 m) above the lake.[^1] By 1858 it had a sixth-order Fresnel lens.[^1] In 1859 Congress allowed the Lighthouse Board to discontinue lights that had become useless, and this one was discontinued that August.[^1] Albert Barry, who had kept it for most of its life, lost his post.[^1]

A pierhead light was authorized for Two Rivers in 1879, but no contract was let until June 22, 1886.[^1] A Manitowoc builder named Boehmer put it up at the end of the north pier, linked to shore by 1,315 feet (401 m) of elevated walkway.[^1] It was finished on October 14, 1886, and first lit on the night of October 25 by Keeper Antony Gauthier.[^1] The tower was square and pyramidal, 35 feet (10.7 m) from base to ventilator ball.[^1] Its lower part was open framework, and its upper part held an enclosed watchroom, reached from the walkway, with a trap door up into the ten-sided lantern.[^1] The lantern showed a red light.[^1]

The Board's reports of the 1890s also list a light at Twin River Point.[^5] In its report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1895, the Board recorded that metal work to modify an old Chicago River light tower for erection there had been completed, and that the foundations for eight columns were finished.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the 1852 light was discontinued in 1859 and does not mention these later reports. The sources read for this story do not show how the two accounts fit together.[^1][^5]

The pierhead had no dwelling.[^1] The Lighthouse Board noted in 1903 that there was none, and Congress funded one in 1907.[^1] The Board bought an existing house and enlarged it into a duplex for two keepers, finished in September 1908.[^1] An oil house followed in 1909.[^1]

## The keepers

The keepers of the 1852 light are listed by the U.S. Lighthouse Society under the pierhead's name.[^4] George D. Ruggles served in 1853, Albert Barry in 1853 and 1854, David Ward in 1854, and Barry again from 1854 to 1859.[^4] Antony Gauthier kept the pierhead light from 1886 to 1915, and Otto H. Schmiling from 1915 to 1941.[^4]

The keeper did not live in the tower.[^2] The watch quarters had a stove, a cot, a chamber pot, and a chair and table for long hours on watch.[^2] The catwalk from shore was often covered with snow, and in wind the tower could sway.[^2]

In 1909 a 1,500-pound fog bell was mounted on the seaward side of the tower, and a striking apparatus wound by a small motor was placed in the watchroom.[^1] Sanford Scott was hired that year as the first assistant keeper to help run it.[^1][^4]

In its report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1920, the Commissioner of Lighthouses asked for money to improve the fog signal.[^6] The report said that craft operating out of Two Rivers "found the bell of little assistance in making the port during fog," and that it is necessary "to maintain two keepers to operate the bell."[^6]

LighthouseFriends dates the $7,400 request to 1919, but the Commissioner's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1919 estimated $6,000 for the work.[^1][^7] The $7,400 estimate first appears in the 1920 report, and again in the 1921 and 1922 reports.[^6][^8]

A diaphone fog signal was installed in 1928, and the light was changed to electric illumination.[^1]

In 1935 a local newspaper reported that assistant keeper Henry Wierzbach had broken his toe.[^1] A manhole cover that rattled under passing cars kept him awake, so he got out of bed and kicked it.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes the item.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In November 1886, a vessel was driven into the pier.[^1] The light and its walkway were damaged, and repairs were made at once.[^1] In 1928 wave action in a storm damaged the tower's north side, which is thirty feet (9 m) high.[^2] It was repaired.[^2]

## After the keepers

The light was deactivated in 1969 and replaced by a modern beacon.[^1][^4] In 1975 the upper part of the wooden tower was moved onto the grounds of what is now the Rogers Street Fishing Village.[^1] A wide wooden walkway was built around it in 1998.[^1]

The lens was broken in the move.[^1][^2] Restoration began in 2005 under the museum's then executive director.[^2] The restorers spent more than a year researching what the lens had looked like, reshaping its bent metal, and making the pieces that had been broken or lost, with help from a Florida firm, Lighthouse Lamp Shop, Inc.[^1][^2] The restored lens was unveiled on May 28, 2009.[^1][^2]

## Today

The tower stands at the corner of Rogers Street and 21st Street, on museum grounds on the banks of the East Twin River.[^2][^3] The museum says visitors can climb up to the tower for a view of the fishing village, and that they can see the restored lens that was housed in the lighthouse.[^3] Visitors can also step aboard the 1936 wooden fishing tug Buddy O.[^3] The museum also offers to flash the light on a chosen date in memory or honor of a loved one, and says the proceeds are used to maintain the lighthouse.[^3] It says its complex is on the National Register of Historic Places.[^3]
