Highland Light stands on the clay bluffs of North Truro, on the ocean side of Cape Cod, where the first lighthouse on the Cape was lit in the 1790s. The tower there now is the third on the spot, and in 1996 the whole station, tower and keeper's house together, was jacked up and moved 450 feet (137 m) inland to keep it from falling into the sea.[^1][^2]

## The place

The shore below the bluff had a bad name long before there was a light. In 1794 the Rev. James Freeman, describing Truro for the Massachusetts Historical Society, wrote that "More vessels are cast away here than any other part of the county of Barnstable," and that a lighthouse near the Clay Pounds might prevent many such accidents.[^2] The sand bars off this coast shifted and stretched as far as a mile from the beach, and the nomination for the station's National Register listing adds that nobody can say how many ships were lost off the Cape, though most estimates run into the thousands.[^2]

The push for a light came from Boston. In 1792 the Humane Society of the Commonwealth of Massachusetts and the Boston Marine Society petitioned Governor John Hancock to ask Congress for a light on Cape Cod, and in February 1796 the Marine Society, backed by the Boston Chamber of Commerce, petitioned Congress directly. Congress approved money on 17 May 1796.[^2] The Boston collector of customs, Benjamin Lincoln, toured the Cape with Boston "marine gentlemen" and chose the Clay Pounds because the land was high, the shore was bold, and a ship a league off could steer for Boston or for the Nantucket shoals without danger.[^2] On 6 August 1796 he bought a ten-acre square of oceanfront from a local farmer, Isaac Small, for $110. Part of the bargain was that Small would be the first keeper: his farm could feed him, so the government could pay a lower salary.[^2] (The 1987 form's chronology names the seller as Frances Small.)[^1]

Thoreau, who visited the Cape more than once, measured the bank opposite the light with a home-made quadrant and put it at 110 feet (34 m) above its base, about 123 feet (37 m) above mean low water.[^4]

## Building the light

The sources do not agree on what the first lighthouse was. The 1987 National Register form says the first light was lit on 12 June 1797 and was a 30-foot (9 m) brick tower with fifteen whale-oil lamps.[^1] The 2011 registration form, which draws on a 1994 National Park Service historic structure report, describes a 45-foot (14 m) octagonal wooden tower with a six-foot-wide lantern, built under a $6,500 contract that President John Adams approved on 27 March 1797. In that account the tower was essentially finished on 8 November 1797 but probably not lit until 15 January 1798, because of delays in getting an "eclipser" (a revolving screen that made the light appear to flash) that worked.[^2] One old inhabitant told Thoreau that the light was built in 1798.[^4]

The first tower did not last well. Inspection reports soon complained of poor workmanship and an eclipser that turned unevenly and dimmed an already weak light.[^2] In 1812 Winslow Lewis cut 17 feet (5 m) off the top, built a new lamp room and installed his Argand lamps for $2,000.[^2] By 1828 the Boston superintendent reported that the structure was "easily wracked by the winds, which shakes the lantern so much as to break out the glass frequently."[^2] On 22 July 1831 a contract went to Lewis for a brick tower and a keeper's house for $3,993. The second tower, close to the first, was round, 35 feet (11 m) high and 22 feet (6.7 m) across at the base, with the dwelling joined to it by a covered brick passage.[^2]

It was also badly made. When the engineer I. W. P. Lewis was hired to refit it in 1840, he found that the walls had been laid without mortar, "the brick being loosely thrown in, and the interstices filled with sand," and his masons took down thirteen feet of the tower before reaching sound brickwork.[^2] The refit cost $5,919, and Stephen Pleasonton, who ran the lighthouse service, criticised the cost, and because the new lamps burned more oil he ordered the old Argand lamps put back.[^2]

The U.S. Lighthouse Board, created in 1852, put the Cape Cod light near the top of its list. It asked for $15,000 that year to fit a first-order lens, and on 3 August 1854 Congress appropriated $25,000 to move the light to a higher site and make it a substitute for the three small Nauset Beach lights.[^2] In September 1856 the Board changed course and decided to build a tall tower on the existing tract. The form says the reason for the change is not known.[^2] The work was overseen by William Buell Franklin of the Topographical Engineers, who later became a major general in the Civil War.[^2] The Board's 1856 report, as quoted in the 2011 form, noted that "a new dwelling has been built for the keeper at Cape Cod Highlands light-house," and the tower followed in 1857; Isaac M. Small wrote later that a temporary light was set up near the cliff edge while it was built.[^2] The present tower is of brick, 66 feet (20 m) to the top of the lantern and 18 feet (5.5 m) across at the base, and it first held a first-order Fresnel lens. The station had a keeper's house and a double house for two assistants, both joined to the tower by covered passages.[^2] The 1987 form gives $15,000 for the tower and $30,000 for the lens; the 2011 form says that more than $17,000 was spent on the project in all.[^1][^2]

In 1901 the lens was replaced. Congress had voted $15,000 on 6 June 1900 to change the light from fixed white to flashing white, and the Light-House Board's report says the new apparatus had four panels and would give a flash of about 192,000 candlepower every five seconds.[^7] It was lit on 10 October 1901, and a temporary skeleton tower with a third-order light served while the old lens was out.[^2]

## The keepers

Isaac Small kept the light from 1797 to 1812, followed by Constant Hopkins (1812 to 1817) and John Grozer, spelled Groser in some records, who served from 1817 to 1840.[^3] LighthouseFriends reports that Small's repeated complaints about the decaying house and tower cost him his place in 1812.[^11] Jesse Holbrook was appointed in April 1840 at $350 a year; LighthouseFriends quotes a statement of his praising the new cast-iron lantern and saying the tower had not leaked since the refit.[^11][^3]

In 1838 Lieutenant Edward Carpender of the Navy inspected the light a few minutes before sundown and found the keeper, "alarmed at the sight of the revenue cutter," hurrying into the lantern for "a hasty rub-up." Few of the lamps were trimmed and the lantern glass was smoked.[^2]

One picture of a keeper's work in the old reflector days comes from Thoreau, who stayed at the light and went up with the keeper to light the lamps. He does not name the man. The lamps were fifteen Argand lamps with 21-inch reflectors in two rings, reached by "a winding and open iron stairway, with a steadily increasing scent of oil and lamp-smoke."[^4] The keeper filled the lamps every morning and trimmed them about once in the night. He complained of poor oil, said the house burned about 800 gallons a year at about a dollar a gallon, and told how on cold winter nights, with the oil chilled, he sometimes had to warm it in a kettle at midnight and fill the lamps again.[^4] Talking with a keeper about the eroding cliff, Thoreau took him for about forty and was startled when the man said he could remember sixty years back.[^4] The 1987 form records that Enoch S. Hamilton, keeper in 1853, wrote in his journal that 1,200 vessels passed the light in twelve days.[^1]

The first-order lens of 1857 changed the work. The 1987 form says three keepers were then needed instead of one, and the archive lists first and second assistants from 1857.[^1][^3] Isaac M. Small, grandson of the first keeper, described the routine in a booklet in 1891, quoted in the 2011 form: the keeper on watch went up half an hour before sunset, and when the sun dropped the light "flashes out over the sea," with watches changing at 8, midnight and 4 a.m.[^2] Small served for much of his adult life as a "Marine Observer"; in 1880 he built the Cliff House on his father's land, and the telegraph that had been kept in the keepers' quarters was moved there soon afterwards. His home was also linked by government telephone to the life-saving stations along the Cape.[^2] The Barnstable Patriot of 1870, as quoted by LighthouseFriends, told how the assistant keeper's dog followed his master toward the top of the tower, slipped, fell down to the oil butts at the foot, and got off with a lame leg.[^11]

Some keepers are easy to follow in the archive's roster. George E. Dolby entered the service in 1882 as second assistant at Cape Cod, was first assistant in 1883 to 1885, and went on to keep West Chop on Martha's Vineyard from 1909 to 1919.[^10][^3] Stephen D. Rich was second assistant in 1890, and principal keeper from 1891 to 1912, twenty-one years; Fred W. Tibbetts was an assistant from 1912 and principal keeper from 1916 to 1935; and William A. Joseph rose from second assistant (1923) to principal keeper (1935 to 1947).[^3] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin noted that 7,300 visitors signed the register at the station in 1922.[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Thoreau noted that after almost every storm one read of vessels wrecked on this shore, and that sometimes more than a dozen wrecks were in sight at once from the light.[^4] Thoreau saw for himself what one storm could do: one in July 1855 moved the sand on the beach opposite the light to a depth of six feet.[^4]

Lightning hit the keeper's dwelling on 28 February 1891 and did severe damage to the clapboarding, masonry and plaster, which was repaired at once.[^5] The fog signal, a first-class Daboll trumpet installed in 1877,[^2] ran some 1,068 hours in the year ended June 1899 and 1,013 hours in the year ended June 1901.[^6][^7] In 1899 a new oil engine cut the wait for the first blast of the trumpet from about forty-five minutes after lighting the fire to ten.[^6]

The steamer *Portland*, lost in a storm in November 1898, is the wreck the sources tie to the light. A news dispatch from North Truro reported her "totally wrecked at 10 o'clock Sunday morning, off Highland Light," with all aboard lost.[^12] A plaque on a granite boulder at the station marks the fiftieth anniversary of the wreck, which the National Register form places about seven miles offshore.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1904 the Navy built one of its first land wireless telegraph stations on the lighthouse tract, and in 1914 it chose Highland as one of two places to test the first radio compasses in the United States.[^2] In 1916 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin told mariners that a direction finder had been installed at the naval radio station alongside the lighthouse, with bearings correct to within 2 degrees but "still in the experimental stage."[^9] In 1932 electric bulbs replaced the kerosene lamps, which made the light for a time the most powerful on the Atlantic coast, visible for 45 miles and reportedly as far as 75 miles in ideal conditions.[^2]

After the war the Coast Guard reworked the station. The sources disagree about when the lens was replaced: the 1987 form says the Fresnel lens was dismantled and scrapped in 1945, the 2011 form says the 1901 lens was replaced in 1946 by an electric first-order beacon, although another section of the same form dates the Crouse-Hinds beacon to 1947. The 2011 form adds that a portion of the original 1857 lens was kept and is now displayed in the museum.[^1][^2] The assistant keepers' house was pulled down in 1962 according to the history section of the 2011 form (1961 in its summary).[^2] The 1987 form said the Coast Guard planned to automate the light in 1986; the 2011 form says in one place that the light was fully automated in 1987 and in another that the old beacon was replaced in 1988 by two automated aerobeacons.[^1][^2]

By then the cliff was the problem. Thoreau had guessed the bluff was losing about six feet a year and that "erelong, the light-house must be moved."[^4] The 2011 form puts the average at about three feet a year, and by the late 1980s the station stood about 143 (the unit is missing in the text, presumably feet, or about 44 m) from the edge, citing a 1988 Army Corps study.[^2] The 1987 nomination had put the cliff only "75' or so" from the station.[^1] The Truro Historical Society took the lead. An Army Corps of Engineers study in 1989 found moving the light cheaper than holding the cliff, and the Society collected more than $150,000 and a petition with over 140,000 signatures; Congress ordered the light preserved in the Coast Guard Authorization Act of 1991.[^2] In 1996 the move was carried out by International Chimney Corp. of Buffalo, New York, with Expert House Moving of Maryland, using $1 million in federal money and $500,000 from the state alongside the Society's funds. The 404-ton tower and keeper's quarters were lifted on hydraulic jacks and carried west on a rail system over 18 days from June. The light was lit again at its new site on 3 November 1996.[^2] The station was listed on the National Register in 1987.[^2]

## Today

The station is owned by the National Park Service as part of Cape Cod National Seashore, and the Coast Guard still operates the light itself. The keeper's house holds a shop and museum exhibits, and a park partner runs tours of the tower. The partner's site gave open days for 2026 as daily from 1 May to 25 October, 9 a.m. to 5 p.m., with the last tour at 4:30. The tower has 69 steps, narrow and steep, with two ladders near the top, and children must be at least 48 inches tall to climb.[^13]
