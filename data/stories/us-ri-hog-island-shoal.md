Hog Island Shoal Light is a black-and-white iron tower standing alone in the water at the mouth of Mount Hope Bay, Rhode Island, with no island, pier or landing beneath it. It was the last lighthouse established in the state, and the only one in Narragansett Bay built to replace a lightship.[^1]

## The place

The shoal is a reef that extends along the south side of Hog Island, near the entrance to Mount Hope Bay.[^1] The National Register nomination says an 1869 report to the Light-House Board already cited the need for a light southeast of the island, to replace a private lightship kept by a steamboat company that ran boats between Newport and Fall River.[^1] LighthouseFriends puts the start of that private vessel in 1866, names the Old Colony Steamboat Company as its owner, and says it protected the company's steamers running between Fall River and New York, so the two sources describe the company's route differently.[^2] A 1927 Lighthouse Service Bulletin list of discontinued stations gives 1866 to 1886 for the privately maintained station.[^3]

In 1886, according to the same account, Lightship No. 12 (a wooden schooner of 1846) was moored off the south end of the shoal and became the government's own Hog Island Shoal Lightship.[^2] Its first captain, Augustus Hall, served only a short time: LighthouseFriends quotes a newspaper item of 10 February 1887 reporting that he had committed suicide by jumping overboard.[^2] The ship aged badly. The Light-House Board described it in its 1892 report as "in a condition of weakness and decay."[^4] By 1897 it was "old and worn," kept on station only by "great care."[^5]

## Building the light

In 1895 the Board changed its request from a new lightship to a lighthouse, saying the vessel "should be replaced by a light-house" that could be built for about $35,000.[^6] Congress appropriated $35,000 by an act approved 3 March 1899 for a light and fog-signal station to take the lightship's place.[^7] The metalwork and erection were put out to a single contract; the Board's report for 1900 says bids were asked.[^8] LighthouseFriends names the contractors as Toomey Brothers of Guilford, Connecticut, who had recently completed Plum Beach Light. It says the foundation was built as a caisson whose bottom ring was pressurised with air, so the men removing mud and silt from the seabed had a relatively dry place to work while it settled, and that 1,500 tons of riprap were laid around the base to protect it from floating ice.[^2]

The National Register nomination describes a prefabricated cast-iron tower on a cement cylinder 25 feet (7.6 m) across, sunk about 8.5 feet (2.6 m) into the shoal. It rises about 60 feet (18 m) to the top of the lantern ventilator, in five levels plus the lantern, in about 10 feet (3 m) of water.[^1] The basement held storage, the first level a kitchen and living area, and the level above a bedroom. There was no landing pier; the only way in was a ladder up the foundation to a hatch in the lower gallery.[^1]

The sources differ on when the light first shone. LighthouseFriends gives 14 November 1901 for the start of service, and 5 April 1902 for the compressed-air siren.[^2] The nomination says a temporary light was installed in October 1901, the tower was finished in March 1902, and the fog signal was established in April.[^1] They also differ on the lens: LighthouseFriends has a fifth-order Fresnel lens flashing white, replaced in 1903 by a fourth-order lens showing a fixed light,[^2] while the U.S. Lighthouse Society archive lists the original lens as a fifth-order Fresnel.[^9] The nomination describes only the fourth-order lens in use at the time of writing.[^1]

## The keepers

Ernst W. Borgstrom, who had been mate on Ram Island Reef Lightship, was the first head keeper. His assistant was Adolph H. Aronson, who had served on the Hog Island Shoal lightship.[^2] Aronson moved up to head keeper in 1903 and stayed until 1908.[^9] On 12 July 1901, in the lightship's last summer, Lieutenant Spencer Wood of the tender *Cactus* judged Keeper William Walin to be drunk. Walin "said he had only been drinking tea," Wood wrote, and ordered him below. An investigation found it was not the first time, and Walin was dismissed from the service.[^2]

Edward F. Duffy came as first assistant in 1916, became head keeper in 1918, and appears in the archive's roster until 1946, nearly thirty years at the light.[^9][^2] His assistants included Mahlon Burdge, listed as first assistant for most of 1925 to 1945, and Francis H. Peloquin, second assistant from 1925 to 1948.[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1935 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that Duffy and Burdge had recovered "the body of a man who had drowned after falling overboard from: his motorboat on July 28."[^10] LighthouseFriends adds that the keepers spent three days dragging the nearby water before they found the body.[^2]

On 30 December 1962 a storm hit with west-northwest winds of 50 to 60 knots. The Coast Guard crew reported to their group commander at Newport that the structure was "moving and shaking steadily," which they thought was rocks shifting at its base; the commander replied that it had behaved that way before in high winds. The tower came through.[^2]

## After the keepers

In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1921 the station was repaired and given tanks for oil storage, at a cost of $1,972;[^11] the National Register nomination places the oil tanks on the deck of the lower gallery.[^1] Automation was authorised in 1959 and began with a power cable laid from Hog Island; the windows were boarded over, a brighter light installed, and a horn replaced the earlier siren, with the work finished in 1964.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the removal of the crew to June 1964.[^2] The roster from 1959 to 1963 lists Coast Guard crews, not civilian keepers.[^9]

The light went through the federal surplus process in 2004 under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act. No qualified recipient came forward, and it was sold at auction on 23 May 2006 for $165,000 to private buyers.[^2]

## Today

The tower is privately owned and not open to the public; it stands offshore.[^9][^2] It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1988 and remains an active aid to navigation.[^2] In 2023 the Coast Guard decided it was no longer safe for its technicians to enter to inspect the light and fog signal. Photographs from 2024 show part of the lower canopy collapsed.[^2]
