Point Betsie Light stands on the east shore of Lake Michigan, at the southern entrance to the Manitou Passage, north of Frankfort in Benzie County. It was lit in 1858, gained a steam fog signal in 1891, and in 1983 became one of the last two staffed lighthouses on the Great Lakes to lose its keepers.[^1][^2]

## The place

French explorers, according to LighthouseFriends, called the point Pointe aux Bec Scies, "point of sawbill ducks"; the name became Point Betsie, and the Light-House Board's own reports of the 1890s spell it "Point Betsey."[^2][^3] In 1852 the superintendent of lights on the Great Lakes recommended a light here that would mark the southern end of the Manitou Passage and serve ships heading for ports farther south on the lake.[^2] Congress appropriated $5,000 on 3 March 1853, and a contract was let in 1854.[^2]

## Building the light

The work was part of a large block of contracts that went to three Milwaukee partners, Alanson Sweet, Luzern Ransom and Morgan E. Shinn, who were also responsible for lights at several other remote sites on lakes Michigan and Superior; LighthouseFriends suggests that the scale of that undertaking may account for delays in finishing some of the lights.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the first lighting to 20 October 1858. The US Lighthouse Society archive also gives 1858 as the year of establishment, though its keeper roster begins in 1859.[^2][^1]

The station was a yellow-brick, circular tower 37 feet (11 m) high joined by a covered passage to a two-storey dwelling. In the lantern sat a fourth-order Fresnel lens showing a fixed white light varied by a white flash, at a focal plane of 53 feet (16 m) above the lake.[^2] The Society's archive agrees on the fourth-order lens and a 53-foot height of light.[^1]

The building did not wear well. LighthouseFriends reports that sand shifting around the tower made it necessary to replace part of the foundation in 1869.[^2] The Light-House Board's report of 1880, as quoted there, was blunt: "The present light has never given satisfaction. The tower was built by contract in 1858 and the work was miserably done." The Board asked for a new, taller tower with a third-order lens at an estimated $40,000.[^2] The request was repeated for some years; then in 1889, the same account says, the Board decided that because the light overlapped South Manitou's, a fog signal and a lens with a quicker flash were what the point needed instead.[^2] In 1890 an underpinning ring of concrete four feet deep was put beneath the tower, and a 240-foot (73 m) revetment was built along the shore in front of it.[^2]

The fog signal followed. An act approved 3 March 1891 appropriated $5,500 for "a steam fog signal," and the contract for duplicate boilers and machinery was made on 23 June 1891.[^3] The Board's next report describes a frame fog-signal house, 22 by 40 feet (6.7 by 12 m), with corrugated-iron siding and roof, built about 120 feet (37 m) north of the tower.[^3] The 10-inch steam whistles, in duplicate, were put in service on 31 December 1891.[^3] The light was changed on 23 April 1892 from fixed white varied by a white flash every ninety seconds to a flash every ten seconds.[^3] LighthouseFriends adds that a circular iron oil house was built in 1892 and that the new fourth-order lens was what changed the period of the flash.[^2]

In 1895 the dwelling was "thoroughly renovated": the Board added a six-room addition and converted the old house into two separate dwellings under one roof, so that more than one keeper's family could live at the station.[^4] In the fiscal year ended June 1897 the Board had storm houses built at the tower entrance, the southwest entrance to the dwelling and the kitchen door.[^5] On 3 November 1899 the tower and dwelling were painted white to make the tower a better day mark, with red roofs.[^6]

## The keepers

The first three keepers, Flury David, Abel Barnes and Pitt D. Barnes, each served only briefly; LighthouseFriends says less than a year apiece.[^1][^2] The long tenure began in 1861 with Alonzo J. Slyfield, who had kept South Manitou Island Light from 1853 to 1859 and stayed at Point Betsie until 1882.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says he also served the neighbourhood as a doctor, coroner and lifesaver.[^2] A descendant, writing in *Northern Michigan* magazine and drawing on Charles Slyfield's autobiography, says his wife Alice tended the lamps when he was called away to the sick, and that the family arrived by boat on 9 September 1861.[^7]

Not everyone thought the light was well kept in his absences. On 13 April 1880 Thomas Matthews, keeper of the nearby life-saving station, wrote in his station journal that "the light has been neglected so much" and that the keeper was "in the habit of leaving boys at the light in charge", according to LighthouseFriends, which quotes the entry.[^2] In 1881 Alonzo, then fifty-six, asked to retire and have his son Edwin succeed him, an unusual request that was granted in 1882. Edwin R. Slyfield stayed until 1888.[^2][^1]

Peter Dues (1888-1893) and Soren Christianson (1893-1895) followed; the station's own personnel record notes that an assistant's post was authorised late in Dues's time, and the fog signal's extra work is the reason LighthouseFriends gives.[^1][^8][^2] Philip Sheridan, head keeper from 1895, held the longest tenure in the station's history. On 14 October 1914 the rope of the swinging seat he was painting the tower from broke; he fell thirty-five feet (11 m), breaking one leg and crushing an ankle, but recovered and went on serving.[^2] The USLHS roster ends his term in 1917, when LighthouseFriends says he and Severin Danielsen of Chicago Harbor Lighthouse swapped stations; the station's own personnel record gives 1919 for the change.[^1][^2][^8] Danielsen, a bachelor, kept Point Betsie until 1928. LighthouseFriends reports that in 1920 he wrote to the Bureau of Public Roads about the rough track to the highway, and that in 1924 the men from the light and life-saving station finished a usable road themselves with community help.[^2]

When Danielsen retired in 1928, the lighthouse superintendent in Milwaukee asked for extra coal: the former keeper, being a bachelor, had heated only one room, but Charlie Tesnow, a keeper with a family, had been sent mainly because school facilities were available, and the quarters would now need heating.[^2] Edward M. Wheaton followed in 1933. Henry J. Lafreniere served as an assistant from 1918 until 1945.[^1][^2] From 1947 the roster also lists Coast Guard personnel at the station.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Before there was a life-saving station, the Slyfields took part in rescues. The *Northern Michigan* piece, drawing on Charles Slyfield's memoir, says that in October 1871 the schooner *Comet* grounded on a bar about a mile north of the point and the Slyfields, together with others already at the wreck, used a line and a skiff to bring her crew ashore, which it calls the first true rescue at Point Betsie.[^7]

A life-saving station was completed just south of the light in 1876, according to LighthouseFriends; the station's personnel record dates shared occupation of the property to 1877-1937.[^2][^8] Its crew appears in the Life-Saving Service's reports. On 30 September 1887 the surfmen put off in the surf-boat about half a mile and told the steamer *Buffalo*, which had lost her way in fog, where she was.[^9] In the same report, the grain-laden steamer *Cuba*, with a crew of eighteen, stranded on 22 June 1888 in dense fog about a third of a mile southwest of the station; the surf-boat crew went out to her, then pulled ashore to telephone Frankfort for a tug and returned to the vessel until it arrived an hour later.[^9] On 1 August 1888 (the report is for the fiscal year ended June 1889) a fishing tug burned at anchor in Platte River Bay; the Point Betsey crew, notified by telephone, pulled their surf-boat some seven miles to her and towed the hulk into shoal water, where it sank.[^10] The report for the year ended June 1889 also lists Point Betsey among stations that needed repairs and improvements.[^10] The Service's report for the year ended 30 June 1893 has the schooner *Michicott* stranding in foggy weather at Point Betsey on 22 May and becoming a total loss; the surfmen landed her crew of three, cared for them at the station, and worked on the vessel at intervals for two weeks afterwards.[^11] The same report names the crew of the schooner *Fleetwing*, near the station on 25 June 1893, among crews helped with stores donated by the Women's National Relief Association.[^11]

The fog signal's work shows in the Board's figures. In its first six months of service the whistles ran about 139 hours; in fiscal 1893 about 252; in fiscal 1894 about 418; and in fiscal 1895, 1,312 hours, burning about 68 cords of wood.[^3][^12][^13][^4]

## After the keepers

The station changed technology twice more. The Bureau of Lighthouses replaced the steam whistles with air diaphones in 1921, and the following year spent $9,820 on the new diaphone, an electric air compressor and electric lights.[^14][^15] LighthouseFriends dates a radiobeacon to 28 February 1927, and the Bulletin index for 1924-1929 lists a Pointe Betsie radio beacon as planned.[^2][^16] The life-saving station was closed in 1937 and sold in 1945.[^2]

In fall 1983 the Coast Guard automated Point Betsie, together with Sherwood Point, the last two staffed lighthouses on the Great Lakes; Scott Sandy was the commanding officer and Niel Martinek one of the two crew.[^2][^1] Coast Guard personnel lived in the dwelling until the heating system failed in 1996.[^2] The lens was taken from the tower that year and shown at the Sleeping Bear Point Coast Guard Station maritime museum.[^2] The National Register of Historic Places record for the station carries the date 19 July 1984.[^17]

## Today

On 5 June 2004 the Coast Guard transferred the lighthouse to Benzie County, which leased it to the Friends of Point Betsie Lighthouse. The Friends renovated the exterior in 2006 for about $1 million and restored the fog-signal building in 2008; the Michigan governor's award for historic preservation followed in 2010, and the fourth-order lens came back to the lighthouse.[^2] In January 2022 the Coast Guard installed an LED beacon in the lantern room.[^2]

The lighthouse is run by the Friends as a museum. Its website lists the 2026 season as 16 May to 11 October, open 10 to 5 daily except Sundays (12 to 5) and closed Tuesdays (and Wednesdays from 16 to 31 May and from 31 August to 11 October), with a few extra October dates; it also offers overnight stays in the assistant keeper's quarters.[^18][^2] The grounds are at the end of Point Betsie Road, west of Highway 22.[^2]
