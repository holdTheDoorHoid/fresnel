Cape Enrage Lighthouse stands on the southern tip of Barn Marsh Island, at the upper end of the Bay of Fundy in Albert County, New Brunswick. Its first light was shown in 1840, the square wooden tower that stands now dates from 1904, and in 2026 its operating board announced that the site would stay closed for the season, citing the lack of a contract with the province.[^1][^3][^7][^9]

## The place

A reef runs out to seaward from the point, and the water around it turns violent when the tidal currents are at their strongest. That is how the cape got its name.[^1]

In 1837 a group of Westmorland County merchants and shipowners, led by Isaac Turner, petitioned the New Brunswick government for a lighthouse here. The legislature appropriated a sum not exceeding £600.[^1] The commissioners had to choose between Cape Enrage and Apple River, on the Nova Scotia side of Chignecto Bay. Three gentlemen wrote in support of Apple River. Only one letter favoured Cape Enrage, but it was signed by thirty-three men who had long coasted the Bay of Fundy, and the commissioners chose the cape.[^1] Their reasons included a hazard that reached at least a third of the way across Chignecto Bay, and ice that often blocked the south shore in winter while the shore around the cape stayed clear.[^1]

## Building the light

The site was bought from Daniel Tingley, who offered five acres on the point.[^1] Rather than buy a new lantern, the commissioners decided to move the one in use at Partridge Island to Cape Enrage, and to order a larger one from England for Partridge Island. The English lantern arrived late, and the light was not active until the spring of 1840.[^1]

The first tower was the eighth lighthouse built in New Brunswick, and the first square one. It was 12 m (39 ft) tall and showed a fixed white light 49 m (161 ft) above the bay, from six lamps and reflectors.[^1]

In 1849 a landing with stairs and skids was built on the east side, so that supplies could come by water.[^1]

In 1868 the first tower was declared worn out. The second tower was finished the following year, but its light was not shown until 16 November 1870, because the fourth-order lens was delayed.[^1] The first tower, minus its lantern room, was kept on as the keeper's house.[^1][^8]

In 1874 a fog alarm building was completed, with an old boiler and engine brought from Partridge Island. The whistle began on 20 October.[^1] In 1888 a new fog alarm building was put up for $1,900, and it housed a Champion foghorn.[^1]

The Department's report printed in 1901, for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1900, says the clapboards on the eastern side of the tower were found to be decayed. They were all removed and renewed, and the building was painted from dome to sills.[^2]

In 1904 the tower was rebuilt. The Department's report printed in 1905, for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1904, describes the new tower as a wooden building, square in plan and painted white, with an octagonal iron lantern painted red, 9 m (29 ft) high to the top of the ventilator. Its light was fixed white, 38 m (125 ft) above high water, and the report says it should be visible 15 miles. The apparatus was a fourth-order dioptric lens burning petroleum vapour under a mantle. The work was done by day labour under the supervision of the inspector, John Kelly, at a cost of $916.88.[^3]

The Department's report for the nine months ended 31 March 1907 says a condemned boiler at the station was removed and replaced by one that had been used at Halifax.[^5]

## The keepers

The first keeper, James Munson, lived with his family in the lighthouse. He was paid for part of 1839, and his full pay began once the light was lit.[^1][^8] LighthouseFriends's keeper roster starts Munson in 1842, which does not fit its own account.[^1] Munson died, and Matthew Grieves was put in charge in 1844.[^1] John Hennebery kept the light from 1850 to 1871, George Tingley from 1871 to 1874, and William S. Starratt from 1874 to 1888.[^1]

In 1877 the marine agent, John H. Harding, found on an inspection visit that the keeper had hired a new man as engineer in place of his son. Harding warned that no keeper should hire an assistant without first notifying the department.[^1] He also thought well of the family. As LighthouseFriends quotes him: "He has a very intelligent and interesting family." Starratt was dismissed in 1888 for being absent from the station and neglecting his duty.[^1]

James G. Barbour, appointed in May 1888, kept the light until 1923. The Department's list of lightkeepers printed in 1905 names him as keeper of the Cape Enrage light and fog alarm.[^1][^4] Lloyd M. Bennett kept it from 1923 to at least 1937, and Francis Bennett from at least 1957 to 1978. Noel Justason was the keeper from 1978 until the station was automated in 1988.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Department's report for 1872, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, said the severe gales of the previous winter had all but demolished the old tower, which by then was the keeper's residence.[^1]

In 1876 lightning struck the south side of the tower just below the lantern floor and ran down the wall, shattering braces and posts. Had it struck a few inches higher, it might have destroyed the lantern and lens. The damage was judged comparatively trifling.[^1]

When the fog alarm went in, Harding, as LighthouseFriends quotes him, said the fog alarm had replaced the old fear of the cape with a sense of safety.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1988 the light was automated and the station was de-staffed. The keeper's house was no longer needed and was to be torn down.[^1][^8]

In 1992 Dennison Tate, a physics teacher at a high school in Moncton, proposed an outdoor adventure program at the cape, run by students, that would raise money to restore and maintain the station.[^1] In the summer of 1993 Tate hired six students, with federal summer work grants, to paint the duplex, fit new shutters and build a stairway down to the beach.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory says the two-storey wooden keeper's house was restored beginning in 1993.[^7]

In late summer 1994 the Province of New Brunswick, with encouragement from the Albert County Heritage Trust, announced that it would buy the station from the Canadian Coast Guard. Tate's group was given permission to launch its adventure program the following summer.[^1] A stairway to the lighthouse was completed in 1997, and a gift shop, the Whistle House, was built in 1999.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory says the shingles on the tower were replaced in 2020 and the tower repainted in 2021.[^7]

## Today

The light is still active.[^7] The Coast Guard's list gives a green flash every six seconds, with a focal height of 40.7 m (about 134 ft) and a nominal range of 10 nautical miles.[^6]

The Coast Guard's list marks the light as a "private aid," and the Lighthouse Directory names the Canadian Coast Guard as owner.[^6][^7] The board of the Cape Enrage Interpretive Centre describes the facility as provincially owned.[^9] LighthouseFriends reports the 1994 announcement of a provincial purchase but does not say whether it was completed.[^1] The sources read do not settle who owns the station now.

In 2026 the centre's volunteer board said the site would not open to visitors this season. It said there had been no formal operating contract with the province since 31 March 2026, and that the centre had welcomed 20,000 to 25,000 visitors a year.[^9] The Lighthouse Directory says the site is open and the tower closed for the 2026 season, and that the provincial government has declined to provide its usual support of $100,000 for repairs and maintenance.[^7] The two accounts differ on whether the grounds are open.

The tower is closed and should not be climbed. The cape is reached by Cape Enrage Road, which runs across Barn Marsh Beach.[^8]
