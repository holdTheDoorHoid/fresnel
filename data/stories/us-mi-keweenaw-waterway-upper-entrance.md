The Keweenaw Waterway Upper Entrance Light stands on a breakwater about 0.4 mile off the Lake Superior shore at Hancock Township, Michigan. Its tower dates from 1950, but a light has marked this entrance since 1874.[^1][^2]

## The place

The Keweenaw Waterway runs across the peninsula.[^2] Its last two and a half miles are a man-made canal, which links Portage Lake to Lake Superior.[^2] A private company was formed in 1864 to build the canal. Excavation ran from 1868 to 1871, and the canal was finished in 1873 after litigation.[^2] Its owners charged a heavy tax on vessels using the waterway, which limited traffic, until the federal government bought the canal in 1891 for $350,000.[^2][^4]

LighthouseFriends says that "During the stormy fall season, the waterway would see a dramatic increase in traffic as vessels preferred its protected waters to Lake Superior."[^2]

The waterway's other end had a light of its own.[^1] The nomination describes a lighthouse "established in 1856 on the Keweenaw Peninsula's eastern shore," named the Portage River Light. It was demolished in 1869 when a taller light replaced it, and it was discontinued in 1920 after the Lower Entrance light at the river's mouth was lit.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress had allocated $14,000 in 1871 for rebuilding the Eagle River light. When a new light there was judged unnecessary, the Lighthouse Board was allowed in 1873 to spend the money "at the entrance to Portage Lake ship-canal."[^2] A roughly one-acre site on the west side of the entrance was deeded in March 1874, and the light was "first exhibited on the night of November 20, 1874."[^2] Its tower was an eight-and-a-half-foot square, "attached to the gable end of a brick keeper's dwelling."[^2] The archive gives the original lens as a third-order-and-a-half Fresnel installed in 1874.[^2][^3]

A pierhead light followed on the west pier.[^2] LighthouseFriends says a wooden tower there showed its light for the first time in 1879, on "November 15 of that year."[^2] The Board's report in the volume labelled 1893–1894 says a steam fog signal for the canal pier "was authorized by the act approved February 15, 1893."[^5] LighthouseFriends says Congress provided $5,500 on 2 March 1895.[^2]

The main light was raised in 1896.[^6] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1897 records "Height of light increased to 66 feet, September 26, 1896."[^6] Breakwaters were completed in October 1901 to protect the entrance from Lake Superior.[^2] In 1912 the light's illuminant changed "from oil to incandescent oil vapor," and in 1920 the fog signal moved "not to the breakwater but to the mainland near the main light."[^2]

The brick light did not last.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that when the canal was widened, "it was necessary to demolish the old brick lighthouse," and that "a forty-six-foot-tall, skeletal tower was erected in 1937."[^2] The archive gives a different date: the old light was "Demolished When Canal Was Widened in 1935 and Replaced with Temporary Building Until New Tower Built in 1950."[^3] The sources do not reconcile 1935 and 1937.

The current light followed the 1949 widening of the entrance.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that "the following year, the current light" went on "a square, white tower" with a focal plane of eighty-two feet (25 m).[^2] The nomination describes a steel light tower about 50 feet (15 m) tall, on a steel caisson, and says the structure "was built using a standardized design developed for the U.S. Bureau of Lighthouses during the middle 1930s."[^1]

## The keepers

The archive's record for the 1874 light lists the keepers, and the first was Peter Malone, "a former captain of ocean-going vessels."[^2][^3] His son John H. Malone took over, and in 1878 the two men "swapped stations" with William Stevens, keeper on Menagerie Island.[^2] LighthouseFriends says John Malone "would serve on Menagerie Island for thirty-two years."[^2]

Afterwards the station had, in LighthouseFriends' phrase, "a string of head keepers with short terms of service."[^2] William McGue was keeper from 1888 to 1901.[^3] In the summer of 1889, LighthouseFriends says, he was "digging out a tree stump on the lighthouse grounds" when he found copper knives and spear points, which were "later purchased by the Chicago Natural History Museum."[^2]

Noah J. Bennetts was an assistant from 1895 to 1897 and head keeper from 1902 to 1933.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he "remained for over thirty years."[^2][^3] The archive also lists a Susan Bennetts as second assistant keeper in 1907.[^3]

James E. Collins was head keeper from 1951 to 1958, and LighthouseFriends says he "retired from Keweenaw Upper Entrance Station after serving for thirty-nine years as a lighthouse keeper" in 1958.[^2][^3]

The archive's record for the 1950 light, its North Side entry, lists Russell H. Korby (1961–1963) and Robert F. Robertson (1963 onward) as keepers.[^7] The nomination says the light was "Initially operated remotely from shore by U.S. Coast Guard personnel."[^1] The sources do not reconcile the keepers with the remote operation. A new three-family keepers' dwelling was completed in 1938.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends says that a storm on 22 September 1895 washed the fog-signal boilers and machinery into the canal, and that a wrecking crew from Marquette was brought in to retrieve them before the signal was finished.[^2] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1896 confirms the loss: the boilers and machinery "were washed into the canal by the storm," and a wrecking outfit "brought from Marquette, Mich." recovered them.[^9]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1895 records an earlier storm. A storm on 30 September 1894 carried away part of the elevated walk and the bridge that connects it at the angle of the pier, and the bridge was rebuilt.[^10]

No wreck at the light appears in the sources cited.

## After the keepers

The nomination says the light's aids to navigation "were automated during the 1970s."[^1] Some buildings of the old shore station survive.[^2] LighthouseFriends says "a few remnants of the shore station survive," including the 1938 building, a barn and the fog-signal building, and that some have been moved and are now privately owned.[^2]

The Coast Guard submitted a National Register nomination for the light.[^1] The Park Service's form carries the date "JUN -6 2014."[^1]

## Today

On 9 July 2025 the General Services Administration announced the light, and the Sheboygan Breakwater Light, were available "at no cost to eligible entities" under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act.[^8] The Coast Guard "will reserve access to the property for the purposes of maintaining the ATON after conveyance."[^8] LighthouseFriends says interested parties had sixty days to submit a letter of interest.[^2] The sources cited do not record a completed transfer, so the current owner should be checked before any visit is planned.

The Coast Guard owns the light, and its tower is closed.[^1][^2] The nomination says it "is accessible by boat and is not open to public visitation."[^1][^2] From the shore, LighthouseFriends says, the light can be seen from "a parking area from where you can view the light at the end of the breakwater" in McLain State Park, off Highway 203 west of Hancock.[^2]
