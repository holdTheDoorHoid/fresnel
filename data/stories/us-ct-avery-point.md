Avery Point Light is a small octagonal concrete tower on the grounds of the University of Connecticut's Avery Point campus in Groton, Connecticut, on the eastern side of the entrance to New London Harbor.[^1] The Coast Guard built it in 1943, shortly after its training center on the old Plant estate opened.[^1][^2] The light was first shown in May 1944 and went out in 1967.[^8] A society and a national lighthouse foundation restored it, and it was relit in 2006.[^6][^7]

## The place

The peninsula at Avery Point forms the eastern side of the harbor entrance.[^1] The nomination says the tower "overlooks the rocky shore of Long Island Sound at the southern tip of the peninsula in Groton, Connecticut, known as Avery Point."[^1] The land had been the estate of the industrialist Morton F. Plant.[^6] His Branford House mansion still stands on the campus.[^1]

The state was to acquire the land first.[^2] The nomination says the plan was for the State of Connecticut to buy the 70-acre Branford House estate from Plant's heirs for $85,000 and then convey it to the federal government.[^2] By 1940 the Coast Guard's technical training at Fort Trumbull had become inadequate, and the service wanted to find quarters near New London.[^2] A request for $3 million for the center was defeated in Congress in April 1941, but a smaller appropriation was eventually approved and construction went ahead.[^2]

The state proposed to convey the land under a law first enacted in 1929 that let the State Treasurer make grants for erecting "beacon lights" and other aids to navigation.[^2] The deed had to contain a reversion clause, so that the land would return to the state if the government did not build the aid.[^2] The nomination says the deed was dated 12 March 1942.[^5] The Coast Guard training center opened on 1 August 1942.[^2]

The light was meant for small boats.[^2] The nomination says it "was intended to particularly benefit small boats seeking to enter Baker Cove on the east side of Avery Point."[^2]

## Building the light

Alfred Hopkins and Associates designed the light, and the R. E. Carrick Company built it.[^5][^11] The architects' drawings are dated 1942, and the nomination says the tower was built in 1943.[^5][^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's place record also gives 1943 as the year the light was established.[^10]

The nomination describes an octagonal concrete-block tower 26 feet (8 m) tall, topped by a wooden lantern.[^1] The overall height is 41 feet (12.5 m), including the poured concrete slab foundation.[^1]

The top deck has an unusual railing.[^1] Its white marble balusters "were salvaged from a garden or terrace on the property at the time the lighthouse was built."[^1] At the time, the Coast Guard described the balusters and arched windows as giving "a touch of Williamsburg." The nomination quotes the phrase from the training center's paper, the *Groton Station News*, of 31 March 1943.[^3]

The lighting was simple. The lantern held "an array of eight hanging 200-watt bulbs," one shining through each arched opening, and an electro-mechanical timer controlled the lights so that they "needed little attention unless a bulb burned out." The nomination says the array gave a beacon of 100 candlepower that could be seen "for up to seven miles at night," from a plane of light 55 feet (17 m) above mean sea level.[^1]

The light was not shown at once. The Coast Guard's Notice to Mariners of 3 May 1944 announced "Avery Point - Light Established."[^5] LighthouseFriends says the light was not activated until 2 May 1944, "due to concerns about possible enemy invasions by sea."[^6] The nomination does not give a reason for the delay.[^2]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends says the light never had a resident keeper.[^6]

## After the keepers

In 1960 the light was changed to flashing green and its candlepower was increased to 200.[^1] The nomination says the increase answered a request from a local group, the Shennecossett Yacht Club, and cites the *New London Day* of 18 March 1959.[^3]

The Coast Guard's training programs left in 1967.[^2] "The Coast Guard relocated its training programs in 1967 and the land was conveyed back to the State," the nomination says.[^2] The Coast Guard kept part of the site as a research and development center.[^2] The lighting apparatus came out of the lantern after the light was discontinued that year.[^1] The Avery Point Lighthouse Society gives the extinguishing date as 25 June 1967.[^8] LighthouseFriends dates the return of the land to the state to 1969; the nomination links it to the 1967 move.[^6][^2]

The university then used the tower for other things.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the building was given the designation Building #41 and used as a physics laboratory and an air sampling station.[^6]

By the late 1990s the tower was unsafe.[^6] LighthouseFriends says its concrete blocks were "crumbling, the result of high sand content."[^6] A petition with over 19,000 signatures asked for its restoration and relighting.[^6] The Avery Point Lighthouse Society was formed in February 2000 to save and relight the tower.[^8]

The restoration took six years and cost $500,000.[^6][^7] A replica of the wooden lantern was lowered into place on 30 September 2005, using the old lantern as a template.[^6] The thirty-two marble balusters were taken down, cleaned, and put back.[^6] The light was relit as a private aid to navigation on 15 October 2006, at a ceremony with speakers from the National Park Service and the university.[^6][^7] The society's own page gives the relighting as 24 September 2005, so the two dates disagree.[^8][^7]

The nomination was listed on the National Register on 23 August 2002.[^9] The American Lighthouse Foundation's page gives 2022 for the listing; the register's record gives 2002.[^7][^9]

## Today

The University of Connecticut owns the lighthouse.[^6] The grounds are open, and a memorial walkway open to the public runs along the shore to the tower.[^6][^7] The tower is closed. Its interior is not open to the public, so do not try to get in.[^6]

The light now shows a green flash every four seconds from a solar-powered LED optic.[^7] As of the nomination, the tower was "the last lighthouse to have been built in Connecticut."[^2]

Popular accounts say the light was built as a memorial to lighthouses and their keepers.[^8] The nomination calls that a misunderstanding that began in 1955, when a Coast Guard magazine article said the deed carried that purpose. The deed says no such thing; it refers to "beacon lights or other buildings and apparatus to be used in aid of navigation."[^4]
