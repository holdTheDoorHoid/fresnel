Browns Head Light stands on the northwest corner of Vinalhaven Island, in Knox County, Maine, where it marks the western entrance to the Fox Island Thoroughfare, the strait between Vinalhaven and North Haven.[^2][^3] A light has been shown there since 1832, although a Belfast newspaper of 1904 gave the year it was established as 1837.[^11] Whether the tower that stands today is that first tower or its 1857 replacement is one of the questions the sources do not settle.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

LighthouseFriends says the light was set to guide trading ships through the Thoroughfare, a strait about a mile wide.[^3] Vinalhaven's granite was the island's other trade. LighthouseFriends dates the start of its growth to 1826 and says the island was one of Maine's largest quarrying centers for the next century.[^3]

The site drew complaints almost from the start. When the engineer I. W. P. Lewis inspected the station in 1842, he dismissed it as "merely a local beacon." He also thought it stood too low to clear the ledges near it.[^3]

## Building the light

An appropriation of $4,000 for the station was approved on March 3, 1831.[^3] Jeremiah Berry built the tower and dwelling for $1,800. LighthouseFriends says the tower stood 22 feet (6.7 m) to the base of its octagonal wrought-iron lantern and showed a fixed white light 42 feet (13 m) above mean high water.[^3]

The sources disagree about what came next. The 1982 National Register nomination says the tower is original to 1832, describes it as a cylindrical granite tower about 18 feet (5.5 m) high, and dates the keeper's house to the 1850s, when the station was rehabilitated.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society gives a cylindrical brick tower established in 1857, 20 feet (6.1 m) from base to the center of the lantern.[^2] LighthouseFriends also dates a new brick tower to 1857, but puts it at 14 feet (4.3 m) to the base of the lantern.[^3] The three accounts do not agree on the tower's date, material or height.

The first station was poorly built, by the account of its own keeper. In 1842 John Calderwood wrote that the lantern "shakes so in a gale of wind that the chimneys rattle like sleigh bells."[^3] His family left the next year. In 1855 an inspector, W. B. Franklin, reported the station worn out and asked for $5,000 for a new one. LighthouseFriends says the new dwelling and brick tower were finished in 1857, and that the tower carried a fifth-order Fresnel lens in place of the old lamps and reflectors.[^3][^2]

Later changes were smaller. The Board's report for the year ended June 30, 1891 records that the station received "a new fuel house, 12 by 20 feet in plan, a brick cistern holding 2,000 gallons, and a new kitchen floor."[^4] The report for fiscal 1894 notes that "Materials for a boathouse and boat slip were landed at the station."[^7] LighthouseFriends says the light's color changed twice. On 15 September 1880 it was changed to white and red, the red marking the west channel. In 1889 the red sector was replaced by a white sector between two red ones, so that white marked safe water and red marked danger.[^3] A fog bell hung from a wooden pyramid arrived in 1908. Before that, a dinner bell by the dwelling door served as the fog signal, as a Belfast newspaper also recalled in 1904.[^3][^11]

## The keepers

David Wooster, of North Haven, was the first keeper to live in the rubblestone dwelling. He served until his death in 1841, at sixty-one.[^2][^3]

John Calderwood followed him at a salary of $350 a year. He had to travel twelve miles (19 km) to Camden for supplies. The station boat was "worn out," and a boat could not land close to the light.[^3]

Howland Dyer kept the light from 1843 to 1864. An inspector in 1850 called him "a first-rate one."[^2][^3]

Benjamin E. Burgess began in 1867 and stayed almost forty years.[^2][^3] In October 1904 the Republican Journal of Belfast ran a profile of him, describing "Mr Burgess, who will be 81 in December this year," still on duty.[^11] The paper says he married his second wife in the station parlor and raised his own seven children at the light. He also reared three grandchildren there, whose young mother had died suddenly.[^11] The paper praised "the exquisite neatness and order" of the station.[^11]

In May 1905 the same paper reported that Burgess had resigned, effective 1 May, and called him "the second oldest lighthouse keeper in the United States service." It said his son, who had been first assistant keeper, succeeded him.[^12] The US Lighthouse Society roster instead lists Charles T. Burgess as principal keeper from 1905 to 1918.[^2] The sources do not settle whose account is right.

The roster continues with Alonzo Morong (1918 to 1929), Ernest V. Talbot (1929 to 1945) and Merrill E. Poor (1945 to 1957).[^2] No woman appears on the roster as a keeper.[^2]

The Coast Guard staffed the light in the later years. The roster lists Roy V. Louder (1957 to 1961) as Coast Guard personnel with no stated position, while LighthouseFriends calls him head keeper.[^2][^3] Pauline DeRaps, who lived there with her husband Ernie, a Coast Guard keeper, in 1961 and 1962, recalled gales that blew spray up to the windows.[^3] Charles Lawson (1983 to 1984) was one of the last keepers before the light was automated.[^2][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Board's reports record the light going dark for ice. The report for fiscal 1893 lists the light as "Discontinued on account of ice, January 14, 1893."[^5] In 1894 it was discontinued again on 26 February and relit on 3 March.[^6] In 1895 the report says the light was extinguished on 7 February "owing to the closing of the thoroughfare by ice," and relit on 21 February.[^8] In 1899 it was discontinued on 11 February on account of ice and relit on 1 March.[^9] In 1901 it was extinguished on 8 February on account of ice and relit on 9 March.[^10]

Winter was not the only danger. A keeper's wife in the 1970s, Gail Baxter, remembered a storm in which lightning split a telephone pole at the top of the hill.[^3] None of the sources cited here ties a wreck or rescue to this light.

In September 1907 the Daily Kennebec Journal reported that repairs were being made to the station.[^13]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1987.[^2][^3] At the August 1987 ceremony the Coast Guard commander of the group at Southwest Harbor said the manned lighthouse "will be a thing of the past."[^3] LighthouseFriends notes that after Browns Head was automated, the only staffed lights left in Maine were at West Quoddy, Fort Point and Owls Head.[^3]

Under the Maine Lights Program the station passed to the Town of Vinalhaven in 1998. For many years the keeper's house was closed and occupied by the town manager, while the grounds stayed open to the public.[^3] The American Lighthouse Foundation took the station in October 2015, and in 2019 it launched a campaign to raise $60,000 for restoration. In 2022 the Foundation passed the station to a new preservation group, the Friends of Browns Head Lighthouse.[^3]

## Today

The Friends of Browns Head Lighthouse own the station, according to LighthouseFriends. The US Lighthouse Society's record still names the American Lighthouse Foundation.[^2][^3] The grounds are open. The house and tower are closed.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the tower's Fresnel lens is still active and that the Coast Guard services it.[^3]

To get there, take the ferry from Rockland to Vinalhaven. The lighthouse is several miles from the ferry landing, so LighthouseFriends advises bringing a car.[^3] Camden Harbor Cruises offers boat trips that include the lighthouse.[^3] In 2015 the Foundation's plans called for overnight stays in the lighthouse. The sources cited here do not say whether those stays have begun.[^3] The station is on the National Register of Historic Places as Browns Head Light Station.[^14]
