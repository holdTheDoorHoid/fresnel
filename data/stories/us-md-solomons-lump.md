Solomons Lump Light is a brick tower standing on a sunk caisson in Kedges Straits, Chesapeake Bay, about eight miles from Crisfield, Maryland.[^1] The keepers' house beside it has been pulled down, and the light has run automatically since 1950.[^1]

## The place

The light stands on the south side of Kedges Straits, between Chesapeake Bay and Tangier Sound.[^1] The first light on this spot replaced one at Fog Point, about a mile to the south, which had been built in 1827.[^1]

In March 1875 the Baltimore German-language newspaper *Der Deutsche Correspondent* reported that the federal government was building new lighthouses at "Solomon's Lump, Md." and at a second site in Virginia. It said they would be finished within a few months, and that they would be 43 feet (13 m) high.[^2] (Translated from the German.)

## Building the light

The first light on the shoal, erected in 1875, was a square cottage-style screw-pile structure.[^1] It stood for eighteen years.[^1]

After the ice wrecked it, Congress appropriated $30,000 to reestablish the light.[^3] The Light-House Board's report says it first planned another screw-pile structure, but then decided "to incur no risks of the destruction of another lighthouse by the ice". It chose a structure that by its mass and weight could offer "an effective resistance to any movement of the ice".[^4] An act approved 21 December 1893 allowed the Board to spend the money left over from the Wolf Trap caisson contract on a caisson here.[^4]

Work began in 1894 at the Lazaretto depot near Baltimore. The caisson was positioned on 20 May 1895 and sunk into the soft bottom of the bay.[^1] The Board's 1896 report describes a cast-iron foundation cylinder "25 feet in diameter, resting on a steel caisson", carrying a brick and wooden superstructure with the lantern on its tower.[^5] Erection of the superstructure began on 30 June 1895 and was "finished on July 26".[^5] The focal plane sat 46 feet 6 inches (14.2 m) above mean high water.[^5]

The fifth-order light was reestablished on 30 September 1895.[^6] The Board's report says the light "was shown for the first time" that day, and that "the fog bell is struck by machinery every 10 seconds during thick and foggy weather".[^5] The keepers' house was a two-story wood-frame building, with the brick tower set on one side of its eight-sided plan rather than in the middle.[^1][^7]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's digital archive lists 36 keeper and assistant entries for the station, from 1875 to 1946.[^7] The first principal keeper, Ephraim Tyler, served from 1875 to 1879.[^7]

Lorenzo Dow Evans was principal keeper from 1879 to 1893 and again from 1895 to 1900.[^7] The roster has no principal keeper between those years, which were the years the light was wrecked and rebuilt. The roster does not say why.[^3][^7]

Henry C. Sterling was a first assistant in 1900 and became principal keeper in 1904. He served until 1937, the longest principal stint on the roster.[^7] Luther E. Bozman was a first assistant from 1919 to 1927 and principal keeper from 1938 to 1946.[^7]

In August 1916 *Der Deutsche Correspondent* printed a list of lighthouse men given merit medals "für Anerkennung im Dienste" (for recognition of service). Among them was "Henry E. Sterling, Solomons Lump, Md."[^8] The notice gives no reason for the awards, and its middle initial differs from the roster's.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Board's 1893 report says that "In January the light-house was wrecked by the impact of moving masses of ice." It adds that "Though not carried from its site", the house was overturned so that part of it lay under water.[^3] The Board's list of discontinued fog signals gives the bell as ended on 2 February 1893, with the note "Station carried away by ice".[^9] The two dates do not agree, and the sources read here do not settle which is right.[^3][^9]

The Annapolis *Evening Capital* of 6 February 1893 counted this as "Another light house in the Chesapeake bay" destroyed by the ice, which made "the total number so far reported as either lost or badly damaged five". It said the Board had "received word of the destruction of the Solomon’s Lump light" and of "its abandonment by the keeper".[^10]

In June 1893 "a lens-lantern light was established on the wreck to mark its position at night and afford assistance to local navigation".[^3] Ice in the winter of 1894–95 destroyed what was left of the old structure and took out that temporary lamp.[^1] The Board's list of discontinued lights records a lens-lantern light at Solomons Lump, "February 22, 1895", with the note "Wreck of structure carried away by ice".[^11] A later list in the same volume gives the same entry with the date "February 22, 1896".[^12]

## After the keepers

In 1919 the fifth-order lens was moved to Cherrystone Light in Virginia, and the fourth-order lens from Cherrystone was installed here.[^1] In 1950 the light was converted to automatic operation and the Coast Guard keepers were reassigned.[^1] Soon after, the keepers' quarters were demolished, because of the danger of fire and vandalism.[^1]

## Today

The light stands 47 feet (14 m) above the water.[^1][^7] It is an active aid to navigation, and the station is not open to the public.[^7] Its light is a flashing white with two red sectors.[^1] The station cannot be reached by car. It lies about 6.5 miles from the nearest road, and only a boat or plane gets there.[^1]
