Black Rock Harbor Light, also called Fayerweather Island Light, stands at the south end of Fayerweather Island in Bridgeport, Connecticut, where it marks the entrance to Black Rock Harbor.[^1] A hurricane in 1821 flattened the wooden light that stood here before, and the stone tower that replaced it still stands.[^2]

## The place

In the eighteenth century Black Rock Harbor, sheltered by Fayerweather Island, was a developing shipping center. LighthouseFriends says it "seemed only natural" to build a light on the island to guide ships into the harbor.[^2] The land was bought from David Fayerweather in 1807 for $200. LighthouseFriends calls it only the second site bought for a lighthouse on western Long Island Sound.[^2] A federal harbor summary printed in the *Bridgeport Evening Farmer* in 1910 records that "between 1836 and 1838, $21,550 was expended in building a sea wall across a breach in the southern part of Fayerweather Island."[^3]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says the first light, a forty-foot (12.2 m) octagonal wooden tower, was built in 1808 on a tract of about nine and a half acres.[^2] The archive gives 1809 as the year the station was established. It lists two contractors, Judson Curtis and Elisha Woodward, with no dates and no work described.[^1]

On 3 September 1821 a hurricane ripped through the island. LighthouseFriends says every vessel in the harbor went ashore and the old tower was laid flat.[^2] The replacement was a 41-foot (12.5 m) tower of coursed sandstone ashlar with rubble mortar, which cost $2,300 to build in 1823.[^2] Its builder claimed it would "withstand the storm of ages," and Edmund Blunt, in the *American Coast Pilot*, called it "most shamefully erected and badly kept."[^2] The archive gives 1817 as the year the present tower was established. LighthouseFriends places the stone tower after the 1821 storm, so the two sources differ on its date.[^1][^2]

The station was upgraded to eight lamps with parabolic reflectors in 1830, and a fifth-order Fresnel lens was installed in 1854, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The archive gives 1856 for the original lens.[^1] The Lighthouse Board's annual report for 1899 records that "a set of improved fifth-order lamps was furnished and fitted to lens."[^4]

## The keepers

John Maltbie, the first keeper, died five months after he took charge.[^2] The next keepers were Charles Isaac Judson, Daniel Willson and Joshua Lester.[^1][^2]

Stephen T. Moore was the official keeper for 54 years, until his death in 1871 at the age of 98.[^2] He had been a West Indian merchant until an injury he suffered while helping a friend with a load of hay. His health failed, and his daughter Kathleen did most of the tending.[^2] The archive lists her as keeper from 1871 to 1878.[^1] LighthouseFriends says she began helping at twelve and was made the official keeper after her father's death, and that she "continued to serve until 1878."[^2] In an interview at age ninety-four she described the light as eight oil lamps which "took four gallons of oil each night."[^2] To reach the tower from the house about 700 feet (210 m) away she walked across two planks, "under which on stormy nights were four feet of water."[^2]

Leonard Clark, a Civil War veteran and former whaling captain, kept the light from 1878 until his death in 1906.[^1][^2] His wife, Mary Elizabeth Clark, looked after the light until John D. Davies arrived two months later.[^1][^2] Davies, LighthouseFriends says, had served in the Irish lighthouse service and was the last keeper, until the light was discontinued on 3 March 1933.[^2] The archive's roster ends his service in 1931.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The one dated storm in LighthouseFriends' account is the hurricane of 3 September 1821.[^2] It also says Kathleen Moore was "credited with saving twenty-one lives" during her years at the light, but it names no wreck or date for any of them.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1931 the Lighthouse Service requested $22,900 for two acetylene lights out in the water. Its description of the shore light said the light stood a considerable distance from the dredged channel, and that a rocky reef extended into the bay, so the light was not of sufficient use to the traffic.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives 3 March 1933 as the discontinuance date, while the archive gives 1932.[^1][^2]

In 1934 Congress transferred the lighthouse reservation to the city of Bridgeport for use as a public park.[^6] The park later suffered from the elements and vandalism, and the keeper's dwelling burned to the ground in 1977.[^2] In 1983 Friends of Seaside Park and the Black Rock Community Council showed interest in the tower and began preservation efforts.[^2] In 1993 David Grant Grimshaw and Patricia Roche, two Bridgeport citizens, began a fundraising effort to preserve the lighthouse.[^2] The tower was restored in 1998. Solar panels and lighting equipment were later installed so that it can be lit, though it is not an official aid to navigation.[^2] Hurricane Sandy damaged the station in 2012, and the repairs were completed in 2017.[^2]

In 2014 the Coast Guard named a 154-foot (47 m) cutter after Kathleen Moore and placed a marker on her grave, which had never had a headstone. The following year the History Committee of the Black Rock Community Council dedicated a stone monument to honor her.[^2]

## Today

The grounds are reached from a parking area at the end of Barnum Boulevard at Seaside Park. The walk out to the lighthouse is across a rough breakwater, which one guide calls somewhat strenuous.[^5] The tower itself is not open to the public.[^5] The Black Rock Community Council maintains the site.[^5] The city of Bridgeport has held the reservation since 1934.[^6] The dwelling is gone; it burned in 1977.[^2]
