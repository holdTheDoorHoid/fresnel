The Little Channel Range Lights were a pair of range lights on the north shore of Prince Edward Island, set to guide vessels through Conway Inlet to the harbour at Poplar Grove.[^1][^3] The rear light, a square wooden tower attached to a dwelling, survives, and it is now a private vacation rental.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The inlet runs between a line of long sandy islands that local people call the Sandhills. The Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society describes a series of three such islands from Alberton to Malpeque, with four main passages between them.[^2] Heavy traffic to the shipyards and fish factories made a light necessary to guide vessels into Conway Narrows.[^2] The range stood on the northern side of Conway Inlet, on the northwest end of Bill Hook Island, which is also called Fish Island.[^1]

## Building the light

The sources do not agree on when the first light was set up. The Lighthouse Society says that in May 1871 two lanterns were set on poles in the sand at the entrance to Hardy's Channel, on the advice of a shipbuilder from Port Hill. It says William Hardy, who owned a lobster factory on the Sandhills, was hired to tend them, rowing over at dusk to hoist the lanterns and putting them out in the morning.[^2] LighthouseFriends and Rowlett both date the station to 1872.[^1][^3] The Society says that in 1872 the government decided on a permanent lighthouse at the spot, and that Peter Miller of Ellerslie took the contract for $900, though local sources name James Henderson as the actual builder.[^2] LighthouseFriends puts the $900 contract in 1876, for the two range towers that were built.[^1]

In 1874, a year after Prince Edward Island entered Confederation, the General Superintendent of Lighthouses toured the Island and called the Little Channel range "of the most temporary description."[^1] The new range towers were first exhibited on 20 August 1877. The front light was an open framework tower 6.1 m (20 ft) high. The rear was a white square wooden tower 9.1 m (30 ft) high, and the two stood 60.3 m (198 ft) apart.[^1][^2]

In 1878 William Mitchell, the Department of Marine and Fisheries' agent for the Island, visited the station and found the lights, which had two circular burners and twenty-inch reflectors, "in good order."[^1] The front light's foundation was renewed in 1885 and again in 1928.[^1]

## The keepers

The Department's report for 1912 lists the Little Channel range among the Island's range lights.[^5] LighthouseFriends names William Hardy as the first keeper of the range lights and says he served for roughly fifty years.[^1] The Department's table of keepers for 1910 gives the station as Little Channel and William Hardy's appointment as 1875.[^4] LighthouseFriends lists him as "William Hardy, Jr.", from 1874 to 1924.[^1] The Lighthouse Society says he began as keeper of the 1871 pole lights and continued until 1917.[^2] The two accounts disagree about when he left.

His son Ernest A. Hardy kept the lights after him. LighthouseFriends gives Ernest's years as 1924 to 1946, and the Society gives 1917 to 1946.[^1][^2] Ernest died in May 1946. His son Everett L. Hardy was appointed keeper and served for a year, until the lights were made unwatched in May 1947.[^1][^2] Everett was afterwards paid $15 a year to light the range at the start of the season and put it out at the end.[^1] LighthouseFriends counts three generations of the Hardy family as the only keepers the range lights ever had.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Society says the station was electrified in April 1947, when a contractor was engaged to remove the kitchen section of the lighthouse.[^2] By 1965 the foundation of the rear range had been undermined, and its wooden tower was in poor condition.[^1]

The Society quotes a modern article which says the harbour mouth had shifted so much that the light had become a hazard to navigation. Buoys then marked the new entrance: a bell buoy at the outside entrance to Hardy's Channel and a light buoy at the mouth of the harbour. The Society says the light on the sandhills was put out in 1968.[^2] LighthouseFriends and Rowlett give 1969 as the year it was deactivated.[^1][^3]

The rear tower was then moved. The Society says it was bought and taken to Freeland in 1972.[^2] The same Society page also says the tower was sold and relocated across Conway Narrows to a spot called Lighthouse Point.[^2] LighthouseFriends and Rowlett place it at Freeland.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends says it was relocated and incorporated into a dwelling about 6.4 km (4 miles) from its original site.[^1] Rowlett says the tower was for years listed as lost, until a researcher found it on the mainland, and that another researcher located it at the end of Henderson Lane in Freeland in 2008.[^3]

## Today

The rear light is private property. It stands at the end of a long private road near Freeland, and the grounds and tower are closed.[^1][^2][^3] It is run as a vacation rental under the name Hardy's Channel Lighthouse Retreat, and visitors can spend the night in it.[^1][^3] The best public view is from across the inlet. The Society notes a distant, partial view from Smith Road.[^1][^2] The Coast Guard's list of lights shows no range light here. It marks the channel with a set of buoys named for Hardy's Channel.[^6]
