East Brother Island is a small rock in San Pablo Strait, in Contra Costa County, California, where a white wooden Victorian light station was first lit in March 1874 to mark the passage between San Francisco Bay and San Pablo Bay.[^1][^2] A coal-fired steam whistle was the first fog signal, later replaced by diaphones, and the station was staffed until 1969; the buildings are now an inn.[^2][^3][^9]

## The place

San Pablo Strait is about two miles wide, and vessels bound for the Sacramento and San Joaquin rivers passed through it.[^1] In 1870 the Light-House Board asked for a light and fog signal at or near Point San Pablo, and in March 1871 Congress paid for one, a sum LighthouseFriends gives as $20,000.[^1] The National Register nomination gives a different figure, $30,000, and dates the request to use the island to February 1873.[^2]

The mainland site was the problem. The government went to court to buy 12.8 acres on the point; a jury set a price of $4,000, the owners appealed, and while the appeal dragged, boat captains petitioned the district's lighthouse inspector to build on East Brother instead, an island the government already owned, lying roughly 1,000 feet (305 m) off the point.[^1] The Secretary of War agreed to the use of the island on the condition that it "shall give way to fortifications whenever it shall be required for that purpose."[^1]

## Building the light

Work began in May 1873, when the top of the island was blasted away to make room.[^1] The plans called for a three-story tower attached to a two-story Victorian dwelling with three rooms on each floor, and the walls were filled between the studs with brick and mortar, which strengthened the building and dulled the noise of the fog signal.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society describes the design as the Stick Style developed by the architect Paul Pelz and used for several other West Coast lighthouses of the time, while the National Register nomination calls it "Carpenters Gothic" built under an East Coast lighthouse designer and names no architect.[^3][^2]

The nomination records the light as first exhibited on 1 March 1874 and the steam fog signal as put in operation on 1 May.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes the first lens as a revolving fourth-order Fresnel, and the first fog signal as alternating eight- and four-second blasts separated by twenty-four seconds of silence.[^1] According to the same source the keepers ran the signal whenever Red Rock, Point San Quentin or the Sisters were hidden in fog.[^1] The nomination adds that the original fog signals were coal-fired 12-inch steam whistles, with a large bell kept ready to be tolled every fifteen seconds if they failed or while steam was being raised.[^2]

## The keepers

The roster lists Samuel M. Farran as first head keeper, from 1874 to 1880, with John Cawley as first assistant.[^5] Charles F. Winsor, who took over in 1880, was head keeper for seven years.[^1] The station logbook, as quoted by LighthouseFriends, records the trouble he had with an assistant, Joseph Page, who made the mail run to San Quentin: on 2 January 1883 "Mr. Page took the mail over to San Quentin, returned drunk." Page resigned on 1 March 1883.[^1]

The long tenures were John O. Stenmark (1894 to 1914) and Willard D. Miller (1922 to 1942), twenty years each.[^5][^1] Stenmark, born in Stockholm, entered the Lighthouse Service as a sailor on the tender *Madrona*, according to the nomination, and had been keeper at Año Nuevo before he was appointed to East Brother on 31 August 1894.[^2][^1] He came with his wife Breta and their three-month-old daughter Annie.[^1] The family kept pigs, goats, chickens and rabbits, and grew vegetables and flowers in a small plot of earth that had been sacked and hauled to the island, according to the nomination, which drew on papers kept by Stenmark's grandson.[^2] Rainwater collected in the domed cistern supplied the island's needs until 1946, when the Coast Guard began bringing fuel and water by tanker.[^2]

Mail and doctors came from Point San Quentin, two and a half miles (4 km) away by rowboat; LighthouseFriends says Stenmark made that trip at least twice to fetch a doctor for a birth.[^1] The government sent a live-in teacher for the four children, until a road reached Point San Pablo and they rowed ashore to walk to school in Richmond.[^1][^2] Annie Stenmark married Charles Morisette, who had courted her for four years by rowing out to the island, in June 1914, the year her father retired.[^1][^2] John Stenmark died the next year aboard a steamer, after what is reported as an attack of heart trouble.[^1]

Miller stayed on in 1939 as a civilian when the Coast Guard took over the lighthouses.[^1] From then until 1969 the station was manned by four Coast Guardsmen working two at a time, 48 hours on and 48 off, in place of the families.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The reports of the Light-House Board give the fog signal's work in hours. In the annual reports for 1893 and 1894 (fiscal years ending 30 June) the whistle ran 161 and 178 hours, burning about 13 tons of coal each year; in 1899 it ran 267 hours and burned about 22 tons.[^6][^7][^8] In 1894 the board's report also records that the old, unsafe landing wharf was removed and replaced by a new one 60 feet (18 m) long and 20 feet (6 m) wide, on steel-shod piles driven into rock.[^7]

On 18 April 1906 the keeper, Stenmark, logged: "A heavy earthquake this morning at 5:15 A.M.." and that all the glass on the island, including the lenses, was broken.[^1] The nomination says the lens cracked and a replacement was ordered from Paris; LighthouseFriends instead dates a new revolving lens to 1912, so the date and source of the later lens are uncertain.[^2][^1]

Electricity reached the island by submarine cable in 1934.[^1] The nomination says the cable was sometimes cut by ships at the Point San Pablo wharves, and that a Coleman lantern hung ready in the tower for such nights.[^2] In November 1939 an anchor disabled the cable and the station ran on gasoline generators.[^1] At 2:50 a.m. on 4 March 1940 Miller went to the boathouse with a kerosene lantern to draw gasoline; he knocked the lantern over, and the fire set off the fifty-gallon drums. The assistant keeper, Earl Snodgrass, and his wife Lillian were woken by the explosions. With the telephone cut, a night watchman on the pier at Point San Pablo raised the alarm, and a Coast Guard crash boat left San Francisco's Pier 43 at 3:30 a.m. The wharf, the boathouse and four boats were lost.[^1]

## After the keepers

The light and fog signal were automated in 1969, and the Coast Guard planned to demolish the buildings and put a light on a pole.[^1][^4] The Contra Costa Shoreline Parks Committee campaigned against this, and the nomination it prepared (dated May 1970) led to listing on the National Register of Historic Places in 1971; the National Park Service's record gives 12 February 1971.[^1][^2][^10] The listing saved the buildings but not their upkeep, and for nearly a decade they decayed.[^1] The nomination also records that the automatic horn had at first been meant to run around the clock in the foggy season, but complaints led to its being triggered by a fog detector instead.[^2]

In 1979 a nonprofit, East Brother Light Station, Inc., was formed, and it restored the station under a licence from the Coast Guard; by late 1980 it was taking overnight guests.[^1][^3]

## Today

The island is reached by boat from the Point San Pablo Yacht Harbor.[^1] The station is run as a bed and breakfast inn; its own website says it has five guest rooms, that dinner and breakfast are served, and that day visits with boat transport and a guided tour are offered on summer Saturdays.[^9] The rooms are in the keeper's house and the fog signal building, and rainwater from the cistern is still the island's water.[^1] The lighthouse is owned by the Coast Guard and managed by the nonprofit.[^1] In April 2021 the cable that brought power to the island failed; volunteers spliced it in May and the inn reopened in September 2021.[^1]
