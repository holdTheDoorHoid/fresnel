Nash Island Light is a square brick tower on Nash Island, at the entrance to Pleasant Bay, off South Addison in Washington County, Maine. The first light on the island was shown in 1838. The brick tower that stands now dates from the 1870s. The light was discontinued in 1982, and it is now owned by a volunteer group, Friends of Nash Island Light.[^1][^2]

## The place

The light was set at the entrance to Pleasant Bay, where it marked several harbors and the western entrance to Moosabec Reach, a busy route for coastal trade.[^2] On 3 March 1837 Congress appropriated $5,000 for a light at the entrance to the Pleasant River. Four acres on Little Nash Island were then bought from the Nash family for $409.[^2]

## Building the light

The first tower was a rubblestone tower, 24 feet (7.3 m) tall, with a spiral stone stair. It was built on a ledge and first showed its light in 1838 from ten lamps set in thirteen-inch reflectors. A dwelling of rubble masonry with three rooms on the first floor and three chambers in the attic stood beside it.[^2]

In 1856 a Fresnel lens replaced the reflectors, LighthouseFriends says, after a new lantern was fitted in 1855.[^2] In 1873 Congress appropriated $3,000 "for rebuilding the upper part" of the light-house tower at Nash's island, coast of Maine.[^6] LighthouseFriends dates the present square brick tower, with its new cast-iron deck, parapet and lantern room, to 1873. The Society's record gives 1874.[^1][^2] The Society's record names a fourth-order Fresnel lens installed in 1874, and gives the light 57 feet (17.4 m) above mean high water.[^1] A frame boathouse, 12 by 20 feet (3.7 by 6.1 m), was added in 1878.[^2]

The Board's report for the fiscal year 1895 records an oil house built on the island. Its report for the fiscal year 1899 records new windows in the dwelling, minor repairs to the fuel house and boat slip, and some 465 feet (142 m) of wire fence.[^4][^5]

## The keepers

The archive lists 23 keeper stints at Nash Island, from 1838 to 1947.[^1] The first was Roy Means, who served from 1838 to 1841.[^1] John Wass kept the light twice, from 1841 to 1846 and from 1849 to 1853.[^1] An inspection in 1850 praised the station as being "in fine order", and LighthouseFriends quotes the inspector's report on him.[^2]

Enos D. Wass served from 1865 to 1872. LighthouseFriends says an inspector's letter of 1872 complained about the keeper at Nash Island, who lit his lamp with matches and let the light go out more than once, and that Enos Wass was dismissed that year.[^2]

Charles S. Holt was keeper from 1883 to 1902. That stint and John E. Purington's, below, are the longest on the roster, at nineteen years each.[^1] In 1892 Holt wrote to ask for a raise. LighthouseFriends quotes the letter, which described the bell put up at the station and said he ended each year no better off than he began.[^2]

John E. Purington was appointed in 1916 and stayed until 1935.[^1][^2] He had nine children, LighthouseFriends says, and the children helped with the fog bell and the brass. Two of his sons, Robert and Raymond, appear in the Lighthouse Service reports (see the next chapter).[^2][^7]

Edwin A. Pettegrow served from 1935 to 1943, Edgar M. Wallace from 1944 to 1945, and Harry B. Dobbins from 1945 to 1947. Larson O. Alley is the last name on the roster, for 1947.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Board's fog-signal report for the year to June 1893 records what the keepers near each whistle said about how well it carried. The keeper at Nash Island, eight miles north-north-east of Petit Manan, said the Petit Manan whistle was "easily heard there" in a north-east or east wind, but "not heard generally in a strong S. or SW. wind, on account of the surf."[^3]

The Society's record dates the fog bell tower to 1882. LighthouseFriends dates it to 1887, when a wooden pyramidal tower was built to hold the bell. It quotes Holt's 1892 letter as saying the striking machine had to be wound every five hours, night or day.[^1][^2]

LighthouseFriends says the schooner *Maine* lost its foremast in a squall in 1916, and that the keeper towed it three miles to safety with the station's boat.[^2]

On 21 June 1917 John E. Purington, keeper of Nash Island, helped remove freight from a Rockland freighter that ran ashore near the light. He furnished food and lodging to the crew until 24 June.[^8]

On 20 December 1920 a Robert Purington, whom the Bulletin calls keeper, helped tow a disabled power boat to safety.[^9] The Bulletin's index also lists him as keeper.[^10] The Commissioner's report for the fiscal year 1921 names Robert and Raymond as sons of the keeper, John E. Purington, but prints the surname as "Purlington". It says the two of them towed a disabled power boat, with passengers aboard, to safe anchorage.[^7] That appears to be the same event the Bulletin reports for December 1920. The two reports therefore disagree about whether Robert was the keeper or one of the keeper's sons.[^7][^9]

## After the keepers

In 1947 the light was automated. LighthouseFriends says the characteristic changed from fixed red to a white flash every ten seconds, and the fog bell was discontinued.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends also reports, from a family member's recollection, that not long after the change the Coast Guard burned the dwelling and the bell tower. When the fire threatened the island's pasture, the family member had to telephone until the Coast Guard sent a crew from the cutter *Snohomish* to put it out.[^2] The Society's record says the other station buildings were dismantled.[^1]

The light was discontinued in 1982 and replaced by an offshore lighted buoy.[^1][^2] In 1997 the Friends of Nash Island Light was granted ownership of the tower under Maine's Lights Program, and volunteers repointed the brick and whitewashed the tower.[^2] They have since pressure-washed and recoated it.[^2]

## Today

The tower is owned by Friends of Nash Island Light, and it is not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the light is best seen by boat, and that some sightseeing tours pass it. The island is part of Petit Manan National Wildlife Refuge. It is closed from April through August to protect nesting seabirds, and the grounds open seasonally. The tower is closed.[^2]
