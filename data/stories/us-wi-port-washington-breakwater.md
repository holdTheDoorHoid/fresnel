A steel tower on a concrete base, with large arches in its faces, marks the end of the north breakwater at Port Washington, Wisconsin, on Lake Michigan. It replaced a wooden pier light of 1889, and the town has made the tower's outline its emblem.[^1][^2]

## The place

Port Washington has no natural harbour. A National Register nomination explains that this stretch of Lake Michigan shore between Milwaukee and Sheboygan is mostly steep bluffs, and the mouth of Sauk Creek was the only place where a commercial harbour could be made.[^2] The first lighthouse, built in 1849, stood on the north bluff above the town, and in 1860 a new light was built there on top of a keeper's house.[^1] The government harbour works that followed produced two parallel piers; the north pier was finished in 1888.[^1][^2]

The harbour was never safe in a blow. The nomination says that two vessels, the schooner *Bohemia* and the steamer *Toledo*, were wrecked within a month of each other in the fall of 1856 after their anchors failed in high seas within sight of the town.[^2]

## Building the light

In its report for 1888, as quoted by LighthouseFriends, the Lighthouse Board said the north pier "should now be lighted" and recommended an open-framework tower at its outer end, showing a sixth-order red light and fuelled by gasoline so that an 800-foot elevated walk would not have to be built. An appropriation of $16,000 covered this and three other pierhead lights on the lakes.[^1] Bids in spring 1889 were judged too high and rejected, so the material and labour were bought on the open market. The light was put in operation on the night of 15 September 1889.[^1] The nomination describes a wooden pyramidal tower with an enclosed service room under an octagonal cast-iron lantern, the light fixed red at 42 feet 6 inches (13 m) above the lake.[^2]

The tower that stands today belongs to the 1930s. In the early Depression a power company chose the shore just south of the channel for a $30,000,000 steam-electric plant, to be fed with coal by ship, 250,000 tons a year at first and up to 700,000 later, and the old harbour could not take such ships.[^2] The government built a 2,535-foot (780 m) north breakwater in 1934, and the 1889 pierhead light and the old north pier were demolished.[^2] After the 1889 light came down in August 1934, a temporary light guided mariners, and work on the new tower began in the spring of 1935.[^2]

The local *Port Washington Pilot*, quoted in the nomination, reported on 28 March 1935 that the "square steel lighthouse, set atop four heavy concrete arches" was about half finished by the Wednesday after work began, the work being done by the crew of the lighthouse tender *Hyacinth* with "a few men of this city".[^2] On 15 August it said the light would throw "an 18,000 candlepower beam, produced by two 500 watt bulbs, from eight bull lenses", turning to show a red flash every seven and a half seconds, with light and foghorn worked by remote control from the old lighthouse on the hill.[^2] The Lighthouse Service announced the work in its *Bulletin* as "a structure 78 feet in height", with an air diaphone.[^4] The nomination gives 78 feet (24 m) as the focal plane of today's optic, and the *Pilot* put the tower "over 60 feet above the normal water line", so the sources do not agree on the height.[^2] The nomination describes a 16-foot (4.9 m) concrete base under a steel superstructure it gives as 40 feet (12 m) in one place and 46 feet (14 m) in another, and calls the first of eight standard Great Lakes lights of this design, built between 1934 and 1950.[^2] Sources differ on the cost: the nomination gives $35,000 and LighthouseFriends about $38,000.[^2][^1]

## The keepers

The 1889 pierhead light had a keeper of its own for only part of its life. The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists Charles H. Lewis, Jr. as keeper of the pierhead from 1889 to 1924, and also as head keeper of the 1860 light from 1880 to 1903, with assistants there from 1889.[^5][^6] The nomination says an assistant was hired once the harbour had two lights to tend.[^2] After the light was electrified in 1924, it adds, Lewis retired but stayed on in the 1860 house as caretaker until 1934, while Lighthouse Service staff from Milwaukee maintained the pierhead.[^2]

The 1935 tower was built to be tended from the shore. The nomination calls it unmanned, and the *Pilot* said it would be operated by remote control from the old lighthouse.[^2] The archive nevertheless lists keepers for the breakwater light from 1935 to 1970, beginning with Arthur S. Almquist as head keeper and Lester O. Struble as first assistant.[^3] LighthouseFriends says Almquist lived in the old 1860 lighthouse from 1935 and was in charge of the breakwater light until 1942, while the archive gives 1946, and that Coast Guard personnel later lived there and looked after the breakwater light.[^1][^3] Later head keepers in the archive include Delphis J. Pelletier, Jr. (1948 to 1958) and Charles W. Graham (1958 to 1962).[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends records that a schooner (unnamed) collided with the pierhead light in 1907 and destroyed its gallery railing.[^1] On 7 March 1921 the City of Port Washington set up a fog signal on the pierhead's balcony, an air siren sounding a seven-second blast every thirty seconds, which the city maintained.[^1]

The nomination quotes a federal report saying the harbour piers were "extensively damaged during the severe storms of 1929", and the north pier began sliding into the channel.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service *Bulletin* for 1924 lists the pierhead as changed "oil to acetylene, fixed to flashing", whereas LighthouseFriends and the nomination say it was electrified in 1924.[^7][^1][^2] The 1935 light was automated later: the nomination says in 1976, and the Society's archive says 1975.[^2][^3] The work took out the original lantern, the rotating optic, and the Type CC diaphone, which the nomination says could be heard 18 miles at sea in good weather; a plastic optic in the open air and a weaker horn replaced them.[^2] The Coast Guard declared the light excess in May 2015, and the City of Port Washington received the deed in April 2018.[^1] The light was added to the National Register of Historic Places in December 2018.[^1]

Ozaukee Press reported in 2025 that the tower had deteriorated after the city took it over, until a $2.2 million state Transportation Alternative Program grant paid for restoration.[^8] LighthouseFriends records the work by TMI Coatings in 2025.[^1]

## Today

The light is owned by the City of Port Washington and is still an active aid to navigation: the nomination gives a one-second red flash every six seconds, visible seven miles on a clear day, and a horn of two blasts every 30 seconds, worked by radio and powered from a solar array.[^2] The tower stands at the outer end of a federal breakwater and is not open to the public.[^3] During the 2025 work the city fenced the breakwater's east end, and a cage was being fitted over the access ladder so that people could not climb onto the structure.[^8] The 1860 light on the hill, a separate National Register property, is run by the Port Washington Historical Society as a museum, with a tower climb on tours; its page showed it closed for the season, reopening on 30 June 2027.[^9]
