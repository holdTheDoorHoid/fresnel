Monhegan Island Light stands on Lighthouse Hill, the highest point of a small island with a fishing economy, about ten miles southwest of Port Clyde in the Atlantic off Maine. A light has shone there since 1824, and the keeper's house is now a museum.[^1][^2][^8]

## The place

Congress appropriated $3,000 in 1822 to establish a lighthouse and keeper's dwelling on Monhegan Island,[^1] the National Register nomination says; Lighthouse Friends gives the date of the appropriation as 7 May 1822.[^2] Later that year Josiah and Mary Starling sold the government a parcel at the summit of what was then called Lookout Hill.[^2]

The island's distance from the mainland shaped the keeper's life. Samuel Albee took charge in 1841 and, after a year at the light, wrote to his superiors that the station was "twelve miles from the nearest point of main land," and that the government had given him no boat "except a small skiff."[^2]

## Building the light

Construction began in the spring of 1824, and the light was first lit on 2 July of that year.[^2] The nomination agrees on that date and names Thomas B. Seavey as the first keeper, who occupied the quarters with his family.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends reports that the 1824 light showed alternating red and white flashes, the first coloured light in Maine. Five lamps on one side of a revolving chandelier faced five behind red glass on the other, and because the red glass made the frame unbalanced, the keeper hung a 24-pound cannonball opposite it.[^2]

By 1842, that source says, the lantern leaked and iced inside, and frost heaves had tipped the tower so that the light no longer turned properly. A $6,000 contract in 1850 replaced the old stone tower.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society archive likewise gives 1850 for the present granite tower and 1856 for a second-order Fresnel lens.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says the lens was installed in 1857.[^2] The Light-House Board's report of 1899 lists the light as "Second order, flashing white every minute."[^9]

The sources differ over the tower's size. The National Register nomination, which dates the lighthouse to 1824, describes a granite tower forty-seven feet (14 m) high that tapers as it rises,[^1] and the archive gives the same 47 feet from base to lantern, at 178 feet above high water.[^3] Lighthouse Friends gives 38 feet for the first tower and 30 feet for its 1850 replacement, which does not match the other two sources.[^2]

A fog signal station was set up in 1855 on Manana Island, just west of Monhegan.[^2] The Light-House Board's 1893 report records its first-class Daboll trumpet running 1,038 hours in the year and burning about 13 tons of coal.[^5] In 1877, Lighthouse Friends adds, a telegraph wire joined the Manana keeper's house to the lighthouse.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive lists 26 head keepers between 1824 and 1960, beginning with Seavey (1824-1834) and ending with Robert E. Libby in 1960.[^3]

In that letter Albee asked for a raise. The light turned by clockwork, and when the clock stopped the keeper had to go out "through a long winter night, and turn the lamp frame by hand," on a salary he called as low as any shore keeper's.[^2]

Joseph F. Humphrey took over on 29 March 1861 with his wife, Betsy, and eleven children, according to Lighthouse Friends. He died on 5 December of that year. Betsy, helped by a son and Assistant Keeper Elisha B. Davis, took charge, and was formally appointed keeper the following April.[^2] The archive lists her as Betsey G. Morrow Humphrey, head keeper from 1862 to 1880.[^3] The same account says her son Albert was killed in the Civil War in 1864, and that she resigned in 1880. Her son-in-law Sidney G. Studley succeeded her.[^2]

William Stanley held the light from 1883 to 1902, and Daniel Stevens from 1902 to 1919.[^3]

## Changes to the station

A frame dwelling for an assistant keeper was added to the station in 1857 and occupied that year by David N. Bond, Lighthouse Friends says.[^2] The government provided $5,000 in 1874 for a two-story frame addition to the stone keeper's house.[^2] The Light-House Board's volume for 1891-92 records that "The old stone house" was demolished and a frame ell, 17 by 20 feet, built on its site.[^4] The report for 1895 notes storm porches built on each dwelling,[^6] and the 1900 report a boundary fence of 1,122 feet and a new plank walk at the keeper's dwelling.[^7]

Lighthouse Friends reports that the light was changed from oil to incandescent oil vapour on 25 September 1912, and that the assistant keeper's post was abolished in 1922.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1959 the old kerosene lamp and its clockwork were replaced by a generator, controlled remotely from the fog signal station on Manana.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the Fresnel lens was dismantled then and that its fate is not known.[^2] The last keeper left in early 1960, and the Manana fog signal stayed staffed for several more years.[^2]

The buildings were declared surplus and sold at auction on 1 June 1962 to Monhegan Associates, Inc., a conservation group formed in 1954.[^1] Part of the keeper's house opened to visitors in the summer of 1966, and the buildings have been open every summer since 1968.[^1] The tower itself passed to the Monhegan Historical and Cultural Museum Association in 1998, and the assistant keeper's house was rebuilt on its old site in 1997.[^2]

## Today

The keeper's house is home to the Monhegan Museum, on Lighthouse Hill. The museum's own page for the 2026 season gives opening from 15 June to 30 September, with lighthouse tours on Tuesdays, Thursdays and Sundays.[^8] Lighthouse Friends lists the grounds and dwelling as open and the tower as closed, so the museum's current notice is the one to trust.[^2] The light is still active, with a solar-powered optic, and the Coast Guard still maintains a light in the lantern.[^2][^3] The island is reached by ferry and visitors should check the museum before planning a visit.[^2][^8]
