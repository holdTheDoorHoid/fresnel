The Nantucket Cliff Range Lights were a pair of small wooden beacons that guided ships into Nantucket Harbor from 1838 or 1839 until 1907 or 1908.[^1][^2] Both towers still stand, on private land on Pawguvnet Lane, where the town calls them the Bug Lights.[^1][^2]

## The place

The beacons stood at Cliff Beach.[^3] They worked with the range lights on Brant Point and a set of buoys to bring vessels into the channel.[^3] LighthouseFriends reports that when Lieutenant Edward Carpender inspected Nantucket's lights in 1838, he noted two beacons being erected at the head of Brant Point, opposite the best water on the bar.[^2] The Town of Nantucket gives the service as 1839 to 1907, a year later than the 1838 start in the other accounts.[^1][^2]

## Building the light

The beacons were small frame buildings, lit by a tin lamp with four wicks.[^2] The first keeper, Peleg Easton, was hired at a salary of $300 a year, with a dwelling provided.[^2] In his report of 1842, as LighthouseFriends prints it, Easton said the two beacons had used 151 gallons of oil in the past year. He also reported that high tides came up to the floors of his house and undermined the hearths.[^2] He put the blame on the foundations: the stone under the house, he wrote, lay on the surface of the sand, and he thought that sinking it two or three feet below the sand would have prevented the hearths from settling.[^2]

In 1889 the front beacon was rebuilt and the rear beacon was made taller.[^2] In 1903 the wooden pyramidal towers were replaced by the conical wooden towers that stand today, and a brick oil house was added.[^2] The Lighthouse Board's 1899 index still listed the pair, as numbers 149 and 150, under the name Nantucket Cliff.[^5]

## The keepers

Peleg Easton kept the lights from 1838 until his death in 1852.[^2] His wife, Mary, then took over the duties and stayed until 1856.[^2][^4] An inspection in 1850 called Easton a "fine old man", found that one tower had been moved to suit the channel, and noted that a wooden platform had been laid at the dwelling.[^2]

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists nine principal keepers on its record for the front light of the pair, from Peleg Easton in 1838 to George E. Dolby, who served from 1902 to 1908.[^4] The archive gives names and years only.

## After the keepers

The sources do not agree on the end of the service. The Town's page ends it in 1907, and the Preservation Trust says the lights were decommissioned in 1908.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends gives 14 July 1908, the day a new set of range lights was set up on Brant Point.[^2]

In 1912 the discontinued front light and the keeper's tool house were sold to Ellenwood B. Coleman.[^2] In 1921 Frank B. Gilbreth, Sr., an industrial engineer, bought the property from the Coleman estate for $1,840, and the lights were turned into living quarters.[^2][^3][^1] The Preservation Trust says the family had twelve children, and that the towers became dormitories for them.[^3] LighthouseFriends counts eleven children.[^2]

The taller tower was moved by horse to the family's lot.[^3] The sources do not date the move. The Preservation Trust mentions it in the same paragraph as the larger cottage that replaced the toolhouse by 1952.[^3] LighthouseFriends, quoting Frank Jr.'s memory of the day when he was ten, places the tower just behind the family's cottage, which it dates to 1952.[^2]

## Today

The Town says the towers are privately owned and not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends notes that the pair is privately owned, and that the grounds and towers are closed.[^2] The Preservation Trust says the towers are still in the Gilbreth family.[^3] In 1991 Frank Gilbreth Jr. wrote that the family had owned the towers for seventy years without once having to replace the shingles on their sides.[^3]
