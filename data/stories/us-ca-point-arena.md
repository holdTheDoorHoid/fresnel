A 115-foot (35 m) concrete tower stands at the tip of Point Arena, on the Mendocino County coast of California. It is the second tower on the site: the brick tower first lit in 1870 was wrecked by the earthquake of April 1906, and the concrete one that replaced it began shining in 1908.[^1][^2]

## The place

The light station covers about six acres at the very end of the point, which rises sixty feet (18 m) above the Pacific and is surrounded by the ocean on three sides.[^1] In the 1860s the coast here grew busier; more goods and settlers arrived, and more products, lumber especially, were shipped out. Storms and thick fog made the passage dangerous, and the National Register nomination says an increasing number of ships foundered or ran aground near Point Arena. The nomination ties the station above all to ships entering and leaving Arena Cove.[^1]

## Building the light

Work on the first tower began in the summer of 1869. According to Lighthouse Friends, which quotes the Light-House Board's report for that year, the stone of the point was judged unsuitable for building, so the builders fired about half a million bricks in three kilns beside the point and brought 114,000 better ones from San Francisco for the tower's outer courses.[^3] The tower was about 100 feet (30 m) tall and held a fixed first-order Fresnel lens; the same source gives the first lighting as 1 May 1870.[^3]

The station's fog signal has a more tangled dating. The National Register nomination calls the fog signal station, dated 1870, the oldest building in the district.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says Congress funded a steam fog signal on 3 March 1871 and that its twelve-inch whistle first sounded on 25 November 1871.[^3] The building that stands today is later than either date. By the Light-House Board's report for 1896 the sea had cut into the point until the fog signal stood on a bluff joined to the rest of the station by a narrow neck of land, "in danger of being entirely cut off, if not destroyed," and a new building was being put up about 125 feet (38 m) farther back.[^4] The following year's report records the fog-signal house as completed in August 1896.[^5]

## The keepers

The USLHS Digital Archive lists Thomas Blackmore as the first head keeper, from 1870 to 1874, with John Emerson as his first assistant.[^6] Over the years the roster grows to well over two hundred entries, because the station kept a head keeper and up to three assistants. In 1875 the archive also lists an Amanda A. Blackmore as a third assistant.[^6]

The sources give a few glimpses of the work. In the year covered by the Board's 1895 report the steam whistle ran some 1,452 hours and burned about 126 cords of wood.[^7] In 1896-97 a wire screen was put up on the north side of the lantern "to protect it from flying wild fowl."[^5] In November 1898 a telephone line was strung on redwood poles to the town of Point Arena, ending in the keeper's quarters at the station and in a hotel in town, whose proprietor was to pass on any message of importance.[^8] The road was a long-running problem: Congress appropriated $3,000 in October 1913 to finish the unfinished portion of the government road from Rollerville to the lighthouse, and the work was completed on 1 August 1914.[^9]

Richard H. Williams was head keeper from 1902 to 1919, seventeen years on the archive's list.[^6] Peter S. Admiral was head keeper from 1921 to 1926, and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1924-1929 index records him as keeper at Point Arena awarded an efficiency flag.[^6][^10]

William (Bill) Owens served at the station from 1937 to 1952, becoming head keeper in 1945.[^6] Lighthouse Friends recounts that he and his wife Cora kept a Jersey cow, chickens and a garden to cut the family's food bill, and that when the cow went missing and was found on a ledge down the bluff near the fog signal building, the keepers hauled her back up with a block and tackle and the family's car. During the Second World War, Lighthouse Friends adds, keepers were required to report what they saw from the watch; Owens thought he saw a submarine off the point.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The keepers' log recorded earthquakes early. Lighthouse Friends says the first was entered on 7 June 1880, and that a keeper described the 1887 shock as having "sounded like a train of cars going over a bridge."[^3]

On 22 November 1896 the steamer San Benito was wrecked near Point Arena. Keeper Jefferson M. Brown, head keeper from 1895 to 1901, tried to launch a boat through the surf to reach the crew. Lighthouse Friends describes two attempts and a third that was abandoned, while the Life-Saving Service's list says he went out three times, so the sources differ on the count.[^3][^11][^6] The Life-Saving Service awarded him a gold medal on 17 October 1899, its list describing "heroic daring in attempting to rescue the crew" and noting that he went out "three times in an unseaworthy boat, only to be hurled back by the force of the seas."[^11] The same list credits a Captain Strand with standing by the wreck in a dangerous sea and sending a boat to it several times, saving the twenty-five people who had stayed with the vessel.[^11] Lighthouse Friends names the two men who went out with Brown as Sam Miller and Lazar Poznanovich, and says the crew had been stranded for thirty-six hours before a passing steamer took them off.[^3] The Life-Saving Service list also carries an entry for Lazar Poznanovich of Elk.[^11]

The earthquake of 18 April 1906 did far worse. George R. Putnam, writing in 1917, judged that of the light stations on the Pacific coast Point Arena "suffered most," with the brick tower and the first-order lens wrecked.[^12] Lighthouse Digest names the men on duty as head keeper Richard H. Williams, first assistant Oscar Newlin and second assistant Malcolm Cady, whose name it spells Malcom; the USLHS roster agrees on the three men.[^13][^6] A keeper's log entry, as quoted by Lighthouse Digest, says the tower "quivered for a few seconds, went far over to the north, came back, and then swung north again, repeating this several times," while the lenses and reflectors in the lantern "were shaken from their settings and fell in a shower upon the iron floor."[^13] Lighthouse Digest adds that the tower was left cracked and holed where bricks had fallen, and that a black bear, apparently frightened by the quake, charged toward the station and was shot.[^13] Lighthouse Friends reports the same bear.[^3]

## After the keepers

A makeshift wooden tower carrying the old lantern was lit on 5 January 1907.[^13] Congress had provided $72,500 on 30 June 1906 for the rebuilding.[^3] Because the Lighthouse Service wanted a structure that could stand an earthquake, the work went to the Concrete Chimney Company of San Francisco, which built the tower of reinforced concrete: the first lighthouse of that material in California, according to the nomination. The circular stair was taken from the 1870 tower and reused.[^1][^13] A new first-order lens, floating on a bath of mercury and flashing twice every six seconds, was lit on 15 September 1908.[^1][^3][^14] In place of the single large dwelling, four separate bungalows were built for the keepers.[^3]

Later notices show the station keeping pace with the service. In 1919 the Bulletin announced that the fog signal at Point Arena had been changed from an air siren to an air diaphone,[^15] and in 1925 the master of the steamship Richmond reported seeing the light on the night of 14 April from 40 miles away.[^16][^17] Lighthouse Friends says control passed to the Coast Guard in 1939, when the whole concrete tower was painted white; in 1960 the bungalows were razed for four ranch-style houses.[^3] The fog signal was silenced in 1975 and replaced with a radio beacon,[^14] and in June 1977 an automated beacon was set on the tower's balcony and the first-order lens retired.[^14] The USLHS roster's last entries run to 1977.[^6]

Local volunteers took the station over: Point Arena Lighthouse Keepers, Inc. obtained a lease in 1984 and became the owner in November 2000.[^3][^14] Lighthouse Friends dates the National Register listing to 1991.[^3] In 2008 renovations costing 1.6 million dollars repaired the concrete, and the Fresnel lens was moved into the fog signal building.[^3]

## Today

The light station is owned by Point Arena Lighthouse Keepers, a non-profit. Its own site describes a store and museums and says the tower is open for tours; hours change with the season, so check before going.[^14] The old keepers' houses are rented as vacation homes.[^14] The tower still carries an automated light.[^14] Lighthouse Friends reports that the first-order lens was moved into the fog signal building in 2008.[^3]
