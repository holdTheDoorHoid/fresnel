Gull Harbour is a small sheltered bay at the northeast tip of Hecla Island, in Lake Winnipeg, Manitoba. Two wooden lighthouses stand on the spit of land beside it, a short one from 1898 and a tall one on a steel frame, dated to the 1920s or to 1930 depending on the source. Lighthouse Friends calls them the only heritage lighthouses in Manitoba, designated under the federal Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act.[^1]

## The place

Lake Winnipeg narrows near its middle. At the southern end of that narrow stretch lie Hecla Island (also called Big Island) and Black Island, less than two kilometres apart, and Gull Harbour sits on Hecla's northeast tip.[^1] Icelandic immigrants settled Hecla Island in 1876, and the community fished. The Canadian Register of Historic Places, in the statement of significance for the 1898 tower, calls commercial fishing the main economic activity of this Icelandic settlement and Gull Harbour a strategic centre for the lake's fisheries, with the light serving fishing boats and barges running between the lake ports, Selkirk and Winnipeg.[^2]

## Building the light

In 1898 the Department of Marine announced two new lights on this part of the lake, one at Gull Harbour and one on Black Bear Island, to serve growing steamboat traffic.[^1] According to the account Lighthouse Friends gives, drawn from the department's report, both were square wooden towers with sloping walls and square wooden lanterns, painted white. The contractor was John W. Scott of Selkirk, whose price for the Gull Harbour tower was $380, and the fixed white lights went into service in the autumn of 1898.[^1] The register describes the surviving tower as wood-framed and shingled, 8.2 metres (26.9 ft) from base to vane, with a pedimented door on its south side.[^3]

The tower was low. A taller replacement lifted the light from roughly eight metres above the water to 27.4 metres.[^1] The date of that second tower is not settled. The Canadian Register of Historic Places names it the Gull Harbour (1926) Lighthouse and says the first was decommissioned in 1926;[^4] Lighthouse Friends gives 1926 on one page and 1930 on another;[^1][^5] and the Heritage Guide's header says built 1929, while its text says the 1920s.[^6] The second tower is a square tapered wooden tower on a four-segment steel base, 23.5 metres (77 ft) high in the register's description, and it was built so that its light could be seen up to 17 nautical miles (31.5 km) away.[^6]

## The keepers

August Magnusson was the first keeper, appointed in 1898 at $150 a year, and the Department's 1901 list of lightkeepers still shows him at Gull Harbour.[^1][^7] Lighthouse Friends says he served through 1903, after which Thor. Fjeldsted took charge until 1924.[^1] The department's own lists agree that Fjeldsted was there in 1906 and 1909, and a table of 1911-12 gives his appointment date as 6 May 1904 (matched by column position).[^8][^9][^10] He appears under the spelling "T. Fjeldsted" in the 1915-16 list, still at Gull Harbour.[^11] After him, per Lighthouse Friends, came B. Halldorson and finally Olafur "Oli" Grimolfson, the last resident keeper. Lighthouse Friends' list of keepers gives Halldorson as 1924 to at least 1937 and Grimolfson as 1948 to 1961.[^1]

Grimolfson was offered the job not long after returning from service in the Second World War. He and his wife Eunice raised three daughters and a son at the light.[^1] His daughter Charlynne recalled a cow, chickens and a garden, and a barrel of ice buried on the point to keep meat through summer.[^1] Twice a day he climbed the tower to check that the mercury bath was full and the light running, and in fog he went into the original 1898 tower to start the foghorn.[^1] A departmental list for 1911-12, decades before his time, shows a hand fog horn at Gull Harbour; the sources do not say whether it was the same equipment.[^12]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Charlynne, one of the Grimolfson daughters, remembered one stormy night (no year or vessel is given) when visibility closed in and her parents were woken by a bright light shining into their dwelling: a large boat was heading straight for the land and the house. Oli ran to the short tower to sound the foghorn while Eunice waved two lanterns near the house. "It turned just at the right time," Charlynne said, and the vessel did not touch shore.[^1]

## After the keepers

Once commercial electricity reached Hecla Island, a resident keeper was no longer needed and the Grimolfsons left the island. Helgi Jones looked after the automated light part-time from 1962 to 1970.[^1] In 2016 both towers were designated heritage lighthouses under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act of 2008.[^1] The Province of Manitoba restored the 1898 tower in 2019, with new wooden shingles and fresh paint.[^1]

## Today

Both towers stand in Hecla-Grindstone Provincial Park, the 1926 tower and the 1898 tower side by side at the end of the spit, and the Manitoba Historical Society says both are tended by the Canadian Coast Guard. It also notes a third lighthouse near the boat marina.[^13] The 1926 tower can be seen from the marina across the harbour.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says the lighthouse is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard, with the grounds open and the tower closed, and that it is a 1.4 km walk from Lakeview Resort Hecla.[^1] View the towers from outside.
