Farallon Island Light stands on the summit of Southeast Farallon Island, about 30 miles west of the Golden Gate Bridge.[^11][^2] It was the sixth lighthouse lit on the West Coast, and the island it stands on is closed to the public today.[^2][^11]

## The place

Spaniards gave the islands the name Los Farallones. The largest, Southeast Farallon Island, rises 358 feet (109 m), and the light stands on its summit.[^2] The distance from the Golden Gate is given two ways: Putnam, writing in 1917, says the light is "twenty-three miles off the Golden Gate," and the refuge gives 30 miles from the Golden Gate Bridge.[^13][^11]

## Building the light

Major Hartman Bache, who took charge of lighthouses on the West Coast in 1855, visited the island on 5 July of that year. He found that the recently completed tower on the summit was too small for a first-order Fresnel lens, and that it would have to be torn down and rebuilt on a nearby site.[^2] The archive gives 1855 as the year the tower and the first-order lens were installed.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the light was first lit on 1 January 1856 by Head Keeper Nerva N. Wines and Assistant John W. Wines, who were paid salaries of $1,000 and $650 a year.[^2] The first-order lens had eight flash panels and turned once every eight minutes. Its clockwork had to be wound every four and a half hours.[^2] By 1918 the lens sometimes stopped on its track, so a nine-pound (4.1 kg) balance wheel was added to the clockwork to keep it turning.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says a Victorian duplex was built on the island in 1878, and an identical one in 1880, for the island's four resident keepers and their families.[^2] The archive dates the keepers' quarters to 1876.[^1]

After workers staged a sit-down strike, a mule was brought to the island to help pack supplies up the slope. LighthouseFriends says the mule, named Jack, stayed for eighteen years.[^2]

The fog signal came later. In 1858 the ship Lucas foundered on the island during dense fog. Bache then built a whistle over a natural blowhole, in 1858-59, according to Arnold B. Johnson's 1889 history, which prints Bache's own description.[^2][^12] The surf that drove the whistle did not always coincide with the periods of dense fog, and a storm in 1875 blew the chimney off its foundation.[^2] A steam siren began service on 20 September 1880, was converted to oil in 1907, and an air diaphone replaced it in 1918.[^2]

The Board's reports measured the siren in hours. In the fiscal year ending June 1894 it ran "some 1,360 hours," and in the fiscal year ending June 1895 "some 1,421 hours." The fiscal years ending June 1899 and June 1900 show "some 1,068 hours" and "some 989 hours."[^3][^4][^5][^6] In the fiscal year ending June 1895 the pumps that fed the boilers "broke down, thus disabling the signal."[^4] In the fiscal year ending June 1893 "the derrick was replaced."[^3] In the fiscal year ending June 1896 the Board reported that "Great difficulty has at certain times of the year been experienced in landing at the North Landing."[^15] In 1912 the Lighthouse Service's bulletin listed Farallon among the stations with an "Illuminant changed to incandescent oil vapor."[^14]

## The keepers

The archive's roster for the island has 312 entries from 1854 to 1972: 35 head keepers, 7 acting or temporary keepers, 217 assistants, 3 laborers and 50 Coast Guard entries.[^1] The first entry is James Powers, for 1854 and 1855.[^1] LighthouseFriends names Nerva N. Wines, who served from 1855 to 1859, as the first head keeper of the lit tower.[^1][^2]

John Kunder was head keeper in two stints, from 1914 to 1920 and from 1924 to 1931.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says there were times when he did not leave the island for four years at a stretch.[^2] Before the radio came, the island had no church, store, telephone or electric light, and it relied on weekly visitors for all its news. A mail delivery was the event people looked forward to.[^2] A radio receiver was installed at the naval radio station. LighthouseFriends quotes Kunder, speaking in 1929 about the radio: "It is our godsend, our light."[^2] Strong gales, LighthouseFriends says, picked up small pieces of granite and sandblasted the buildings, which kept the painters busy.[^2]

William A. Beeman was head keeper from 1890 to 1900.[^1] On Christmas Day 1898 Royal Beeman, the eleven-year-old son of Keeper William A. Beeman, became gravely ill.[^2] After a violent storm had been lashing the island for days, his parents decided the only chance was to get him to a hospital in San Francisco. They went in the station's fourteen-foot (4.3 m) rowboat, which had been rigged with a homemade sail, and Assistant Keeper Louis Engelbrecht went with them.[^2] They reached the San Francisco Lightship eight hours after leaving the island, and a pilot boat rushed the party on to San Francisco.[^2] Royal died on 3 January 1899.[^2]

Benjamin O. Cameron, an assistant keeper in 1899 and 1900, sent out a letter to numerous eligible women while he was looking for a wife. LighthouseFriends says the letter somehow reached the San Francisco Call.[^2][^1]

Frank W. Ritchie was head keeper from 1931 to 1936.[^1] The Lighthouse Service's bulletin reports that on 9 September 1935 a broadcast from the island, made through the National Broadcasting Company, included an interview with the keeper, F. W. Ritchie.[^10]

Supplies came by boat. The Board's report for the fiscal year ending June 1900 says the gasoline schooner Ida A had been arranged to stop at the San Francisco light-vessel every other week, on her way to and from the Farallon Islands, to carry mail, men and supplies.[^6] The Weather Bureau set up a radio station on the island in 1902, and the Navy in 1913.[^2] When the Second World War began, the island's population had grown to more than seventy.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lucas foundered on the island in 1858.[^2] A storm in 1875 took the fog-signal chimney.[^2] In 1895 the signal failed when the pumps broke down, as noted above.[^4]

The Beeman boat trip of Christmas 1898 ended in Royal Beeman's death on 3 January 1899.[^2]

The Lighthouse Service kept a gun and life-saving equipment on the island by 1930. The bulletin says the equipment was operated by the lighthouse personnel, with Navy personnel assisting at the radio-compass station.[^9]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service had protected the reservation for some years before 1916, when its bulletin reported that the bird life had increased.[^7] After the war the Navy left the island, and the Coast Guard, which had taken over the lighthouse in 1939, stayed until 1972.[^2]

The top of the tower was shortened and its lens removed. LighthouseFriends says a plaque by the tower records a reconstruction in 1969, and that an automated aero beacon was placed on the shortened tower.[^2] The archive lists two cisterns, an oil house, a radio beacon and a generator building from the military occupation, and the original brick keepers' houses, which were razed in 1969.[^1] The Bulletin of 1925 gives the light's candlepower as 280,000 and its "geographical range" as 26 miles.[^16][^8]

## Today

The Fish and Wildlife Service says the refuge "is closed to the public due to the sensitivity of wildlife," and that it was "established in 1909 to protect seabirds and marine mammals."[^11]

The archive lists the light as an active aid to navigation, with a VEGA VRB optic, on a conical tower without a lantern.[^1] The original first-order lens is gone from the tower.[^2] The archive lists the island as not open to the public.[^1]
