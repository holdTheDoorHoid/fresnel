Île aux Perroquets is a small rock at the western end of the Mingan Archipelago, on the north shore of the Gulf of St. Lawrence, where puffins nest in the peat and a lightstation has stood since 1888. Its keepers left more writing than most: a count turned naturalist, a keeper whose journal still survives, and a keeper's wife who wrote a book.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The island takes its name from the puffin, called the "sea parrot", or *perroquet de mer*, in French. Its cliffs are capped with a layer of peat in which, according to Lighthouse Friends, great numbers of puffins burrow and rear their young.[^1] Parks Canada calls it a spot for watching seabirds, set in a "treacherous water passage".[^3]

The passage had already claimed ships. Placide Vigneau, who later kept the light, wrote that the *Clyde* was lost on the reefs of the neighbouring Île du Naufrage in 1859 and the *North Briton* in 1861.[^4] A local history of the island counts five major wrecks around the Mingan islands between 1857 and 1885, and says they added to pressure for a light. The council of Pointe-aux-Esquimaux, now Havre-Saint-Pierre, had asked for lights on this island and on Walrus Island as early as 1877.[^5] Vigneau, writing after the event, remembered it as nearly twenty years of petitions, a longer span than the 1877 request alone suggests: "il y avait près de vingt ans que l'on envoyait requête sur requête pour l'obtenir" ("for nearly twenty years request after request was sent to obtain it", our translation).[^6]

## Building the light

The Department of Marine and Fisheries reported that the lighthouse went up in the 1888 season on the north-westernmost of the four Perroquet Islands. It was lit on 1 September, a revolving white light that reached its greatest brilliancy every 30 seconds, 27 m (87 ft) above high water and expected to be visible 15 miles away. The building was a square wooden tower with the dwelling attached, white with a red iron lantern, 17 m (55 ft) from ground to vane. The light was catoptric (reflectors, not lenses), and the work cost $7,816.12. Lighthouse Friends reproduces that report.[^1]

## The keepers

The first keeper was Henry de Puyjalon, a French count who had come to Canada in 1872 and was later the province's inspector of fisheries and game. Lighthouse Friends gives his pay as $400 a year.[^1][^7] His wife gave birth to their second son on the island on 19 September 1891, and he wrote that he "presided, alone" over the birth; he resigned soon afterwards.[^1] Quebec's heritage register dates his time as keeper 1888 to 1891.[^7]

His successor was Charles-Eustache Forgues. Vigneau, who took over from him, wrote that Puyjalon resigned in 1891 (others said he lost the post that October) and that Forgues, a surveyor from La Malbaie, was already installed when the news reached the coast.[^8] The station journal held at Sept-Îles has Forgues keeping the entries from 15 October 1891.[^9] Vigneau wrote that on 6 May 1892, in a strong north-north-westerly breeze, Forgues set out in a small canoe for Longue-Pointe to fetch chimneys sent by the steamer *Otter*, and drowned with his hired man.[^8] The Sept-Îles archive's description of that journal gives the same date and names the aide as Baptiste Saint-Cœur.[^9] Lighthouse Friends gives a different errand, saying the two were crossing to fetch a doctor for Forgues's expectant wife.[^1] Accounts also differ on the aftermath. Lighthouse Friends says Forgues's wife apparently delivered her baby without a doctor.[^1] Vigneau, who landed on 10 May, wrote that she bore a daughter and that the widow left the island on 12 June "après qu'elle fut bien rétablie" ("once she had fully recovered", our translation).[^10][^11]

Vigneau, born in the Magdalen Islands and settled at Havre-Saint-Pierre since 1858, then took the light, and held it until 1912.[^1][^12] His journal for 1892 to 1900 survives and is transcribed on Wikisource. It records the work of a keeper-farmer: in September 1893 he painted the outside of the tower and the house, and finished stacking the hay he had mown, counting 58 stacks.[^13] When a cyclone hit on 30 August 1893 he wrote only that the sea rose very high, and noted later that some twenty boats were broken between Sept-Îles and Pointe-aux-Esquimaux.[^14]

The keeper lists give his successor as Hector Vigneault, 1912 to 1948 (the 1915 salary table prints "H. Vignaux"), then J. Robert Kavanagh to 1976 and John Collin to 1978.[^1][^15] Kavanagh left after twenty-eight years on the island.[^1] His wife, Mary Collin-Kavanagh, wrote *Femme de gardien de phare*, an account of daily life that included the hard boat crossings to the mainland.[^16]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In December 1954, by that book's account as summarised by Québec maritime, Kavanagh and his assistant had engine trouble crossing to the mainland, were caught in the ice, and he promised the Virgin Mary a statue if they were saved. Ice opened just wide enough to row through, and the statue was set up on the island.[^16] Lighthouse Friends gives no year, placing the crossing after the station had closed for the shipping season, says the men rowed back to the island, and describes the statue on a concrete pillar.[^1]

## Changes at the station

A boat was supplied to the station in the year covered by the 1900 report; none could be had on the Labrador coast, so the keeper was allowed to buy one in the Magdalen Islands for $60.50, and a carpenter had to be sent from Quebec by the supply steamer to renew the dwelling floors.[^17] Lighthouse Friends says a diaphone fog alarm began in 1918;[^1] the Department's report printed in 1920 lists a new fog-alarm plant at Perroquet Island, so the dates may differ by a year or so.[^18] Two oil tanks were added in the 1923-24 report year.[^19]

The present tower, an octagonal reinforced-concrete one, was built in 1951 to the Department of Transport's standard plan, replacing the 1888 wooden one. The two keepers' houses date from 1951 and a boathouse from 1908 to 1915.[^20]

## After the keepers

The Corporation de l'Île aux Perroquets was founded in 2010 to protect and restore the station.[^21] Tenders for the rehabilitation were invited in April 2012, and the corporation says the buildings' restoration ended in October 2013.[^1][^21] On 11 April 2014 the lighthouse was designated Quebec's first heritage lighthouse.[^1]

## Today

The island lies in Mingan Archipelago National Park Reserve and is reached by boat; Lighthouse Friends lists a tour operator that runs trips there, and reports the grounds open and the tower closed.[^1] The corporation offers overnight stays under the line "Become one of the lighthouse keepers of l'Ile aux Perroquets for one night."[^22] Parks Canada asks visitors to stay on established trails and warns of falling rock near the cliffs.[^3]
