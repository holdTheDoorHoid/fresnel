A red-shingled, eight-sided wooden tower stands on a steep hill above the village of La Martre, on the north shore of the Gaspé Peninsula in Quebec, where the first light was lit in 1876 and where the Fresnel lens still turns on its original cable-and-weight clockwork.[^1][^2]

## The place

La Martre lies on the Gulf of St. Lawrence coast of the Gaspé.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory describes the present lighthouse as sitting on a steep hill just off Route 132, in the village.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says the name comes from the nearby Marten River and that the first tower was built in what it calls the tiny village of La Marte.[^1] The federal heritage review credits the light with a place in the Department of Marine's late-century upgrading of the St. Lawrence navigational system, as one of a series of Gulf lighthouses rebuilt with a higher grade of illumination.[^2] Official papers also know the place as Sainte-Marthe-de-Gaspé and Rivière à la Martre.[^2][^4]

## Building the light

The first lighthouse was a square, tapering wooden tower, 64 feet (about 20 m) tall, with the keeper's dwelling attached, built in 1876 at a cost of $7,347.04.[^1] Nine wick lamps in 21-inch reflectors gave a fixed white light; in 1877 the number was cut to five, and in 1881 two black horizontal bands were painted on the white tower so mariners could pick it out by day.[^1]

The present tower dates from 1906 (the Lighthouse Directory gives "1906 (station established 1876)").[^3] It is an octagonal timber-frame tower in three sections, divided by a plinth and a string course near the top, with a pedimented porch; its walls are lined with red-painted shingles and carry a white vertical stripe on the seaward side.[^2] A fog alarm building went up with it, with a 60-foot brick chimney for the steam engine and a concrete cistern under the boiler room. According to Lighthouse Friends the diaphone fog signal began work on 10 August 1906, sounding a five-second blast in each minute when needed. Duplicate ten-horse-power oil engines replaced the steam engine in 1919.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists five keepers between 1876 and 1972.[^1] Jean-Baptiste Gauthier, the first, raised eight children, two of whom entered the lighthouse service; he retired in 1902 and received the Imperial Service Order the next year.[^1] Auguste Leclerc followed him (1902 to 1912).[^1] Lighthouse Friends says Leclerc lost the post when Gauthier's son Louis-Philippe was elected the member of Parliament for Gaspé in 1912, and that his place went to Joseph L'Italien, who had married the MP's sister Elliane. Joseph kept the light for 34 years while Elliane worked the telegraph, and the couple raised twelve children at the station.[^1] Their son Raymond helped at the light from 1929 to 1946 and was keeper for a year after his father retired.[^1] In 1947 a Liberal MP replaced Raymond with Paul-Roger Caron, a young Second World War veteran; when La Martre was automated in 1972 he was moved to Cap-des-Rosiers.[^1]

The Department of Marine and Fisheries' report covering the fiscal year 1908-9 (labelled 1909) records that the keeper's dwelling at "Riviere a la Martre" was thoroughly repaired and a new coal and oil shed built, by day labour, for $1,691.92.[^4] Lighthouse Friends adds that a new dwelling replaced the old one in 1957.[^1]

## After the keepers

After automation, Yves Foucrault (spelled Foucreault by Lighthouse Friends) petitioned for funds and hired a few students each summer to help build a museum and maintain the station.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory credits him with preserving the lighthouse for many years.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says La Martre was declared a Recognized Federal Heritage Building in 1988,[^1] and the Lighthouse Directory reports that it received the Heritage Lighthouse designation in January 2026.[^3]

## Today

The lighthouse is a visitor site. The Lighthouse Directory says the site is open and the tower is open to guided tours daily from mid June to early September, and names the Canadian Coast Guard as owner and operator, with the Municipalité de La Martre (Musée des Phares) as site manager.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says the former keeper's house serves as town hall and welcome centre and that the fog alarm building holds a museum of lighthouse history and equipment.[^1] It adds that La Martre is the only light in Quebec still operated in a non-automated way during the day, so that visitors can see the lens rotating in its mercury bath on its original weights and cables.[^1]
