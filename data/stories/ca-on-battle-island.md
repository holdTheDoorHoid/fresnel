Battle Island Light stands at the west end of Battle Island, in Lake Superior at the eastern mouth of Nipigon Bay, 9.7 km (6.1 miles) from Rossport, Ontario.[^1] It has been shown from the island since 1877. The octagonal tower it uses now replaced the first wooden one, and the new light was lit in 1915.[^1][^7] The last keeper retired in 1991, and since July 2025 the federal government has suspended the arrangement that let the Friends bring visitors to the island.[^1][^6]

## The place

Nipigon Bay is protected by a string of islands.[^1] Only two of its five ship channels suited large vessels: Nipigon Strait, used by ships coming from the west, and Simpson Channel, used by ships coming from the east, which runs between Simpson Island and Battle Island.[^1] In 1875 the Department of Marine set out to mark the two entrances, with one light on Battle Island and one on Lamb Island.[^1]

The island's name was already in Department of Marine records by 1875. A later story that ties the name to a skirmish during the 1885 North-West Rebellion therefore cannot be where the name came from.[^1]

## Building the light

The contract for the two lights went to Joseph White. The work ran late, and the Battle Island light was not shown until 1877.[^1] In August 1876 the Superintendent of Lights above Montreal, Darius Smith, landed a lantern room on the island and found construction materials there but nobody present. The lantern room was covered with lumber and left on the island for the contractor.[^1] The two lights together cost $9,405.26.[^1]

LighthouseFriends quotes the Department's report for 1877 on the new light. It describes a revolving light that showed red and white in turn, raised 32 m (105 ft) above the water and, in clear weather, should be seen 29 km (18 miles) away. The tower was a square wooden building 9 m (30 ft) high to the vane of the lantern, painted white, with the keeper's dwelling a short distance away.[^1] Charles McKay had been appointed keeper by Order in Council in September 1876, at $500 a year. He was not ready to go aboard the Lake Erie when she sailed, so Andrew Dick kept the light from 2 August until McKay arrived on 27 August and the light was shown for the first time.[^1]

In 1901 the Department reported that a hand fog horn had been supplied to the station, and that the island's position on the charts had been corrected.[^2]

In 1914 work began on a wooden building for a diaphone fog alarm, an octagonal tower about 9 m (30 ft) high to replace the first wooden one, and a double dwelling for the two keepers the station now needed.[^1] The Department's table of works for the fiscal year 1914-15 lists the same station as the erection of a standard fog alarm building, a double dwelling and a 9 m (30 ft) reinforced concrete tower with a 2.4 m (8 ft) cast-iron lantern, with the work to be completed the following year.[^3] That table is the only source that gives the tower's material. LighthouseFriends calls the new tower octagonal and does not name one. The table calls the building a standard fog alarm; LighthouseFriends says it housed a diaphone.

The new light was lit on 12 May 1915. It showed three flashes in each 24-second cycle, burned petroleum vapour under a mantle, and had a fog alarm that sounded for 3.5 seconds every 30 seconds when needed.[^1]

## The keepers

Charles Stephen McKay kept the light from 1877 to 1913. LighthouseFriends counts 36 seasons. The Department's statement of keepers for 1906 still lists "McKay, Chas. S." at Battle Island, appointed 27 August 1877 at $500 a year.[^1][^5]

In his first two winters he kept the light near Rossport. The village was first called McKay's Harbour, after the fur trader Alexander McKay and his son Charles, the keeper. It was renamed in 1884.[^1] In his third winter McKay rowed a small skiff 480 km (nearly 300 miles) along the north shore of Lake Superior to reach Sault Ste. Marie by Christmas Eve.[^1]

In 1913 his son Edward took over from a father who was then 75. The Department's statement for the fiscal year 1913-14 lists "E. McKay" against Battle Island, appointed 4 April 1913.[^4] Edward kept the light until 1922, when Malcolm Sutherland took charge.[^1] Sutherland drowned in August 1932. LighthouseFriends records his death as the only fatality on Battle Island. His widow kept the light for the rest of that season, and John J. Sutherland took charge in 1933.[^1]

George Brady was keeper from 1948 to 1961.[^1] In 1953 a moose was born on the island. It was kept as a pet, then grew into a nuisance that charged the keeper whenever he went outside. LighthouseFriends reports that in 1954 Department of Transport officials asked the Forestry Department for help. A game warden and two gunmen missed the animal with four shots. Four days later the warden came back with a Toronto Telegram photographer, got the moose into the water and directed it toward another island.[^1] LighthouseFriends cites a 1955 Department of Transport newsletter for the episode.[^1]

Willard Hubelit kept the light from 1961 to 1967, and John Joiner from 1967 to 1991.[^1] Bert Saasto retired in November 1991 as the last keeper. He later came back each summer as a volunteer caretaker and interpreter until about 2013.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 10 August 1899 the steamer *Ontario*, bound for Nipigon to pick up a load of pulpwood, ran aground on the island in dense fog. LighthouseFriends says she was a total wreck, but her crew was rescued. Saasto liked to point out the boiler from her to visitors.[^1] The footnoted sources tie no other wreck to the station.

Saasto also described a November 1977 gale with winds of about 130 km/h (80 miles an hour). He put the waves at 15 m (fifty feet), high enough to wash up the tower and smash the glass out of the lantern. In his words, "the storm lasted for three days and nights."[^1]

## After the keepers

After the last keeper left in 1991, the Friends of Battle Island Lighthouse worked to restore and maintain the station and to open it to visitors.[^8] Over about a decade they painted the tower, improved the keeper's house, put in a power supply and restored the water service.[^6] The light is now listed as a single white flash every ten seconds, one second of light and nine of dark, with a range of 10 nautical miles.[^7] The footnoted sources do not date the change from the 1915 three-flash pattern.

## Today

The light belongs to the Canadian Coast Guard.[^8] It is entry 1117 in the Coast Guard's List of Lights, which gives its focal height as 36.0 metres, its tower as 13.1 metres, and the station as seasonal.[^7]

Battle Island is reached only by boat.[^8] In July 2025 Fisheries and Oceans Canada suspended its agreement with the Friends, which had let their volunteers work on the island and bring visitors there. The department said the suspension was "to allow for a review of activities taking place on the island."[^6] The Pays Plat First Nation's land claim includes the island, and as of December 2025 the Friends and the First Nation were still talking about access.[^6] The Lighthouse Directory says the island was closed to visitors after that suspension, but its own entry for the site still says "Site open, tower closed."[^8] Do not plan a landing on the island until the review is over.
