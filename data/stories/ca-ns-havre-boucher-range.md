The Havre Boucher range lights are a pair of towers on the southwest shore of Havre Boucher harbour, in Antigonish County on Nova Scotia's Northumberland Shore.[^1][^4] Lined up with each other, the front and rear lights mark the position of the dredged channel entering the harbour.[^1]

## The place

The lights were put up to mark the entrance to the harbour.[^1] In 1878 the Department of Marine invited tenders for range lights to do that job.[^1] The Department's description of the finished work says that, when the two lights are in one line, they "indicate the position of the dredged channel entering Harbour au Bouche."[^1]

The sources give the station an older start than the towers. Rowlett's Lighthouse Directory gives 1842 as the year the station was established, and the NSLPS list dates one of the rear lights to that year.[^2][^5] The sources cited here do not say what stood on the site before the towers the Department described.

## Building the light

The Department awarded the contract to William Kaulback of Harbour au Bouche, for $575.[^1] He did not finish the work to the Department's satisfaction, and LighthouseFriends says it appears he was paid $475.[^1] The whole job, including $476 for the lantern and lamps and $110 for land and rights of way, came to $1,309.[^1]

The description, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says two range towers were erected during the past season and put in operation "in October last."[^1] The front was a square wooden tower 9.8 m (32 ft) high, showing a fixed white light 11 m (36 ft) above high water.[^1] The rear tower stood 433 m (1,420 ft) away, was "similar to the front," and showed a fixed red light 32.6 m (107 ft) above high water.[^1] The description says both were "visible 9 miles in fair weather," without saying which mile.[^1]

The date of first light is not settled. The description was printed the year after the tenders, so "October last" points to 1878.[^1] Rowlett and the NSLPS front-light entry both give 1879 for the towers.[^2][^4]

## The keepers

William J. Webb was appointed the first keeper, at an annual salary of $200.[^1] Members of the Webb family kept the lights until they were automated in 1963. The exception was 1916 to 1924, when Jeffrey T. Crispo was keeper.[^1] LighthouseFriends also lists a Mary Webb as keeper in 1896, with no role or other detail given.[^1]

The Department's list of light-keepers for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1903 names Patrick Webb as keeper at Harbour au Bouche, appointed 19 February 1896.[^6] The 1907 list still places him at the station.[^8] LighthouseFriends gives his service as running to 1916.[^1] Joseph K. Webb is listed by LighthouseFriends as keeper from 1924 to 1963.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Department's annual report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1914 still lists "Havre bouche, range" in its alphabetical list of stations.[^7]

The sources do not agree on when the towers were replaced. The NSLPS list gives 1941 to 1954 for the first replacement of each tower, 1945 for the second towers, and 1969 for the third.[^4][^5] LighthouseFriends mentions no 1945 or 1969 towers, but records a new concrete foundation for each tower in 1954.[^1] Automation is also dated two ways: LighthouseFriends gives 1963, and the NSLPS list says the 1969 rear tower was automated by 1973, though the same list's header gives 1993.[^1][^5]

LighthouseFriends records that both towers were stripped, shingled and given new sills in 1890, and that a coloured stripe was added to their seaward faces in 1987 to help them show by day.[^1]

By 2010 the pair had rotted beyond repair, according to LighthouseFriends, and the Coast Guard replaced both towers. The front became a skeletal tower with slatting on one side.[^1] For the rear, the Coast Guard chose a modular tower instead of a steel skeletal one, after consulting the local community. The modular tower resembles the previous lighthouse.[^1] The NSLPS describes the new rear tower as having an aluminum frame, sheathed with plywood and sided with vinyl that looks like cedar shingles, and says the front tower is now a galvanized steel structure.[^4]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard maintains both lights, which its current list gives as seasonal.[^3] The front is a fixed green light on a square skeleton tower 7.5 m high, with its focal height at 8.3 m.[^3] The rear is a fixed green light on a white pyramidal tower 9.6 m high, with its focal height at 32.4 m.[^3] The rear stands 433.2 m from the front, on a bearing of 194°31'.[^3] Both lights have a nominal range of 8 nautical miles.[^3]

Rowlett describes the site as open and the tower as closed, and names the Canadian Coast Guard as site manager.[^2] The rear light stands on a hill above Highway 4, just off the Trans-Canada at the Havre Boucher exit.[^2] The NSLPS lists the site as requiring a hike.[^4] The towers are closed to visitors and should not be climbed.[^2]
