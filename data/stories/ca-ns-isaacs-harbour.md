Isaac's Harbour light stands on Holly Point, at the western entrance to Isaac's Harbour on Nova Scotia's Eastern Shore. A light was first shown there in 1874. The two-storey wooden house that carries the present lantern went up in 1928 or 1929 (the sources differ), and since 2018 the lighthouse has been a private property, closed to visitors.[^1][^2][^7][^8]

## The place

Isaac's Harbour is a nearly five-kilometre inlet, separated from the much longer Country Harbour by a narrow peninsula.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the harbour takes its name from Isaac Webb, the patriarch of a Black family described as the first non-native settlers on the inlet. It gives the arrival of the first white settlers as the 1830s.[^1] In 1861 Joseph Hynes found gold in a quartz vein just east of the harbour. Goldboro, on the east side of the inlet, and Isaac's Harbour, on the west side, became mining towns; at the peak there were over a dozen stores on the harbour and at least as many wharves.[^1] The Parks Canada statement says the station was initially established to aid the fishing, shipbuilding and gold-mining industries.[^2]

## Building the light

The Department of Marine's annual report for 1874, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, describes the first light as "a square wooden building 20 feet high and painted white," erected on Holly Point on the west side of the harbour entrance. It was first put in operation on 9 November, a fixed red light 24 m (80 ft) above the sea, which the report says could be seen about nine miles away in clear weather.[^1] LighthouseFriends says J. T. Sinclair built it under contract for $870.[^1] The Department's 1900 statement of light-keepers and stations gives the station's name as "Holly Point, Isaac's Harbour."[^9]

Mariners found the red light too feeble. On 1 May 1877 it became two fixed white lights, one in the lantern and one from a three-sided bay window 6 m (20 ft) lower on the tower. LighthouseFriends says two white lights were needed so that mariners would not confuse this light with the single fixed white light on Country Island.[^1]

A report made in 1877 found that the keeper had only about 1.1 square metres (12 sq ft) of living space on the middle floor, because the lower lantern and the oil took up most of the lower floor. LighthouseFriends says the heat and smell of the burning oil, from above and below, could be very oppressive. The Nova Scotia agent, H. W. Johnston, had a proper dwelling added to the lighthouse.[^1]

By the year ended 31 March 1910 the two white lights had been replaced by one occulting white light. The Chief Engineer's report for that year says the apparatus was "dioptric of the 4th order", burning petroleum vapour under a mantle, and that the lighthouse "has been surmounted by a new octagonal iron lantern painted red."[^4] The report gives the cycle as 25 seconds of light, a 5-second eclipse, 5 seconds of light and a 5-second eclipse, in every 40 seconds.[^4] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society dates the same 4th-order occulting lens to 1922, so the date of the change is disputed.[^8]

## The keepers

Charles W. Bigsby was put in temporary charge in 1874. LighthouseFriends says his appointment was soon made permanent and that he kept the light for twenty years.[^1] He was allowed to blast some large rocks to make a better landing near the lighthouse.[^1]

Ira L. Giffin followed him. The Department's list of light-keepers in the report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1909 gives his appointment as 28 April 1894, at $265 a year. That list prints the name Griffin; LighthouseFriends spells it Giffin in its keeper roster and Griffin in its narrative.[^6][^1] By 1900 he was given an extra $25 a year for the extra work of sounding a hand foghorn whenever vessels' whistles were heard nearby.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends has him as keeper until 1924.[^1]

After him came W. J. Sinclair (1924 to 1937), J. MacMillan (from 1937) and Ernest B. Davidson (1938 to 1966).[^1] Davidson, the last keeper, retired in 1966 and agreed to stay on as caretaker of the automated light, replacing burned-out bulbs and reporting vandalism or maintenance problems.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 the Department set up hand foghorns at several Nova Scotia stations, Isaac harbour among them, to be used whenever vessels' fog signals were heard.[^3]

The Department's casualty return for the year ended 31 March 1912 records that on 25 July 1911 "SS. John Irwin foundered off Isaacs harbour, vessel total loss, twelve lives lost."[^5] The return does not mention the light, and the other sources here do not tie the wreck to it. The sources give no dated storm damage to this station.

## After the keepers

Commercial power reached the station in 1954, LighthouseFriends says, and by the time Davidson retired in 1966 the light was automated.[^1]

The sources disagree about the first building. Parks Canada says the present lighthouse was built in 1928 "to replace the original destroyed by fire."[^2] LighthouseFriends says that the old lighthouse, no longer needed, was demolished after the new one went up.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory and the NSLPS date the present light to 1929.[^7][^8]

The Coast Guard declared the lighthouse surplus in January 1990 and planned to replace it with a fibreglass tower. Before the Coast Guard tore down the building, the Federal Heritage Building Review Office evaluated it; LighthouseFriends says it scored fifty-four, enough for it to be declared a recognized federal heritage building on 13 December 1990.[^1]

The light was discontinued in 2009, LighthouseFriends says, and the Lighthouse Directory says it has been inactive since 2009. Parks Canada gives 2008.[^1][^7][^2] A Parks Canada backgrounder on the year's heritage lighthouse designations is dated 4 June 2018.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the recognition to May 2018, and the Lighthouse Directory to June.[^1][^7] Later that year the lighthouse was sold to a private buyer, who began restoring the interior, LighthouseFriends says.[^1][^7]

## Today

The lighthouse is privately owned, and the grounds and tower are closed.[^1][^7] A gate on the access road stands about 600 m from the lighthouse; LighthouseFriends says permission is reportedly needed to reach the property. The lighthouse can be seen from the end of the access road, and from Highway NS-316 across the water.[^1][^7] The present building is a two-storey combined lighthouse and keepers' dwelling, 12.8 m (42 ft) high, with its lantern centred on the roof.[^2][^7] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society lists the present light as still standing but no longer operating.[^8]
