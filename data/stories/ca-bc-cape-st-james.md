Cape St. James Lighthouse stands on St. James Island, at the southern tip of the archipelago now called Haida Gwaii, which was known as the Queen Charlotte Islands until 2010.[^3][^5] The Department of Marine built a concrete tower on the island in 1913, and the light was announced for about 15 February 1914.[^1][^3] The concrete tower has since been demolished, and a light now shows from a white mast on the island.[^5][^6]

## The place

Captain George Dixon sailed around the southern end of the archipelago on 25 July 1787, the feast day of St. James, and gave the cape its name.[^3][^4]

Rowlett's directory describes the island as high, at the southern end of Haida Gwaii, and reachable only by boat or helicopter.[^5] LighthouseFriends says powerful winds often swept the island and the weather was often poor.[^3]

Before the work at the cape, the Department had sent its chief engineer to the islands to survey lighthouse sites. In late June 1911 a Department vessel carried him to the Queen Charlotte Islands, and the annual report for that fiscal year describes him as one "who made surveys of sites for new lighthouses."[^2] The report does not name the sites, or say whether this cape was among them.

## Building the light

The Department's report for the fiscal year 1913–14, which ran from April 1913 to March 1914, records work at the cape at the start of the year and again in the autumn. At the beginning of the year the vessel was under the direction of the resident engineer, "laying out new work" at the cape and other stations on the coast.[^1] After 10 November 1913 she carried workmen to the cape "to complete tower, erect lantern, and instal apparatus and keeper," and then returned to Victoria.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the Department put up a reinforced concrete tower 13 m (44 ft) tall in 1913, with a wooden dwelling near it and a boathouse at the shore. It says R. Chrystal supervised the construction, which cost $18,114.[^3]

The Department's notice to mariners, as LighthouseFriends prints it, gave about 15 February 1914 as the date the light would be established. The lens was to be a third-order dioptric unit, giving a flashing white light of 100,000 candlepower with one flash every five seconds. The notice put the light 94 m (310 ft) above high water, on an eight-sided white concrete tower with a red metal lantern. The lamp burned petroleum vapour under an incandescent mantle, and a white-painted wooden dwelling stood 24 m (80 ft) north of the tower.[^3]

The notice gave the range as 24 miles, without saying whether they were nautical miles. The Coast Guard list now gives 13 nautical miles.[^3][^6]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends names Taylor Ash as the first head keeper. In its words he "stayed only a few months."[^3] Laurance Thompson replaced him and served until 1918.[^3] The longest serving keeper, by that list, was Stanley F. Lawrence, from 1921 to 1931.[^3]

Keepers changed often in the 1930s and early 1940s. Seven stints began between 1931 and 1942, held by six different men; James N. Kelly served there twice.[^3]

The radar history says the lighthouse was manned by one keeper. It also says the keeper and his wife were always included in the station's social life, and it does not name the wife.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The radar history calls the cape "well known for its hurricane force winds and heavy seas which would stop traffic for days on end," and says that in winter the station could go 20 to 25 days between boats because of wind.[^4]

In 1925 fire destroyed the keeper's dwelling. LighthouseFriends says a new dwelling had to be built, and that the keeper moved into temporary quarters in the boathouse while it went up.[^3]

Just before the Second World War the Department had considered doing away with the light altogether, the radar history says. The light was useless for much of the time, because it was in the clouds, and there was no fog horn.[^4] On 7 December 1941 a wartime instruction put the light out, the first time it had been dark since 1914.[^4]

In 1943 a military station was built on the island, a radar unit of the air force.[^4] LighthouseFriends calls it a military radio station, established on 26 August 1943 with 65 men.[^3] The radar history gives 11 November 1943 as the start of the unit and 10 August 1945 as its end.[^4] On 27 April 1945 one member of the unit went fishing alone near the station and disappeared.[^4] The search lasted several days and included many surface vessels and aircraft, and he was never found.[^4]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends dates the automation of the light to 1961, the year its keeper list ends.[^3] The radar history says the site was automated in 1992, and that the old buildings were dismantled except for the light mast.[^4] The two dates may refer to different things, or one may be wrong; this story does not choose between them.

The lantern came off in 1958, when an aerobeacon was installed, and in 1984 a metal tower replaced the original tower.[^4] Rowlett says the original octagonal concrete tower was demolished after it was deactivated.[^5] In 2021 a flashing white light was shown from a white mast on the summit of the island.[^3]

## Today

The light is an active aid to navigation.[^5][^6] The Coast Guard list gives its character as a white flash every five seconds, shown from a white mast.[^6] Rowlett says the station is reached only by boat or helicopter, and gives its site status as unknown.[^5] LighthouseFriends says access to the island is very hazardous and may be restricted.[^3] None of these sources describes a way for visitors to reach the island.
