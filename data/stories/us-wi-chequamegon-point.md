Chequamegon Point Light stands at the western tip of Long Island, on the Lake Superior shore of Ashland County, Wisconsin, at the entrance to Chequamegon Bay.[^1][^2][^8] Its 1897 iron tower is to be dismantled in 2026, after shoreline erosion and storm damage caused structural failures that threaten to bring it down into Lake Superior.[^8]

## The place

Long Island was once an island, and it is now part of a peninsula that reaches nearly eight miles (13 km) into Lake Superior. That natural breakwater shelters the bay.[^2] The bay's name comes from an Ojibwe word meaning long sand bar.[^2]

A light had guided ships since 1858 from the east side of the bay entrance, on Long Island, to the harbor at La Pointe on Madeline Island.[^3][^2] When Ashland became the main port in the area in the late 1880s, LighthouseFriends says "another light was needed to mark the western extremity of Long Island known as Chequamegon Point."[^2]

## Building the light

The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1892 says that "the act of October 1, 1888, authorized a fog signal" at the existing east-side light, and it renews a recommendation for further improvements there.[^3]

In the report for 1893 the Board describes an authorized project: "The removing and rebuilding of the main light and the establishment of a harbor light and bell, at a cost not to exceed $10,000, were authorized by the act approved February 15, 1893, but no appropriation therefor has yet been made."[^4] An act approved 2 March 1895 provided the money: "$10,000 for moving and rebuilding the main La Pointe light and establishing a harbor bell and light at or near Chequamegon Point."[^5]

Negotiations for the site opened in July, which LighthouseFriends dates to 1895. It says they "finished on March 18, 1896."[^6][^2] The Board's 1896 report records contracts for the metal work of "an iron beacon for Chequamegon Point."[^6] LighthouseFriends says Fulton Iron and Engine Works of Detroit cast the tower and its shaft for $862, and that work stopped on 30 October 1896 for lack of funds.[^2]

An extra $1,500 was appropriated on 4 June 1897, and LighthouseFriends says work "resumed in August 1897."[^7][^2] The Board's 1897 report says "the title papers to the site and right of way were recorded," and a note in the same report gives 11 October 1897 as the date the light was established.[^7] The tower stood "forty feet tall" (about 12 m), in LighthouseFriends's measure, and the Park Service gives 42 feet (13 m).[^2][^8] It carried the fourth-order lens from the old La Pointe light, and a fog bell that LighthouseFriends describes as a 1,200-pound (540 kg) bronze bell.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive's place record for the point lists no keepers.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that maintaining the point was added to the duties of the keepers at La Pointe, and that a plank walk joined the tower to the La Pointe dwelling, which stood 2,700 feet (820 m) to the east.[^2]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says three log cribs were placed on the point in 1904 to protect the site from further erosion.[^2] The lights at the point and at La Pointe were electrified in 1937 and automated in 1964.[^2][^1] The characteristic changed from red to green in 1947.[^2]

The archive gives 1986 as the year the light was discontinued. LighthouseFriends says Long Island was added to the Apostle Islands National Lakeshore that year.[^1][^2]

In 1987 the Coast Guard set up a new light on a cylindrical D9 tower, and the old iron tower was moved about 100 feet (30 m) to protect it from the eroding shore.[^2][^8] The two sources disagree on the direction: the Park Service says the tower was moved 100 feet east, while LighthouseFriends says it was moved about 100 feet inland.[^8][^2] LighthouseFriends says a helicopter brought in for the move ended up dragging the tower across the ground, "causing damage to its legs, cross braces, and access ladder."[^2] Between 2006 and 2008 the Park Service hired contractors to "straighten the bent legs and place the tower on new concrete footings."[^2]

## Today

The National Park Service announced in May 2026 that "the deteriorating 42-foot tower can no longer be preserved in place." The agency said the tower threatened to collapse into Lake Superior.[^8] The plan is to dismantle the remaining structure and move its pieces to an off-site storage location.[^8] In late August the Park Service said it had awarded the contract for the work, which was "expected to begin this fall and conclude by Oct. 31, 2026."[^9]

Only the exposed steel lattice frame and the lantern room remain of the tower.[^8] The tower is a contributing feature of the Long Island Light Station historic district, but it is not individually listed on the National Register of Historic Places.[^8] The Park Service says the historic aids on Long Island "have been replaced by a single USCG buoy offshore."[^8]

Long Island is hard to reach. The Park Service cites shifting sands, shallow water and "lack of regular boat service."[^8] LighthouseFriends says the point is "best seen from the water, or by landing on the island."[^2] The Park Service owns the light, and LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^2] The fog bell is on display at the Madeline Island Historical Museum.[^2]
