Eagle Harbor Light stands at Eagle Harbor on the Keweenaw Peninsula's Lake Superior shore, a red-brick tower rising from the corner of its keepers' house. It was built in 1871 to replace a stone dwelling with a wooden tower, put up in 1850 by one account and 1851 by another, and it is still a working navigation light, with a museum complex around it.[^1][^2]

## The place

Eagle Harbor was settled in the copper-mining years of the 1840s. Edward Taylor built a log warehouse and wooden piers there in 1844, and the Eagle Harbor Mining Company followed the next year.[^2] The Keweenaw County Historical Society says that Lake Superior's unreliable disposition meant passing ships needed navigational help.[^3] Congress appropriated $4,000 for a light at Eagle Harbor on 3 March 1849, and asked the Secretary of the Navy to appoint a commander to judge whether it, and two other lights funded at the same time, were needed. Commander Bigelow reported in favour of Eagle Harbor.[^2]

## Building the light

The first station was built in 1850 on the west side of the harbor entrance: a one-and-a-half-story rubble-stone dwelling, 36 by 34 feet (11 by 10 m) with five rooms, topped by a short wooden tower and an octagonal iron lantern. Eight lamps in 14-inch reflectors showed a fixed white light 47 feet (14 m) above the lake.[^2] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster begins John Griswold's service in 1850, while the account on LighthouseFriends has the light first exhibited in 1851 and Griswold keeping it until 1859.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends dates a fourth-order Fresnel lens to 1857, with two revolving panels around the fixed light producing a flash every two minutes; the Historical Society instead says the original lighthouse already carried a fourth-order Fresnel lens, and dates that building to 1851.[^2][^3]

The stone house did not last. In 1868 the Lighthouse Board asked for $14,000 for a replacement, describing the dwelling as "built of rubble stone, laid together in the rudest manner" (as LighthouseFriends quotes the request).[^2] Congress appropriated $14,000 on 15 July 1870, under the heading "repairs and renovation of Eagle Harbor light-station", and over the following year a seven-room, one-and-a-half-story brick dwelling went up with a tower at its northeast corner, square at the base and octagonal above. The old lighting apparatus moved into the new ten-sided lantern.[^2] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record gives the keeper's quarters as built in 1871, attached to the tower, and the light as 58 feet (18 m) above the water.[^1]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society lists 22 principal keepers between 1850 and 1982, and the Keweenaw County Historical Society counts 22 since 1851.[^1][^3] The roster, which gives years only, shows short early tenures: John Alexander in 1859, George R. Griswold 1859 to 1860 and James Bouden 1860 to 1861, before Richard C. Satterlee served 1861 to 1865.[^1]

Peter C. Bird took charge in 1865 and stayed to 1874.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he was a Civil War veteran wounded at Gettysburg (its account of his rescue from the field is family lore), and that he and his wife Mary Jane had their first three children at Eagle Harbor, where they watched the new lighthouse go up.[^2] Stephen Cocking, appointed in 1877, had come from England as a boy, worked in the Eagle River mines, enlisted in the 23rd Michigan Infantry in 1862 and kept Gull Rock Light for eight years; he died at the Eagle Harbor light of pneumonia in 1889.[^2]

The station grew with its signals. An assistant keeper joined the head keeper in 1895, when the fog signal was built, and a second assistant in 1913.[^2][^1] In March 1898 a Calumet paper noted the return of James Carson, "the range light keeper", from a winter spent elsewhere, and that Will Rohrig "of the lighthouse force here" had finished building a large sailboat.[^4] Thomas J. Bennetts, head keeper from 1913 to 1927, appears in the index to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1912 to 1917 as winning an efficiency flag for the station.[^1][^5]

Edward W. Byttyla rose through the station's ranks: second assistant from 1929, first assistant from 1931, head keeper 1940 to 1962.[^1] Albert E. Stridfelt, who succeeded him, retired in 1967 as the last civilian lighthouse keeper in Michigan.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

**A fog signal, won slowly.** The Lighthouse Board wrote in its report for 1891 that "A steam fog signal would be a valuable addition to the light-station at this point", and a year later renewed a request it had made in its "last two annual reports".[^6] Congress authorized one on 16 February 1893 at a cost not to exceed $5,500, but no money followed, and the Board pressed again.[^7] An act of 2 March 1895 provided $5,000.[^8] Work began in September 1895 with excavations and concrete footings, and the steam whistle was established on 30 November 1895.[^9] The station also had a water-supply and landing crib sunk in the lake to feed the boilers.[^9]

The reports then count the whistle's work. In the report covering its first part-year, from its establishment on 30 November 1895, it ran some 73 hours and burned about 6 tons of coal.[^9] In the Board's report for 1899 it ran some 438 hours on 38 tons of coal, and in the report for 1900 some 312 hours on 26 tons.[^10][^11]

**Abyssinia, November 1900.** The Life-Saving Service reported that the schooner *Abyssinia* was dragging her anchors at Eagle Harbor, 30 miles from the Ship Canal station, in a heavy north-northeast snowstorm. When a tug refused to go because of the gale, the Ship Canal keeper brought his surfboat and beach apparatus by special train. The schooner's master, L. Coleman, wrote from Eagle Harbor on 9 November 1900 that the life-saving crew "came off last night while the wind was blowing hard from the NNE. and a big sea running."[^12]

**Late 1926.** In a letter of 31 December 1926, the Coast Guard district commander at Green Bay thanked the keepers at Eagle Harbor, Copper Harbor and Mendota for their help while the Eagle Harbor Coast Guard crew assisted the steamers *Thomas Maytham* and *City of Bangor*.[^17] The Eagle Harbor keeper and his assistant kept the discontinued range lights going. The Coast Guard officer in charge reported that although it was "almost impossible to obtain food in this isolated section", the light keepers shared the best they had.[^13]

**Ice.** The index to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1936 to 1939 lists "Eagle Harbor Lighthouse, Mich. withstands severe ice conditions."[^14]

## After the keepers

The light and its signals changed with the century. The Lighthouse Service switched the lamp to incandescent oil vapor in 1913, and LighthouseFriends dates the change to 20 June and says it made the light about eight times stronger.[^15][^2] Ruby glass was added to one flash panel in 1924, giving alternating red and white flashes, and the lake-facing side of the tower was painted white on 13 April 1925. An air diaphone replaced the whistle in 1928 and a radiobeacon followed in 1929.[^2] The Coast Guard replaced the Fresnel lens with a pair of aerobeacons in 1962.[^2][^3]

The light was automated in 1980 and the station was de-staffed on 8 January 1982, leaving Two Harbors, Minnesota as the only staffed lighthouse on Lake Superior.[^2][^3] The Keweenaw County Historical Society took temporary stewardship in 1982, and Congress transferred ownership to it in 1999.[^2][^3]

## Today

The Keweenaw County Historical Society runs the light station as a museum complex: the lighthouse itself, a maritime museum in the old fog signal building, a fishing museum and a Keweenaw history museum in other buildings.[^3] The Coast Guard still operates the light, so the top of the tower is not open to visitors.[^3] The station is at 670 Lighthouse Road. Its published season for 2027 runs from 20 June to 10 October, and the Society lists a lighthouse cottage rental.[^16][^3] The Society also lists an Eagle Harbor Lifesaving Station as a separate site.[^16]
