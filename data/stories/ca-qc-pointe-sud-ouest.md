The Pointe-Sud-Ouest light stands on the southwest point of Anticosti Island, in the Gulf of St. Lawrence, Quebec.[^3] Its stone tower was first lit in 1831 and was the island's first light; the Quebec heritage register also calls it the first rotating light on the St. Lawrence.[^1][^2] A fire in 1958 damaged the tower, its top fell in 2011, and the Canadian Coast Guard still lists a light on the point.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

## The place

The Coast Guard places the light on the extremity of the point.[^3] Reefs had already caught many ships off the island.[^2] In 1829 the Quebec harbour master, John Lambly, and Captain Bayfield of the Royal Navy recommended a light here, because most shipping then passed the south side of the island and the limestone and sand needed for building lay at the point.[^2] The register says the Trinity House of Quebec chose the site to keep trade between Britain and its colonies moving, and that navigation at the entrance to the St. Lawrence and around Anticosti was particularly precarious.[^1]

The land was not all the government's.[^5] In 1900 the Department's chief engineer reported that an acre had been surveyed round the lighthouse, that a telegraph house built in 1881 stood about 183 m (600 ft) from the tower and outside the surveyed acre, and that, according to Mr. Menier's surveyor, "some government buildings are beyond the government property" at this station.[^5]

## Building the light

Work began in 1830. A contractor, W. Lander, built the tower to a design by a Mr. Chillus, and the lantern came from England.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives the tower as 22 m (72 ft) tall and 11 m (36 ft) across at its base.[^2] The register gives the height as 22 m (72 ft) and says the tower was built of rubble limestone quarried nearby, lined inside with fire brick.[^1] The Route des phares and the National Trust give 24 m (79 ft).[^4][^6] The sources do not settle the height.

The light was first shown on 25 August 1831, with Lieutenant James Harvey of the Royal Navy as keeper.[^2] LighthouseFriends says it burned twenty-one flat-wick lamps behind reflectors.[^2] The tower also held the keeper's home.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that water came through the mortar in storms and that, when the wind blew the wrong way over the chimney, the tower filled with smoke in less than fifteen minutes.[^2] The tower was clapboarded in 1865 to make it watertight.[^2]

The paint is one point where the sources differ. The register says the tower was built white with two wide horizontal red bands.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the red bands were painted on in 1881.[^2] Also in 1831 a depot of food and clothing for shipwrecked sailors was set up in the lower storey of the tower, and signposts along the shore marked where its supplies lay.[^2]

## The keepers

The first keepers were Harvey (1831 to 1837), William Hall (1838 to 1839) and John Elias Hammond (1839 to 1843).[^2] Edward Pope, a retired naval officer from Prince Edward Island, took over in 1843.[^2] The Popes kept the light for more than fifty years.[^2] Edward Pope's son, Edward Pope Jr., succeeded him in 1857 and served until 1892.[^2] Herbert Pope, Edward Pope Jr.'s son, followed until 1899.[^2]

In August 1869, while the Pope family had typhoid fever, the revolving mechanism broke.[^2] LighthouseFriends says Keeper Pope turned the apparatus by hand every night from sunset to sunrise until the shipping season ended in December.[^2] In the spring he had help from his daughter, who had survived the fever, until the mechanism was replaced at the end of June.[^2] Edward Pope senior died in 1871 at seventy and is buried at the station.[^2] The cemetery at the foot of the tower, the Société historique du Golfe says, holds the shipwreck victims and the family members who lived at the light, and it served four generations.[^7]

Herbert Pope kept a kitchen garden with lettuce, cabbages, turnips, potatoes, carrots and rhubarb, which LighthouseFriends calls quite a feat on Anticosti's ground.[^2] Zephirin Lemieux was made keeper when Herbert Pope died in 1899, and LighthouseFriends gives 1900 as his first year.[^2] The Department's keeper table for the fiscal year ending March 31, 1908 lists "Z. Lemieux" on the South West point row.[^8] The meteorological inspections of 1902 and 1903 also name a new observer at South West Point, "Mr. E. Lemieux" and then "Mr. Lemieux".[^9][^10] The reports do not say whether the observer and the keeper were the same man.

After Zephirin Lemieux's stint ended in 1924, LighthouseFriends lists L. C. Lemieux (1924 to 1930), Placide Duguay (1930 to at least 1937), Sauver Duguay, Roger Poulin and Evariste Ferguson (1957 to 1958). It gives no dates for Sauver Duguay or Roger Poulin.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends dates to 6 December 1871 the loss of the barque *Russia*, of Sligo, Ireland, which foundered off the point in a winter gale after the impact ruptured her bottom.[^2] A crewman, Laughlin McLaughlin, tried to reach shore on a raft and was washed off and drowned.[^2] The boatswain, Hugh Harrison, also tried to reach shore on a raft and was washed off into the soft ice; Keeper Pope and his assistant, William Nadean, got him ashore.[^2] According to the captain's account as LighthouseFriends prints it, they carried him up a cliff to a fire they had made in the woods and warmed him through the night.[^2] The survivors stayed at the light through the winter.[^2] For the rescue, Pope and his assistant received inscribed watches from the government.[^2] LighthouseFriends also quotes the 1873 report of the department's inspector, who wrote that Pope had given valuable assistance in saving life and property in wrecks near the station.[^2]

A vessel record kept by the Shipping and Shipbuilding Research Trust gives a different account of the *Russia*: a wreck on Anticosti Island on 10 May 1872, on passage from Quebec to Troon with timber, with one crew member lost.[^11] The two accounts agree on one death and on the island, not on the date. This story keeps the date LighthouseFriends gives, and the disagreement stands.

On 22 November 1874 the brigantine *Orient*, of Carbonear, Newfoundland, struck the reefs off the island with sixteen men on board.[^2] LighthouseFriends says six were lost trying to reach the shore, and the captain, John Edgar Joyce, and seven others froze to death in the rigging.[^2] The mate, Gilbert Joyce, and a seaman, Charles Moore, survived, badly frozen, and stayed with the Pope family at the lighthouse until spring.[^2] The headstone in the station cemetery records the captain and seven other sailors of the *Orient*.[^7]

On 13 September 1905 the outward-bound *Wastwater*, loaded with timber and deals, went ashore on the island near South West Point.[^12] A formal inquiry was held at Quebec, and the ship sailed for England before its finding could be delivered.[^12]

In the fiscal year ending March 31, 1915 the Department's table lists, under "Southwest point, Anticosti," the provision and installation of a single-flash, long-focus reflector with a 55 mm burner.[^13] LighthouseFriends dates a single-flash, long-focus reflector to 1914.[^2] The table's entry falls in the fiscal year from April 1914 to March 1915, so the two may be the same installation, but the sources do not say so.

In August 1919 a British steamer, the *Admiral Hastings*, ran aground.[^14][^15] The Quebec Salvage and Wrecking Company's list in the Department's report says she ran ashore off South West Point and was salved between 23 and 30 August.[^14] The Department's wreck inquiry for the same year says she stranded on Bagot Bluff on 22 August.[^15] The two records do not agree on the place, and this story does not choose between them.

A fire in 1958 destroyed the keeper's house and damaged the tower.[^1][^2] The register says the lantern and the wooden cladding were lost.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the fire damaged the lighthouse.[^2]

## After the keepers

The register says the tower lost its navigation role during the 1950s.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that after the 1958 fire the light was replaced in 1959 by an automated light on an eighty-foot tower.[^2] In 1972 that tower gave way to a fifty-foot skeleton tower, which shows a white flash every six seconds.[^2] The Coast Guard list gives the present light as Fl W 6s, with a focal height of 20.7 m (68 ft), a range of 8 nautical miles, and a square skeleton tower 15.6 m (51 ft) high, lit year round.[^3]

The keepers were withdrawn, and the tower fell into disrepair.[^2] The register says that, apart from the tower, the station's buildings were destroyed in 1984.[^1] The National Trust for Canada says the Coast Guard abandoned the lighthouse and demolished the keeper's quarters, and that the light has been out of use since the early 1990s.[^4] The Route des phares describes the site as decommissioned and threatened.[^6] The Coast Guard list still gives a light. The sources do not agree on whether it is in service, and this story does not settle that.[^3]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^2] The top of the tower collapsed in August 2011.[^4] What stands now is the stone tower without its top, the steel skeleton tower that carries the current light, and a few remains.[^1][^3][^4]

The cemetery at the foot of the tower holds the headstone of the *Orient*'s captain and crew and the Pope family plot, which is surrounded by a weathered fence with missing slats.[^2][^7]

LighthouseFriends says the point is reached by four-wheel-drive vehicle once on Anticosti, and that the last stretch is a rugged shore track about 6 km long.[^2] The LighthouseFriends writer, describing a visit, says the journey took most of a day.[^2]
