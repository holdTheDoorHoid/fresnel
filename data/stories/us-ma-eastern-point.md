Eastern Point Light stands on a rocky point on the east side of the entrance to Gloucester Harbor, Massachusetts, where a stone beacon put up in 1829 became a lighthouse in 1832 and was rebuilt twice. The brick tower seen today dates from 1890, and the station's keepers also looked after the light at the end of the Gloucester breakwater.[^1][^2][^3][^5]

## The place

Lighthouse Friends says Gloucester residents appealed for a lighthouse to protect their fleets. A light on Ten Pound Island, inside the harbor, had been built in 1821, but Lighthouse Friends says it was not visible to ships outside the harbor, and that mariners sometimes confused Bemo Ledge with Eastern Point and several ended up wrecked in Brace Cove.[^3] Before the beacon was built, a grove of oak trees had served as a landmark for entering the harbor.[^3] In 1829 the Boston Marine Society wrote that a monument on Eastern Point would be useful to navigation in Boston Bay, "the old landmarks of trees being nearly decayed and gone."[^3]

The federal government bought the land in 1829. The National Register nomination gives the price as $300, Lighthouse Friends says a plot was bought from George Burnham for $100, and a Gloucester timeline drawing on Joseph Garland's *The Gloucester Guide* says only that an acre was bought at the end of the point.[^2][^3][^5] The point was then part of the Niles farm, the largest farm in Gloucester, according to the nomination, which also describes the station today as sitting on a rocky promontory overlooking Dog Bar Reef.[^2]

## Building the light

The first tower was a stone beacon with a lantern added. Lighthouse Friends says the beacon went up in 1829, that a lantern of wrought iron and a copper dome were added, and that the light first shone on 1 January 1832, with ten whale-oil lamps behind it.[^3] New England Lighthouses gives the same first-lighting date.[^4] Sources disagree over the cost: Lighthouse Friends gives $1,188 for the beacon and $2,579 for the conversion, New England Lighthouses $2,450 for the lighthouse.[^3][^4] Lighthouse Friends adds that President Andrew Jackson at first stood in the way of the project before Congress pushed it through.[^3]

It was a poor building. In 1843 the engineer I. W. P. Lewis inspected it for a report to Congress, and keeper Samuel Wonson wrote an account of the station for Lewis's report to Congress. As Lighthouse Friends reproduces it, the tower "leaks in every direction; the walls inside are covered with ice in the winter, and green mould in summer."[^3] The tower was torn down and a new brick tower, 34 feet (10 m) high, was built in 1848; the National Register nomination and Lighthouse Friends agree on the year, and Lighthouse Friends names Winslow Lewis as builder.[^2][^3] The sources disagree on when the light turned red. New England Lighthouses says the 1848 tower showed a fixed red light and became known as the "ruby light."[^4] Lighthouse Friends says it showed a fixed white light from 3 November 1848, dates the change to red to 1 April 1878, and says the light became a flashing red in 1882.[^3] New England Lighthouses also dates the change to flashing to 1882.[^4] A fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed in 1857, and the U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive gives the same year.[^1][^3] A hand-wound fog bell mechanism was installed the same year, and Lighthouse Friends says the extra work led to an assistant keeper being assigned.[^3]

The wooden Gothic Revival double dwelling, built in 1879, still stands.[^1][^2] The present tower replaced the 1848 one in 1890, on the old circular foundation. Lighthouse Friends says a temporary light was shown fifteen feet to the south between 28 April and 30 June 1890 while the old tower came down.[^2][^3] Sources give the new tower as 36 feet (11 m) high (Lighthouse Friends and the U.S. Lighthouse Society) or 38 feet (12 m) (the nomination).[^1][^2][^3]

## The keepers

Samuel Wonson was the first keeper, from 1832 to 1849, at $400 a year; Lighthouse Friends says he and his wife Lydia lived in the tiny brick keeper's house beside the tower.[^1][^3] The National Register nomination spells his name "Monson."[^2] After him the roster in the U.S. Lighthouse Society archive lists James Rowe, Lemuel G. Norwood and a succession of keepers who stayed a few years, until Charles Friend, who served from 1872 to 1882.[^1] George G. Bailey followed in 1882, and George E. Bailey kept the light from 1892 to 1926, a span of thirty-four years.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1913 records that George E. Bailey was commended for his work in repairing damage at the station.[^6] The roster lists a George W. Bailey as first assistant in 1901-02 and 1910-11, but no source here says how the Baileys were related.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends and the National Register nomination say the painter Winslow Homer lived at Eastern Point Light in 1880.[^2][^3] Museums that hold his work say otherwise: the Cape Ann Museum says he stayed on Ten Pound Island that year, and the Philadelphia Museum of Art says he lodged with the keeper there.[^7][^8] The sources do not agree, and this story does not settle it.

Frank H. Parsons was first assistant from 1918 to 1922.[^1] Lighthouse Friends reports that he was helping to paint the lighthouse when he fell from the staging, struck the rocks below and was killed instantly, at forty-nine, after four and a half years in the service; its list of sources includes a *Boston Daily Globe* article of 9 September 1922, which was not read for this story.[^3] Carl D. Hill was head keeper from 1934 to 1945, and the roster lists Coast Guard crews from 1947.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The station also had a fog signal. In May 1894 the Light-House Board's party landed at Eastern Point to test the sound of the fog bell, which the report says weighed 2,019 pounds and was struck a double blow by machinery every twenty seconds.[^9] A heavier bell followed: the Light-House Board's report for 1897 says the station got a 4,000-pound bell worked by an oil engine, a 130-foot (40 m) boat slip, and a rebuilt and extended covered way.[^10] Lighthouse Friends dates the new bell to January 1897 and New England Lighthouses to January 1907.[^3][^4] The Board's reports give the bell's running time: about 553 hours in the year to June 1899 and about 474 hours in the year to June 1900.[^11][^12]

The Gloucester Breakwater, begun in 1894 according to Lighthouse Friends, ran out from the point across Dog Bar Reef, and the keepers took on the job of caring for the light at its end. Lighthouse Friends says nearly forty ships had struck it before a light was placed there, and that the work could be dangerous when waves broke over the granite and ice coated it.[^3] The Board's report for 1900 records that on 22 March 1900 the gas buoy at the end of the breakwater was damaged in a collision and replaced.[^12]

A severe March gale in 1931 knocked blocks off the breakwater, severed the cable to the breakwater light and fog signal, and washed away the outer section of the boat slip, according to New England Lighthouses.[^4] In the hurricane of 21 September 1938 the Lighthouse Service reported damage at Eastern Point, the most easterly point from which damage was reported.[^13] A grandson of keeper Carl Hill later wrote to New England Lighthouses that his mother, a nursing student, took the last train to Gloucester and was picked up by the keeper, who "just made it back" to the light; she told of waves washing over the residence and the road washed out for days.[^4] The great blizzard of 6-7 February 1978 did great damage to several buildings and much of the station's machinery, the same site reports.[^4]

## After the keepers

Coast Guard crews lived at the station into the 1980s.[^1][^4] The light was automated in 1985 (the nomination says 1986), and the U.S. Lighthouse Society archive lists Chris Benton in the roster to 1985, and New England Lighthouses calls him the last Coast Guard keeper.[^1][^2][^4] About twenty neighbors attended the ceremony, where Carroll Wonson, a descendant of the first keeper, was given the honor of turning the light on by hand for the last time; Lighthouse Friends calls him a great-grandson, New England Lighthouses a great-great-great-grandson.[^3][^4] After it was replaced by a modern optic, the fourth-order Fresnel lens went on display at the Cape Ann Museum in Gloucester.[^3][^14] The nomination, recorded in 1981, called Eastern Point the largest intact station in Massachusetts.[^2]

## Today

The station is an active aid to navigation owned by the U.S. Coast Guard, which uses the dwelling for housing. It is not open to the public.[^1][^4] The road to it runs through a private neighborhood, and Lighthouse Friends describes the tower, the dwelling and everything inside the station fence as off limits. The lighthouse can be seen from the breakwater walk and from boats leaving Gloucester.[^3][^4]
