Grandique Point Light stands on a beach on the north side of Isle Madame, Nova Scotia, near the east end of Lennox Passage, the channel between the island and Cape Breton.[^6][^8] It began in 1884 as a fixed red light on a mast.[^6] The tower there now is a white pyramid with a red top, showing a green flash of two seconds in every six, in the season.[^8]

## The place

Lennox Passage is roughly nineteen kilometres long.[^6] Small craft travelling between the Strait of Canso and St. Peters Canal often chose the passage as a shorter route that kept them clear of the open Atlantic.[^6] Grandique Point is the northernmost point on Isle Madame.[^6] The Lighthouse Directory says the light marks a dangerous spit that points north.[^9]

The name has moved about. Department reports wrote it "Grand Digue" or "Grande Digue."[^1][^2][^5] LighthouseFriends says the name comes from the French for a large dike or bank, and that the locality only formally became Grandique in 1956.[^6]

## Building the light

In 1884 three pole lights went up along Lennox Passage, one of them at Grandique Point, to serve the steamers that carried passengers and mail between Port Mulgrave and Sydney by way of St. Peters Canal.[^6] Each showed a fixed red light from a small lantern on a mast 7.6 m (25 ft) high, with a small shed at its base. LighthouseFriends says the lights could be seen "about five miles."[^6]

In 1899, LighthouseFriends says, the mast and its shed were moved "to a safer position."[^6]

Work on a tower to replace the mast began in 1906.[^6] The Department's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1906 listed "Grand Digue, a tower built to replace a mast light" among the works then under way.[^1] Its report for the year ending March 31, 1908 described the finished tower: "The tower is a wooden building, square in plan, with sloping sides, surmounted by a square wooden lantern, the whole painted white."[^2] It stood on the south side of the road to the old wharf, "distant 70 feet southward of the site of the old pole light" (about 21 m).[^2]

The 1908 report gives the height as 32 feet (9.8 m) from the base to the top of the lantern's ventilator.[^2] Its list of changed lights calls the same tower "a new 25-foot tower fitted with a 6th order dioptric light."[^3] The report prints both figures without reconciling them.[^2][^3] The lens was a sixth-order dioptric, "fixed red," elevated 29 feet (8.8 m) above high water and "visible 7 miles from all points of approach."[^2] The work went by contract to "Lawrence Mury, of West Arichat, N.S.," for $452.[^2]

The Lighthouse Directory and the Society both give 1907 for the change, which falls within the period the reports cover.[^9][^7]

## The keepers

Daniel Clough was the first keeper, and LighthouseFriends says he was paid $60 a year.[^6] The Society calls him the son of "the merchant Nathaniel Cough," a recipient of one of the first Crown land grants on Isle Madame. The same sentence spells the son's name "Clough" and the father's "Cough."[^7]

A statement of light-keepers in the report printed in 1901 gives the date "July 4, 1884" beside the Grandique Pole Light, the year the lights went up.[^4] The statement in the report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1906, printed in 1907, names "Clough, Daniel" at the "Grand Digue Pole Light."[^5] He looked after the light for twenty-four years, "longer than any subsequent keeper."[^6]

After Clough, LighthouseFriends names D. A. Kaulbach (1908 to 1909) and C. A. Kaulbach (1909 to 1919), then Charles J. Shannon (1919 to at least 1923). It names John Doiron and Henry Young without dates, then Melvin Shannon (1939 to 1960).[^6] Stanley Forgeron took charge in 1960, and the Society says he "oversaw the conversion to an automated light in 1961."[^6][^7] LighthouseFriends adds that after automation his role "was reduced to that of a caretaker."[^6]

## After the keepers

In October 1961 the tower received two parallel sets of five large single-cell batteries, giving a six-volt supply that a sun switch controlled.[^6] Commercial power reached the light in 1977.[^6] The Society's list gives the light a sixth-order dioptric lens, fixed red, with a season from April to January between 1922 and 1941.[^7]

Erosion has moved the tower. The Lighthouse Directory says it was moved southward in 1963 and again in 2010 to escape beach erosion.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the tower was placed on its present concrete foundation in 2010.[^6] The Society dates the provincial park on the Crown land under the light to 1981.[^7]

## Today

The Society says the Government of Canada owns the light.[^7] The Lighthouse Directory names the Canadian Coast Guard as owner and Lennox Passage Provincial Park as site manager.[^9] The Coast Guard's List of Lights gives the light's characteristic as a two-second green flash in every six seconds, 9.2 metres above the water, visible 7 nautical miles, and seasonal.[^8]

The grounds are open to visitors and the tower is closed.[^6][^9] The light is reached by a gravel road inside Lennox Passage Provincial Park.[^9]
