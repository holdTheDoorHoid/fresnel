Brockton Point Lighthouse stands at the point of that name in Stanley Park, Vancouver, British Columbia, where a light marked the sharp turn toward First Narrows from 1890 until it became inactive in 2008.[^12][^13][^14] The tower there today dates from 1914 or 1915, depending on the source.[^12][^14][^15]

## The place

Brockton Point is named for Francis Brockton, an engineer aboard HMS *Plumper*, which charted the British Columbia coast in 1857 and 1860.[^12] Once the transcontinental railway reached Vancouver, shipping traffic in and out of Burrard Inlet grew.[^12] The Prospect Point light, set up in 1888 on the south side of First Narrows, guided ships into Vancouver, but the dense forest of Stanley Park hid its light from outbound ships.[^12]

The hazard was under the water. A survey of Burrard Inlet, reported by a San Francisco paper in June 1892, found that Parthia Shoal, which lies northwest of Brockton Point, had only a few fathoms over it at low water.[^4] In 1897 day marks were put up on Brockton Point to clear Parthia Shoal at First Narrows, the entrance to Vancouver harbour.[^2] A Seattle paper's notice of September 1897, listing changes to marks in British Columbia waters, mentions Parthia Shoal and the "masts on Brockton point."[^5]

## Building the light

The first light was a mast with red and white lanterns, placed at the point in 1890 to mark the sharp turn toward First Narrows for outbound ships and toward Coal Harbour for inbound vessels.[^12][^13]

By the time the Department of Marine and Fisheries issued its report for 1902, the light had moved. "A lighthouse with dwelling attached has been built S. 5° W. 85 feet distant from the place at which the light was previously shown," the report says.[^1] The new building was "a wooden building, painted brown and yellow with red roof." Its light was shown from a wooden lantern on the first floor, and the building was 8.8 m (29 ft) high.[^1] The report adds that "As the building is conspicuous it was made somewhat ornate to suit its surroundings."[^1]

The new lighthouse also ended the 1897 marks. The report says they were removed because "the new lighthouse, built to replace the pole light on Brockton point, interrupted the range."[^2] The same report's notes on Vancouver harbour record that the "Intervals between strokes of Brockton point fog bell" had been corrected.[^3]

The present tower dates from 1914 or 1915. The Vancouver Heritage Foundation says it was "Designed and constructed by Colonel William Anderson."[^12] Russ Rowlett's directory gives the date as 1915 and names William P. Anderson beside it.[^14] The federal heritage description, as copied on Flickr, calls the tower "a simple, reinforced-concrete square tower."[^16]

## The keepers

William D. Jones was keeper from 1890 to 1924, according to the Vancouver Heritage Foundation.[^12] The Department's statement of lightkeepers for 1908 gives "W. D. Jones" at Brockton Point, appointed 20 August 1890.[^17] A Department list of names and stations from 1907 gives "Jones, William D" at "Brocton Point, Burrard Inlet."[^7]

Lighthouse Friends says Jones was given no house, so he built a cottage out of driftwood from the shore.[^13] It credits the impending visit of the Duke of Cornwall and York to Stanley Park with the government's decision to build him a more substantial dwelling, completed in 1902.[^13] The Department's 1902 report describes the new building with its attached dwelling.[^1] From 1909, Lighthouse Friends says, Jones also controlled outgoing ship traffic from a semaphore, a duty that ended in 1918.[^13]

Lighthouse Friends also gives an account of a rescue in 1905. Jones was eating dinner when he heard screams from the harbour. He rowed out, and he and another man pulled a man from the water just as a yacht arrived. The account says the man died of shock: "Had he not died of the shock, we would have saved him."[^13]

After Jones, the Vancouver Heritage Foundation lists E.E. Beck (1924 to 1925), John H. Walsh (1925 to 1936), Alex McLeod (1936 to 1940) and G. B. Kilgour (1940 to 1956).[^12]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 21 July 1906 the steamer Princess Victoria collided with the Chehalis near Brockton Point. The Department's report describes the Princess Victoria on a voyage from Vancouver to Victoria, and says the Chehalis sank, with "nine persons lost their lives."[^6] The Department calls the Chehalis a steamer; the Western News and Lighthouse Friends call her a tug.[^6][^10][^13]

The San Francisco Call's report carried the headline "Nine Persons Meet Death."[^9] The Daily Alaskan of Skagway, printing a Vancouver dispatch, said: "Nine persons were drowned."[^8] A Vancouver dispatch dated 23 July, printed in the same Daily Alaskan issue, reported that the Princess Victoria's captain, Griffin, had been arrested "on the charge of manslaughter."[^8]

Lighthouse Friends and a Vancouver history blog both give the toll as eight.[^13][^15]

The accounts also disagree about the Princess Victoria's course. The Department's report has her on a voyage from Vancouver to Victoria. The Western News of Stevensville, Montana, says she was "leaving Vancouver port for Victoria and Seattle."[^10] The Daily Alaskan says she was "coming in from Seattle."[^8]

The Department's casualty list for the fiscal year 1908 to 1909, which runs from July 1908 to June 1909, records that the steamer Charmer was ashore at "Vancouver narrows, near Brockton point" on December 3 to 5, 1908, in "dense fog." It says she was "pumped out and conveyed to Victoria."[^11]

## After the keepers

The Vancouver Now and Then blog says the tower "was staffed until 1956."[^15] Rowlett's directory gives the light as inactive since 2008, and notes that "a decorative light may be displayed."[^14]

## Today

Brockton Point is in Stanley Park.[^12] Rowlett describes the tower as a 10.5 m (35 ft) square tower.[^14] The federal heritage description says the Stanley Park seawall passes under arched openings at the tower's base.[^16]

Rowlett gives the owner as the Canadian Coast Guard and the site manager as the Vancouver Board of Parks and Recreation, and says the site is open and the tower closed.[^14] The Parks Board has managed the lighthouse since 2006.[^12]
