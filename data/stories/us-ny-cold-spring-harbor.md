Cold Spring Harbor Light stood for 75 years on a shoal at the entrance to Cold Spring Harbor, on Long Island's north shore.[^5][^6] It was a wooden tower on an iron caisson, first lit on January 31, 1890, and it was deactivated in 1965.[^1][^5] The wooden tower was then moved ashore to private land on Centre Island.[^1][^5] The caisson and a metal superstructure remain at the original site.[^5]

## The place

A muddy shoal reaches out from the eastern side of Centre Island, and vessels entering the harbor had to keep close to Lloyd's Neck to avoid it.[^5] Congress appropriated $20,000 in 1875 for a plan of two range lights on the mainland.[^5] Acquiring the land proved difficult, because the owners were reluctant to sell.[^5][^6] Bachand dates the switch to a lighthouse on the shoal to 1888, when the Light House Service opted instead to erect one.[^6] LighthouseFriends says a workable plan for an offshore station was approved in 1889.[^5]

## Building the light

The site chosen was the easterly point of the shoal. Construction began in the late summer of 1889 with the sinking of a cast-iron caisson.[^6] The wooden square pyramidal tower rested on a concrete-filled iron caisson.[^5] Its fourth-order Fresnel lens was powered by kerosene, and the light was first lit on January 31, 1890.[^5] A fog bell was struck automatically every thirty seconds when visibility was limited.[^5] A boat hung from davits, which keepers used to reach the mainland.[^5]

The light first showed a fixed red.[^5] On 31 May 1892 the Light-House Board recorded its change to fixed white with a fixed red sector.[^2]

## The keepers

The first keeper, William S. Keene, was chosen from a pool of seventy-five applicants but served for just a month before resigning.[^5][^1] His replacement, Samuel Leonard Turner, spent a decade at the light, one of the longest terms at this cramped station.[^5][^1]

The roster then shows keepers serving shorter stints. Adolph Obman served from 1901 to 1903, Knute O. Ericson from 1903 to 1908, and Chester Rowland's stint is printed as 1908.07.01 to 1908.07.31.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says that on August 3, 1908, Rowland returned to the station after watching motorboat races at Huntington Harbor. He slipped on the steep ladder and fell into the water. The men on shore could not find him, and he could not swim. His body was found three days later at Whitehead Point.[^5] The roster's end date of July 31 does not match this account.[^1]

Arthur Jensen took charge next, but he remained for only eight months before a transfer to Eaton's Neck.[^5] LighthouseFriends says President Theodore Roosevelt, who had a summer home nearby at Sagamore Hill, visited the station several times while Jensen was the keeper.[^5]

Louis P. Brown kept the light from 1913 to 1924, the roster says.[^1] Robert G. Bachand's history says Brown arrived on October 19, 1913, aboard the tender *Larkspur*.[^6] LighthouseFriends quotes Brown's own account of one rescue, in which he said that his wife was on the station and kept the light while he was away.[^5] Ellsworth J. Smith (1924 to 1938) and Albert G. Possell (1938 to at least 1942) are the last keepers on the roster.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 2 November 1914 Brown towed a disabled launch to safety in a northwest gale.[^3] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's December 1914 issue listed the rescue among those of October and November.[^3]

In February 1918 a cake of ice about a mile square came out on the tide and struck the station.[^5] LighthouseFriends dates the gale to 25 February, while Bachand gives the night of 19 February.[^5][^6] Brown's letter, printed in the Lighthouse Service Bulletin, says that the ice "struck this station with such force it" and that the blow threw "a number of articles from shelves, oil cases" about the room.[^4][^5] He put out the light for an hour and ten minutes and used a hand lantern instead.[^4][^5] LighthouseFriends says an inspection the following spring found the ice had caused $12,800 worth of damage to the station.[^5][^6]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1948, and maintenance passed to Coast Guard personnel at Eaton's Neck.[^5] In 1965 the wooden tower was removed from the caisson and replaced by a steel skeleton tower.[^5][^6] The USLHS record instead dates a skeletal replacement on the old foundation to 1926.[^1]

The old tower was to be torn down, but a resident living on Centre Island purchased it for one dollar.[^5] Bachand says the Coast Guard had planned to burn it at Eaton's Neck, and that the buyer's family saved it instead.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the tower went out on a barge that grounded on a sandbar for almost a year before it floated free.[^5] Bachand gives a different sequence: the tower was found floating, tethered to its old foundation, and after the purchase it was floated ashore and dragged across a shallow marsh with a truck's winch.[^6] Both accounts end with the tower on the buyer's land, and LighthouseFriends says it overlooks the water where it formerly stood.[^5][^6] LighthouseFriends also says it is the only lighthouse on Long Island that has been moved from its original site.[^5]

## Today

The lighthouse is privately owned, and the grounds and tower are closed.[^5] The USLHS record shows the station as not open to the public.[^1] Centre Island is a gated community, so it is not open to the public either.[^5] The original caisson can be seen from Lloyd Beach.[^5] The wooden tower can be seen from the Centre Island shore across the harbor.[^5]
