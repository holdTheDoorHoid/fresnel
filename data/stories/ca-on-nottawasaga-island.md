Nottawasaga Island Light is a limestone tower on a small island just offshore from Collingwood, Ontario, at the southern end of Georgian Bay. It was one of the six "Imperial Towers" built on Lake Huron and Georgian Bay in the 1850s, and it is now the sole intact structure left on its island.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Nottawasaga Bay is the southernmost part of Georgian Bay.[^1] The island lies in a line of reefs and shoals that parallel this stretch of shore, and the Escarpment article, which draws on a Collingwood Museum podcast, calls that reef line one of the main reasons a lighthouse was needed here.[^2] The island is about 2 ha (five acres) in size.[^2] Nearby ledges include Lockerbie Rock and the Mary Ward ledges.[^2]

Collingwood was chosen in 1851 as the northern terminus of the Ontario, Simcoe and Huron Railway, and the first train reached the town in 1855.[^1] The Escarpment article lists the opening of the Bruce Peninsula and the influx of settlers, a free-trade agreement with the United States, the opening of the Sault Ste. Marie Canal and the railway among the reasons that navigation aids on the Great Lakes became urgent.[^2] For many years locals knew the island as Clark Island, after one of its early keepers.[^2]

## Building the light

In 1855 John Brown, a builder from Thorold, was contracted to erect eleven lighthouses. Only six were built. The Escarpment article notes that the reason for the name "Imperial" is not known.[^1][^2]

The Nottawasaga tower was finished during 1856, together with a one-and-a-half-storey dwelling. The lantern and lighting apparatus were still to come. Lighthouse Friends says the apparatus was supplied by the Parisian manufacturer Louis Sautter, and the Escarpment article says a French crew was needed to assemble the lantern rooms.[^1][^2] The Escarpment article spells the maker's name "Salter". The Canadian Register describes the tower as tall, round and slightly tapered, with rusticated limestone walls and a twelve-sided cast-iron lantern under a copper roof.[^3]

The Escarpment article quotes Collingwood Public Library records giving a cost of $14,120 for the lighthouse. It also says a tower, keeper's house and outbuildings cost between $32,000 and $37,000 at each of the Imperial Towers, depending on the site.[^2]

A temporary light was shown from the top of the tower in 1857. The permanent light, with a second-order Fresnel lens that revolved to give a flash, was first lit on 30 November 1858.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends gives the tower's height as 85 feet, and the Escarpment article gives 86 feet (about 26 m).[^1][^2] The article says the light could be seen for 17 miles (27 km); it does not say whether those miles are nautical.[^2]

The first lamps burned sperm whale oil in Argand lamps. The Escarpment article, drawing on a book by Marion E. Sandell, says these were replaced in 1868 by Doty's Patent, a lamp made for petroleum oils.[^2] The light turned from sunset to sunrise from April to December, and the weights that drove it had to be wound up every four and a half hours. Lighting up each evening and closing down each morning took about twenty minutes each.[^2]

The Department of Marine and Fisheries' annual report for the fiscal year ending 31 March 1909 records that a 55 mm "Diamond vapour installation" on an adjustable iron stand replaced the station's duplex lamp.[^4]

## The keepers

Captain George F. Collins applied to be the station's first keeper, but he was placed in charge of Cove Island Lighthouse instead. In the 1859 season he swapped stations with David McBeath, who had been keeper here. Lighthouse Friends says Collins wanted to be closer to his home in Collingwood.[^1] Collins served here from 1859 until 1890.[^1][^2] The Escarpment article credits him with saving 52 lives in those years.[^2]

In 1879 a Masonic lodge in Boston presented him with a silver tea service for saving four of its members. Lighthouse Friends says he capsized four times reaching them.[^1] His son Charles, who had gone out with him in the 1872 rescue, later drowned.[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives no date. It says Charles was knocked overboard while fishing, became tangled in his nets and drowned, and that his father was not present to save him.[^1]

The Escarpment article describes "Captain Clark", who was orphaned at thirteen, went to sea young, carried mail by schooner from Collingwood to Manitoulin Island and ran a fishing business before he became keeper. It dates his keepership from 1890 to 1902.[^2] Lighthouse Friends names Arthur George Clark for 1890 to 1903. It then lists a Sarah Clark as head keeper from 1903 to 1904, and it does not say how she was connected to him.[^1]

The next keeper on the list, John F. Burmister, served from 1904 to 1911.[^1] The same report's list of lightkeepers gives "J. F. Burmister" at Nottawasaga Island with a salary of $555.[^5] The list then runs through James McNabb (1912 to 1913) and Anne McNabb (1913 to 1915), Thomas W. Bowie (1915 to 1924), Thomas Edward Foley (1924 to 1932), Samuel Neally Hillen (1933 to 1942) and William "Scotty" Hogg (1943 to 1952).[^1]

Jim Keith kept the light from 1952 to 1956, according to the Escarpment article. The article says that when he had appendicitis, he reached hospital in time for surgery, with his wife Doris's help.[^2] Lighthouse Friends prints his name as "Jams A. Keith" for 1953 to 1956.[^1]

The article says there was no communication system on the island, no running water and no electricity, and the house was heated with wood cut from dead trees or gathered as driftwood. Wives and children often joined the keepers at weekends or in summer. Keepers' children remembered summers spent swimming, boating and fishing, and townspeople came out on Sunday visits for tours and picnics.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1861 the beaches on both sides of the tower had washed away, and the tower's foundation was partly undermined.[^1] In 1876 the water level was so high that waves broke against the tower in stormy weather.[^1] In 1878 Joseph White of Ottawa was paid $950 to build a rip-rap breakwater.[^1]

In 1889 a red sector was added to the light to mark the outer edge of the shoals between the island and Collingwood Harbour.[^1]

Late on 24 November 1872, the 37 m (120 ft) steamer *Mary Ward* ran aground on Milligan's Reef, about two kilometres off Craigleith, a few kilometres west of Nottawasaga Island. Lighthouse Friends says she was carrying coal oil, salt and twenty-seven people, and was bound for Collingwood.[^1] The Escarpment article says the ship ran aground on Milligan's Reef in November 1872, and that the reef was later renamed Mary Ward Shoal.[^2]

A quick inspection found little damage, and because the bay was calm, the passengers and crew stayed aboard to wait for help.[^1] Just after midnight the wind shifted and the seas began to rise.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says eight men who tried to follow a first boat ashore later that day drowned when a large wave overturned their lifeboat.[^1] When the storm abated that afternoon, Keeper Collins and his son Charles launched their lifeboat. A tug sent out to help turned back. Frank Moberly, who headed a Canadian Pacific Railway survey party aboard the ship, went by train to Thornbury for fishermen. Setting out from Thornbury with Moberly and several fishermen, George and Charles Collins helped save those still aboard.[^1] The government paid fifteen dollars to George and Charles Collins and eleven other men for helping.[^1]

The Escarpment article says Collins helped rescue 24 people on one day. It does not name the ship for that day, so this story does not link the two accounts.[^2]

## After the keepers

A fire badly damaged the keeper's house in 1958, and the era of resident keepers ended.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends says the light was automated at that point. The Escarpment article says it "would be fully automated in 1959".[^1][^2] The sources therefore disagree on the year.

The keeper's house, abandoned for many years, was torn down in 1971.[^2]

An engineering study commissioned in 2002 estimated that the tower's outer wall would fail in about five years if the needed repairs were not made. Lighthouse Friends says the visible cracks were likely caused by lightning strikes and years of freeze-thaw cycles.[^1] The Coast Guard decommissioned the light in 2003. The solar-powered light kept running on its own for several more months.[^1] The Escarpment article says the tower was struck by lightning in 2004 and damaged, and that bands were placed around it to slow the damage.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says that in late 2004 a large section of the outer wall on the northeast side collapsed.[^1]

In 2005 the Minister of Fisheries and Oceans announced $230,000 in federal money to stabilise the tower. The contractor, Roof Tile Management, Inc., chipped away loose rock, coated the exposed inner surface and wrapped the tower with seven reinforcing bands. Lighthouse Friends says the estimated cost of full restoration had risen from about $600,000 to several million dollars.[^1] The Escarpment article says the Coast Guard stopped all further maintenance in 2010, leaving the tower's future in doubt.[^2] Volunteer engineers were allowed onto the island in 2014 and found the structure still stable.[^1]

The Nottawasaga Lighthouse Preservation Society was incorporated in 2015 as a volunteer, not-for-profit group. It hopes to acquire and restore the tower and to rebuild the keeper's residence.[^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and the grounds and tower are closed.[^1] The site is listed in the Canadian Register of Historic Places, which cites Federal Heritage Buildings Review Office Building Report 98-019.[^3] Since no one lives on the island any more, it has become an important bird sanctuary.[^2]

The island is closed, so visitors should not land on it. Lighthouse Friends suggests two places to see the tower from the mainland: a distant view from Princeton Shores Park, and the spit that protects Collingwood Harbour.[^1]
