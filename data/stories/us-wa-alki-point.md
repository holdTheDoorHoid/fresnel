Alki Point Light stands on the tip of Alki Point, across Elliott Bay from downtown Seattle, where it marks the southern entrance to the bay.[^2][^3] The first official light was a lantern on a wooden post, placed there in 1887.[^2][^3] The present 37-foot (11 m) octagonal tower, joined to a fog-signal building, was completed in 1913, and it still shows a white flash every five seconds.[^2][^3][^16]

## The place

Alki Point forms the southern boundary of Elliott Bay.[^3] In 1912 the Lighthouse Service described the new site as directly across the bay from Seattle, at an important turning point for vessels bound from Seattle to Tacoma.[^4] The point was settled early. LighthouseFriends says twenty-four people from the schooner Exact landed there in November 1851 and formed a colony.[^2] HistoryLink dates the Denny Party's settlement of the land to September 1851.[^3] The two sources give different months.

The land changed hands several times. Hans Martin Hanson and his brother-in-law Knud Olson bought the 320-acre farm in 1868 for $450.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends says the pair would reportedly light a lamp on the point as a service to mariners.[^2] HistoryLink calls the first light a legend from the 1870s, when Hanson is said to have hung a lantern from a post by his barn.[^3]

## Building the light

The Lighthouse Board decided Alki Point was a hazard to maritime traffic and needed an official signal light.[^3] In 1887 the Lighthouse Service placed a lens-lantern atop a wooden post at the point, and Hanson was hired to care for it at $15 a month.[^2] HistoryLink says that post lantern was used "at many locations until a permanent lighthouse could be built," and that the post lantern used at Alki ran from 1887 to 1912.[^3]

The money came slowly. In 1895 the Lighthouse Board asked for $6,000 for a fog bell and a suitable dwelling at the point. Congress appropriated the money on 28 June 1902.[^2] By 1907 the fog-signal site still had not been obtained, and another $8,000 was allocated. An act of 17 June 1910 authorized $33,000 more, which brought the total to $47,000.[^2] HistoryLink says the Bureau of Lighthouses, newly created that year, received the appropriation.[^3] The government bought a pie-shaped piece of 1.5 acres at the tip of the point from Edmund Hanson for $9,999.[^3]

The 1912 *Lighthouse Service Bulletin* tracked the construction in short notes. It noted that plans had been approved,[^5] that bids were opened on 1 April,[^6] that the bids were rejected and the work readvertised,[^7] that a contract was awarded,[^8] and that work on the building was progressing.[^9] By 1913 the north and south dwellings were nearly finished.[^10]

The 1912 *Bulletin* said the tower and fog signal would be of concrete, with frame dwellings for the keepers.[^4] HistoryLink describes a 37-foot (11 m) octagonal tower of concrete and masonry.[^3] The archive gives the tower material as brick, so the two sources disagree.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the fog-signal building was completed on 29 April 1913, with the attached octagonal tower, and that the station was activated on 1 June.[^2] HistoryLink gives 1 April 1913 as the day the station was ready for operation.[^3] The 1913 *Bulletin* lists the light changed from fixed to flashing, with oil-vapor illumination, and a fog signal established at Alki Point.[^11] A 1920 list of new aids gives the station's completion year as 1913, as a light and fog-signal station.[^14]

The lens was a fourth-order Fresnel made in Paris by L. Sautter and Lemonnier.[^2] A clockwork mechanism driven by a 110-pound weight turned it every ten seconds, which made a group of five white flashes followed by a five-second eclipse. The keeper had to wind the weight every five hours.[^2] HistoryLink says the lens stood about two and a half feet (0.8 m) high and weighed about 500 pounds, and it was visible for at least 12 miles.[^3]

The fog signal was a Daboll three-trumpet signal run by compressed air from two engines.[^3] LighthouseFriends gives its pattern: a three-second blast, six seconds of silence, a second three-second blast, then eighteen seconds of silence.[^2] Two keepers worked alternate 12-hour shifts, seven days a week, and each earned $800 a year plus housing.[^3] The light's power changed as well. HistoryLink says an electric bulb had replaced the lamp by 1918.[^3] A 1919 list of illuminant changes includes Alki Point's light, which went from incandescent oil vapor to electric incandescent.[^15] In 1919 an electric motor replaced one of the oil engines in the fog-signal building, LighthouseFriends says.[^2]

## The keepers

The Hanson family kept the first light. Hans Hanson was the official keeper, and his son Edmund, his six daughters and his niece Linda Olson helped him.[^3] Hanson died on 26 July 1900 and left the farm to his children. Edmund inherited the tip of the point and the keeper's job, and HistoryLink says he kept the lamp burning for the next ten years with his cousin's help.[^3] LighthouseFriends says Seattle city directories listed Edward Hanson as keeper from as early as 1891 to as late as 1907.[^2] The sources spell his first name differently, and their dates for him do not line up.

The archive lists the official keepers of the new station from 1913. Harry D. Mahler was the first head keeper.[^1][^2] Before Alki he had been an assistant at New Dungeness from 1890, and then head keeper at Patos Island, Cape Meares and West Point.[^2] He retired in 1930 at sixty-five, after forty years in the service.[^2] Ezra E. Marr was first assistant from 1913 to 1920, and Ray E. Dunson from 1920 to 1932.[^1]

Charles N. Elliot was head keeper from 1935 to 1942.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls him a "one-man reference library" on Walt Whitman, and says he used the long foggy nights of his watches to study the poet.[^2] George J. Smith followed him from 1942 to 1951.[^1]

Albert G. Anderson was first assistant from 1950 to 1951 and head keeper from 1951 to 1970.[^1] He joined the Lighthouse Service in 1927, first on the Columbia River lightship, then at Tillamook Rock and Cape Blanco, before he came to Alki.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he was one of the last two civilian lighthouse keepers in the country, and that he retired at the light in 1970.[^2] HistoryLink says he spent his last twenty years on the job at Alki.[^3] When Anderson was interviewed in 1969, the salary of a civilian keeper had been $5,100 a year for more than a decade. He said the pay had not risen in a long time.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The tower stood on the most exposed part of the point, and about 7,000 yards of sand and gravel were added to protect the buildings from heavy swells and high tides in stormy weather.[^3] The station also had a fog signal. The 1919 *Bulletin*, describing a hydro-turbine air compressor, says the fog signal at Alki Point consisted of a third-class reed.[^12] In 1947 the original fog-signal equipment was replaced with an electric compressor and twin horns.[^3]

## After the keepers

In 1921 the Commissioner's report asked for $3,000 to improve the station and noted that it had no workshop or storehouse, which it called urgent.[^13] The Coast Guard took over the lighthouses in 1939. Congress abolished the Bureau of Lighthouses and the Lighthouse Service that year, and civilian keepers stayed in their jobs until they retired.[^3]

In 1962 the Fresnel lens was replaced by an airway beacon, a rotating reflecting light like those at airports. It flashed every five seconds and was six times brighter than the lens, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] HistoryLink gives the candlepower as 80,000, against 13,000 for the old lens, and says the beacon was visible for 15 nautical miles.[^3]

The old lens-lantern from 1887 was on display in the lighthouse until the early 1970s, when a thief broke in and stole it. LighthouseFriends says the lantern was later traced and now sits in the Coast Guard Museum in Seattle, with a replica at the lighthouse.[^2] Accounts of the Fresnel lens itself differ. HistoryLink and LighthouseFriends say the original lens is on display at the Admiralty Head Lighthouse on Whidbey Island.[^2][^3] The Salish Sea Lighthouses site says the original fourth-order Fresnel lens was stolen in the 1970s, later recovered, and is now at the Coast Guard Museum.[^16]

In 1976 the station was designated as an historic place by the state and listed on the Washington Heritage Register.[^3] HistoryLink's account gives two statements about the National Register: one says the station is on it, and another says it is eligible for listing.[^3]

In October 1984 the light was fully automated, and the Coast Guard stopped keeping watches on it.[^2][^3] One of the two keepers' houses was remodeled for the commander of the 13th Coast Guard District. The commander later moved his official quarters to Medina.[^2][^3] The foghorn was removed in August 2005.[^3]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the lighthouse.[^2] The Salish Sea Lighthouses site lists it as owned by the Coast Guard and the Auxiliary, and says tours run on Sundays from May to September, from 1:00 to 4:00 p.m.[^16] LighthouseFriends says the grounds and tower are open during tours, and the dwellings are closed. It also says tours are "currently canceled due to high lead levels in the lighthouse." Check before you go, since the tour status can change.[^2]

The light still flashes from a rotating beacon, a VRB-25, once every five seconds.[^1][^3][^16] The original fog-signal house, with its generators and compressors, is kept in pristine condition for historic purposes.[^3]
