Baccalieu Island North Point Light stands at the north end of Baccalieu Island, a seabird island about two miles off the tip of the Bay de Verde Peninsula, in Conception Bay, Newfoundland and Labrador.[^1][^2] The first light was shown in 1858 from a brick tower at the north end of the island. The present light is a square steel skeleton tower, built in the 1990s, which stands near the original tower and is the one the Coast Guard lists today.[^1][^2]

## The place

Baccalieu Island is the largest protected seabird island in Newfoundland, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] The Town of Bay de Verde's heritage exhibit says the island has been listed on maps since the 16th century and has played a very important role in navigation.[^4]

The need for a light was raised early. Lighthouse Friends says Newfoundland's Commissioners of Lighthouses noted in 1840 that the island commanded the bays of Trinity and Conception, and that the St. John's and Conception Bay vessels usually took their departure from it.[^1] Funds were short, and other sites came first. An act letting part of the light dues pay for a light on Baccalieu was not passed until 1857.[^1]

## Building the light

In June 1857 Inspector Robert Oke visited the island to mark the site for the tower, for the contractors Haw and Saunders. Mr. Saunders died after the tender was accepted, the tenders were called again, and the contract went to John T. Nevill.[^1] Work on the tower and dwelling did not begin until the last week of July 1858.[^1]

The lantern room and lighting apparatus were supplied by David and Thomas Stevenson, engineers of Edinburgh. They reached St. John's on 16 October 1858 and were shipped to the island three days later aboard the steam tug *Blue Jacket*.[^1] Lighthouse Friends calls the revolving apparatus, with nine lamps and holophote lenses, the first use of a catadioptric holophote. The claim rests on that one source.[^1]

The tower was circular and of red brick, 7.6 m (25 ft) tall. Its lofty perch gave the light a focal plane of 135 m (443 ft), and Lighthouse Friends says the keepers at Cape Spear, more than forty miles away, could see its flash in clear weather.[^1] The light was shown for a few hours on 10 November 1858 and was officially placed in operation on 20 December 1858.[^1] A square, two-storey dwelling was joined to the tower by a covered way, painted white with a red roof.[^1]

The brick did not last well. The tower was coated in cement in 1859, and the coating needed repair. An 1884 inspection, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, said that "the brick and free stone work of the tower continue to perish." Iron boiler plates went over the brick in 1893.[^1] Iron beams replaced rotting wooden ones for the lantern room in 1880, and a two-storey addition enlarged the dwelling in 1882.[^1]

## The keepers

James Ryan was appointed the first keeper.[^1] Lighthouse Friends lists the North Point keepers as James Ryan (1858 to 1874), John Ryan (1875 to 1891), Francis Ryan (1891 to 1941) and James Ryan again (1941 to 1950). After them came Alphonsus Keough from 1950, and later keepers named without years, among them Felix Noonan, who was there at least in 1963.[^1] The Bay de Verde exhibit says the Ryans operated the north light for almost a hundred years.[^4]

When the Ryans left in 1950 for Toronto, a St. John's paper paid them a tribute, saying they were "ready at all times to risk their lives to save others."[^1]

Keeping the light had its troubles. In September 1867 Captain Kerr of the surveying steamer *Gulnare* complained that the light had seemed to show fixed for some time one night. Inspector Oke found no entry for it in the logbook, and he rejected the keeper's explanation.[^1] In 1881 the assistant keeper left his post after finding a substitute for his duties. Inspector Nevill called that "so gross a want of discipline" and had a new assistant sent out at once.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Bay de Verde exhibit says that shipwrecks have been common at Baccalieu Island, with more than 200 lives lost in more than 20 documented wrecks. It adds that families living on the island played key roles in saving the lives of others. It does not name the wrecks or give dates.[^4] No single wreck or rescue at the North Point light is dated in the sources.

The island's fog gave its own story. Lighthouse Friends says that in 1964 it was noted that, although the island had a light and fog alarm, the cries of the birds themselves served as an aid to navigation in fog.[^1]

## After the keepers

A second dwelling was built at the north station in 1950.[^1] In 1965 the original dwelling burned. The Coast Guard did not rebuild it. It automated the north light and moved the keepers to the southwest station, a separate light at the other end of the island.[^1]

In the 1990s the north light moved from the 1858 tower to a new tower. Lighthouse Friends gives its height as 45 ft. The Coast Guard's list gives the present square skeleton tower as 19.2 m (63 ft).[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends also says there were plans to demolish the 1858 tower, and that residents of Bay de Verde protested by letter, writing that demolishing the stone tower would destroy part of their culture.[^1]

The island was given provisional ecological reserve status in 1991 and became a designated ecological reserve in 1995.[^3]

## Today

The Coast Guard's list gives the north light as a white flash every six seconds: a one-second flash and a five-second eclipse. Its focal height is 176 m and its nominal range is seven nautical miles. The tower is a square skeleton tower with a red and white daymark. The list says the light operates year round, and it adds that the light "is often obscured by fog when the lower part of the island is clear."[^2]

Lighthouse Friends says the lighthouse is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard, and that the grounds are open but the tower is closed.[^1] The island is part of the Baccalieu Island Ecological Reserve. It can be seen from shore across Baccalieu Tickle, from Red Head Cove.[^3] During the breeding season, from April 1 to October 30, access to the seabird nesting areas is limited to scientific researchers and people with valid permits. Other parts of the island may be visited at any time without a permit. Boat landings at Ned Walsh's Cove and London Cove can be dangerous and should be tried only by experienced boaters, and aircraft may not land in the reserve or fly below 300 m in that season.[^3] The Bay de Verde Heritage House exhibit, opened in 1999, has displays on the island and its lighthouse.[^4] Do not climb the tower.
