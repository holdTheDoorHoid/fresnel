Charity Island Light stood on the northwest point of Big Charity Island, one of two islands in the middle of Saginaw Bay on Lake Huron, Michigan.[^2] It was a brick tower about 39 feet (12 m) tall, beside a keeper's dwelling, built in 1857.[^1] The archive gives 1939 as the year it was discontinued, and LighthouseFriends gives 1942.[^1][^2] Today the keeper's house is privately owned, the tower belongs to The Nature Conservancy, and the island is reached by boat.[^2]

## The place

Big Charity Island is about 230 acres, and Little Charity, its sister island, is about four.[^2] Early fishermen named them for God's charity, which they believed had placed the islands in the bay as a refuge from storms.[^2] The islands were also a hazard, ringed by flats for two miles in every direction.[^2]

In 1837 Lieutenant G. J. Pendergrast, who had been sent to choose lighthouse sites on the Great Lakes, reported that "as the trade of Saginaw increases, there will be a light required on Charity island." In 1838 Lieutenant James T. Homans added that the bay "is oftentimes as boisterous as that of the adjoining lake."[^2]

## Building the light

On 18 August 1856 Congress appropriated $5,000 for a lighthouse on the island.[^2] The structure was built in 1857 on the northwest point.[^2] The archive gives the same year.[^1]

The tower was a cylindrical brick tower eight feet (2.4 m) across, with a decagonal cast-iron lantern holding a fourth-order Fresnel lens, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The archive describes the tower as conical, and gives the light as 45 feet (14 m) above the water and the tower as 39 feet (12 m) to the lantern.[^1] The two accounts differ on the tower's shape. A wooden one-and-a-half-story dwelling stood beside it, joined to the tower by a covered passage.[^2] The lighthouse cost $4,819.35, and Colin Graham was hired as its first keeper.[^2]

The first lamp was a kerosene lamp, and the archive lists an acetylene lamp as the current optic.[^1]

The Lighthouse Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 records that "a brick oil house with a capacity of 360 gallons was erected, and various repairs were made."[^3]

## The keepers

The archive lists 31 keeper stints at Charity Island, from 1857 to 1916.[^1] Six principal keepers served there, beginning with Colin Graham (1857–1865) and ending with Charles McDonald (1882–1915).[^1]

McDonald is the keeper the sources describe in most detail. LighthouseFriends says he served more than thirty years as head keeper, "far longer than anyone else," and that before coming to the island he had been in charge of the lifesaving station at Pointe aux Barques.[^2] For the first several years on the island his wife and children lived with him. When the children were of school age, he bought a farm near Caseville for his family, and the lighthouse could be lonely after they left.[^2]

Harriet Howard served as a first assistant keeper from 1877 to 1879.[^1] Joseph N. Singleton was first assistant from 1909 to 1916.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls him the last keeper on the island, who was then transferred to Two Harbors Lighthouse.[^1][^2]

McDonald also watched the birds. During the spring migration of 1910 he picked up 174 brightly colored dead birds that had struck the lantern in one night, LighthouseFriends reports.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the night of 4 December 1885 the steamer *Oconto* was crossing Saginaw Bay in a blinding blizzard, with passengers and crew aboard.[^2] Her second engineer gave the *Detroit Free Press* an account, which an Alpena newspaper printed. He said the boat had left Oscoda on a Friday afternoon, and that the crew mistook the Charity light for the Tawas light: "everybody took it for the Tawas light."[^5] The boat grounded near the island.[^5]

The passengers were sheltered on the island.[^2][^5] The two ladies and the child "were taken direct to the light-keeper's house," where, the account says, "they received every attention." The men moved into two fish huts that the account describes as ready for housekeeping.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the lifesaving crew at Tawas was later sent to bring the others to the mainland.[^2]

The keeper himself was in trouble in July 1900.[^2] On 6 July McDonald was returning to the island with the four men who were building the oil house, when a squall capsized their sailboat.[^2] In a letter of 5 September 1900, the Eleventh District inspector passed on McDonald's report that he had been "capsized in Lake Huron on July 6, while en route from Caseville to his light station." The life-saving crews of Port Austin and Tawas set out to help, but "he and his comrades had been rescued by fishermen" before they reached him.[^4] LighthouseFriends tells it differently. It says the men clung to the upturned boat for twenty hours until boys on the beach saw them, and that the steamer *Sprite* then took them off.[^2] The two accounts disagree on who rescued them.

## After the keepers

In June 1916 the light was changed to a white flash every ten seconds, after an automated acetylene light was installed in the tower.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1916 records the change as "fixed white to flashing white, oil to electricity."[^6] The archive and LighthouseFriends both say acetylene, so the Bulletin's wording conflicts with them.[^1][^2][^6]

In 1926 the government sold the island to the Gillingham Fish Company, keeping only the lighthouse reservation.[^2]

The light was discontinued when Gravelly Shoal Light, a few miles east, came into service.[^1][^2] The archive gives 1939 as the year of discontinuance.[^1] The island's owners say the light was abandoned in 1939, when the Gravelly Shoals light went into operation.[^7] LighthouseFriends says the Charity light was discontinued in 1942, when Gravelly Shoal Lighthouse was established a few miles east.[^2] The sources do not agree on the year.

In 1963 five businessmen from Saginaw acquired the island for development.[^2] The General Services Administration received a top bid of $15,500 for the lighthouse reservation.[^2]

In 1992 new private owners bought the island.[^2][^7] They sold most of it to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and a few acres to The Nature Conservancy, and kept five acres, which included the collapsed keeper's dwelling.[^2] The owner was told the dwelling could not be saved, and it was removed in 2003 and rebuilt on the old foundation.[^2]

## Today

The island is reached by boat. The tower is owned by The Nature Conservancy, and the keeper's house is privately owned, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The Arenac County Historical Society's Charity Island Preservation Committee is working to restore and preserve the tower.[^2]

LighthouseFriends reports that cruises to the island give visitors a chance to see the lighthouse, and that the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^2] The island's owners advertise a "Light Keepers House Rental Information" page.[^7] Check access with the owners and the Nature Conservancy before going ashore, and do not climb the tower, which is closed.
