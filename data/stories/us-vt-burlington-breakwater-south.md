Burlington Breakwater South Light stands at the south end of the breakwater that shelters Burlington harbor on Lake Champlain, in Chittenden County, Vermont.[^1][^2] Its tower is a replica of the smaller light of about 1857.[^1] By LighthouseFriends' account, the breakwater has carried a light at its southern end since the 1850s.[^1]

## The place

The breakwater is one long wall of stone and timber cribs, built out from the shore in stages. Its oldest section, built from 1836 to 1854, lies near the middle. The south end was extended in the 1860s and 1870s, and between 1874 and 1886 the wall was lengthened in both directions.[^2]

The breakwater protects a harbor that had little shelter before it was built. LighthouseFriends says the harbor lay open to winds from the south and the northwest until federal work began in 1837.[^1]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says wooden lights were first placed on the ends of the breakwater in 1857, and moved as the wall grew. Its original towers showed fixed white lights from about 21 feet (6.4 m) above the water, with sixth-order lenses.[^1] The nomination gives a different start. It says a steamboat company kept a lantern on the breakwater in 1842, and that lighthouses were built at each end in 1886, when Fresnel-lens beacons replaced the signal lanterns.[^2]

In the fiscal year that ended in 1891 the Lighthouse Board lists a "South Beacon" on the breakwater. Its report says 210 feet (64 m) of plank walk was built to reach the beacon from the landing.[^3] The same report records a new light on the south end of the north extension, to mark the opening between the main wall and the new section. LighthouseFriends calls that light the "Middle Light".[^3][^1] The Board's list does not say which light was the "South Beacon", so the name alone does not settle which light this story is about.[^3]

The two replica towers were built to show different periods. The south tower copies the circa 1857 design, and the north tower the circa 1890 design.[^1] The replica south light has a focal plane of about 12 feet (3.7 m) above the water.[^1]

## The keepers

The keepers who tended the breakwater lights are told in the north light's story. LighthouseFriends gives the same keeper list for this light, from George W. Beckwith in 1856 to Rolla W. Hill in 1937 and 1938.[^1] The Society's roster for the south light lists no keepers. It names two contractors with no dates, James E. Cashman and Luther Whitney.[^4]

LighthouseFriends names keeper James Wakefield, who served from 1873 to 1890, as the man who made the rescue of December 1876. The nomination calls him a ship chandler. The rescue is told below.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In February 1876 a northwesterly storm drove ice floes against the south end. The nomination says 220 feet (67 m) of the southern end was materially injured. Two cribs, each 110 feet (34 m) long, were pushed apart, leaving a clean 26-foot (8 m) gap, which was repaired in 1877.[^2]

On 9 December 1876 the 88-foot (27 m) sailing schooner *General Butler* was carrying marble to Burlington when her steering failed in a northerly gale. The nomination says she ran onto the southern end of the breakwater with five people aboard. Each time the vessel lifted onto the breakwater, one of the people had to make an eighteen-foot (5.5 m) jump to the ice-covered wall.[^2]

The Burlington newspaper's account says the schooner struck a little way beyond the southern light house. Almost every wave broke over the wall and soaked the party. Most of the party landed with little or no injury; Mr. Goodsell struck his head and was knocked senseless.[^5] The newspaper calls the cargo stone, where the nomination and LighthouseFriends say marble.[^5][^2][^1]

The nomination names James Wakefield, a Burlington ship chandler, who watched from shore and knew that something had to be done. With his young son he took a fourteen-foot (4.3 m) boat, which the nomination calls a Burlington lighthouse rowboat, out to the wall. They brought the freezing people to the harbor, and a doctor in town looked after them.[^2] The newspaper names Mr. James Wakefield and one of his sons as the rescuers, and does not give his trade.[^5] LighthouseFriends calls the rescuer the keeper, who rowed his skiff out to the breakwater.[^1]

The *General Butler* was never raised. The wreck is now a popular dive site and is listed on the National Register.[^2]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the wooden towers were replaced in 1925 with skeletal towers that had tank houses at their bases.[^1] A bell rung by machinery, with a double blow every twelve seconds in thick weather, was set on the south light on 21 October 1907, LighthouseFriends says.[^1]

Automatic lights, which did away with the need for a light tender, did not come until 1938, the nomination says.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes the last hand-lit evening that year, when Keeper Rolla W. Hill was taken back to shore by twelve boats carrying seventy-five people.[^1]

The replicas came later. Burlington's mayor proposed copies of the original lights, and Senator Patrick Leahy's office obtained $250,000 for the project through a transportation bill in 2001. The south light was activated on 12 September 2004 before a crowd.[^1]

## Today

The City of Burlington owns the light, according to the Society's roster and LighthouseFriends.[^4][^1] The tower is closed.[^1] LighthouseFriends suggests a view from the Burlington wastewater treatment facility, and says good views are possible from cruises aboard the *Spirit of Ethan Allen*.[^1]
