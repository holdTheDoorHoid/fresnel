Swift Point Light, also called Green Head Light, stands on the west bank of the Saint John River, just northwest of Saint John, New Brunswick, near the narrow entrance to the Reversing Falls.[^1] It was one of six lights set along the river in 1869.[^1] The tower that replaced the first light burned in June 2017, and the Canadian Coast Guard rebuilt it the following winter.[^1][^4][^5][^6]

## The place

Swift Point works with Sand Point Light, on the east side of the river, to guide ships along the Saint John River and to locate the narrow entrance to the Reversing Falls.[^1][^2] The Canadian Press ties the falls to the powerful tides of the Bay of Fundy.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the reach around the falls is often confusing, with its tributaries, bays and islands.[^1] The Department's keepers' list for fiscal 1902–03 calls the station Greenhead, on the St. John River.[^3]

## Building the light

The six river lights were placed in operation on 20 August 1869. Building the lights and their apparatus cost $2,342, and the sites about $600 more.[^1] At Swift Point the first light was shown from a mast.[^1]

The Department of Marine's report for 1892 noted that the light at Green Head was in bad order.[^1] It was 1896 before a new tower was built.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes the Department's report for 1896, which describes a white square wooden building 15.2 m (50 ft) high, set 7.3 m (24 ft) east of the old site.[^1] G. W. Palmer, the lowest bidder, did the work under contract for $579.[^1]

In 1899 the pressed lens in the tower was replaced with a seventh-order dioptric lens.[^1]

## The keepers

John Nelson Williams was the first keeper, from 1869.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he resigned in 1886 after eighteen years of service, although its own dates from 1869 give seventeen.[^1] Thomas H. Raynes followed him that year, and Thomas E. Looney took over the same year.[^1] LighthouseFriends carries Looney until at least 1923.[^1] The Department's keepers' list for fiscal 1902–03 has "Looney, Thos. E" on the same line as "Greenhead, St. John River".[^3]

In 1900 Looney was given a hand foghorn to sound whenever the fog signals of vessels were heard near the station.[^1] In 1914 a mechanical fog bell, installed by day labour under Inspector John Kelly at a cost of $206, replaced the hand foghorn.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1872 the lantern caught fire and forced the light to be suspended for a few nights.[^1]

On the night of Tuesday 13 June 2017 the Saint John fire department was told of a blaze at the light, around 10 p.m.[^4] The site is remote, and the firefighters had to walk in through the woods with their gear.[^4] By the time they arrived, LighthouseFriends says, the lighthouse was almost completely destroyed.[^1] At the time of the fire, officials had not determined a cause.[^5]

## After the keepers

No source read says when the keeping of the light ended.

After the fire a review found that local sailors needed the light for navigation, and the Coast Guard then decided to put up a similar-looking structure.[^6] A foundation was poured in November 2017.[^6] The new tower is square and pyramidal, and shorter than the old one.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it was built in Saint John and carried to the point.[^1] The Coast Guard, speaking to CHSJ, gave a different account: its aluminum frames were built in a shop in Nova Scotia, delivered to Saint John before Christmas, and installed on site by local Coast Guard personnel.[^6] The cladding is PVC and vinyl made to look like traditional cedar-shake shingles.[^6] The light has been back in operation since about the middle of January 2018, and it runs on solar power.[^6][^5]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse. The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1] The approach is on foot, about a kilometre through the woods from the main road.[^1][^5]

The light shows a green flash every four seconds, from a white square tower with a red upper portion, 13.9 m (46 ft) high. Its focal height is 28.1 m (92 ft). The Coast Guard list marks it as seasonal.[^2]
