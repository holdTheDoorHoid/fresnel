A square brick tower pushes up through the middle of a square wooden-framed house on a rock island at the mouth of Frenchman Bay, Maine, about four miles out from Bar Harbor.[^2][^3] Egg Rock Light was first lit in 1875 and is still an active aid to navigation; the National Register of Historic Places calls it the only surviving light station of this configuration in Maine.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Egg Rock sits in Frenchman Bay between Mount Desert Island to the west and the Schoodic Peninsula to the east. The National Register nomination says the light was "undoubtedly" a response to rising shipping in the bay, much of it tied to the summer tourist trade around Bar Harbor.[^2] LighthouseFriends, drawing on the Light-House Board's records, says Congress put up $15,000 on 23 June 1874 for a light and fog signal at or near the rock because of growing seasonal ferry traffic.[^3]

## Building the light

Getting title to the island took time, and building began in 1875. The same account says the Light-House Board found landing materials on the exposed rock hard going; by 1 July 1875 the foundation was laid, the brickwork nearly done and the ironwork ready to set, and the station went into operation on 1 November 1875.[^3] The tower carried a fifth-order Fresnel lens showing a fixed red light.[^3][^4]

The dwelling was altered about 1900. The Light-House Board proposed in its 1899 report that the house be enlarged with a half-story under the roof, and the nomination notes that this changed the roofline from a shallow hip to a high hip with dormers.[^2] New England Lighthouses dates the second story to around 1900, and LighthouseFriends links it to an assistant keeper added in 1898.[^4][^3] In the fiscal year ending June 1901 the Board also reported a cellar built under the dwelling and the old striking machine replaced with one rebuilt in its Boston machine shop.[^5] A fourth-order lens replaced the fifth-order in 1901. The sources disagree about the new light: LighthouseFriends calls it a white flash every five seconds, New England Lighthouses a flashing red every five seconds.[^3][^4]

## The keepers

The first keeper was Ambrose H. Wasgatt, who served from 1875 to 1885.[^1] New England Lighthouses describes him as a Bar Harbor native wounded twice in the Civil War.[^4] Amaziah R. Small followed him until 1889, then Lewis F. Sawyer until 1899.[^1] Lewis Sawyer's son Heber G. Sawyer was assistant keeper in 1898-99 and then head keeper until 1912.[^1][^4]

After Heber Sawyer came William H. C. Dodge (1912-1916), Winfield P. Kent (1916-1921) and Augustus B. Hamor, who kept the light from 1921 to 1930 after eight years as assistant. Jaruel B. Pinkham was Hamor's assistant from 1921, then head keeper from 1930 to 1942.[^1] In 1933 Clinton "Buster" L. Dalzell arrived as assistant.[^1] LighthouseFriends writes that he came with his wife and two small sons and that mail and supplies reached the station only twice a month.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sea reached the house early. LighthouseFriends quotes the Light-House Board as saying that a March 1876 gale carried away the fuel shed and moved the bell tower about 30 feet, broke the seaward windows and flooded the dwelling; it gives the date as the 21st, while New England Lighthouses says the 25th and reports Wasgatt entering in the log that the island was "swept of anything movable."[^3][^4] New England Lighthouses also records a December 1887 storm under Small that damaged the bell tower and washed away the outhouse and henhouse, after which a new bell tower was bolted to the rock.[^4] Of a gale on 1 February 1908, LighthouseFriends says it moved rocks weighing up to thirty tons.[^3]

In March 1900 a gale carried off the boathouse; the Light-House Board's report for the year records that it was rebuilt, with 20 feet of new boat slip and a repointed tower deck.[^6] New England Lighthouses dates the storm to 2 March and says Heber Sawyer's log called it the worst in the area in 24 years.[^4]

Wrecks happened close to the rock. In October 1894 the Boston fishing schooner Amy Hamsen struck a ledge about half a mile southwest of the light, and her 18 crew got ashore at Bar Harbor before she sank.[^4] On Christmas Eve 1899 the fishing pinky Julia Ann broke up on the rock itself, and the two men aboard drowned.[^4] Fog hours were counted, too: the Lighthouse Service Bulletin tabulated 2,043 hours of fog at Egg Rock in fiscal 1916, about 23 per cent of the year and the highest figure on the Atlantic coast.[^7]

In July 1904 the fog bell gave way to a new signal. A notice to mariners printed in the Daily Kennebec Journal said that about 30 June 1904 the bell had been discontinued and a first-class Daboll trumpet established.[^8] Within weeks the Portland Daily Press reported that summer cottagers at Bar Harbor were complaining that the noise was "a great annoyance," that the engine was a gasoline one, and that Senator Hale had stepped in.[^9] The same page printed an answer that the horn was "simpiy carrying out the mandates of Congress" (the newspaper's OCR text), and a Portland schooner captain who knew the horn thought it did its job.[^9] The National Register nomination calls the 1904 signal steam-powered, which does not match the newspaper, and LighthouseFriends describes an oil engine.[^2][^9][^3]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded keepers helping vessels in trouble. It commended Assistant Keeper Jaruel B. Pinkham for rescuing three men from a power boat on the rock's southwest ledge on 3 August, and Keeper Hamor and Pinkham for helping the lumber-laden schooner Lillian Louise, stranded near the light on 30 October.[^10][^11]

The station's best-known tragedy followed in 1935. The accounts differ on details. Lighthouse Digest says Dalzell left in a small boat on a February day; LighthouseFriends says March. Both say Keeper Pinkham found Dalzell's overturned boat with no sign of him, and that the Coast Guard searched without success.[^3][^12] Lighthouse Digest says the body was found over a month later by the Gloucester trawler Eliza C. Riggs between Cat Island and Egg Rock; LighthouseFriends puts the recovery in July 1935.[^3][^12] Both say his third child was born three weeks after his death.[^3][^12]

## After the keepers

Henley C. Day was assistant from 1935 to 1937, and Ernest H. Mathie, assistant since 1937, became head keeper in 1943. Coast Guard crews followed, and the roster's last head keeper, Vince Wixted, is listed to 1976, the year the station was automated.[^1][^2] During automation the Coast Guard took the lantern room off the tower to make room for a rotating beacon, and in 1986 it built an aluminum lantern; the nomination calls that lantern a 1986 replacement.[^3][^2] Of the station's four original outbuildings only the brick fog signal building survives.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that by 1999 the light and fog signal ran on solar power.[^3]

Two Rodman/Dahlgren cannons placed on Egg Rock in 1898 for the Frenchman Bay coastal defense battery are said to stand now at the end of the Shore Path in Bar Harbor.[^3]

## Today

The tower is 40 feet (12 m) from base to the center of the lantern, and the light stands 64 feet (19.5 m) above mean high water.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that in 1998 the lighthouse was one of seven transferred to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and that the seabirds had left Egg Rock after the station was built.[^3] In 2009 the Service supplied materials for a repair project carried out by local residents and businesses.[^3]

Egg Rock is closed to the public and is part of the Maine Coastal Islands National Wildlife Refuge.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds are closed from April through August, and that the light is seen from boat cruises out of Bar Harbor or from a distance on the Acadia loop road.[^3] Please enjoy it from the water.
