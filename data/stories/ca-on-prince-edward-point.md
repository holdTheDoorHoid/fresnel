Prince Edward Point Lighthouse stands at the south-east tip of Prince Edward County, on the north shore of Lake Ontario.[^1] Its square wooden tower of 1881 has had no lantern since 1959, when a skeleton tower beside it took over the light.[^1][^2] Seguin said local fishermen often called the old light "the red onion," because it was one of the first lighthouses with a red light on top.[^3]

## The place

LighthouseFriends says the point was known as South Bay Point when the money for a light was voted, and that it has also been called Point Traverse.[^1] The Department of Marine's report for 1909–10 lists the station as "South Bay Point."[^4] A petition from numerous ship captains, LighthouseFriends says, led the government to appropriate $1,200 for a light there in 1879.[^1]

The Register statement says the lighthouse "marked the entrance to Point Traverse Harbour," and that it marked "the only protected harbour for a distance of approximately 60 nautical miles."[^2] A lighthouse had been built on False Duck Island, just off the mouth of South Bay, in 1828, but mariners wanted an additional aid to mark this important harbour of refuge.[^1]

The Register statement says the lighthouse is associated with various shipwrecks in an area it calls the "Graveyard of Lake Ontario," but it names none.[^2]

The local historian Marc Seguin told a 2021 walk that Point Traverse is about a kilometre and a half back, and that technically the lighthouse is the Prince Edward Point Lighthouse, though most locals call it the Point Traverse lighthouse.[^3]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says the contract for the lighthouse went in 1880 to John W. Fegan, and that the light was in operation by 1881.[^1] Thomas Fegan had charge of it from June to October that year.[^1] The Picton Gazette's report of the 2021 walk gives June 1881 as the date the lighthouse was erected.[^3]

The Department's description of the light, as LighthouseFriends prints it, was "a fixed red catoptric, elevated 36 feet above water mark." It describes "a square wooden tower, 36 feet high, with keeper's dwelling attached," so the tower was an 11 m (36 ft) square wooden structure.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the total cost to 1883 as $2,308.70, with $36.70 more for a boat and boathouse.[^1]

The light first came from a catoptric apparatus, lamps and reflectors. In 1909 a fourth-order, 360-degree French lens replaced it. The Department's report for 1909–10 records: "South Bay Point. A 4th order 360° French lens with a 35 mm. Diamond vapour installation and adjustable iron stand replaces the old catoptric light."[^4]

## The keepers

Daniel McIntosh was the official keeper from 1881, at $150 a year.[^1] In 1902 the Department cancelled his appointment, which LighthouseFriends quotes as "owing to advancing years, and for the efficiency of the service." He was sixty-nine.[^1]

His successor was Marcellus Vorce. The Department's salary table for 1909–10 gives his appointment as "Nov. 21, 1902," at $240 a year.[^5] LighthouseFriends says his salary was $200, the same as McIntosh's had been.[^1] The two sources disagree on the salary, and this story does not choose between them.

LighthouseFriends lists the next keepers as Waite J. Hudgin (1912 to 1927) and William J. Ostrander (1927 to 1941).[^1] The Department's 1913–14 list names "W. Hudgins" as keeper of South bay point, appointed 15 June 1912, which fits the start LighthouseFriends gives for Hudgin, though the first name and initial differ.[^6] The Department's annual reports in the archive end in 1923–24, so Ostrander's 1927 start rests on LighthouseFriends alone.[^1]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the government discontinued the light in 1912 but put it back into operation when navigation opened in 1913.[^1] The light was automated in 1941, and its colour changed from fixed red to flashing green.[^1] A skeleton tower 13 m (42 ft) tall went up beside the old one in 1959 to show a flashing white light, and the lantern room was taken off the old tower at the same time.[^1] The Picton Gazette says the lighthouse was decommissioned in 1959.[^3]

According to the Gazette, the coastguard's policy on decommissioned lighthouses was that demolition followed, especially where a skeleton light tower had been built beside the lighthouse. The article says the government never toppled this lighthouse, and that ownership passed to Parks Canada in 1975.[^3]

LighthouseFriends dates its recognition as a Heritage Lighthouse to 2015.[^1] The Picton Gazette dates the petition by Save Our Lighthouses to 2015, with the designation following soon after. The government then put a new cladding on the tower, which the article says was finished in 2016.[^3] The Prince Edward Point National Wildlife Area was established in 1978, and the Register says the lighthouse is located in it.[^1][^2]

## Today

The old tower belongs to Parks Canada.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1] The Register statement describes the lighthouse as "set on the water's edge of a rocky beach on Lake Ontario and surrounded by low-lying shrubs and coniferous trees." It says the lighthouse is the "main feature" of the Bird Observatory's logo.[^2] The Picton historian said in 2021 that the federal government had no plans to restore it or open it as a museum, though it was open to a licence of occupation for a community use.[^3]

The working light is the 1959 skeleton tower. The Register says that, with the old tower, it "remains a familiar landmark for local mariners."[^2]
