Cape Blanco is the westernmost point of Oregon, and its brick lighthouse, first lit on 20 December 1870, is the oldest standing light on the state's coast. It sits on a bare tableland about 245 feet (75 m) above the Pacific, about six miles north of Port Orford by the nomination's reckoning (Lighthouse Friends says nine), in Curry County.[^1][^3]

## The place

The cape projects about a mile and a half from the general line of the coast, and the nomination for the National Register of Historic Places describes the lighthouse as sited on the "westernmost rampart" of Oregon.[^1] The name is older than any American presence: the nomination credits the Spanish explorer Martin d'Anguilar (the nomination's spelling) with naming it Cape Blanco, "white cape," in 1603 for the chalky colour of its rock, and says Captain George Vancouver renamed it Cape Orford in 1792, a name that did not stick.[^1] The same document places the cape in the ancestral territory of the Rogue River Indians, an Athabascan-speaking people, and notes archaeological features on the cape.[^1]

The need for a light grew with the gold-rush settlement at Port Orford, to the south. According to the nomination, steamers and mail ships called there on the San Francisco to Portland run, and many wrecks occurred at the headland for want of a guiding light; before the lighthouse, a Port Orford hotel proprietor is said to have kept a large lantern in a seaward window.[^1] Lighthouse Friends names that proprietor as Louis Knapp and says his lamp became unnecessary once the new light began to burn.[^3] The United States bought the lighthouse reservation, 47.7 acres, in 1867.[^1]

## Building the light

Work was meant to start in 1868 but was held up by weather. The Light-House Board's 1869 report, quoted in the nomination, called the rainfall at Cape Blanco "excessive," equalled at only two other places in the country where records had been kept, and said the cape had been cleared of timber, which was thought to reduce the fog.[^1] With no road to the cape, the Board arranged for a local man to make 200,000 bricks on the spot at $25 per thousand rather than ship them from San Francisco.[^1][^3] The sources differ on the first batch: the nomination says 8,000 bricks were made in the fall of 1868, while the report as quoted by Lighthouse Friends says "about eighty thousand" were accepted. Both agree the second kiln, burned in the spring of 1869, was rejected.[^1][^3] Lighthouse Friends adds that the first shipment of supplies, landed through the surf in May 1870, was partly lost when a gale drove the vessel onto the beach.[^3]

Army engineer Robert S. Williamson signed the final plans, and Lieutenant R. A. Williamson (as the nomination names him) supervised the work.[^1] The tower was planned at a focal-plane height of 18 feet but raised to 50 feet because a bluff would have hidden a lower light, giving a conical brick tower about 59 feet (18 m) high; the tower and keepers' house together cost around $100,000.[^1] The house was a two-and-a-half-story brick duplex with a daylight basement, standing about 100 feet south of the tower by the nomination's account (Lighthouse Friends says 125 feet); Lighthouse Friends adds that the principal keeper took one side and the two assistants shared the other.[^1][^3]

The lens was made in Paris by Henry-Lepaute at a cost of $20,000, and was lifted up the cliff to the tower.[^1] The records disagree about its size. The nomination says the Light-House Board's correspondence called it first order, but that the lens was somewhat smaller than a standard first-order lens and its dimensions more closely matched a second-order one.[^1] Lighthouse Friends and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1914 both call the lens first order.[^3][^6] A Pendleton newspaper in 1910 said the lenses in its tower were three inches greater in diameter than those at any other light station on the coast.[^5] The light was first shown on 20 December 1870, as a fixed white light.[^3][^4]

## The keepers

The first keeper was Harvey B. Burnap, who had been living at Port Orford; Lighthouse Friends says he had earlier served at the Umpqua River light when it collapsed in 1863.[^1][^3] The roster of the US Lighthouse Society's archive lists him as principal keeper from 1870 to 1874.[^7] Two figures then dominate the roster. James S. Langlois came as an assistant in the 1870s and stayed more than forty years; the nomination says he put in 42 years and "became synonymous with the station."[^1] His dates differ by source. The archive lists him as first assistant from 1876, acting principal keeper from 1883, and principal keeper from 1885 to 1919; Lighthouse Friends gives 1883 to 1919; the nomination says head keeper from 1883 until 1912 and dates his arrival to 1875.[^7][^3][^1] A December 1910 newspaper feature called him one of the oldest keepers in the service and gave 12 August as the day he began, though its damaged text does not print the year clearly.[^5][^2]

James S. Hughes was the second son of Patrick and Jane Hughes, whose 2,000-acre ranch bordered the station, and he served at Cape Blanco for 37 years by the Heritage Society's count.[^4][^3] The archive lists him first as second assistant in 1888, then first assistant from 1889 to 1919, and principal keeper from 1919 to 1926.[^7] In 1910 the Pendleton paper noted that Langlois and second assistant Ezra E. Marr lived with their wives on the station grounds, while Hughes had a ranch on the Sixes River.[^5] By the late 1890s, Lighthouse Friends says, Hughes had two children and Langlois five, and the duplex was crowded.[^3] The Light-House Board repeated the same complaint in its report for fiscal 1900: "The quarters are insufficient for the three keepers," and a new building, it estimated, could be put up for not more than $4,500.[^8] The request, first made in 1897 according to the nomination, was finally met by a new frame house completed in 1909 or 1910 depending on the source.[^1][^3]

In March 1903 Mabel E. Bretherton was appointed second assistant, which the Cape Blanco Heritage Society counts as the first woman keeper in Oregon.[^4] Her husband, Bernard Bretherton, an assistant keeper at Yaquina Head and then Coquille River, had died that February; a public radio history says she was left with three children under ten.[^3][^9] She was moved to North Head, Washington, in 1905.[^3]

The nomination says the station was largely self-sufficient, with a large garden, animals for transport and food, and a fish pond.[^1] The wagon road to Port Orford ran along the beach for half its length and crossed quicksand at the Elk River mouth; a 7,000-foot (2,100 m) road to the county road was finished in 1886, according to the nomination, and Lighthouse Friends dates it to 1885.[^1][^3] Even afterward, the Light-House Board reported that trees fell across the road in December 1892 and were cleared in January 1893.[^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The cape took a beating. Lighthouse Friends reports a gale in February 1878 that damaged the dwelling roof and a storm on 14 October 1878 that tore off a large part of it; solid shutters went up on the exposed sides to protect windows from sand and gravel.[^3] The nomination dates a "tornado type gale" to 9 January 1880.[^1] In the winter of 1932, severe storms swept the Pacific Northwest.[^3] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports a wind of 100 miles per hour at the station, asbestos slates stripped from roofs, windows broken and fences blown down, and says gravel stones were driven through the plate glass of the lantern "like bullets."[^11]

The nomination lists three losses near the cape: the *Victoria* on an offshore reef in 1883, the steamer *Alaskan* in 1889, and the oil tanker *J. A. Chanslor* in 1919.[^1] A newspaper gives a different picture of the *Alaskan*. A Montana paper of 23 May 1889 reported her foundering off Cape Blanco on Monday, 13 May, supposedly after meeting "a very severe gale," with "only the officers and crew" aboard, 34 in all; the steamer *Columbia* picked up the captain, the mate and eleven of the crew, five men were first reported drowned, and eleven more later came ashore near Florence.[^12] The nomination's figure of 31 dead of 47 aboard does not match that contemporary account.

On 19 October 1903 the steamer *South Portland*, bound from Portland for San Francisco, struck a reef off the cape in fog and heavy seas. The San Francisco Call reported "twenty-one are believed to" have drowned out of forty aboard; Lighthouse Friends counts eighteen lost.[^13][^3] Lighthouse Friends quotes Langlois's logbook entry that the keeper patrolled the beach north and south of the station and found nothing of the wreck or the drowned; Mabel Bretherton later found an officer's coat about a mile north, and Langlois's son Oscar recovered an oar and a stool.[^3]

On Thursday, 18 December 1919, the tanker *J. A. Chanslor* struck a reef off Cape Blanco at about 6:15 in the evening and broke in two.[^14][^1] The New-York Tribune's headline was "Only 3 Known Rescued of 40 Aboard," and it said the master, Captain A. A. Sawyer, "was rescued by the men of Cape Blanco" Lighthouse; he was in a Bandon hospital with pneumonia from exposure.[^14] The other sources count differently: the nomination says only three crew members survived and the Heritage Society says three of 39 passengers.[^1][^4]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says mineral-oil lamps replaced lard oil in 1888.[^3] In September 1910, the Pendleton paper reported, an oil-vapor burner was installed, a method it said North Head had used first.[^5] By 1914 the lens was fixed with two occultations of 2 seconds separated by 3 seconds of light every 20 seconds.[^6] Lighthouse Friends says a radiobeacon began working on 30 November 1925.[^3] In early 1936 the station was electrified and the old lens was replaced by a second-order Henry-Lepaute lens with eight bull's-eyes, which, Lighthouse Friends says, had earlier served at Ship Shoal Light in Louisiana.[^3][^4] The Heritage Society says it does not know what became of the original lens after it was sent to the Tongue Point depot at Astoria.[^4] The nomination adds that the tower became part of the radio-navigation system from 1939, that a Navy lookout was built on the cape in World War II, and that buildings multiplied through the 1950s and 1960s.[^1]

Automation dates differ: 1980 in Lighthouse Friends, 1979 in the Heritage Society, which adds that a last grounds-keeping "keeper" left in December 1987.[^3][^4] The keepers' houses were torn down between 1967 and 1974, Lighthouse Friends says, and the nomination dates the 1870 duplex's demolition to about 1967.[^3][^1] In 1990 part of the reservation (15.4 acres) was transferred to the National Park Service, which deeded a portion to Oregon State Parks for a state park; many station buildings built from the 1950s to the 1980s were later torn down.[^1] In 1992 two teenagers smashed part of the lens with a sledgehammer; it was repaired with Pyrex by the spring of 1994 at a cost of $80,000.[^3] The station was added to the National Register on 21 April 1993, and the tower reopened to visitors on 1 April 1996.[^16][^3]

## Today

The light is still an active aid to navigation owned by the Coast Guard, with the second-order lens in place.[^1] Cape Blanco Heritage Society says the lighthouse is open to the public through a cooperative agreement among the Bureau of Land Management, Oregon State Parks, the Siletz and Coquille tribes, Curry County and the society.[^4] The tower itself is not open: the society says it has been closed to tours since 2020 for structural problems, and a 2022 assessment estimated $682,000 in repairs to the tower alone.[^15] The road from the gate to the lighthouse is also in poor repair, so visitors walk about a quarter mile from the gate to the greeting center and gift shop, from which the exterior can be viewed.[^4] The exterior can be seen, and the society's greeting center and gift shop are in Cape Blanco State Park; check the society's page for current hours.[^16]
