East Chop Light stands on Telegraph Hill in Oak Bluffs, on the eastern approach to Vineyard Haven Harbor.[^6] A private light that Silas Daggett, one of the Vineyard pilots, set up there burned in December 1871 and was rebuilt.[^3][^5][^6] The government's cast-iron tower, put up in 1876 or 1877, is the one that stands today.[^1][^5][^11] The Martha's Vineyard Museum has been its steward since 1994.[^6]

## The place

The bluff was a signal station before it was a light. LighthouseFriends says a semaphore station was set up at East Chop in 1828 to show shipping information, and the Museum says the hill was part of a coastal semaphore system that signaled the arrival of vessels to Boston.[^5][^6]

Many local residents felt that a proper lighthouse was needed at the approach to the harbor. LighthouseFriends says a local mariner, Captain Silas Daggett, lobbied for one without success.[^5] In 1869 he built his own light and published a notice calling it the "Coaster's Light." The notice promised a fixed red light from 5 June and asked owners and underwriters to contribute to its support.[^5] The light was kept going by subscriptions from the masters and owners of vessels.[^3][^5] In its 1873 report the Light-House Board recommended a fourth-order light there, saying that the light had been kept up for years by subscriptions from steamship owners.[^5]

## Building the light

The private light burned on Saturday, 16 December 1871. The New York Herald reported the loss on 19 December, and the Worcester Daily Spy printed the story on 21 December, saying the light "was totally destroyed by fire on Saturday forenoon."[^2][^3] The Spy said the keeper, Capt. Luce, and his daughter were "quite severely burned" while trying to put the fire out, and that "there was no insurance."[^3] The nomination dates the fire to 1869, and gives 1872 as the year the new tower was built.[^1] The newspapers, LighthouseFriends and the Museum put the fire in 1871.[^2][^3][^5][^6]

After the fire Daggett put a light back up. LighthouseFriends says it was relit on 2 May 1872, with three reflectors that the Board had lent him, set atop his house at a height of 76 feet (23 m) above the sea.[^5]

In February 1875 the Worcester Daily Press printed a list of appropriations that included $5,000 "for establishing a light upon East Chop, Martha's Vineyard."[^4] The nomination says the government bought the property in 1875.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the purchase to 1876, and quotes the government's description of Daggett's dwelling with its attached tower as "a slight wooden structure, little better than a shanty."[^5] The Museum gives 1875.[^6]

The cast-iron tower is also dated differently. The nomination says it was built in 1876, is a "four drum" Italianate design, and stands 79 feet (24 m) above the water.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a new dwelling and a cast-iron tower were under construction by mid-1877, and that the forty-foot (12 m) conical tower stood about fifty feet (15 m) southeast of the old light.[^5] The Archive's place record gives 1877 as the year the tower was established.[^11] A fourth-order Fresnel lens first shone from the top of the tower at 79 feet (24 m).[^5]

In 1895 a storm porch was put over the tower door.[^7] In 1897 an oil house was added to store the kerosene that fed the lantern, according to LighthouseFriends.[^5][^8]

## The keepers

The Archive's roster lists Grafton Luce as principal keeper from 1869 to 1876.[^11] The 1871 newspaper names only "Capt. Luce" as the keeper, so the match with Grafton Luce depends on the roster and the years.[^3][^11]

George Silvey kept the light from 1876 to 1898, and George H. Fisher from 1898 to 1912.[^11] The Archive also lists Fisher as keeper at Cape Poge from 1883 to 1898, before he came here.[^11][^12]

George W. Purdy was keeper from 1912 to 1934, according to the Archive.[^11] LighthouseFriends says he had come from Newfoundland, where he had been a lobsterman, and that after moving to Massachusetts he took a job on the lighthouse tender Azalea. An accident in the tender's engine room cost him an arm, and he was then assigned to lights at Sankaty Head and Gay Head before East Chop. One of the Purdys' four children was born at East Chop.[^5]

In May 1928 the Vineyard Gazette praised Purdy's work on the supply landing below the bluff, as LighthouseFriends reports it. He had built a runway of planks and timbers about 40 feet (12 m) long, so that a boat could come in to the beach.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The only disaster the sources describe for this station is the fire of 16 December 1871. It burned the private light, badly burned the keeper and his daughter, and left the owner with no insurance.[^2][^3]

## After the keepers

In 1922 the illuminant was changed from oil to electric incandescent, and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin listed East Chop among the lights changed that year.[^9]

In January 1925 the keepers of nineteen lights in the second and third districts sent data on the eclipse of the sun to the Navy Department's Hydrographic Office. East Chop was among the stations named.[^10]

The Archive records the light as automated in 1933.[^11] LighthouseFriends dates the automation to 1934, when the Purdy family was offered the chance to keep renting the house but declined, and the buildings except the tower were torn down. The light was fixed white until 1898, when it began to flash red, and it changed to flashing green in 1934.[^5]

The keeper's house and the other ancillary buildings are gone, according to the 1981 nomination.[^1] Daggett's first dwelling survives, LighthouseFriends says, built into a large residence not far from the lighthouse.[^5] In 1988 the daymark was returned from a dark reddish-brown to white, because the dark paint was causing excessive heat and condensation in the tower.[^5]

## Today

The Museum calls itself the steward of East Chop Light. It says the restoration work of the late 1980s and early 1990s, carried out under a federal license, returned East Chop, along with the Island's other active lights, to local stewardship, and that it became steward of East Chop in 1994.[^6]

The Museum says the doors are kept open during visiting hours, and a lighthouse keeper is available to answer questions. Adults pay $5, and children 12 and under go free. It also opens for Sunday sunsets from mid-June.[^6]

Parking is limited on East Chop Drive. The Vineyard Transit Authority bus stops at East Chop Drive and New York Avenue, and visitors walk up the hill from there.[^6] The land around the tower is a public park that Oak Bluffs acquired.[^6]

Ownership is not settled by the sources. The 1981 nomination lists the Coast Guard as owner.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard owns the light and the Museum manages it.[^5] The Archive lists the Martha's Vineyard Museum as owner and manager.[^11] The Museum calls itself the steward.[^6]
