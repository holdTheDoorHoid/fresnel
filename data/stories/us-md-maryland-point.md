Maryland Point Light stood in the middle of the Potomac on a shoal off Maryland Point. It was a hexagonal wooden house on seven screw piles, first lit on 15 December 1892.[^1][^2] The Coast Guard announced in 1954 that the light would be automated,[^3] and the wooden house was taken down in 1963. A steel skeletal tower on the same foundation now shows the light.[^2][^4]

## The place

The shoal was the trouble. The Board's report for 1887, as LighthouseFriends prints it, says "there is but 10 feet of water on the apex of the shoal at low stages," so that vessels drawing more than 10 feet (3 m) "are liable to take the ground."[^2] The station stands in the middle of the river.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the station formerly stood on a shoal off Maryland Point, close to the middle of the river.[^2] The Directory places it midstream, between Wellington Beach on the Maryland side and Fairview Beach in Virginia.[^4]

Congress paid for it in 1890. "An appropriation of $50,000 was made by the act of August 30, 1890," LighthouseFriends prints from the Board's report.[^2]

## Building the light

Bids for the iron work were opened on 22 July 1891. "The lowest bid received was $8,475."[^5] The house was a wooden dwelling resting on seven wrought-iron screw piles, 40 feet (12 m) long, with its piles set on disks "which rest on the surface of the shoal."[^6][^2]

The light was first exhibited on 15 December 1892, and was a fourth-order light flashing white, as LighthouseFriends prints the Board's description.[^1][^2] A fog bell was struck by machinery in thick weather.[^2]

In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1896 the Board placed "about 2,100 tons of heavy riprap stone" in two piles, one above and one below the house, "to serve as ice breakers."[^7]

## The keepers

The roster has 46 lines, from 1892 to 1954: fourteen principal keepers, thirty assistants and two Coast Guard names with no position. The first principal keeper was John Peterson, from 1892 to 1896.[^8]

Loch W. Humphreys was principal keeper here on the roster from 1905 to 1909.[^8] His archive record also lists him as principal keeper at Piney Point.[^9]

Andrew J. Jarvis was an assistant here in 1903 and again in 1917 and 1918.[^8] His archive record lists him as principal keeper at Upper Cedar Point from 1918 to 1923.[^10] In May 1918 the Bulletin shows him at Maryland Point, helping an aeroplane's occupant.[^11]

John E. Morgan was principal keeper here from 1925 to 1933.[^8] The archive's record for him also lists Upper Cedar Point before that.[^12]

In 1954 the Star named the keeper as George W. Austin, "a civilian who made his home in the 42-foot steel-stilted structure since August 16, 1944."[^3][^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 25 May 1918 A. J. Jarvis, assistant keeper here, helped the occupant of a disabled aeroplane.[^11][^13]

In February 1920 the ice in the Potomac threatened the house. The Star printed a Baltimore dispatch dated 7 February: the keepers had been taken off "yesterday by the Navy tug Howard P. Green," and "The ice has gorged about the structure and a part of the gallery platform has been carried away."[^14] LighthouseFriends gives the date as 5 February, when the keeper "abandoned the structure," and says the keepers returned on 10 February.[^2] The Star's "yesterday" would put the removal on 6 February, so the two accounts differ by a day.[^14]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1921 records that "1,000 tons of riprap were deposited to form an ice breaker" at the station.[^15] The fog bell had been knocked from its place by moving ice in March 1904, and was replaced.[^2]

In September 1926 the keeper, J. E. Morgan, rendered assistance "to the occupants of a motor boat" disabled near the station.[^16]

## After the keepers

On 1 September 1954 the Evening Star reported that the Coast Guard would convert the light to automatic operation on 15 October. The paper said the lamps had "guided vessels there since 1892," and that Maryland Point would be "the last of seven offshore lighthouses on the river to succumb to modernization."[^3]

The wooden house came down in 1963, and a light was shown from a skeletal tower on the screwpile foundation.[^2][^4] The Directory says a rotating mechanism from the original light is displayed at the Bodie Island Lighthouse visitor center in North Carolina.[^4]

## Today

The Directory lists the light as active. It is a steel skeletal tower on the original foundation, with a white flash every six seconds and a focal point of 42 feet (13 m). Two red sectors cover nearby shoals.[^4] It is accessible only by boat and can be seen from Wellington Beach off Maryland Route 224. The site and tower are closed, and the Coast Guard owns the site.[^4]
