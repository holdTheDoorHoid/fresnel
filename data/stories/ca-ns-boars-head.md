Boar's Head light stands on the northeast tip of Long Island, in the Municipality of the District of Digby, where it marks the west side of the north entrance to Petit Passage.[^2][^3] The first lighthouse was opened there in 1864.[^1][^9] The white tower standing now dates from 1957.[^1] For sixty-five years the light was kept by one family, from Henry M. Ruggles to his son Frank.[^2]

## The place

The Parks Canada statement says the first lighthouse went up at the entrance to Petit Passage in 1864, three years before Confederation. The passage, it notes, is the shortest route from Saint John, New Brunswick, to Yarmouth, Nova Scotia.[^1] Tiverton, less than a kilometre away, was founded by Loyalists in 1787.[^1] By the middle of the nineteenth century it served the Bay of Fundy fishery and the farms of Long Island, and the statement says the light's opening recognised those fishing communities.[^1]

The 1881 *Sailing Directions* for the south-east coast of Nova Scotia, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, called the passage "almost entirely free from dangers" while noting that it was the shortest route between Saint John and Yarmouth.[^2]

## Building the light

Lighthouse Friends says the first lighthouse was erected in 1863 with Henry M. Ruggles as its keeper.[^2] Islands History gives the same year for the building and says it was officially opened in 1864.[^9] The Parks Canada statement says it was erected in 1864.[^1] The sources disagree on the year of building. This story gives 1864, the year of the opening, and notes the 1863 date.

The first light was a square wooden building painted white.[^2] The 1881 *Sailing Directions*, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, described the light as "an alternating light, 70 feet above high water," showing red and white flashes every minute, and visible in clear weather from 14 miles, by the guide's own measure.[^2] Lighthouse Friends adds that the foundation of the original light is still visible.[^2]

The lighthouse stood on land owned by a Tiverton couple until the federal government bought it. On 9 March 1868, Islands History says, Simondson and Cecelia Outhouse sold 9.97 acres to Peter Mitchell, Minister of Marine and Fisheries, for $120, and the deed recorded that the lighthouse was already standing on the land.[^9]

In the report for the fiscal year 1907-08, the Department noted that the light at Boar's Head had been changed from a revolving light to a fifth-order dioptric light.[^4] Lighthouse Friends dates the fog signal to 1910, when a fog alarm with gasoline motors and compressors was set up and connected to a diaphone.[^2]

The present tower was built in 1957 to replace the aging building.[^1] It is a square, tapered wooden tower built to a standard Department of Transport plan.[^1] The Coast Guard list gives its height as 11.6 m, while Lighthouse Friends gives 10.7 m.[^3][^2]

## The keepers

Henry M. Ruggles kept the light from its first years until his death in 1901, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^2] The family lived in the lighthouse. Living quarters were built onto its back, and a cistern under the tower held rainwater from the roof for drinking and domestic use.[^9]

In 1893 a notice, which Islands History quotes, reported that Mrs. Ruggles, wife of the keeper at Boar's Head, had died "under peculiar circumstances." It said that a careful examination had convinced the doctors she died of lead poisoning, and that the water used had been caught from the roof, which was painted white.[^9] The author of Islands History concludes that the family was poisoned by lead from that roof. Lighthouse Friends prints the same notice but names the *Saint Croix Courier* as the paper. Islands History's caption gives the *Yarmouth Light* of 24 July 1893 for it, so the newspaper is not settled.[^2][^9]

Frank G. Ruggles, the son, took over in 1901 and served until his death in 1929.[^2] The Department's keepers' table in its 1907 report lists "Ruggles, Frank" at Boar's Head.[^5]

Lighthouse Friends names the assistants who followed. It says Elmer Wyman was assistant keeper when the fog signal was activated, and dates him to at least 1910.[^2] Harry Ruggles served from 1919 to 1933, Clayton Leeman from 1939 to 1943, Albert Blinn from 1947 to 1950 and Frank Thomas from 1963 to 1979.[^2] Earl A. Leeman was head keeper from 1930 to 1963.[^2] Murray Outhouse, an assistant from 1950, became head keeper in 1963 and held the post until 1981.[^2] Jack Tinker is listed for 1982, and Angus Smiley for 1982 to 1987.[^2] Smiley, the final keeper, transferred to Machias Seal Island when the station was destaffed in September 1987.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Parks Canada statement says the lighthouse "was unable to prevent all wrecks in the area," and that two ships were wrecked exactly a year apart, on 16 March 1911 and 1912. The statement does not name either ship.[^1]

On 7 June 1912 a vessel grounded at Boar's Head.[^6] The Wreck Commissioner's statement lists the case among the investigations of the fiscal year 1912-13. It gives a preliminary inquiry at Yarmouth on 8 June and a formal investigation at Halifax on 8 July. The master was found alone to blame, and his certificate was suspended for three months.[^6]

A Department account of payments for the year ending 31 December 1912 lists "To surveys on ss. Amelia, stranded on Boar's Head, Bay of Fundy," dated 3 July 1912.[^7] On the wreck page, the Amelia registered at the Magdalen Islands sits on the row beside the 7 June entry, but the same table also lists a second Amelia, which stranded at Clark's Harbour on 8 August.[^6] The link between the June grounding and the Amelia therefore rests on that row, on the survey account and on the place.

## After the keepers

The station was destaffed in September 1987. Lead paint chipped from the old structures had contaminated the ground around them.[^2] In September 2009 the contaminated soil was removed and replaced with clean fill, at a cost of $216,000 paid under the federal Contaminated Sites Action Plan.[^2]

The Department's report printed in 1921 records protection work to the dwelling at Boar's Head, and repairs to the road.[^8]

The lighthouse was designated a heritage lighthouse under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act in 2015.[^2] The Municipality of Digby took ownership in August 2016, and the Tiverton and Central Grove Historical Association agreed to manage the property.[^2] In 2018 the association unveiled a memorial, about 1.2 m (four feet) tall, near the light that lists the keepers who served there.[^2]

## Today

The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^2] To reach the light, leave the ferry at Tiverton and follow the paved road along the hillside to a gate.[^2] If the gate is shut, the walk up the steep road is about one kilometre.[^2] The Coast Guard lists the light as a white flash every four seconds, operating around the clock.[^3] Boars Head is a popular picnic spot, as the Parks Canada statement notes.[^1]
