Cape St. Elias Light stands at the southwestern end of Kayak Island, in the Gulf of Alaska, where it was a landfall light for vessels bound for Prince William Sound or Cook Inlet.[^10][^5] The light was first lit in September 1916.[^4] The station was unmanned in 1974, when the Coast Guard downgraded it to a minor light.[^1] The Coast Guard still owns the site, and an association leases the buildings and takes guests.[^10][^13]

## The place

Kayak Island is uninhabited. The lighthouse is 62 miles southeast of Cordova, the nearest town, according to the association; LighthouseFriends gives 65 miles.[^13][^10] Off the island's southwestern point stands Pinnacle Rock, which the 1975 National Register nomination calls an important landmark for navigators since the early 1800s.[^1] The same nomination calls the cape one of the most dangerous points on the southern Alaska coast, because of fog, winter darkness and frequent storms.[^1] A second hazard, Southeast Rock, lies two and a half miles off the cape. Some believed a lightship would better mark it. The compromise was a lighthouse on the cape and a lighted buoy on the rock.[^10]

## Building the light

Congress approved a station here in 1911.[^1] A Cordova newspaper of January 1911 had called it "by all odds the most important aid to navigation yet recommended for Alaska," a line the nomination quotes.[^1] The money came two years later. The act of 22 October 1913 gave $115,000 for a light and fog signal at or near Cape St. Elias.[^2]

A survey team visited the cape in June 1914 and set up a temporary acetylene light at the base of Pinnacle Rock.[^10] The builders chose instead a shelf on the southwest end of the island. The lighthouse was built on a terrace, forty-two feet (13 m) above the water.[^10]

Sources give different starting dates for the work. The 1916 Commissioner's report says construction began on 1 June 1915.[^3] The nomination says it began in early 1915.[^1]

The first season ended on 7 October 1915.[^3] The Commissioner's report says the station was "left in charge of two caretakers" for the winter, and that work resumed on 17 April 1916.[^3] LighthouseFriends says two men were left behind to tend a small light and serve as camp caretakers.[^10]

The lantern room and watch room were shown at the Panama-Pacific International Exposition in San Francisco in 1915, before they were shipped north.[^10][^1] The third-order Fresnel lens had two flash panels and rested on a mercury float. A weight in a shaft made it revolve three times a minute, so that the light showed two white flashes every twenty seconds.[^10]

The date of the first light is disputed. The Lighthouse Service's Bulletin says the light "was first lighted on September 6," and the Commissioner's report for fiscal 1917 gives the same day.[^7][^4] The nomination gives 16 September.[^1] The Service rated the light at 300,000 candlepower.[^5] The nomination gives 390 candlepower instead.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives its range as about 15 nautical miles, and the nomination says the light was visible for about eleven miles.[^10][^1] On 9 September 1916 the Anchorage Times and Cook Inlet Pioneer reprinted a Juneau Empire article. The Alaska superintendent of lighthouses, passing through Juneau, had said the light "will be in commission" shortly after 1 September. The article said the station would be "one of the greatest aids to navigation on the northern steamer run."[^12]

The fog signal was the second part of the station. Its sirens were blown through a pair of Y-shaped copper trumpets, and the shape of the trumpets sent the sound around either side of Pinnacle Rock. It went into commission on 30 January 1917.[^6][^10]

During construction the Service ran a radio station here under the call letters NLQ.[^8] A radio beacon was installed in 1927.[^1]

The nomination describes the keepers' dwelling as a two-story, reinforced concrete building for three keepers.[^1] LighthouseFriends says more than 190,000 bricks, made on the island from sand and gravel, went into the boathouse, the hoist house and the keepers' dwelling.[^10] The two accounts disagree on the dwelling's material.

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive has a keeper roster for the station that runs from 1916 to 1971 and lists more than a hundred stints, for head keepers, assistants and Coast Guard crew.[^11] The first name on the head keepers' list is Carl E. Peterson, who served from 1916 to 1919.[^11] Frank W. Ritchie was head keeper from 1919 to 1923.[^11]

Some keepers moved up through the ranks. Edward M. Toman was second assistant from 1920 to 1922, head keeper from 1923 to 1924, and head keeper again from 1925 to 1927 and from 1930 to 1931.[^11] Two head keepers have no end date on the roster: Sidney L. Jackson, listed at "at least 1950-", and Gerald R. King, from 1966.[^11]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The station's buoys went astray in 1918 and 1919.[^9] On 13 September 1918 the gas, whistling and submarine-bell buoy No. 2 "went adrift during a strong southeast gale," and by 1920 it had not been recovered.[^9] Its replacement, placed in June 1919, was also lost. In February 1920 a commercial steamer reported it ashore on the east side of Elizabeth Island, at the entrance to Cook Inlet, 240 miles west of the cape.[^9]

In May 1961 a brown bear of about 800 pounds tried to force its way into the keepers' residence. LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard crew fired a shot over its head, and the bear came back an hour later.[^10]

On Good Friday, 27 March 1964, the earthquake that struck Prince William Sound was felt at the station for five minutes. LighthouseFriends says a quake-triggered rockfall on Pinnacle Rock broke the leg of Coast Guard crew member Frank O. Reed. Three of his comrades went to search for him. While they were carrying him back, the crew was caught in a tsunami, and a ten-foot surge swept them all into the sea. Reed drowned; the other men survived.[^10] The same account says newspapers reported the light out for three days after the quake, but the station's logbook shows the light active the night after it.[^10]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends reports a Cordova Times notice of 31 October 1968 saying that the Coast Guard's Seventeenth District planned to automate five Alaska lights, including this one.[^10] Cape St. Elias and Cape Hinchinbrook both lost their personnel in 1974.[^10] Sometime before 1973 the Fresnel lens was taken out of the tower and replaced by a smaller beacon.[^10]

LighthouseFriends quotes an entry in the station logbook on the automation: "Saint Elias light is now history."[^10] The lens is now on display at the Cordova Museum.[^10] The station was placed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1975.[^1][^10]

The Cape St. Elias Lightkeepers Association was organized in 1997 to restore and preserve the station's buildings. It holds a 30-year lease from the Coast Guard.[^13][^10]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the lighthouse. LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open, and the dwelling and tower are closed.[^10] The association's site says the boathouse is the only habitable building, with bunks for as many as ten paying visitors, and that the light has been automated since 1974.[^13] The association says that for the past 25 years it has worked to keep the lighthouse open to visitors. Overnight stays are booked through the association.[^13][^10]
