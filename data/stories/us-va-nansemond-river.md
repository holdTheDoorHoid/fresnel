Nansemond River Light was a white, six-sided wooden cottage on a pile foundation at the mouth of the Nansemond River in southeastern Virginia.[^1][^2][^7] Its sixth-order lens showed its first light on November 1, 1878.[^2][^3] The keepers left in 1933, a steel skeletal tower took over in 1935, and the light was discontinued in 1974.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The light was built for a shoal at the river's mouth.[^2][^4] The shoal lay off Pig Point, on the eastern side of the entrance.[^2][^4] In 1876 a letter from the Office of the Light-House Inspector made the case for a light there, arguing that vessels could round it close and avoid "the more dangerous Oyster shoals on the starboard hand."[^4] The letter, citing "reliable authority," said some four hundred large schooners and several steamers were engaged in the river's trade.[^4]

Congress appropriated $15,000 for the light on July 31, 1876.[^2][^3] The Virginia legislature had passed an act ceding the land in 1874.[^3] In 1877 the title to a tract for the site was obtained from the governor of Virginia.[^2][^3]

## Building the light

The superstructure was framed at the Lazaretto depot in Baltimore in February and March 1878.[^3] Part of the metalwork came from the old foundation at Roanoke Marshes, North Carolina, according to the Board's report as LighthouseFriends quotes it.[^2] The Chesapeake Chapter's timeline gives the same source without saying how much.[^3] The tender *Tulip* carried the men and materials from Baltimore, leaving on July 5 and reaching the site on July 10.[^2] Piles for the foundation were driven between July 18 and July 22.[^2]

The sources do not agree on the foundation. The US Lighthouse Society's record calls it a screw-pile foundation.[^1] The Board's report for 1879, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, describes a "sleeve-pile structure."[^2] The Chesapeake Chapter says engineers judged the river bottom too hard for screw piles, so the wooden piles were encased in cast-iron sleeves.[^7]

The light was first exhibited on the night of November 1, 1878, a fixed red light from a sixth-order lens, burning mineral oil.[^2][^3] The Chesapeake Chapter says a Stevens fog bell struck once every seven seconds.[^7]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists ten principal keepers, forty first-assistant entries and one laborer for the station, from 1878 to 1933.[^1] Some men served in both posts. Exum White, William A. Bond and Alphonso Royster each served as first assistant before becoming principal keeper.[^1]

On the roster's dates, Temple Ripley was principal keeper from 1908 to 1933.[^1] That is twenty-five years, the longest principal's tenure on the roster.[^1] Suffolk River Heritage ends his service in 1924, and the Chesapeake Chapter says at least 1926.[^4][^7] In August 1915 the Evening Star reported that the Bureau of Lighthouses had awarded the Nansemond River station its efficiency pennant, and that "Temple Ripley is the keeper there," with John Marchant as assistant.[^6] LighthouseFriends also lists the 1915 award.[^2]

Ella L. Edwards served as first assistant keeper in 1903 and again in 1904.[^1] She is the only woman on the roster.[^1]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1899 says that "New model fifth-order lamps were supplied."[^5] The Chesapeake Chapter's timeline says the lens was replaced that year with a fifth-order Fresnel lens.[^3] The sources do not agree on what changed. In 1906 the machine-operated fog bell stopped working on May 16 and was repaired by July 10.[^3]

## After the keepers

On May 23, 1933 the superintendent of lighthouses began the process of automating the light.[^3] It would change the illuminant from oil to acetylene, end the fog bell, and end the keeper and assistant posts.[^3] The Chesapeake Chapter says it would save $3,420 a year, and that the change was approved on June 2.[^3][^7]

The original tower was torn down in 1935.[^1] A steel skeletal tower with an automated light was set on the remaining screwpile foundation.[^1][^3] The Chesapeake Chapter's timeline gives a deactivation date of December 12, 1934. Its article and the US Lighthouse Society's record give 1935.[^1][^3][^7] Suffolk River Heritage says that Representative Colgate W. Darden Jr. asked the Lighthouse Board to keep the light, and that the acting Commissioner of Lighthouses assured him it would remain. It also says a steel tower replaced it later that year. The sources do not agree on when the change happened.[^4]

The automated light stayed in service until 1974, when it was discontinued as an active aid to navigation.[^3][^7]

## Today

LighthouseFriends says that no light exists at the site today.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society lists the station as not open to the public.[^1] The site lay on the water off Pig Point.[^2] Whether any of the 1935 tower or its foundation survives is not recorded in the sources.
