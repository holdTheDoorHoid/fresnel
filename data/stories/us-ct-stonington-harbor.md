Stonington Harbor Light is a stone tower attached to its keeper's house on the point at the east side of Stonington's harbor, in the southeast corner of Connecticut. It is the second light on the point. The first tower went up in 1823, and in 1840 the government took the old stones and rebuilt the light farther back from the sea. The light was discontinued in 1889, and the house is now a museum.[^1][^2][^5]

## The place

Fishing, whaling and shipbuilding were the town's main trades, according to LighthouseFriends.[^7] Congress appropriated $3,500 for a light at the harbor in 1822.[^2] The trouble was the point itself. An inspector in 1838 found that 22 feet (6.7 m) of the original property had been swept away by the sea, and the government had to choose between building a sea wall and replacing the structure.[^1] The village had faced a British attack in 1814, and the museum says artifacts in its collection document that defense.[^4][^7]

## Building the light

Benjamin Chase of Newport built the first tower, a 30-foot (9.1 m) stone tower with a detached stone house.[^1][^2][^7] The 1929 *Lighthouse Service Bulletin* says the tower was built in 1823 and lighted in 1824. The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record gives 1823 as the year the station was established.[^2][^5] The Bulletin's index credits Winslow Lewis with the lamps and reflectors.[^3] The 1975 National Register nomination names "I.W.P. Lewis" as the designer and supplier of the original lighting equipment. It does not connect him with the Winslow Lewis of the index.[^1][^3]

The lamp descriptions belong to different towers. For the 1823 tower, LighthouseFriends describes ten whale-oil lamps in thirteen-inch (33 cm) reflectors.[^7] For the 1840 light, the nomination and LighthouseFriends both give eight lamps in sixteen-inch (41 cm) reflectors, and the nomination says whale oil fueled both lights.[^1][^7]

By 1840 the sea had come too close.[^2] The government moved the light back from the point and reused the old stones in a new one-and-a-half-story granite keeper's house and a 35-foot (10.7 m) octagonal tower.[^1][^2][^7] John Bishop of New London did the removal.[^2] The new site raised the focal plane 15 feet (4.6 m).[^1] The house as built in 1840 had a nearly flat roof with simulated battlements, the museum says, and it leaked so badly that two years later local craftsmen put on the gabled roof it has today.[^4]

The nomination and the U.S. Lighthouse Society's record give 1856 for the sixth-order Fresnel lens that replaced the lamps.[^1][^5] LighthouseFriends places the lens one year after widow Potter's tenure ended in 1854, which would be 1855.[^7]

## The keepers

William Potter was appointed the first keeper in 1823 at a salary of $300 a year.[^2] He kept the light until his death in 1842.[^5][^7] His widow, Patty Potter, was born in 1782 and died in 1869. The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record calls her the wife of Keeper William Potter.[^6] She took the post and kept it until 1854.[^5][^7] LighthouseFriends says that a few years after she took the post, an inspector found her house neglected, and it quotes his description.[^7]

The roster that follows her is short. Luther Ripley kept the light from 1854 to 1861. Winthrop Hand held it for 1861 alone. Henry Burgess served from 1861 to 1869, Charles E. P. Noyes from 1869 to 1872, and Benjamin F. Pendleton from 1872 to 1889.[^5] LighthouseFriends runs Pendleton's tenure to 1890.[^7] The museum says the house had been home to seven keepers and their families.[^4]

## After the keepers

On 1 November 1889 a 25-foot (7.6 m) iron tower with a fourth-order red light began operating on the breakwater at the harbor entrance.[^7] The old light was discontinued that year.[^2] The museum says it was "supplanted by beacons on the harbor breakwaters."[^4] Benjamin Pendleton went on living in the old quarters and kept the breakwater light.[^7] The house went on housing breakwater keepers until 1908 according to the Bulletin, or until 1909 according to the museum.[^2][^4] The National Register form records a new keeper's house built next door in 1908.[^1]

Federal money still went to the point. The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported that $500 appropriated in 1911 for repairs to the sea wall at Stonington Point was completed in November 1915.[^8] In 1925 the government sold the lighthouse with part of its reservation, and the Stonington Historical Society acquired it for $3,650.[^2][^4] In 1927 it opened as a lighthouse museum, which the museum calls the nation's first.[^4] The Bulletin's index lists the sale and the museum under the old light.[^3]

## Today

The Lighthouse Museum at 7 Water Street is run by Historic Stonington, formerly the Stonington Historical Society.[^4] It is closed for renovations from 4 October 2026 and reopens in May 2027.[^4] When it is open, visitors may climb the 29 circular steps and a short ladder to the top of the tower.[^4] The climb is offered with the museum, so do not try to climb the tower until it reopens.[^4]
