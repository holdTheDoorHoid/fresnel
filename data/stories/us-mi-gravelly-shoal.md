Gravelly Shoal Light is a steel tower in Saginaw Bay, Michigan, at the eastern end of Gravelly Shoal, placed to mark the deeper water of the passage toward Big Charity Island.[^1] It is a working light with no house, and its tower is closed to visitors.[^2][^6]

## The place

Gravelly Shoal runs some three miles southeast from Point Lookout, on the western shore of Lake Huron, toward Big Charity Island.[^1] In August 1856 Congress appropriated $5,000 for a light on the northwest shore of Big Charity; work began that season and the light was lit the following year.[^1]

The island light was a constant source of maintenance problems. It was one of the first to be automated, with acetylene, in 1900, and that year an occulting white Pintsch gas buoy was placed at the southeastern end of the shoal.[^1]

By the late 1930s the old island light and the gas buoy had "outlived their usefulness," as one history puts it.[^1] In his 1931 report, Commissioner George R. Putnam noted the requests from shipping interests for a light at the bay's entrance. LighthouseFriends quotes him: "Many requests have been received from shipping interests for the establishment of a lightship or a light station at the entrance to Saginaw Bay."[^2]

## Building the light

The new light was built as a Public Works Administration project, No. 56.[^1] Its design came from two Lighthouse Service engineers who reused a plan from the Lake Erie lights at Conneaut and Huron.[^1]

The contract went to John C. Meagher of Bay City, at $75,000. LighthouseFriends spells the name "Meager" and says the steel tower was made by the Wickes Boiler Company of Saginaw for $24,000.[^1][^2]

The crew set out for the shoal on 12 April 1939, drove five clusters of piles, and poured a concrete pier 50 feet (15 m) across.[^1] In July the water turned rough, and in one thirty-day stretch the crew tied up at the pier only once.[^1] In August the steel tower came out on a barge and was lowered onto its bolts.[^1] Terry Pepper's history gives its height as 61 feet (19 m); LighthouseFriends, quoting the 1941 Lake Carriers' Association report, gives 50 feet (15 m).[^1][^2] That 1941 description gives the foundation as a rock-filled steel caisson capped with concrete, not a concrete pier.[^2]

The lamp was a 375-millimetre lens with an electric lamp, and the light had a 75-foot (23 m) focal plane. Twin diaphones gave a 30-second fog cycle.[^1][^2]

The dates do not agree. Pepper says the light went into service in October 1939.[^1] LighthouseFriends says work began in 1938, the structure was completed in the fall of 1941, and the light was placed in commission at the opening of navigation in 1942.[^2]

## After the keepers

The station was built as an automated electric light, under the control and maintenance of the Coast Guard station at Tawas.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it was run by remote control from Tawas.[^2]

About fifteen years after it was finished, the Coast Guard decided that winter ice floes were placing undue stress on the pier walls. Meagher was asked to add seven feet (2 m) of concrete. He had quoted $7,000 for that work while the station was being built; the job cost $54,000.[^1]

In May 2014 the Coast Guard declared the light excess, and it was offered to eligible public and nonprofit bodies under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act.[^2] The 2015 program report says that long-running legal questions over Michigan's bottomlands had held up offshore transfers, and that they had been resolved.[^3] The light was then conveyed by public sale in 2015 for $16,000. The high bidder defaulted before closing, and the light was resold in 2016.[^3][^4]

LighthouseFriends reports that after the high bidder failed to close, the General Services Administration offered the light to the second-highest bidder, a man who already owned other lights in Michigan and Delaware.[^2] A 2015 news report said only nonprofit organisations could bid,[^5] while the 2015 program report's table records a private owner. The same report's text calls 2015 the year of "the first public sale of an offshore light to a non-for-profit entity."[^3] The sources do not reconcile these.

## Today

The tower is closed to visitors, and LighthouseFriends says the light is privately owned.[^2] A 2026 listing offers Gravelly Shoal and Skillagalee Light together for $179,900, and says a nonprofit owns both and will not split them. The sources do not agree on the owner.[^6] The listing calls both Michigan towers working lights with no house, and says a visit needs a boat.[^6] Terry Pepper's history reported the light still an active aid to navigation in 2001.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the best view is from the shore at the tip of the peninsula six miles east of Au Gres, and that a ferry to Charity Island gives a closer view of the tower.[^2]
