Gibraltar Point Lighthouse stands on Centre Island in the Toronto Islands, and is the oldest surviving lighthouse on the Great Lakes.[^1] The province had its stone tower built in 1808, and it kept a light until the late 1950s.[^1][^2]

## The place

Lieutenant Governor John Graves Simcoe moved the province's capital to York in 1793 and had a blockhouse put on the tip of the peninsula, which he named Gibraltar Point.[^2] The first building settlers made on the Toronto Islands was that blockhouse, built in 1793 by the Queen's Rangers.[^1] Once the point had a light, the Library's history says, vessels could travel the lake at night.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1803 the Parliament of Upper Canada authorized three lighthouses, one of them at Gibraltar Point.[^2] The act set a toll on vessels using the harbour to pay for them and for their upkeep.[^1] On 16 March 1808 the York Gazette announced that the light was "to be immediately completed."[^1] Lieutenant Governor Francis Gore visited the peninsula on 6 April 1808 and chose the site.[^2]

The tower was built of Queenston stone, carried to York by a vessel called the Mohawk. It was hexagonal, 16 m (52 ft) high including the lantern, with walls 1.8 m (6 ft) thick at the base.[^1][^2] Beside it stood the keeper's cottage, a two-room frame house of planks joined with nails from the blacksmith's forge at Fort York.[^1]

## The keepers

John Paul Radelmüller, hired as the first keeper, served from 1809 until his death on 2 January 1815.[^2][^1] The York Gazette of 14 January 1815 reported it and said: "From circumstances there is moral proof of his having been murdered."[^2] Three months later the Gazette reported: "No conviction of the supposed murderers of the late J.P. Rademuller."[^2]

Lighthouse Friends says legend has it that soldiers from the York garrison visited Gibraltar Point that night to get beer from the keeper, and that the soldiers became enraged, killed the keeper, and disposed of his body. Some accounts say the keeper refused to sell to them, and others say he overcharged them.[^2] The Library's version has the soldiers beat him to death after he refused to produce more liquor.[^1]

William Hollaway kept the light from 1816 to 1831.[^2] (The Library spells the name Holloway.)[^1] James Durnan took over in 1832, the year the tower was raised, and his son George succeeded him in 1854.[^2][^1][^3] In 1877 Darius Smith, superintendent of lights above Montreal, described the station, and Lighthouse Friends quotes his report: a white hexagonal tower, 19 m (62 ft) high (the Library gives 25 m (82 ft) to the vane after 1832), with an iron lantern and eleven flat-wick lamps. The inspector wrote that the lantern was rusted and decayed by long use, and that the keeper, George Durnan, had a family of twelve children.[^2]

The keepers' list in the Department's report printed in 1905 still gives George Durnan at Gibraltar Point Light and Fog Alarm.[^3] The list printed in 1907 gives Patrick McSherry at Gibraltar Point.[^4] Lighthouse Friends gives Captain Patrick J. McSherry as keeper from 1905 to 1912, and Blake Matthews from 1912 to 1918, and calls Matthews the last official keeper.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Library's history says a schooner named Toronto was wrecked on the south shore of the peninsula in the winter of 1811 to 1812, and that the skipper appears to have been confused by the light.[^1]

During the Battle of York in 1813, the Library notes, the blockhouse and the lighthouse on Gibraltar Point were untouched.[^1]

On the night of 18 December 1832 the schooner Sir John Colborne, loaded with lumber, ran aground on a bar north of the point. Lighthouse Friends says an investigation found the light was not lit that night. A custom-house officer testified that the keeper was supposed to come ashore on the 17th to get more oil but did not come until the 19th, so the lamp ran out of oil on the 18th. The owner, Freeman Bay, was awarded £100.[^2]

In 1878 a steel lantern and a lightning rod went on the tower. The Library reports that lightning struck it in 1879, and that without the rod "the tower may have been in ruins today."[^1]

In 1893 Lighthouse Friends says George Durnan uncovered a jaw bone on the point.[^2] The Library says the keeper of that time allegedly found parts of a human skeleton buried close to the lighthouse, and that the story "remains controversial."[^1]

On 22 November 1906 the steamer Resolute, of Deseronto, foundered in a gale while she lay at anchor under Gibraltar Point, waiting for the wind to ease before entering the harbour. Six of her crew reached shore, and six were drowned.[^5]

## After the keepers

In 1832 the tower was raised to about 25 m (82 ft) overall, and the lamp began burning sperm whale oil, about 200 gallons a year.[^1][^2] The Library says the light could be seen about seven miles then; the 1877 inspection gave 18 miles in clear weather.[^1][^2] Coal oil replaced the whale oil sometime after 1863, and in 1883 a revolving apparatus changed the light to a white flash every forty-five seconds.[^1][^2]

A fog alarm was built in 1885, about 350 m (1,150 ft) south-west of the tower.[^2] The Department's report printed in 1906 says the Gibraltar Point fog alarm "has been permanently discontinued," after a new fog alarm went up at East Gap.[^6]

The Department's report printed in 1909 says that on 30 June 1907 the light from the Gibraltar Point lighthouse "was permanently discontinued."[^7] The reports on file do not say when it was lit again. The report printed in 1917 describes a "white revolving" light there.[^8] Lighthouse Friends says a fourth-order lens was set atop the tower in July 1915, and that the light was electrified in the winter of 1916 to 1917.[^2][^1]

In 1945 the light was changed to a fixed light, which Lighthouse Friends says was green.[^1][^2] In 1958 the Library says the light was replaced by a signal tower nearby, and on 23 May 1958 the station passed to the Metropolitan Toronto parks department, which made changes in 1961 and 1962.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates the deactivation to 1959.[^2]

The Library says that almost every Halloween a new story is written about how the lighthouse came to be haunted.[^1]

## Today

The City of Toronto owns the lighthouse, through Toronto Island Park.[^9][^2] The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^9][^2] The Library says the building occasionally opens for tours or for Doors Open Toronto.[^1] The tower was about 8 m from the water when it was built; it now stands about 100 m from the water.[^1] To visit, take a ferry to the islands, then walk about 2 km.[^2]
