Cape Hatteras Light is the black-and-white spiral-striped brick tower at Buxton, on Hatteras Island in Dare County, North Carolina.[^5] It was built in 1868–70 and is the tallest brick lighthouse in the United States.[^5] In 1999 the whole light station was jacked up and rolled 2,900 feet (880 m) inland to get it away from the sea.[^12][^5]

## The place

Offshore of the cape, the Gulf Stream meets the Virginia Drift, a branch of the Labrador Current. The National Park Service says that this pushes southbound ships toward Diamond Shoals, a dangerous sandbar twelve miles long.[^5] The Park Service reckons the wrecks there number "hundreds and possibly thousands", while Paul Cloyd, an engineer writing in 1999, repeated an estimate of more than 2,200 craft lost near the cape.[^5][^6] The Park Service describes the site as a low-lying beach, and Cloyd writes that there were no landmarks visible to mariners unless they steered too close to the dangerous coast.[^5][^6]

The sources differ on when Washington decided to light it. The Park Service's timeline says Congress authorized the first tower in 1794; Cloyd writes that Congress recognized the need in 1797.[^1][^6]

The shoals outlasted several attempts to mark them from the sea. The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1892 recalls that the caisson meant to carry a lighthouse onto Outer Diamond Shoal was destroyed on July 8, 1891, and that no further attempt had been made since.[^17] The Park Service counts three Diamond Shoals lightships, the first in service from 1824 to 1827, when gales destroyed it.[^2]

## Building the light

**The first tower.** The Park Service says construction began in 1799 and the first lighthouse was lit in October 1803: a sandstone tower 90 feet (27 m) tall, lit by lamps burning whale oil.[^5] LighthouseFriends gives a slightly different sequence: Henry Dearborn was contracted in 1798, the structures were substantially finished by the end of 1802, and the lights at Cape Hatteras and Shell Castle were working by late 1803, after oil had been obtained from Nantucket.[^7] A Lighthouse Service article of 1937 says simply that a lighthouse was constructed on Cape Hatteras in 1798.[^20] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists its first keeper from 1802.[^10] Which of these years counts as "first lit" depends on whether one means the contract, the finished tower or the lamps.

By 1839 the tower held eighteen 14-inch reflectors, and by 1849 fifteen 21-inch ones, burning whale oil.[^4] In 1850 the keeper, Joseph C. Jennett, was living in a leaky dwelling, and an inspector wanted a larger house "so that seamen can be accommodated when wrecked about the cape." A report the following year judged the light "of very little use, in consequence of its limited range," according to LighthouseFriends, which quotes both.[^7]

**Raising the tower.** After the Lighthouse Board took charge in 1852, the tower was raised by 60 feet (18 m) to 150 feet (46 m) and given a first-order Fresnel lens made by Henry-Lepaute of Paris.[^2][^5][^8] According to Kevin Duffus, that lens was one of the first two first-order lenses the United States bought, and it was the centrepiece of the Lighthouse Board's exhibit in New York in 1853.[^8] LighthouseFriends says the revolving light began work early in 1854 and needed three keepers.[^7] The Park Service says the heightened tower was white below and red above; LighthouseFriends says the bottom twenty feet were grey.[^2][^7]

**The new tower.** Shortly after the Civil War the tower was judged to need replacing.[^2] Congress appropriated $75,000 in 1867; the final cost was $167,500.[^1] Construction began in October 1868, and LighthouseFriends calls Dexter Stetson the Board's superintendent of construction, while the Park Service and Cloyd call him the foreman.[^7][^5][^6] Stetson, born in Durham, Maine, in 1815 according to the obituary the Island Free Press cites, had made his home at Nahant, Massachusetts, where his most praised work was the village church built in 1852.[^11]

The plan called for a foundation on driven piles, but the sand under the site was so hard that the work could not be done that way. The sources give different depths: Cloyd says Stetson could dig only about six feet before reaching hard, compact sand, and project engineer J. H. Simpson wrote that Stetson's sounding rod "could not make it penetrate the sand at the site of the Tower more than 9 feet." The Park Service describes the finished foundation as about 7½ feet deep.[^6][^11][^2] Stetson laid a grillage of crossed yellow pine timbers directly on the bottom, below the water table, and built granite on top of it.[^6][^11] The Park Service gives the timbers as 6 by 12 inches and 12 feet long.[^2] When the tower was moved 129 years later, the contractors found the timbers sound.[^6]

The tower has a double wall with a hollow space between, braced by cross walls "resembling the spokes of a wheel."[^5][^6] The octagonal base is of dressed granite, the quoins coming from Vermont; the iron spiral stairway was cast by Bartlett and Robbins, and about 1.25 million dark red bricks came from kilns along the James River near Richmond, Virginia.[^6] LighthouseFriends says nearly 100 local men were hired and trained, at $1.50 a day.[^7] There are 269 steps from the ground to the lens room.[^5] The sources do not agree on the height. The Park Service gives 198.49 feet (60.5 m) from the bottom of the foundation to the top of the pinnacle; Cloyd gives 207 feet (63 m) for the same span, and a Lighthouse Service article of 1937 calls it 193 feet (59 m).[^5][^6][^20]

The new light was lit on December 16, 1870, according to the Park Service's visitor page, LighthouseFriends and the Island Free Press; a different page of the Park Service's gives December 1.[^5][^7][^11][^2] LighthouseFriends says Keeper Benjamin C. Jennett and his assistants lit it.[^7] The old tower was demolished in February 1871; LighthouseFriends says by dynamite, and the ruins remained until a storm in March 1980 swept them away.[^5][^7][^6]

**The lens.** The Park Service says the first-order Fresnel lens of the old tower was moved to the new one, with more than 1,000 prisms and 24 bull's-eye lenses.[^1][^4] LighthouseFriends is of two minds: it says the lens of the old tower was shipped to California for Pigeon Point Lighthouse, and also that in 2002 the lens in the new tower was found to be the one the Confederates had taken.[^7] Duffus's account supports the second claim: the lens was repaired in Paris in 1867, held at the Staten Island depot, and sent to the new Cape Hatteras tower.[^8] The lens turned by clockwork driven by 150-pound iron weights, which were wound by hand to the top of the tower each morning.[^4]

The principal keeper's house is of brick; Cloyd says one account claims it was meant to be wood, but brick left over from the tower was used.[^6] The Park Service dates it to 1870, the Lighthouse Society to 1871.[^3][^10] The assistants lived in the double keepers' quarters, built in 1854.[^3][^6] In 1873 the tower received its black and white spiral bands: four, two black and two white, each making one and a half turns.[^6][^7]

## The keepers

The staff was a principal keeper and two assistants; later a third assistant was added, who in LighthouseFriends's account looked after a beacon light away from the tower.[^3][^7] The Park Service says the pay was up to $800 a year, with housing, staple foods and medicine, and each keeper stood a four-hour watch at night, rotating from day to day.[^3]

The Lighthouse Society's archive lists Adam Gaskins as the first principal keeper, from 1802 to 1808. Three Farrows followed: Joseph (1808–21), Pharoah M. (1821–30) and Isaac (1830–42).[^10] The same few family names recur in the archive's list over the next century, among them Jennett, Fulcher, O'Neal and Simpson; the list does not say how the keepers were related.[^10] LighthouseFriends does say that the Jennette family sold the government the land for the first tower.[^7]

Benjamin T. Fulcher kept the light twice, from 1845 to 1849 and from 1860 to 1862.[^10] According to Duffus, he took part in the removal of the lens for the Confederates in 1861 and had escaped with his family to Hyde County before any action could be taken against him.[^8] The same account says that his great-grandson, Unaka Jennette, was photographed polishing that lens in 1933 for *National Geographic*.[^8]

Two careers on the roster show a climb through the ranks. Tillman F. Smith appears as third assistant in 1878, second assistant in 1878–79, first assistant in 1879–87 and principal keeper from 1887 to 1898.[^10] In the autumn of 1895 the Light-House Board mounted a lighthouse exhibit at the Atlanta exposition and detailed working keepers to tend it "as a mark of appreciation of faithful service"; Smith had the exhibit from November 9 to December 5.[^13] Fabius E. Simpson served as third assistant in 1884–85, first assistant in 1893–1900 and principal keeper from 1906 to 1919.[^10]

The dwellings were adjusted as the staff grew. In the report for fiscal 1892 one of the kitchens in the wings of the assistants' dwelling was moved to the rear and the building altered so that two families could use it.[^17] LighthouseFriends ties this to the new third assistant, whose quarters had been scant.[^7]

Unaka B. Jennette became principal keeper in 1919 and stayed until 1939, the longest tenure of any Cape Hatteras keeper, according to LighthouseFriends, which counts eighty-three keepers in all.[^10][^7] Before he came he had twice served on the Diamond Shoals lightship, as quartermaster and later as captain.[^7]

In 2001 the names of the eighty-three keepers were engraved in the granite blocks that mark the old site, and more than 1,000 of their descendants gathered there that May.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

**The Civil War.** The Park Service says Confederate forces tried to destroy the tower in 1861 but were prevented, and that they took the lens.[^1] In October 1861, after the Chicamacomico Races, Union soldiers camped at the lighthouse.[^9] Duffus describes the lens's journey: a cost estimate for removing it was dated June 24, 1861; it was hidden for nine months in a warehouse in Washington, North Carolina; and when a Union expedition came to recover it in March 1862, it had gone up the Tar River in a light-draught steamer. By April 18, 1862 it sat in a boxcar at Townsville, near the Virginia line.[^8] General Burnside told the Secretary of War that "the light belonging to the Hatteras light-house" had been removed up the river, in Duffus's quotation of the letter.[^8] LighthouseFriends says the tower was relit in 1862 with a second-order lens and fitted with a first-order lens the next year.[^7]

**Lightning and an earthquake.** On April 17, 1879 lightning struck the tower; the keepers later blamed cracks in the inner wall on it, though the Park Service now attributes the cracks to thermal expansion.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the district engineer blamed imperfect grounding and had the ironwork connected to a buried iron disk.[^7] The Charleston earthquake of 1886 shook the tower. One keeper reported that "the tower would tremble and sway backward and forward like a tree shaken by the wind"; LighthouseFriends records only cracked storm panes and a few overturned objects.[^7]

**The beacon on the point.** A smaller beacon stood about a quarter of a mile from the tip of Cape Hatteras Point.[^13] In November 1895 the keeper reported it was in danger of destruction by the waves: the beach in front had been cut away 35 feet in 24 hours.[^13] The Board moved it 276 feet nearer the lighthouse on November 25, 1896, and moved it another 435 feet on February 24, 1899.[^14][^15] The severe storm of August 1899 overthrew its light-post; it was re-erected and lit again on August 24.[^16]

**A submarine and a lightship.** On August 6, 1918 a German submarine fired on Diamond Shoal Light Vessel No. 71 and sank her; the twelve men aboard took to a boat and landed about a mile north of the lighthouse, having lost all their belongings.[^18] The Park Service's list names the sunk vessel as No. 69.[^2]

**1933.** A hurricane on August 22, 1933 flooded the reservation. The Lighthouse Service's bulletin says the cisterns were filled with salt water, water came up through the floors of the keepers' dwellings, and the beach was further eroded.[^19] Jennette wrote to his superiors that this was "by far the highest sea tide recorded since I have been at Cape Hatteras," that "the entire reservation is completely submerged," and that he had been forced to move his family away from the station.[^7] LighthouseFriends reports another storm three weeks later with more erosion and damage.[^7]

## After the keepers

The Park Service dates the electrification of the light to 1934.[^1] The sea was by then at the tower: it stood about 1,500 feet (460 m) from the water in 1870, and the Park Service says it was within a few hundred feet by 1919 and less than 100 feet (30 m) by 1933, despite groins from 1930.[^1][^2][^6]

The light was abandoned and moved to a steel skeleton tower about a mile northwest, in Buxton Woods, on land Unaka Jennette provided.[^1][^7] The date is given as 1935 by the Park Service, as 1936 in a Lighthouse Service article of 1937, and as sunrise on May 13, 1936 by Duffus.[^1][^20][^8] Jennette minded the new light until the Coast Guard took over in 1939, then chose to stay a civilian and moved to Roanoke Marshes Lighthouse, from which he retired in 1943.[^7]

The Park Service gives November 9, 1937 for the transfer of the station to its care, while its timeline and Cloyd say 1936.[^5][^1][^6] The Coast Guard used the tower as an observation tower in 1942–45.[^1] Meanwhile, without keepers, the lens was stripped. By 1944, according to Duffus, two of its center flash panels had been jimmied loose, and in time prisms were being carried off; after the remains were shuttled between Park Service storage sites, including the Little Kinnakeet station, a couple of surviving panels were stolen and eventually dumped in a ditch north of Avon.[^8] The Park Service dates the vandalism to 1946 and the removal of the lens apparatus to 1949.[^1] On January 23, 1950, with the beach rebuilt, the Coast Guard returned a 250,000-candlepower beacon to the tower.[^5] The seashore was established in 1953 and the lighthouse opened to the public.[^1]

The sea came back. The Park Service reports the tower closed in 1975 for structural cracks, and 50 to 70 feet from the shoreline in 1980.[^1] A National Academy of Sciences committee recommended in 1988 that the lighthouse be moved.[^6] By 1997 the sea had cut the distance from about 1,600 feet (490 m) in 1870 to 118 feet (36 m).[^6] North Carolina State University engineers agreed with the move in 1996, Congress appropriated $9.8 million in October 1998, and Dare County commissioners sued to stop it; a federal judge dismissed the suit in April 1999.[^6]

The International Chimney Corporation won the design-build contract.[^6] The tower itself moved between June 17 and July 9, 1999, pushed by hydraulic jacks along a track in 5-foot (1.5 m) increments, 2,900 feet (880 m) in 23 days, at a cost of $11.8 million.[^12][^1] The keepers' quarters, oil house and cisterns had gone first, in March.[^1] It came to rest 1,500 feet (460 m) from the shoreline, the same distance it stood from the sea in 1870.[^5] LighthouseFriends says it was lit again on November 13.[^7] The stones that ringed the old site were buried by Hurricane Sandy in 2013 and later reset as the Keepers of the Light Amphitheater.[^7]

## Today

The tower and keepers' quarters are in the care of the National Park Service, within Cape Hatteras National Seashore; the Coast Guard operates the automated light.[^5] It is a pair of beacons turned by an electric motor, giving an 800,000-candlepower flash every 7.5 seconds.[^4][^1] The Park Service lists the official range as 24 nautical miles.[^4]

The address is 46379 Lighthouse Road, Buxton. The Park Service says the lighthouse will not open for climbing in 2026 because of restoration work, and offers a virtual tour and webcam instead.[^5] A new bookstore and visitor center were built in 2003, and the Double Keepers' Quarters was made a museum in 1954.[^2][^6] The pedestal and clockwork were reunited with the remains of the lens at the Graveyard of the Atlantic Museum in Hatteras, ten miles southwest; LighthouseFriends says the frame and a few remaining prisms can be seen there.[^1][^7]
