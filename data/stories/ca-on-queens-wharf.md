Queen's Wharf Lighthouse is an octagonal wooden tower, built in 1861 as one of a pair of range lights that guided ships into Toronto Harbour.[^1][^3] The harbour was filled in around it, and in 1929 it was moved about 450 m to Fleet Street, where it stands today.[^1][^3]

## The place

Queen's Wharf began as a pier. Lighthouse Friends says a pier was extended into the harbour in 1830 as the New Pier, and was renamed Queen's Wharf after Queen Victoria came to the throne in 1837. The Architectural Conservancy's record gives 1833 as the year the wharf was built.[^1][^3]

The wharf did two jobs, according to Lighthouse Friends. It gave ships a place to dock, and it narrowed the harbour entrance so that faster water would keep a sandbar from growing across the harbour mouth from an offshore island.[^1] By 1901 the shoal had grown. The Harbour Commissioners reported that the north-west point of the shoal had extended 122 m (400 ft) in ten years and was still growing.[^1]

By the report for the year ended 31 March 1908 the harbour had two entrances: the western, called the Queens Wharf channel, and the eastern one, dredged and faced with piers, which was the chief entrance.[^6]

## Building the light

The first light at Queen's Wharf was a square wooden tower, 5 m (16 ft) tall, placed on the wharf in 1838 as a guide for vessels entering the harbour.[^1]

The taller tower dates from 1861, when a pair of range lights was built. Lighthouse Friends says tenders were advertised in the provincial papers in May and June of that year, and the lighthouse and its keeper's dwelling were completed later that year.[^1] Kivas Tully designed the lighthouse and the dwelling for the Harbour Commissioners.[^1] The marker says Tully later became architect of the Ontario Department of Public Works, and that he designed the tower's octagonal plan.[^2] The tower is about 11 m (36 ft) high.[^3]

The Commissioners called the front range light the White Lighthouse and the rear one the Red Lighthouse.[^1] The front light was later destroyed by fire, so the tower that survives is the rear, or Red, light.[^1][^10]

In 1901 the front light was moved to the north-west corner of the wharf. Lighthouse Friends quotes the Commissioners as saying it moved about 9 m (30 ft).[^1] The Department of Marine's report for the same year gives 12 m (40 ft), and says the front light then stood 66 m (215 ft) south-west by south of the back or red light.[^4]

In 1908 a contract was awarded to build a new western channel into the harbour, finished in 1911. It had parallel piers 122 m (400 ft) apart, set 305 m (1,000 ft) farther south than the old entrance.[^1] During 1912 the new entrance received its own range lights: a reinforced concrete front tower and a steel skeletal rear tower with a wooden lantern.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists the keepers of the Queen's Wharf lights from the Harbour Commissioners' reports. Henry Smith served until 1843. James Hickman is recorded from at least 1849 to at least 1850. Captain Archibald Taylor kept the light from 1877 to 1896, Captain William Hall from 1896 to 1907, and John Mead Allan from 1907 to 1911.[^1] The list does not separate the keepers of the 1838 tower from the keepers of the 1861 pair.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 22 November 1906 the steamer *Resolute*, of Desoronto, foundered in a severe gale early in the morning. The Department's report says she had been lying at anchor outside the western entrance, waiting for the wind to moderate so that she could enter the harbour. Six of her crew reached shore and six were drowned.[^5]

Lighthouse Friends tells the wreck differently. It dates the loss to 21 November and says the steam barge *Resolute*, with a partner schooner, had come from Erie with coal. When the *Resolute* began taking on water, two lifeboats were launched, and one capsized with its five occupants.[^1] The two accounts differ on the date, the cause and the number of dead. This story does not choose between them. Both place the wreck at the western entrance, which the two range lights marked.[^1]

On 8 July 1908 a fire broke out at the Queen's Wharf and consumed the boat house and its contents. The Commissioners reported that the lighthouses and the dwelling house were saved, though "more or less injury was done to both."[^1]

In the 1912 season, the Department's reports say, signals were displayed at two Toronto stations, Queen's Wharf and Eastern Gap, on sixteen occasions.[^7]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the range lights on Queen's Wharf were discontinued at the end of the 1911 shipping season, once the new western entrance was in use.[^1] The marker says the lighthouse was decommissioned in 1911.[^2] The Department's reports record the lamps lit in late March and discontinued on 8 December in both years: lit on 28 March 1910 and discontinued on 8 December 1910,[^8] and lit on 30 March 1911 and discontinued on 8 December 1911.[^9] No report read here gives a lighting for 1912, which fits the end-of-1911 date in Lighthouse Friends and the marker.

Lighthouse Friends gives 1 September 1918 as the day the abandoned front range light, a white one, was destroyed by fire.[^1] The Department's report, which includes the Harbour Commission's account for the year ending 31 December 1917, gives the same day but in 1917, and says the white range light was built in 1856. Lighthouse Friends and the marker date the pair to 1861.[^10][^1][^2]

In 1929 the Toronto Harbour Commission transferred the remaining tower to the City of Toronto. Later that year it was set on wooden rollers and moved about 450 m to its present site. Lighthouse Friends says the move was west; the Architectural Conservancy's record says north.[^1][^3] The City of Toronto restored the building in 1988.[^3]

## Today

The City of Toronto owns the lighthouse. The Architectural Conservancy lists it as a listed heritage building with a cultural use.[^3] Lighthouse Friends lists the grounds as open and the tower as closed.[^1] A 2012 Heritage Toronto marker stands at the intersection of Fleet Street and Angelique Street, near the tower.[^2]
