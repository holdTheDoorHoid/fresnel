Egmont Key Light stands on the island at the mouth of Tampa Bay, across the channel from Fort De Soto.[^3] The first light was shown in 1848 from a brick tower.[^1] The sources disagree on what the hurricane of that September did to it: Fort De Soto History says it battered the tower, while LighthouseFriends says the tower survived the storm.[^1][^2] The tower that stands today was built in 1857 and first lit in 1858, and the light has been automated since 1989 or 1990, depending on the source.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

George Gauld named the island in 1765 after John Perceval, second Earl of Egmont. In his journal he wrote that the soil was "hardly any thing but sand and shells."[^1]

More and more vessels were running aground on the shoals around the island as shipping into Tampa Bay grew in the 1830s and 1840s.[^2] In March 1837 the Treasury asked Lieutenant Napoleon L. Coste, commander of the revenue cutter *Campbell*, to report on the coast. Coste wrote that "the western coast of Florida is very low, and similar in appearance," so the mariner needed "some mark by which he could take his departure." He thought a light would help, since much property was "now annually lost by shipwreck."[^1]

Florida became a state in 1845. Its legislature petitioned Congress in December 1846, and in 1847 Congress granted $10,000 for a light on Egmont Key.[^1] Half a century later the island had a different use. In 1898, during the Spanish-American War, Fort Dade was built there, and the fort was deactivated in 1923.[^1]

## Building the light

Francis A. Gibbons of Baltimore agreed to build "a lighthouse and dwelling at a cost of $6,250." The contract called for "a forty-foot, brick tower" (12 m) under an octagonal lantern for thirteen lamps. Winslow Lewis supplied the lighting apparatus for $1,330.[^1]

The St. Marks customs collector, Mr. Walker, oversaw the work. He recommended that the dwelling stand at least 100 feet (30 m) from the tower "in consequence of the heavy gales of wind in this country," and he insisted that the tower rest on driven pilings, not on the "dry shells and sand" that a Treasury official favored.[^1] LighthouseFriends says "It was likely due to Walker's pile foundation that the tower survived the storm" of 1848.[^1]

Work began in the summer of 1847. A supply ship carrying bricks from New York "ran aground on Orange Key," and about half the bricks had to be thrown overboard to refloat her.[^1] The Fort De Soto History article dates the certification to April 1848.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives the date as 19 April, when the tower was certified, and says that the first keeper, Sherrod Edwards, lit the lamps soon after. At that time it was "the only one between Key West and St. Marks."[^1]

Later works followed in order. Congress appropriated $16,000 in 1856, and a new tower "twice as tall as the original" was completed in 1857, roughly ninety feet (27 m) inland.[^1] The archive dates that tower to 1858 and records it as brick.[^3] A third-order Fresnel lens showed a fixed light at a focal plane of eighty-six feet (26 m).[^1] A fourth-order lens was lit on 2 June 1866.[^1] In 1893 a red sector was placed in the light, and in 1896 a third-order lens with two red sectors replaced it.[^1]

Other buildings went up as well. A new dwelling for the assistant keeper was built in 1899 with a $3,500 appropriation.[^1] A 1907 Light List describes a conch shell sounded in answer to signals from passing vessels, and the signal was replaced on 18 February 1916 by a bell struck by machinery.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1916 reports that the fog signal was changed "from hand horn to bell operated by machinery," which differs from the conch shell.[^7] In 1915 an acetylene light on a concrete pile structure formed range lights with the lighthouse.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive gives Sherrod Edwards as the first keeper, serving from 1847 to 1850.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he left soon after the 1848 hurricane: "Not too long thereafter, Keeper Edwards rowed his family ashore and resigned."[^1] The two sources disagree on how long he stayed.

In February 1852 Keeper John F. Hagan reported a rare occurrence: "We have had ice here three-eighths of an inch thick, and the fish have been so chilled as to be unable to navigate."[^1] The archive lists Hagan from 1851 to 1853.[^3]

The archive lists William T. Coons as head keeper in two stints, 1866 to 1871 and 1871 to 1876. LighthouseFriends gives one span, 1866 to 1876.[^3][^1] The roster also lists Caroline Coons as a first assistant from 1873 to 1876.[^3] The sources do not say how she was related to the keeper, and they do not say she kept the light by herself.

Charles Moore served as first assistant from 1877 to 1878 and then as head keeper from 1878 to 1910.[^3] George H. Gibson was head keeper from 1911 to 1922.[^3] The Service's Bulletin index for 1912 to 1917 says "Gibson, George H., keeper Egmont Key light station, efficiency pennant received."[^10] Cody W. McKeithen served from 1927 to 1942.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A hurricane covered Egmont Key with several feet of water on 23 September 1848, LighthouseFriends reports. "According to local legend," the keeper and his family rode out the storm in a small boat tied to a palmetto tree.[^1] The Fort De Soto History article calls it the "Great Hurricane of 1848," which "struck Tampa Bay."[^2] LighthouseFriends also says that the tower was later struck by lightning, which "opened cracks in the tower." The account does not give a date.[^1]

The Civil War reached the island in 1861.[^5] The *Daily Dispatch* of Richmond, reprinting a Tampa paper, reported in July that the steamer *Cuyler* and the cutter *John Appleton* had been "cruising about Egmont Key, at the entrance to Tampa Bay."[^5] The Tampa *Peninsular* of 20 July reported that the blockading *Cuyler* had landed thirty or forty men with three eighteen-pounder guns, who had built a battery on the island. The *New York Herald* reprinted that report under the headline "EGMONT KEY OCCUPIED BY A FEDERAL FORCE."[^6] In September the *Daily Richmond Whig* reported that local guard companies had gone to the island and "removed the lamps, oil" from the lighthouse, bringing them to Tampa. The paper added: "Three lamps will never again give light to the benighted followers of King Abe."[^4]

LighthouseFriends tells the story differently. It names keeper George V. Rickard, who it says feigned loyalty to the Union blockaders, crated up the Fresnel lens, and fled to Tampa with it. In its account the lighthouse "soon fell under Union control and was reactivated using a makeshift light."[^1] The Fort De Soto History article says the Confederates took the lamp and oil and hid the lens in Tampa, where it was not found until after the war. In its account "The light stayed dark for years, and didn't resume regular operation until 1866."[^2] The Richmond paper confirms the lamps and oil. It does not mention the lens or name the keeper.[^4]

On 25 October 1921 a hurricane damaged aids to navigation on the Gulf coast of Florida, "from Cape Romano to Cedar Keys," according to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin.[^8] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year 1922 (July 1921 to June 1922) refers to aids "damaged or destroyed in the storm of October 21-26, 1921." It asks for more money to repair and rebuild structures and make needed improvements at the Egmont Key depot, and at light stations that were damaged in the storm or are "seriously deteriorated from age." It lists repairs at the Egmont Key light station, but it does not say which of those repairs were storm damage.[^9]

## After the keepers

In 1944 the upper portion of the lighthouse was removed along with the Fresnel lens, and a rotating beacon was placed on the capped tower.[^1] Fort De Soto says the original lantern room was removed that year.[^2] The remaining keeper's dwelling was demolished in 1954 and replaced by a one-story barracks.[^1]

In 1974 Egmont Key became a National Wildlife Refuge, managed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and in 1978 the island was added to the National Register of Historic Places, because of the lighthouse and the remains of Fort Dade.[^1]

The date of automation is in dispute. LighthouseFriends says "When automated in 1989," while Fort De Soto says the Coast Guard "continued to staff the light by hand until 1990."[^1][^2] Fort De Soto reports that in 2000 "sand had to be pumped onto the beach in front of the tower" to hold back the water, and that the Coast Guard has proposed deactivating the light and has "declared it surplus property."[^2] LighthouseFriends says a modern LED beacon replaced the DCB-24 beacon atop the tower in 2018 or 2019.[^1]

## Today

LighthouseFriends describes what remains on the island: "a truncated lighthouse, crumbling remains of the fort, a small colony of gopher tortoises, and a park ranger to interpret the island's history."[^1] The archive records the station as open to the public.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, Egmont Key State Park manages it, and the grounds are open while the tower is closed. It says ferry service is provided by Hubbard's Marina from Fort DeSoto County Park.[^1] Fort De Soto says the light still burns, "automated, unmanned, but lit."[^2] Do not climb the tower.
