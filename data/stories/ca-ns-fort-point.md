Fort Point Lighthouse stands at the end of Main Street in Liverpool, Nova Scotia, on a point on the shore of Liverpool Harbour. Its square wooden tower was first lit on 1 January 1856, was taken out of service in 1989, and now houses a museum in a municipal park. It is one of the oldest surviving lights in the province.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The point had been a defended place before it had a light. The register says it held a signal station in the eighteenth century, and that during the American Revolution an earthwork and palisade with cannon were built there to protect the harbour from privateers.[^1] The preservation society says the battery on the point protected the town from the 1760s to the 1860s.[^2]

Coffin Island, which can be seen from the park, had carried the first light for the Liverpool area since 1815, at the entrance to Liverpool Bay.[^2][^4] By the 1850s the booming timber trade was pressing for a harbour that was easier and safer to enter by day or night. In 1855 a petition from "merchants, shipmasters and other inhabitants of Queens County" persuaded the Nova Scotia legislature to build a harbour light.[^2][^4]

The register says the decision to build followed two losses: the keeper of the nearby Coffin Island light drowned in a storm, and the brig *Wave* was wrecked on Fort Point. It gives no dates for either event.[^1]

## Building the light

The first light was a small square wooden tower with a pyramidal roof, 5.2 m (17 ft) high by the register's measure.[^1] The lantern held two catoptric lamps with reflectors and one Argand lamp without a reflector.[^4] LighthouseFriends describes the original building as only the small square front part of the present structure.[^4]

The sources disagree about the year of the first light. The register gives 1 January 1856 as the day the light was first lit.[^1] The society's data table gives 1855 as the year it was lit.[^2]

The sources disagree about the colour. The register and LighthouseFriends describe a fixed white light from the start. The Region of Queens says the light was red until 1926, when it became flashing white.[^1][^3][^4] The society's data table gives flashing white from 1950.[^2]

The sources also disagree about the range. The preservation society gives the range as eight miles. The Region gives seven miles from an elevated height of 12 m (39 ft), and says the range had grown to eleven miles by 1981.[^2][^3] Neither source says whether the miles are nautical.

The Department of Marine and Fisheries listed a separate light in its report for the year ended 30 June 1910: an electric light on a dolphin at Barrel Rock Bar, 201 m (660 ft) from the Fort Point light.[^8] In its report for the year ended 30 June 1924, the Department listed repairs to the fence and breakwater at Fort point.[^9]

## The keepers

Samuel T. N. Sellon kept the light from 1855 to 1888.[^4] LighthouseFriends says he earned fifty pounds a year with ten pounds for fuel, and that the cramped quarters prompted him to live in town. The society says the first keeper soon moved out to live in town as well.[^2][^4]

Archibald J. Campbell followed from 1888 to 1896, and John E. Misener from 1896 to 1936.[^4] The Marine Department's keeper tables list "Misner, John E" against Fort Point in the report for the year ended 30 June 1903, and give his appointment as 16 May 1896 in the report for the year ended 30 June 1909.[^6][^7] LighthouseFriends says Misener served longer than any other keeper of the station.[^4]

Misener kept the light when a kitchen was built onto its side around the turn of the century.[^2][^4] In 1901 he was given a hand foghorn to answer vessels' fog signals, and his pay was raised for the extra work.[^4] The Marine Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1901 says hand fog horns had been supplied at "Fort point, Liverpool" to answer fog signals.[^5]

James H. McLeod kept the light from 1936 to 1951.[^4] The society calls him the last lightkeeper, and quotes his 1944 letter, in which the family of five had been living in "a small kitchen for the past four years" after the tower room was given to weather forecasters.[^2]

The sources do not name a woman keeper at Fort Point. In 1859 or 1860, depending on the source, a lighthouse official recommended a board fence for "the protection of the females who attend the light".[^2][^4] LighthouseFriends suggests that a keeper's wife or daughters must have helped with the light, but no source names them.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The register gives the wreck of the brig *Wave* on Fort Point as one of the reasons for the light, but no date.[^1] The keeper's drowning at Coffin Island is undated too.[^1]

In 1870 a review of Nova Scotia lights described Fort Point as "of the greatest importance to the trade of the port".[^2] The society also says the station signalled approaching bad weather for shipping until the 1950s, by hoisting drums and cones on a mast.[^2]

During World War II, weather forecasters took over a room in the tower, and the McLeod family's living space was cut back.[^2][^4]

## After the keepers

According to the society, the light was electrified and automated in 1951, and LighthouseFriends gives the same year for electrification.[^2][^4] The society's data table says it was automated by 1989.[^2] The Region of Queens gives 1964 as the year the keeper's services were no longer needed.[^3] The sources disagree on the date of the change.

In 1946 federal plans to demolish the old light were stopped after the Town of Liverpool raised concerns. The town helped maintain it from the 1950s, and by 1970 it had taken possession of the light and the land around it.[^2][^4]

The light was discontinued in 1989. The society says it was shut down early that year, after radar and depth-finders made small harbour lights less useful.[^1][^2][^4]

The register says a park was created at the point in the 1930s, with a monument to privateers.[^1] The society says the point had become a park in the late 1800s.[^2] The interpretive museum park opened in September 1997.[^2][^4]

## Today

The lighthouse stands at 21 Fort Lane, at the end of Main Street. The society says the Region of Queens Municipality owns it, and the Region says it has been caring for the building since 1970. LighthouseFriends says the community of Liverpool owns it.[^2][^3][^4] The Queens County Museum runs it as a museum and gift shop.[^2][^3]

LighthouseFriends lists summer opening hours, from 9:30 to 5:30 daily from June 1 to September 1, with weekend hours after Labour Day until Thanksgiving, and says visitors can climb to the light. The register says the lighthouse is open seasonally. Check the hours before going.[^1][^4]
