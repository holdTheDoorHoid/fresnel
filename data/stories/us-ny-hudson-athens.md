The Hudson-Athens Lighthouse is a red-brick house on a granite caisson, standing in the Hudson River between the City of Hudson and the Village of Athens.[^1][^2][^6] It was first lit on 1 November 1874 to mark a shoal called Middle Ground Flats. It still acts as a navigation aid, although the structure is now endangered.[^1][^2][^4][^6]

## The place

LighthouseFriends describes the hazard as a mud flat, called Middle Ground Flats, that was completely submerged at high tide.[^1] Ships often found themselves unexpectedly stranded on it, and a call for a lighthouse to mark it was made in 1838, although the matter was not given high priority.[^1] Whalers from Nantucket and Martha's Vineyard moved their business inland, up the Hudson, and chose a site on the river for a new home.[^1] The City of Hudson was chartered in 1798, and Athens was incorporated across the river in 1805.[^1]

The shoal has changed since then. The Times Union says the lighthouse was built to warn ships off a tidal sandbar, a job it still does.[^4] The bar has since been raised permanently above the waterline as the river bottom was dredged for larger ships, and the spoil went onto the bar, which is now called Hudson Middle Ground Flats.[^4]

## Building the light

On 10 June 1872 Congress appropriated $35,000 for a lighthouse "on the Middle Ground, in the Hudson River, near the city of Hudson," LighthouseFriends says.[^1] Work on the structure, officially the Hudson City Lighthouse, began in 1873. LighthouseFriends says pilings were driven fifty feet (15 m) into the riverbed and capped with a limestone pier, and that the north end of the pier was shaped like a ship's prow against winter and spring ice floes.[^1] The Times Union describes the base differently, as 200 wood pilings driven 50 feet (15 m) into the riverbed.[^4]

The National Register record names two firms, Powley & Gage and Noone & Madden, in its architect field.[^3] LighthouseFriends says a two-story, Second Empire brick structure, similar to Stepping Stones Lighthouse, was built under a separate contract on top of the granite foundation.[^1]

Henry D. Best, the first of nine keepers, lit the beacon on 1 November 1874.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends describes a sixth-order Fresnel lens showing a fixed white light until 1926, when a fifth-order lens and a white flash every two seconds replaced it. The Lighthouse Society's record gives a fifth-order Fresnel lens as the original optic, so the sources disagree on the first lens.[^1][^2] A fog bell struck by a Gamewell apparatus began working in July 1894. LighthouseFriends dates it to 15 July, but the Light-House Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1895 gives 15 July in one place and 16 July in another.[^1][^9]

## The keepers

Henry D. Best kept the light from 1874 until 1892. LighthouseFriends says he made several rescues during his service, and that after his death his son Frank took over.[^1] The archive dates Frank Best's service from 1893 to 1918, not from 1892.[^2] The Light-House Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1899 records that a boat landing was built at the station and that "minor repairs were made."[^7]

In 1912 Frank Best heard a crash near the lighthouse. It was a collision between a tug and the passenger steamship *Isabella* of the Hudson-Catskill Line. He took the women passengers off the steamer in his rowboat, and then ran lines to tugs so they could pull the *Isabella* off the bar.[^1]

His daughter Bertha Munn launched the station's boat in 1918 and saved a man and a boy from drowning, LighthouseFriends says.[^1] LighthouseFriends also prints her memory of crossing the river on the ice as a child, when a flat-bottomed boat fitted with runners was pulled along with a pike pole. It credits the account to a 1960 Chatham Courier article.[^1]

When Frank Best died in 1918, his wife Nellie was offered the post. She resigned that September, and William J. Murray came from Little Gull Island Lighthouse to take charge.[^1] The archive nonetheless lists Nellie Best as principal keeper for 1918, and the sources do not explain the difference.[^2] Murray and his wife had three children when they moved in. LighthouseFriends prints Murray's account of a night when a fourth child was nearly born at the station. He rowed his wife ashore, then used a mail wagon to reach the hospital, something LighthouseFriends says he knew was illegal.[^1] The archive lists Murray at Hudson City from 1918 to 1920, and at Little Gull Island from 1909 to 1918.[^2][^8]

August Kjelberg kept the light from 1920 to 1930, according to the archive.[^2]

Emil J. Brunner kept the light from 1930 to 1949.[^2] LighthouseFriends says his family lived at the lighthouse for the first seven years, after which they moved to a house in Athens and he rowed out to tend the light.[^1] In September 1935, according to LighthouseFriends, which cites a New York Post article of 15 December 1935, a jar of peaches exploded as Mrs. Brunner opened it. Glass from the jar went into her left eye, and the eye had to be removed.[^1] In July 1946 the Saturday Evening Post artist Mead Schaeffer had the Brunner family pose at the lighthouse for a Christmas-week cover.[^1] Brunner, LighthouseFriends says, was the last civilian keeper at the lighthouse, and he retired in 1949.[^1]

The archive also lists three more men after automation, all under the same heading, "Principal Lighthouse Keeper": G. E. Speaks (1949–1957), Perry Peloubet (1957–1966) and William Nestlen (1966–1986).[^2] The sources read do not say what their work at the light was.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources record three such events at the station: the 1912 collision, in which Frank Best helped the passengers of the *Isabella*; Bertha Munn's rescue of a man and a boy in 1918; and the barge strike of 2025.[^1][^5]

On 30 April 2025 a barge hit the lighthouse, and the National Trust says it destroyed the dock and stairway.[^5] A November 2025 engineering report, commissioned by the owners, found further damage: the impact pushed some of the base's stones in and rotated others.[^5]

## After the keepers

The lighthouse was automated in 1949.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard then planned to demolish it and put a pole light in its place, but the Hudson River Valley Commission stepped in and recommended a recreational park instead.[^1] The Hudson-Athens Lighthouse Preservation Society (HALPS) was formed in 1982, and the Coast Guard leased the lighthouse to it in 1984.[^1][^4] On 3 July 2000 the title passed to HALPS, LighthouseFriends says.[^1]

Little was done to preserve the lighthouse until 1982.[^1] In 2003 the foundation was found to be sagging. Repairs in 2008 included an underwater inspection and the repair of thirty-seven timber piles.[^1] In 2019 the submarine cable that supplied the lighthouse's interior power failed, and a solar array was installed instead.[^1]

An engineering study completed in 2022 warned that the lighthouse might collapse into the river within five years, the Times Union reported in June 2023.[^4] The Times Union says the society had begun repairs with a $500,000 state grant, which would only pay to stabilize the structure. LighthouseFriends says the state parks agency granted $500,000 in January 2022 to replace the roof.[^4][^1] The Times Union gives the full project an estimated cost of $7.5 million.[^4] In 2024 the National Trust for Historic Preservation named the lighthouse to its list of America's 11 Most Endangered Historic Places.[^5] In December 2024 Senator Chuck Schumer joined HALPS in announcing that the lighthouse had been selected for the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers protection program. LighthouseFriends says the Corps allocated $50,000 for a feasibility study.[^1]

The National Trust gives the repair estimate after the 2025 strike as $1.9 million to $3.6 million.[^5] The National Register asset record is dated 29 May 1979, and places the lighthouse in the Hudson River Lighthouses multiple listing.[^3]

## Today

The National Trust describes HALPS as the lighthouse owners.[^5] HALPS describes itself as a not-for-profit and as a museum chartered in New York State.[^6] Its work includes tours for schools, community organizations, the general public and special events.[^6] A Fresnel lens used in the lighthouse is now in the possession of the South Street Seaport Museum in New York City, LighthouseFriends says.[^1]

HALPS's homepage describes the base as the focus of restoration efforts, after a report warned that the lighthouse could collapse.[^6]
