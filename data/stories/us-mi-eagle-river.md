Eagle River Light stands at the mouth of the Eagle River, on the south shore of Lake Superior in the Keweenaw Peninsula.[^1] Its first tower was lit in 1858, the light was discontinued in 1908 and sold at auction the same year, and the station is now a private vacation rental with its grounds closed to visitors.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Copper made Eagle River. The Cliff Mine opened near the river in 1845, and docks and warehouses went up along the river for shipping the copper.[^1] Congress made money available for a light at the river mouth in 1850. LighthouseFriends gives the sum as $4,000 for a light "at or near the mouth of Eagle River," and Terry Pepper's page gives $6,500.[^1][^2] The Michigan Legislature ceded the site in January 1853.[^1]

The boom did not last. Terry Pepper says the Cliff Mine closed in 1873, and the river mouth silted until the harbor was open only to the shallowest vessels.[^2] The Lighthouse Board's 1901 report describes a rocky reef about half a mile offshore, running for several miles along the coast.[^7]

## Building the light

A contract for the station went to Alanson Sweet, Luzerne Ransom and Morgan E. Shinn in July 1854.[^1] The sources disagree on the dates. LighthouseFriends says the station was completed in 1855, that the superintending engineer rejected the work, and that it was lighted later in 1858. Pepper says construction was not finished until 1857.[^1][^2] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record gives 1858 as the year the station was established.[^3]

The first tower's description also varies. LighthouseFriends describes a nineteen-foot (5.8 m) stone tower with a sixth-order Fresnel lens. Pepper describes a twelve-foot-square (3.7 m) brick tower that stood twenty-four feet (7.3 m) tall, in a one-and-a-half-story yellow brick dwelling, with its light visible ten nautical miles.[^1][^2] In 1867 large cracks opened at the base of the tower.[^2] LighthouseFriends quotes the 1868 Board report as saying that the corner of the dwelling supporting the tower was in danger of falling.[^1]

Congress set aside $14,000 for a new light in 1869. Pepper says the appropriation was recalled and the project canceled in July 1870. LighthouseFriends says the money reverted to the Treasury and was provided again in 1871.[^1][^2] The second tower was a square tower built into the northeast corner of the dwelling, and the sixth-order lens was moved into it.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls that dwelling brick. The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record gives the tower and keeper's quarters as stone and dates the tower to 1874.[^1][^3] In 1884 the walls were rebuilt and the dwelling renovated.[^2]

## The keepers

John Griswold, the first keeper, came from Eagle Harbor, where he had kept the light for ten years. He died in 1861 at the age of fifty-one, and his wife Julia took charge of the light and served for four years.[^1][^3] Julia A. Griswold is the only woman on the station's roster, which lists her as principal keeper.[^3]

Henry F. Feiser, who was appointed in August 1865 after his Civil War service, had been born in Germany in 1838 and had begun work as an engineer at the Cliff Mine. He joined the Twenty-Seventh Michigan Volunteers as a sergeant in 1862, and a gunshot wound in 1864 led to the loss of an arm.[^1] He kept the light until his death in 1889.[^1][^3]

Thomas J. Bennetts succeeded him and served until 1908. His brother Noah was in charge of the Portage Lake Ship Canal Lighthouse at the same time.[^1] In 1908 Bennetts was moved to Manitou Island, where he and his brother had earlier served as assistants. After five years there he spent fourteen years looking after Eagle Harbor Light, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Board wanted the light moved. Its 1892 report says the light at Eagle River "has outlived its usefulness" and proposes moving it to Sand Hills, which the report places twelve miles west of Eagle River, "where it would be of great service."[^4] The same passage also gives Sand Hills as seven miles southwest of Eagle River, and Pepper says it is seven miles west.[^2][^4] In 1893 Congress authorized the move at a cost not to exceed $20,000.[^5] The Board's reports for the late 1890s still said no money had been appropriated.[^6] The 1901 report says the river mouth was "blocked up with sand," that the old dock was now rotten, and that the dwelling was "in a far advanced state of decay."[^7]

The light was discontinued in 1908. LighthouseFriends and Pepper give that year, and the U.S. Lighthouse Society's record gives 1909.[^1][^2][^3] Major Charles Keeler of the Eleventh Lighthouse District decided to sell the property at public auction, according to Pepper.[^2] On 2 August 1908 John Vertin of Calumet bought it with a winning bid of $925.[^1][^2] A lighthouse was not built at Sand Hills until 1919, and William R. Bennetts, Thomas's younger brother, was its first keeper.[^1]

The Vertin family held the light until about 1996, and later private owners followed.[^1] The most recent owner, who bought it in 2014, restored it and offered it as a vacation rental.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is privately owned, and its grounds, dwelling and tower are closed to visitors.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record lists it as not open to the public.[^3] It is now available as a vacation rental.[^1] Terry Pepper reports that a four-unit condominium has been built on the strip of land between the bluff and the lakeshore, which he calls a modern intrusion on the station's view of the lake.[^2]
