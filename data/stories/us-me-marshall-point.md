Marshall Point Light stands on a rock outcrop at the eastern entrance to Port Clyde Harbor, in St. George, Maine.[^1] The station was established in 1832, and the round granite tower there now dates from 1857.[^1][^3] The light was automated in 1971.[^3] A committee of the St. George Historical Society runs the site, which is open to visitors.[^3][^10][^11]

## The place

Port Clyde was a working harbor.[^1] The National Register nomination says its economy rested on fishing, on shipbuilding from the 1830s, on an ice business and a guano and oil company from the mid-1870s, and on a canned lobster plant from the 1880s. All of these relied on water transport, and the nomination ties that transport to the light.[^1]

The tower stands apart from the mainland.[^1] A four-span wooden walkway, carried on three granite piers, joins it to the shore.[^1]

## Building the light

The station was established in 1832.[^1] The nomination says the original dwelling was of stone, as Light-House Board reports describe it, but the shape and location of the first tower are not known.[^1] The first keeper on the roster is John Watts, who served from 1832 to 1835.[^3]

The present tower was built in 1857 to replace the first one.[^1] It is a round granite tower, 24 feet (7.3 m) from its base to the focal plane of the original lens, with a polygonal lantern and an iron parapet.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives the tower's construction as granite with brick above it, a fifth-order Fresnel lens installed in 1857, and the light 31 feet (9.4 m) above mean high water.[^3]

The keeper's dwelling was rebuilt in the 1890s.[^5] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1896 says the old stone house was demolished and a new wooden dwelling "was built on" its foundation.[^5] The National Register puts the keeper's house at 1896, and the Society's record gives 1895.[^1][^3] The sources do not agree on the year, and the story does not choose between them.

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 records that a fog bell, "struck by machinery," was established at the station on 10 August 1898.[^6]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists 23 keepers' stints, from John Watts in 1832 to William J. Boddy, whose stint ended in 1971, the year the light was automated.[^3] Oren Prescott served twice, first from 1853 to 1857 and again from 1861 to 1868.[^3]

Charles C. Skinner's stint was the longest on the roster. He was keeper from 1874 to 1919.[^3] A 1932 Lighthouse Service Bulletin notice of his death, at Warren, Maine, at the age of 89, says he entered the service at Marshall Point on 1 July 1874 and "served continuously over 44 years in this position," retiring on 15 January 1919. It adds that he had served in the Union forces in the Civil War.[^9][^3]

In 1921 the Kennebec River's Doubling Point range lights lost their keeper to Marshall Point. The *Daily Kennebec Journal* reported that Edward H. Pierce, who had been keeper of the Doubling Point range lights for 23 years, was transferred to "the light, fog and weather signal station at Marshall Point, near Port Clyde," and that he had entered the service in 1891.[^7] The roster lists Pierce at Marshall Point from 1921 to 1933.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In June 1895 lightning struck the dwelling.[^4] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1895 says that it was "struck by lightning in June and badly damaged, and a contract has been entered into for rebuilding it."[^4] The 1896 report describes a new wooden dwelling built on the old foundation, most likely that rebuilding.[^5]

The *Lighthouse Service Bulletin* for 1929 reports that Pierce, then keeper, "rendered timely assistance" on 6 July to a yacht in distress in the vicinity of the station during a very thick fog.[^8]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1971, according to the US Lighthouse Society's record.[^3] The National Park Service lists the station in the National Register with a publication date of 23 March 1988.[^2] Its current optic is a VLB-44 LED light, installed in 2016.[^3]

## Today

The grounds are open to visitors year-round, from dawn to dusk, and the lighthouse committee's website says the lighthouse remains free to visit. Its museum, housed in a replica kitchen built in 1995, closed for the 2026 season.[^10][^3] The 2026 fundraiser made possible a refurbished tower, a painted and repaired keeper's house, and a fresh coat of paint on the walkway.[^10] The walkway has been repaired.[^11]
