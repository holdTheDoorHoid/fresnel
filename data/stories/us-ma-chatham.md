Chatham Light stands on a bluff overlooking Chatham Harbor, at the "elbow" of Cape Cod, where the Cape turns from Nantucket Sound toward the open Atlantic.[^1][^2] It began in 1808 as a pair of wooden towers, the Twin Lights, which were set on skids so they could be repositioned for the shifting sand bars.[^1][^3] The tower standing now is an 1877 cast-iron tower. Its twin was moved to Nauset Beach in 1923.[^1][^2]

## The place

A marker at the site calls the waters off Chatham dangerous because of shoals and currents.[^3] The National Register nomination says the light warns shipping of Monomoy Point and the Pollacks Rip shoals.[^2] Congress made its first appropriation for a Chatham light in 1806, and the building was finished in 1808.[^3]

The first towers were wood, because the contractor could not find stone on the Cape.[^3] Placed 70 feet (21 m) apart on movable skids, they could be repositioned for the shifting sand bars, and together they gave ships a range.[^1][^3]

## Building the light

Samuel Nye was appointed keeper by President Thomas Jefferson on 7 October 1808, after petitions in his favor.[^1] In 1841 the wooden towers, "entirely unfit for use," were torn down and rebuilt in brick. LighthouseFriends says Winslow Lewis built them for $6,750; the marker says the Treasury Department appropriated $6,750 for the two brick towers.[^1][^3] Fourth-order Fresnel lenses burning lard oil were installed in 1857.[^1][^3]

The bluff was the problem. The marker says Keeper Josiah Hardy reported serious erosion in 1875, and that by 1877 the towers were only 48 feet (15 m) from the edge.[^3] Hardy's own log gives the distance from the south tower to the bluff as 190 feet (58 m) in December 1874, 126 feet (38 m) in September 1876, and 95 feet (29 m) in February 1877.[^1]

The authorities approved a new station across the road quickly.[^1] Two cast-iron towers, lined with brick, were built, and the lenses were moved into them on 6 September 1877 and lit that night.[^1][^3]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Service roster names Samuel Nye, Joseph Loveland and Samuel Stinson as the first three keepers, from 1808 to 1841.[^6] LighthouseFriends says Stinson resigned in 1839 after an inspector charged him with dereliction of duty, and its keeper list gives Lot Norton for 1839 to 1841. The roster keeps Stinson on until 1841 and does not name Norton.[^1][^6]

Collins Howes, a fisherman who had lost a leg, took the post in 1841 at $400 a year.[^1] A marker says he complained that the construction was inferior and that rats got into the cellar.[^3] He lost the post in 1845, when Simeon Nickerson was appointed.[^1] Nickerson died in October 1848, and his widow, Angeline M. Nickerson, kept the light until 1862 while she raised four young children there.[^1][^6]

The marker calls Josiah Hardy the most notable keeper. It gives his service as 1872 to 1900, while the roster and LighthouseFriends end it in 1899.[^1][^3][^6] LighthouseFriends says he kept a daily log that recorded the erosion of the bluff.[^1] The marker recalls that in the 1880s he told two boys playing by the lights that he had seen "as many ships as there are days in the year."[^3]

The keepers who followed were Charles H. Hammond (1899 to 1907), James T. Allison (1907 to 1927), George F. Woodman Jr. (1928 to 1940) and George T. Gustavus (1940 to 1945).[^6] LighthouseFriends says Woodman received superintendent's efficiency stars, and that he received 1,500 visitors between mid-July and mid-September 1936.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Nearby, the Life-Saving Service moved its Station No. 13 in 1880 from south of the town to a point about two miles (3 km) north of the Chatham lights. The report gives encroachments of the sea as the reason.[^4]

The old brick south tower fell to the beach on 15 December 1878, according to LighthouseFriends. The marker gives 15 December 1879.[^1][^3] The north tower followed about fifteen months later, and LighthouseFriends adds that the 1841 keeper's house went too.[^1][^3]

On 18 February 1952 the tanker Pendelton broke in two during a violent snowstorm. The marker says Station Chatham, whose headquarters is in the light complex, made the daring rescue.[^3]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1932 says Cape Ann was then the last twin light in the United States, and gives the years when the duplicate lights were discontinued. Its list includes "Chatham, Mass., 1923."[^5]

In May 1923 the government moved the northern tower to Nauset Beach, where it became Nauset Light. The marker places the move at Eastham.[^1][^2][^3] The remaining south tower received a fourth-order lens with a clockwork drive.[^1] An electric motor replaced the clockwork in 1939.[^1][^3]

In 1969 the old lens and lantern room were removed and replaced by a larger lantern with a modern aerobeacon.[^1][^3] The old lens is now displayed at the Atwood House Museum.[^1]

LighthouseFriends dates automation to 1982. The marker dates the present lamp to 1994, while LighthouseFriends dates the new aerobeacons to 1993.[^1][^3]

The National Register listed the Chatham Light Station on 15 June 1987.[^2]

## Today

The marker says the light complex also serves as headquarters for Coast Guard Station Chatham, and that the present light shows two white flashes every 10 seconds, visible 24 nautical miles at sea.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is owned by the Coast Guard, and that the grounds and tower open during scheduled tours offered by the local Coast Guard Auxiliary, while the dwelling stays closed.[^1] Treat the rest of the complex as working Coast Guard property, and keep out of it unless you are on a tour. The old lens is at the Atwood House Museum.[^1]
