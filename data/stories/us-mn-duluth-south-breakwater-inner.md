The Duluth South Breakwater Inner Light stands at the inner end of the south breakwater of the Duluth ship canal, on Lake Superior. With the outer light on the same breakwater, it forms a range. Keeping the two lights one above the other gives a ship a direct course to the opening between the piers.[^1][^3]

## The place

The gap was narrow. Terry Pepper writes that with only 300 feet (91 m) between the north pier and the south breakwater, identifying the correct entry course was "somewhat risky."[^3] The outer light had been commissioned in June 1874.[^2]

In 1880 the Lighthouse Board recommended $2,000 for a light on the inner end of the same breakwater. Congress made the appropriation on 2 March 1889.[^3]

## Building the light

The first rear light was a timber pyramid with a frame watch room and an octagonal cast-iron lantern.[^3] LighthouseFriends describes it as a four-story wooden pyramid topped by a lantern room, built in 1889 for $2,264.58.[^2] It was first shown on the night of 1 September 1889.[^2][^3]

The two sources describe the first optic differently. LighthouseFriends gives a fourth-order Barbier & Fenestre lens with ten flash panels, giving a red flash every six seconds.[^2] Pepper describes a red fourth-order Fresnel lens with a single flash every six seconds.[^3]

The Lighthouse Board's report for the year to June 1894 lists a rear-range item: "A brick oil house was built and provided with a suitable shelving to store 360 gallons of oil."[^4]

The rear tower that stands today is a later building. The Historic American Engineering Record (HAER) form, inventoried in 1979, says "The present structure was begun in June 1900, completed in August 1901, and consists of a pyramidal steel skeletal frame supporting a round watchroom and an octagonal cast iron lantern."[^1] Pepper says that in the summer of 1901 "the iron work for the permanent tower was delivered," and that work began with the foundation blocks.[^3] The Board's report for the year to June 1901 already refers to "the iron beacon now being built at Duluth rear range light-station."[^5] The sources do not agree on when the work began.

The sources also disagree on height. The HAER form gives 67 feet (20.4 m) to the top of the ventilator ball, with the lens focal plane 68 feet (20.7 m) above mean low lake level.[^1] Pepper gives seventy feet (21.3 m) "from the base of the cylinder to the top of the ventilator ball."[^3]

The Board's report for the year to June 1901 records "transferring the rear beacon of the old Duluth range to Superior pierhead range light-station." The report does not say which tower that beacon came from.[^5]

The HAER form describes the lantern as housing a fourth-order Fresnel lens "made by Barbier and Benard of Paris in 1896 with six flash panels."[^1] The Coast Guard replaced that lens in 1995 (see below).[^3]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends says the station had a single keeper from 1873 until 1885, when an assistant was added to help with the newly commissioned fog whistle. A second assistant came in 1889, when the rear light went into operation.[^2]

The sources disagree about the keeper's house. LighthouseFriends dates a seven-room keeper's dwelling to 1873, and says the head keeper was living in the 1873 dwelling when the new lights were lit in 1901.[^2] Pepper calls the crew's only dwelling "the small frame house built for the keeper in 1874." Pepper says the two assistants had to rent their own dwellings in town, at $10 and $15 a month. The duplex built for them was not completed and occupied until February 1913.[^3]

The U.S. Lighthouse Society files these keepers under its place record for the Duluth Harbor North Breakwater, which is dated 1910 and does not say which Duluth light each man served.[^8] Its roster of principal keepers begins with Ernest R. Jefferson (1873 to 1888), then James Prior (1888 to 1908), Charles Lederle (1910 to 1919) and Edwin C. Bishop (1919 to 1934).[^8] LighthouseFriends prints the same head keepers for this light.[^2]

LighthouseFriends calls Edwin C. Bishop the longest-serving head keeper of the breakwater lights at Duluth. He began in 1897 as a third assistant at Stannard Rock Lighthouse, then took the same position at Spectacle Reef in 1899. In 1913 he became head keeper of Wisconsin Point Lighthouse. He was later transferred to Duluth, where he served until his retirement in 1934.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Pepper records one dated event at the rear light. On 17 September 1889, sixteen days after the light was first shown, the steamer India had problems making her way between the piers and smashed into the breakwater at the foot of the new light, damaging its foundation. Repairs were made quickly at the expense of the vessel's owners.[^3] The account rests on Pepper alone.

The sources give few other dated events for this light.

## After the keepers

By 1922 the rear light had changed its illuminant. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1918 to 1923 lists a "Duluth Range Rear light, change in illuminant."[^7] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' list of lights gives the rear light's illuminant as "electric incandescent from Incandescent oil vapor."[^6]

In 1995 an inspection found the fourth-order Fresnel lens "in dire need of repairs." The Coast Guard replaced the old lens with a modern acrylic optic and donated the Fresnel to the Maritime Museum. The lens was later restored.[^3]

In May 2007 the Coast Guard, which had deemed the inner light excess, offered it at no cost to eligible entities, including government agencies, non-profit corporations and educational organizations. No qualified group was found to take it. An online auction opened on 16 September 2008 and closed on 2 December 2008, with the lighthouse selling for $31,000 to private buyers.[^2]

## Today

The light is privately owned. Its grounds are open, but the tower is closed to visitors.[^2] The restored lens and its pedestal are on display in a museum gallery in Duluth.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the lens formerly used in the light is at the Lake Superior Marine Museum.[^2] The sources do not say whether the museum's optic is the 1896 lens described in the HAER form or an earlier one.[^1][^2]
