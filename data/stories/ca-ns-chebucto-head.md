Chebucto Head Light stands on the headland that forms the southwest side of the entrance to Halifax Harbour, reached by a road that turns off just before Ketch Harbour.[^1] A light has been shown there since 21 August 1872. The concrete octagonal tower standing now dates from 1967, and the Coast Guard list gives its light as one white flash every 20 seconds, visible 10 nautical miles.[^1][^3]

## The place

Lighthouse Friends says the headland rises to just over 32 metres and defines the southwest side of the harbour entrance.[^1] In 1890 William P. Anderson, an engineer of the Department of Marine, and William H. Smith, chairman of the Board of Examiners of Masters and Mates, were asked to recommend improvements to the approaches to Halifax. They proposed moving the steam fog whistle from Sambro Island to Chebucto Head. Sambro, they judged, was ringed by shoals that made the air uneven and so bad for carrying sound, while Chebucto Head "stands out boldly into the channel."[^1]

Lighthouse Friends also says the Mi'kmaq name for the headland, anglicised as Chebucto, meant "the big harbour."[^1]

## Building the light

The Department's report for 1872 noted the first lighthouse on the head: a square wooden tower about 6.9 m (22½ ft) high, painted white, with a keeper's dwelling attached to it.[^1] The contract went to Jacob Bowser for $2,375, and $1,435.69 was spent on the illuminating apparatus.[^1] The first keeper was appointed at $400 a year. Lighthouse Friends spells him Edwin Johnson in its quotation of the 1872 report and Edward Johnson in its keeper list.[^1]

The light stood on a rocky cliff with no protection from trees, fully exposed to the elements, and needed frequent repairs.[^1] Rock was blasted away for a landing slip and a road, and a capstan was set up to haul the station's boat.[^1] The road to a public road about 800 m (half a mile) away was not finished until 1883, and before then a footpath led to Duncan's Cove.[^1]

The fog alarm came in 1892. Dynamite was used to blast a foundation for a red-brick fog-alarm building and a reservoir capable of collecting 117,000 gallons of water, and a tramway carried coal from the landing.[^1] The whistle was placed in operation on 1 June 1892, giving a ten-second blast each minute during periods of limited visibility.[^1]

In 1906 a submarine bell was added. Cables ran from a wooden control building on shore to two tripods on the sea floor off the headland, and a ship with listening equipment could take the bearing of the sound. The Department's report for the year to June 1906 says that the "signal service off Chebucto head, Halifax harbour, has been established and is in operation."[^1][^4]

Lighthouse Friends dates the next tower to 1930. It says A. S. MacMillan of Halifax built a concrete octagonal lighthouse under a contract for $16,900, with two single dwellings.[^1] NSLPS says a concrete tower with a red lantern went up "in about 1928" and calls it the second lighthouse.[^2] Both say it was destroyed around 1940, when the site was needed for a gun battery.[^1][^2]

A square white two-storey dwelling with a red lantern room was built on the site in 1940, at a cost of $16,940. NSLPS calls it the third lighthouse and a cupola lighthouse, and says that in wartime a light at the head was needed to guide convoys in and out of Halifax Harbour.[^1][^2] A one-and-a-half-storey dwelling for an assistant keeper followed in 1949, and a new fog-alarm building in 1951.[^1] The current concrete octagonal tower was completed in 1967.[^1][^2][^3]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends gives the keepers in order: Edward Johnson (1872 to 1906), Richard Holland (1906 to 1929), Edward Gallagher (1929 to 1949) and Stanley Flemming (1949 to 1980).[^1] The Department's keepers' table for the year to June 1909 lists "Capt. Richard Holland" against Chebucto Head, with an appointment date of 1 October 1906.[^5]

Edward Gallagher was placed in charge in 1929, and he and Catherine moved into the dwelling that year.[^1] NSLPS's keeper list gives 1928 for both the end of Holland's stint and the start of Gallagher's.[^2] NSLPS has a photograph of keeper Edward Gallagher and his family in front of the lighthouse, which it uses to show that a second tower stood there.[^2]

Catherine Gallagher learned many folk songs from her mother, Lighthouse Friends says. In 1943 Dr. Helen Creighton visited her at the station and recorded a few sea songs and ballads. After Creighton publicised her singing, Catherine performed on the radio and gave concerts for several years.[^1] The Nova Scotia Archives holds a Creighton photograph captioned "Recording Mrs. Gallager at her lighthouse home, Chebucto Head," dated about 1949. The caption gives no first name, but it fits the account in Lighthouse Friends. The date differs from 1943, so the photograph may show a different session.[^6]

Stanley Flemming kept the light from 1949 until he retired in 1980, Lighthouse Friends says.[^1] NSLPS says the keepers who lived in the 1940 dwelling took part in automation programmes in the 1970s, which saw de-staffed lights such as Maughers Beach and Devils Island controlled remotely from Chebucto Head.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 24 August 1873 a gale toppled the boathouse's brick chimney and caused considerable damage to the roof.[^1]

A life-saving station stood at Duncan's Cove, near Chebucto Head. In the year to June 1900 the Department placed a Lyle gun and beach apparatus there, "near Chebucto Head," under the station's coxswain, John Holland.[^7] The sources do not connect him with the lightkeeper Richard Holland.

On 13 March 1912 the schooner Isleworth was reported ashore at Chebucto Head, in thick fog with a heavy sea running. The Duncan's Cove lifeboat put out, but found the crew already preparing to land in their own boat, and the lifeboat stood by until everyone was off. The Herring Cove boat was called out to the same vessel, but after pulling for some hours against a head sea it was forced to turn back.[^8]

On 15 March 1913 the Herring Cove boat was called out again, to "the wreck of the ss. Isleworth" at Chebucto Head. The report says a stiff gale was blowing with a heavy sea, and that after pulling for some hours the boat was forced to return to her station.[^9] The 1912 report calls the Isleworth a schooner and the 1913 report calls her a steamer. The sources do not say whether these were one vessel or two events.[^8][^9]

On 12 January 1913 the steamer Uranium stranded at Chebucto Head. The Department's account of its buoy steamer, the Lady Laurier, says she went to the Uranium's assistance, took 600 passengers from the stranded steamer, landed them at Halifax, and stood by the ship until the 16th.[^10] The Department's wreck list gives the stranding as 12 January and says the master was found guilty of negligence and his certificate was suspended for three months.[^11] The Duncan's Cove report gives its own call-out as 17 January, when the crew helped move passengers to tugs and carried lines to the steamer.[^9]

On 8 June 1916 the A. W. Perry, registered at Halifax, was stranded and lost on Chebucto Head. A formal investigation was held at Halifax on 17 June, before Captain L. A. Demers, the Dominion Wreck Commissioner.[^12]

Sound carried a long way from the station. In April 1910 the ship E.M.S. Victorian heard the Chebucto Head bells at 5.6 km (three and a half miles), the Department's report says.[^13]

## After the keepers

In the year to June 1922 the Department changed the light from four flashes in 30 seconds to four flashes in 20 seconds.[^14] The Coast Guard list now gives one white flash every 20 seconds, which is a different pattern, and the sources do not explain the change.[^3]

Lighthouse Friends says the station was automated not long after Flemming returned to work, and that two dwellings were removed in 1983 and 1984, leaving the 1940 dwelling standing.[^1] NSLPS says the station was once the traffic-monitoring centre for Halifax Harbour, and that the traffic control work later moved to Bedford Basin, where controllers watch the harbour by radar.[^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light. Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1] NSLPS says the road to the light is gated because of vandalism, so visitors park at the gate and walk in.[^2] Lighthouse Friends recorded a locked gate that meant a walk of the last 750 metres.[^1]

The 1940 dwelling burned in May 2004. Lighthouse Friends calls the fire suspicious, and NSLPS says it "is judged to have been suspicious but those responsible have never been found."[^1][^2] The Chebucto Head Lighthouse Society, formed in 2003 to preserve the buildings, could not raise enough money to rebuild the dwelling and gave up its plans.[^1]
