Long Point Light stands on the spit of sand at the tip of Cape Cod's arm, where it closes the south side of Provincetown Harbor. A light has been shown there since 1827, and the square brick tower that stands now dates from 1875.[^1][^2][^3] The grounds are open and the tower is closed, LighthouseFriends says.[^3]

## The place

By 1818 there was a salt works and a fishing settlement at Long Point, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] The light also warned of a sandbar that ran a quarter mile seaward from the tip of the point.[^3] The Lighthouse Board bought four acres there in 1826, according to the nomination.[^2]

The point kept wearing away.[^3] In 1869 it was suggested that "it may be necessary at no distant day to move the light further inland."[^3] By 1873, LighthouseFriends says, the foundation of the keeper's dwelling was exposed, and the building was resting on decayed piles.[^3]

## Building the light

A May 1826 Act of Congress provided $2,500, LighthouseFriends says, and four acres near the end of the point were purchased after it.[^3] The nomination puts the cost of the first tower at $15,000 in 1827.[^2] The $2,500 is LighthouseFriends' figure for the 1826 Act, and the sources read do not say whether the two sums cover the same work.

The first light was shown from a short, octagonal tower on the peaked roof of a wood-frame keeper's house.[^3] The house was built on pilings sunk eight feet (2.4 m) into the sand.[^3] LighthouseFriends calls the style "Cape Cod style."[^3] Lieutenant Edward W. Carpender of the Navy, writing in 1838, found it "an exceedingly useful light, but far larger than is necessary," as LighthouseFriends quotes him.[^3]

A fifth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps and reflectors in 1856, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] The Society's record gives a sixth-order Fresnel for that year.[^1] In 1874 $13,000 was approved for a new dwelling and tower, along with a fog signal.[^3] The square brick tower went up in 1875.[^2][^3] The nomination says the former keeper's dwelling was retained, while LighthouseFriends describes a one-and-a-half-story frame dwelling that was first attached to the new tower.[^2][^3] A fog-bell tower stood north of it, with a 1,200-pound bell struck by machinery.[^3] The tower is 38 feet (11.6 m) high.[^2]

The nomination thinks the brick oil house was probably built "the same year as the tower."[^2] LighthouseFriends says a brick oil house, "dating from 1904 remains standing."[^3]

## The keepers

The Society's roster lists 22 stints by 20 principal keepers at Long Point, from 1826 to at least 1950.[^1]

Charles Derby kept the first light.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he had come to Provincetown in 1798 as the four-year-old ward of the captain of the whaling ship Polly.[^3] The roster gives him 1826 to 1847 and again 1849 to 1853, with Elisha Freeman, Jr. in between.[^1] In 1842 Derby's boat was damaged in a storm, and he spent $25 of his $350 annual salary repairing it, LighthouseFriends reports.[^3]

John Thomas Dunham kept the light from 1870 to 1882.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he was a Provincetown native "fitted with a wooden leg as a result of a whaling accident."[^1][^3] In 1880 he was still bothered by "flies and stench coming from the nearby fish-oil works."[^3]

Samuel S. Smith kept the light from 1888 to 1904, and again from 1909 to 1919.[^1] In the Portland Gale of November 1898 he was ill. LighthouseFriends says he had been at the station since 1888 and was ill when the Portland Gale hit.[^3] He still had to wind the fog-bell mechanism every few hours, and a wave struck him in the chest.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the same 1899 Globe article described a second storm, in which the keeper was ill again and his wife would not let him leave the house at the worst point of the hurricane, when the fog bell stopped. She climbed a ladder outside the bell tower to oil the mechanism, and so the bell "could continue to sound."[^3]

Allison G. Haskins kept the light from 1927 to 1932.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls him "A.G. 'Ted' Haskins." His nine-year-old daughter rode a thirty-six-foot powerboat to school and back, a trip of more than a mile each day. A 1932 Boston Globe article, as LighthouseFriends gives it, noted that she "had not missed a single day of school" in over a year.[^3]

Theodore L. Chase kept the light from 1932 to 1942, according to the roster.[^1] In May 1933 the striking mechanism of the fog bell failed. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin names the keeper "Thomas L. Chase," and says he struck the thousand-pound fog bell by hand during two nights of fog.[^5] LighthouseFriends, which gives the name Theodore, says he pulled the bell by hand "for nine straight hours," first with his right hand; after a few hours' sleep he returned to the task with his left.[^3] The Bulletin's first name differs from the roster and LighthouseFriends. Its "thousand-pound" bell also differs from the 1,200 pounds LighthouseFriends gives for the bell in 1875, and the sources do not explain either difference.[^1][^3][^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The erosion of late 1898 and early 1899, LighthouseFriends says, "looked poised to transform Long Point into an island."[^3] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1900 includes Long Point in a list of stations where "Repairs more or less extensive were made," and gives no detail for it.[^4]

## After the keepers

The nomination notes a fourth-order Fresnel lens with a visibility of ten miles in 1926, and says that in 1927 the lens was reduced to 29,000 candlepower.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the fourth-order lens replaced the earlier one in 1927, and "was replaced by a modern optic" when the light was automated.[^3]

The light was automated in 1952, and the station was left unmanned.[^2] The roster's last keeper, William H. Swanson, is listed from at least 1950.[^1]

In 1979 the light was modernized and suggested for conversion to solar power.[^2] In 1982 solar arrays were installed on the lantern deck.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives 1981 for the panels.[^3] In 1986 the Coast Guard reconsidered plans to demolish the oil house.[^2] The keeper's dwelling and the fog-station building were removed long ago, LighthouseFriends says.[^3]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light, and the American Lighthouse Foundation maintains it, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] The Society's record gives the owner as the Coast Guard, licensed to the foundation, and lists the light as not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^3]

To reach the light, LighthouseFriends says, visitors can make "the strenuous, 2.7 mile walk to the lighthouse," or take a shuttle, or paddle a kayak to Long Point.[^3] The light is still active, "with a fixed-green light and an automated fog signal."[^3] The Society's record gives a solar-powered LED optic.[^1]
