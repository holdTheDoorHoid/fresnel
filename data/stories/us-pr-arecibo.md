The Faro de los Morrillos de Arecibo, known in English as the Arecibo Light, stands on Punta Morrillos, a promontory east of the town of Arecibo on Puerto Rico's north coast.[^1] The Spanish colonial government built it in 1897 and 1898, and it was finished only months before the island passed to the United States.[^1][^2] From seaward, the nomination says, the lighthouse "is projected against the Central Mountain Range."[^1] The light is 120 feet (37 m) above the sea.[^1][^3]

## The place

The nomination calls the port of Arecibo the second most important on the island's northern, Atlantic coast, after San Juan.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that between 1851 and 1858 around fifteen ships grounded or sank at the port for want of navigational aids.[^2] The nomination places the Arecibo light among a series of lights that Spanish colonial authorities built at the end of the 19th century, and says Arecibo was assigned one of the larger designs.[^1] The setting is rough. The nomination describes "rough coral formations reaching to the sea below."[^1]

## Building the light

The dwelling is a rectangle, 84 by 40 feet (25.6 by 12.3 m), and the nomination describes a hexagonal tower attached to its north face.[^1][^2] The tower and dwelling are of rubble masonry, brick and stone, with lime mortar. The nomination says they were painted white with lead-gray trim, while LighthouseFriends says red.[^1][^2] The floors of most rooms were laid with white and gray Genoa marble, and the keepers' rooms with wood.[^1]

The first lens was a third-order Fresnel lens with five panels, made in Paris. The nomination dates it to 1897, but the archive gives 1898 as the year the original lens was installed.[^1][^3] Its lantern was cast iron, copper and glass, and the lens sat on an iron pedestal.[^1]

The nomination says the dwelling was divided into two units, for a first-class and a third-class keeper, each with a living room, two bedrooms and a kitchen-dining room.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes two identical five-room apartments instead.[^2] A cistern beside the dwelling collected rain from the roof, and the oil room was in the basement under the tower.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists twelve principal keepers and twelve first assistants, from 1901 to 1964.[^3] The earliest entries are for 1901, when the roster lists "Eulalio Hernandez (at least 1901-)" as principal keeper and Rafael Velilla as first assistant.[^3]

Manuel Perez Castillo is the keeper the sources describe most fully. He was appointed first assistant in 1905 and became principal keeper in 1906, a post he held until 1913.[^3][^2] In 1912 the Bulletin's index credits "Arecibo light station keeper Manuel P. Castillo" with an efficiency pennant, and LighthouseFriends says it was for having the model station in the district.[^5][^2] An oil house of reinforced concrete was completed at the station on 6 January 1914.[^2]

Esteban Parra Furniz succeeded him in 1913. Pedro Albino Gonzalez served as first assistant in 1916 and again from 1922 to 1924, and as principal keeper in 1924.[^3] The last keepers on the roster are Gilberto Sanchez, from 1963 to 1964, and Angel Rodriguez before him.[^3] A National Archives photograph from 1900 is captioned, in LighthouseFriends, "Arecibo Lighthouse with goats and keepers in 1900."[^2] The sources name no woman keeper at this light.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Light-House Board's report for fiscal 1900 has a Porto Rico section, on printed pages 68 and 69. It says that each Porto Rican light "received minor repairs," and that the few damages the hurricane inflicted were repaired.[^4] A table of construction dates on page 69, under the same Third District heading, lists Arecibo, but its columns run together in the scanned text, so this story takes no date from it.[^4] No wreck or rescue tied to the light appears in the sources cited for this story.

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1924 to 1929 records that the Arecibo light station was changed to a flashing electric light.[^8] LighthouseFriends quotes the Bulletin's account of the change, which describes switching "from a fixed white oil vapor light to a flashing light," and using electricity in the lens partly so the dwelling could be lit from the same plant.[^2] The same account calls the station "located on a wind-swept point." It says the station "had never been inspected when the weather was found to be calm," and for that reason a wind-driven generator was proposed there "in the interests of economy."[^2]

The 1928 Bulletin dates the installation of the wind-driven plant at the station to September 1926.[^6] LighthouseFriends quotes the same article saying the plant "passed through various periods of success and failures," until a new propeller and a second battery were added.[^2] The Bulletin of 1938 says a wind generator was installed at Arecibo Lighthouse in 1930, and that it supplied 90 percent of the current required to run the station.[^7] The two dates disagree.

The nomination says the light's characteristic was changed in 1930. A year later the incandescent oil-vapor system was replaced by an electric lamp that was hooked to a windmill.[^1] In 1959 the keepers' rooms were remodeled with bathrooms, and the brick roof was replaced by a reinforced-concrete one.[^1][^2]

In 1964 the light was automated, and LighthouseFriends says the station was disestablished on 22 May 1964.[^1][^2] The nomination says the lighthouse was vandalized sixteen times between 29 October 1969 and 28 October 1977.[^1] The Fresnel lens was damaged in 1975, and in 1977 almost all of the lantern and the entire lens were destroyed.[^1] The nomination says the lantern had become "a target of sharpshooters for the last years," and that the interior was "vandalized with scratchings and painted commentary."[^1] Part of the old wooden door still hangs on its original hinges, and some marble slabs remain in the floor.[^1] The nomination was prepared in March 1977, when the community of Arecibo planned to restore the light as a center for educational and recreational activities.[^1]

The City of Arecibo began a restoration in 2001, and a year later a new lantern had been installed, LighthouseFriends says.[^2]

## Today

The U.S. Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the dwelling is a museum.[^2] The archive lists the light as an active aid to navigation, gives the current optic as "190 MM," and records the light as open to the public.[^3]

The lighthouse is now home to a small museum, and it anchors the Arecibo Lighthouse & Historical Park.[^2] The park also has a replica Taíno village, a Spanish ship, a pirate ship and a saltwater aquarium. Its website, in Spanish, calls the park a nonprofit organization and lists its hours and admission prices.[^9] Check the park's current hours and fees before a visit.
