The Duluth Harbor North Pier Light stands at the outer end of the north pier of the Duluth Ship Canal, about 1,000 feet (300 m) out into Lake Superior.[^1] It was first lit on 7 April 1910.[^1][^2] When the National Register nomination was written in 2016 the light was a Coast Guard aid to navigation; LighthouseFriends reports that in 2023 its ownership passed to Rethos, a St. Paul nonprofit.[^1][^2]

## The place

The Duluth Ship Canal was dug across Minnesota Point in 1870 and 1871, and it opened in April 1871.[^1] The piers were rebuilt in the years around 1900.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says the act of 3 June 1896 unified the harbours of Duluth and Superior and provided over $3 million for improvements, and that part of the money went to replacing the piers with timber and monolithic concrete structures.[^2] The National Register nomination dates the money to 1895, when Congress "appropriated three million dollars to improve the ports of Duluth and Superior."[^1] The two sources give different years, and this story does not choose between them.

The north pier was completed in 1901, and "Like its predecessor pier, it was not lighted."[^1][^2]

The approach was a hazard before the light existed. The National Register says the canal's narrow entrance "made its approach hazardous in stormy conditions or foggy weather," and that the Army Corps of Engineers "took over sole responsibility" for the canal in 1887.[^1] In 1908 the Lighthouse Board said that "The approach to Duluth Harbor is one of the worst and most dangerous on the whole chain of lakes," as LighthouseFriends quotes its report.[^2] In the same year the Lake Carriers' Association "arranged for private lights to be exhibited on the north pier in 1908."[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $4,000 on 4 March 1909 to light the north pier.[^1][^2] Work on the tower began in autumn 1909,[^1] and the light was first lit on 7 April 1910.[^1][^2]

The superstructure is a conical steel tower with a lantern, 37 feet (11.3 m) tall, on a foundation of a "rock-filled timber crib and concrete pier."[^1] The light carried a fifth-order Fresnel lens "manufactured in 1882 by Henri Lepaute of Paris," according to the nomination.[^1]

In 1912 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin described the light as "occulting, two seconds light and two seconds eclipse," shown from a fifth-order fixed lens and "supplied from the city electric lighting system."[^4] The Bulletin's 1915 list of lighting changes includes the North Pier light, converted from oil to electric incandescent.[^5]

The keepers' houses came later. The National Register says a frame dwelling of 1874 "was used as quarters for the chief lighthouse keeper," and that the assistant keepers "rented housing in the community from 1901 until 1913," when a brick duplex was built "for them and their families."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the head keeper lived in "a frame building that had been built in 1873," when the first light on the piers was established.[^2] The sources differ on that year.

## The keepers

The USLHS place record for the Duluth Harbor North Breakwater light lists 100 lines of keepers, assistants and Coast Guard crew from 1873 to 1970.[^3] The lines from 1873 to 1909 date from before the north pier light existed.[^3][^2] The National Register says the same keepers who maintained the south breakwater lights looked after the north pier light.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that when the light was built, three keepers were assigned to the range lights on the south pier as well as the pierhead light on the north pier.[^2]

Ernest R. Jefferson was head keeper from 1873 to 1888, and James Prior followed until 1908.[^3] Alexander Shaw rose through the ranks: second assistant from 1890 to 1891, first assistant from 1891 to 1893, and head keeper from 1908 to 1910.[^3] Charles Lederle was head keeper from 1910, the year the light was first lit, until 1919.[^2][^3] Edwin C. Bishop served from 1919 to 1934, and John Woods from 1934 to 1940.[^3] Hobart J. Beyschlag served from 1946 to "at least 1951."[^3] The roster's "at least" spans are documented years, not necessarily whole terms.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the evening of Sunday, 30 April 1967, "gale-force winds pounding the Duluth Ship Canal" hit the harbour, and the National Weather Service says "a 20 foot wave crashed over the wall" of the north pier, "sweeping the brothers off the pier." The Service names the three brothers as Eric, Nathan and Arthur Halverson.[^6]

The Coast Guardsmen were called in to try to save them. Boatswain Mate First Class Edgar A. Culbertson "was swept off his feet by a strong wave into the canal." The Weather Service says the Halverson brothers and Culbertson "lost their lives," and that the Coast Guardsmen, including Culbertson, "were awarded the Coast Guard Medal."[^6]

LighthouseFriends gives a different sequence. Witnesses saw a huge wave sweep one brother away, and the Coast Guard crew went out to look for two boys reported to be stranded at the pierhead light. On the way back along the pier, a twenty-foot wave swept Culbertson off his feet.[^2] Both accounts agree that Culbertson and the three brothers died, and that Culbertson was given the Coast Guard Medal. LighthouseFriends says a plaque on the north pier commemorates him.[^2][^6]

After the storm, fencing was installed that "closes off the walkway towards the lighthouse when strong waves come over the walls."[^6]

## After the keepers

The latest principal keeper on the USLHS roster is G. G. Bannan, listed for 1970.[^3]

A radiobeacon was established at the Duluth Canal on 24 August 1927. It originally transmitted in four half-hour periods at six-hour intervals, for general navigation.[^2]

In 2014 an LED optic replaced the original fifth-order Fresnel lens.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the change cut the light's range from about fourteen nautical miles to about ten and a half.[^2] The nomination says the LED beacon "can be seen for 16 miles in clear weather," so the two sources do not agree on its range.[^1][^2] The National Register nomination is dated 26 February 2016, and LighthouseFriends says the light was added to the Register that year.[^1][^2]

## Today

In 2023 ownership passed to Rethos, a St. Paul nonprofit focused on historic preservation, education and economic development. Rethos plans tours involving the lighthouse from the summer of 2027.[^2] Before that, the nomination says the light was owned by the U.S. Coast Guard and identified as number 15855 on the Great Lakes Light List.[^1]

The light signals a red light that "illuminates for three seconds followed by three seconds off."[^1] The lens is now on display at the Lake Superior Marine Museum, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2]

The nomination says that "The deck surrounding the lighthouse is open to public visitation but not the light tower's interior," and that the light is reached on foot by the north pier.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the same arrangement: grounds open, tower closed.[^2]
