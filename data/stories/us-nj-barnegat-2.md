Barnegat Lighthouse, known along the Jersey Shore as "Old Barney", is a tall brick tower at the north end of Long Beach Island in Ocean County, New Jersey. It was built at an inlet whose sands move, and much of its history is the story of keeping the sea away from it.

## The place

Dutch explorers of the mid-1600s named the inlet at the north end of Long Beach Island, and shoals offshore create the breakers that the name recalls.[^1] In 1933 the Lighthouse Service described the spot as a bend in the New Jersey coast about 50 miles south of New York, where several lines of shipping converge or cross.[^2] A 1927 notice in the Bulletin called it a low-lying shore fringed with shallow water, which compelled deep-draft vessels to keep well off, and recorded 50 strandings in the vicinity between 1904 and 1923.[^3]

## Building the light

Sources differ on the first tower. Congress appropriated $6,000 in 1834 and the Historic American Buildings Survey says the first lighthouse was built that year, while a 1993 local history and the Lighthouse Service itself give July 1835 and 1835.[^1][^4][^5][^2] Lighthouse Friends says it was built by Winslow Lewis, stood between forty and fifty feet tall and showed eleven lamps in fourteen-inch reflectors.[^1]

Mariners were not satisfied. In 1852 a Navy mail-steamer captain complained that the light was frequently mistaken for a vessel's light; a fourth-order Fresnel lens followed in 1854.[^1] In 1855 Lieutenant George Meade inspected the station and wrote that Barnegat "is emphatically a seacoast light, and should be of the first class."[^1] Congress budgeted $60,000 in 1856.[^1] Before the new tower was done the old one fell: the HABS record says it toppled into the water because of the encroachment of the sea, and a temporary wooden tower carried the salvaged lantern, with living quarters for keeper James Fuller in its base.[^5][^1]

Work on the present tower began after a ten-acre parcel was bought on 1 July 1857, on a granite foundation set deep in the sand.[^1] The old site, the Lighthouse Service said in 1933, was by then in the middle of the inlet, 900 feet north of the present tower.[^2] The stair has 217 treads.[^1] Sources disagree on the height: Lighthouse Friends gives 163 feet, the local history 172 feet and the Lighthouse Service 160 feet.[^1][^4][^2] Lighthouse Friends says the first-order lens was made in 1856 by Henry-Lepaute of Paris and the light was first shown on 1 January 1859; the museum, which holds the lens, gives its date of manufacture as 1847.[^1][^7] The HABS record says the tower was finished in 1858.[^5]

## The keepers

The first keeper, Henry V. Low, served from 3 August 1835 until his death on 12 March 1838.[^1] The archive's keeper registers then list John H. Low (1838-1839) and Ellen S. Low (1839), the latter as principal keeper, followed by Garret H. Herring.[^6] Once the first-order light was lit, the station had a head keeper and two assistants.[^1]

Some keepers stayed for decades. William T. Woodmansee was first assistant in two stints from 1883 to 1896 and head keeper from 1896 to 1915; Clarence H. Cranmer rose from second assistant to first assistant (1896-1915) and head keeper (1915-1926).[^6] The local museum tells of a rescue in 1870, when nine-year-old Clarence Hazelton Cranmer fell from a boat and was swept into the inlet and Caleb Parker dived in and saved him; the museum does not say what post Parker held, and the archive lists a Caleb Parker as first assistant only in 1862-1864.[^7][^6]

Andrew E. Applegate served as second assistant (1919-1926) and was head keeper in 1926-1927.[^6] Lighthouse Friends reports that in 1924 he fired a shotgun at a fisherman who had struck his wife, the station's postmistress, in a dispute over mail; both men were found guilty of assault and each fined $100.[^1] Applegate drowned in 1928 while netting fish near the lighthouse, and his seventeen-year-old son Robert, who tried to haul in the net, was then appointed lamplighter, according to Lighthouse Friends. The archive instead lists Robert E. Applegate as principal keeper in 1928-1932, and William E. Rothas from 1932 to 1940.[^1][^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

By Lighthouse Friends' account, the distance between the lighthouse and the inlet had halved to about 150 yards by the time short jetties were built in 1869-1870; the local history puts the 1869 distance at about 450 feet.[^1][^4] In 1893 the keepers' dwelling was remodelled, with an addition to the second assistant's house because it had only two rooms for him and his family; the keepers moved in on 14 September 1893.[^8] The next year a timber and brush bulkhead about 190 feet long was built to protect the beach and reservation.[^9]

Storms returned after the First World War. The northeast storm of 10-12 April 1918 cut a 20-foot gap between the end of the stone jetty and the reservation.[^10] In February 1920 another storm destroyed a recently built riprap breakwater, with about 1,000 tons of stone lost, and overturned 400 feet of concrete retaining wall around the station.[^11] Lighthouse Friends says the 1919-1920 storms moved the high-water mark 275 feet toward the tower, and that the keepers' dwelling was sold for $125 to salvagers.[^1] In 1933 the water was two feet from the foundation at high tide, and a $12,000 federal project in 1934 ringed the base with steel.[^1]

## After the keepers

The station was handed to New Jersey in 1926 (Lighthouse Friends) or 1925 (HABS) at the State's request.[^1][^5][^2] In 1927 the Bulletin announced that a lightship would be established about 8 miles east of the lighthouse; among its reasons, it said there was no sound fog signal along more than 100 miles of New Jersey coast between New York and Delaware Bay.[^3] The first-order lens was removed in 1927 and a lesser electric light kept in the tower.[^1][^2] The museum says the lens was given to it in 1957; Lighthouse Friends says 1955, adding that the lens came back from Chicago.[^7][^1] The light was discontinued in 1944.[^1]

A piece of metal fell from the tower in 1988 and the state closed it; after repairs funded by $660,000 in grants it reopened in June 1991.[^1] In late 2008 it was announced that a new beacon would be lit on 1 January 2009, 150 years after the first lighting.[^1]

## Today

The tower stands in Barnegat Lighthouse State Park, dedicated on 11 July 1957.[^1] The Barnegat Light Historical Society museum in the nearby town displays the Fresnel lens.[^7] Hours and whether the tower can be climbed change from season to season; check with the park before travelling.
