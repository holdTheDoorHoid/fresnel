Pointer Island Light is a small white light on a rocky islet where Fitzhugh Sound meets Lama Passage, on British Columbia's Inside Passage.[^1][^6] It was first lit in November 1899, has been unstaffed since 1989, and can be reached only by boat.[^1][^6]

## The place

Ships leaving Fitzhugh Sound must make a turn of nearly ninety degrees to enter the narrow Lama Passage.[^1][^6] LighthouseFriends says the island takes its name from that turn.[^1] The island covers about 0.6 ha (one and a half acres) and lies off the north-east tip of Hunter Island.[^1][^6] The Coast Guard's list places the light at the south-east end of the island, south of the passage's east entrance.[^5]

In 1896, LighthouseFriends reports, the British Columbia Board of Trade called for a lighthouse here to help mark the Inside Passage for prospectors heading north for the Alaska gold rush.[^1]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says the project took just over three months.[^1] Around 1 August the Department's steamer *Quadra* left Victoria with building material and a work crew.[^1] Her master was Captain John T. Walbran.[^1] The Department's report for 1900 prints his name as "Captain Jno. T. Wallbran".[^2]

The light went into operation on 15 November 1899.[^1] The Department's notice, as LighthouseFriends prints it, describes a white square wooden building with a red roof and a red wooden lantern, 9.1 m (30 ft) high.[^1] The fixed white light stood 12.8 m (42 ft) above high water and was visible for 12 miles, from a dioptric apparatus "of the 7th order".[^1] Departmental labour put up the building under the foreman D. M. Fraser of Vancouver.[^1]

In November 1948 heavy seas destroyed the nearby Egg Island light.[^1] In 1949 the Department decided to build a similar combined tower and dwelling of concrete here, with a fog-alarm building.[^1] The light's characteristic changed in the same year, from fixed white to four seconds on and four off.[^1] Until then the keepers had answered a steamer's whistle with a hand horn. The new diaphone sounded a three-second blast every twenty seconds in thick weather.[^1]

In 1964 a second dwelling went up to house a second keeper.[^1] Three years later the concrete building was replaced by a new dwelling and a skeletal tower for the light.[^1]

## The keepers

James Codville had come to Vancouver in 1887 and from 1890 worked for the Department as a guardian on the Skeena and Nass rivers.[^1] After hearing that King Island would be the western terminus of the Grand Trunk Pacific Railway, he put his savings into land there in 1894. King Island lies across Fitzhugh Sound from Pointer Island.[^1] The railway chose Prince Rupert instead, and in 1899 Codville took work helping build the Pointer Island light.[^1]

The Department hired him as keeper at a salary LighthouseFriends gives as $360 a year.[^1] The Department's keepers' table in the report printed in 1910 gives his appointment as 26 December 1899, and his salary as $465 by 1909.[^3] The table printed in 1913 lists "J. Codville" at the station, but gives his appointment as 24 October 1900 and his salary as $570.[^4] The sources read do not explain the difference.

In 1900 Captain Walbran of the *Quadra* stopped with supplies.[^1] Codville told him the pay would not feed his family.[^1] Walbran's account, as LighthouseFriends prints it, describes "a more weak looking and famished family I never came across".[^1] Codville stayed on until his death in 1917, at the age of 83.[^1]

His son Benjamin, called Ben, took over the light and kept it for 29 years, until 1946.[^1][^7]

LighthouseFriends tells how the search for Ben's wife reached Captain William Oliver of the mission boat *Udal*.[^1] Oliver contacted the Salvation Army, and the matron of its girls' home in Vancouver thought a young woman named Annie might be a match.[^1] She agreed to sail north and meet Ben.[^1] On a later voyage Oliver stopped at the island and married them.[^1] A Find a Grave memorial transcribes the 1919 marriage entry, which gives the date as 12 July 1919 at Pointer Island.[^7] LighthouseFriends says Annie knitted the second-highest number of socks for soldiers of the British Empire during the war.[^1] When Ben retired in 1946 he received the Imperial Service Medal, and the couple moved to Prince Rupert, where the Department had offered to take them.[^1]

After 1946 the head keepers changed often.[^1] LighthouseFriends names six between 1946 and 1989.[^1] The longest term it gives is Lyle Dean Bigelow's, from 1960 to 1983.[^1] In the Bigelow years, LighthouseFriends says, Santa Claus came by helicopter, and a pine from a nearby island was cut down and decorated at the station.[^1]

## After the keepers

The station was de-staffed in March 1989.[^1] Its buildings were then demolished, and a cylindrical tower was erected to show an automated light.[^1] Rowlett gives the present tower as an 8 m (26 ft) round white tower, dated 1989.[^6]

The sources describe the light's pattern differently over time. LighthouseFriends gives the 1949 pattern as four seconds on and four off.[^1] Rowlett gives four seconds on and two off.[^6] The Coast Guard's list gives today's characteristic as occulting white with a six-second period, "Oc W 6s".[^1][^6][^5] The two current descriptions agree. None of the sources read dates the change between them.

## Today

The light is an active aid to navigation maintained by the Canadian Coast Guard.[^6] The Coast Guard's list gives a focal height of 12.9 m and a nominal range of 8 nautical miles.[^5] The station is owned by the Coast Guard.[^6] It is reached only by boat. The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1][^6] LighthouseFriends says the light is best seen from the water, and that the ferries serving the Discovery Coast and Inside Passage from Port Hardy pass it.[^1]

LighthouseFriends notes that nothing on the island now recalls the families who lived there.[^1] The Codville name survives on neighbouring King Island, and Codville Lagoon Marine Provincial Park lies just east of the light.[^1]
