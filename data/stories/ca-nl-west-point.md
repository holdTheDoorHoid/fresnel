West Point Light is a lighthouse on a rocky headland on the western side of the entrance to François Bay, a fjord on the south coast of Newfoundland, where it marks the approach to the outport of François.[^1][^3]

## The place

François sits at the head of a deep, steep-walled fjord about two kilometres long. The fjord's entrance lies between West Point and East Point.[^1] Above the village a rock called the Friar rises more than 200 metres.[^1] The village lived by the fishery.[^1][^3] Its catch was bought first by Newman & Company of Gaultois, and from 1908 by John Penny & Sons of Ramea, who opened a store and a branch office in François in 1922.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls François the farthest inhabited place in Atlantic Canada from a highway, served by a regular ferry from Burgeo.[^1]

## Building the light

The first building these sources date on the headland is a fog alarm station, not a light. In 1929 it was built on West Point: a flat-roofed white dwelling with an engine house. The alarm was sounded by air compressed by an oil engine, and gave one two-second blast every minute when it was needed.[^1] Landing supplies and carrying them up to the alarm was hard work.[^1]

The light came later. LighthouseFriends dates it to 1958, when a square pyramidal tower 4.25 m high was set up.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory gives 1929 for the fog signal station and 1958 for the light.[^4]

In 1966 a combined light tower and fog alarm building replaced the older structures, and a one-storey duplex was built for the keepers.[^1][^3] Parks Canada describes a two-storey wooden tower with a red shingle roof and an octagonal aluminium lantern, built to a variation of a standard plan of the Department of Transport. It was built after Newfoundland joined Canada in 1949.[^3]

The sources disagree about the height. The Coast Guard's list gives the light's structure as 4.7 m (15 ft).[^2] The Lighthouse Directory describes a concrete tower of about 14 m (46 ft).[^4] Parks Canada gives no height; it describes a wood frame on a concrete foundation.[^3]

## The keepers

The sources name few of the people who worked here. LighthouseFriends says Benjamin Durnford was the fog alarm's first attendant, but gives no dates for his work.[^1]

In 1950 Albert Dollimont, a widower from François with four children, was killed at the station while unloading drums of oil.[^1] LighthouseFriends records the death but does not say whether he worked for the station.

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Directory dates the historic tower's deactivation to 2015. It says the tower has been inactive since then and that the active light is now shown from a square skeletal tower in front of it.[^4] The same source reports that in 2015 the Coast Guard announced it would demolish the dilapidated keeper's house but keep the historic tower.[^4] Parks Canada's 2007 statement noted that the lightkeeper's dwelling "remains as a reminder of the presence of lightkeepers."[^3]

## Today

Parks Canada recognised the historic tower as a Federal Heritage Building in 2007. The designation is confined to the footprint of the building.[^3] The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, according to LighthouseFriends, and Parks Canada names Fisheries and Oceans Canada as custodian.[^1][^3]

The Lighthouse Directory says the site is accessible only by boat, and the sources name no public access. LighthouseFriends says a view of the lighthouse is possible from the ferry between François, Grey River and Burgeo.[^1][^4]

The sources also disagree on the light's rhythm. The Coast Guard's list gives West Point Light a green flash every six seconds, with a range of 12 nautical miles.[^2] The Lighthouse Directory gives the active light a green flash every five seconds, on a square skeletal tower in front of the historic one.[^4] The sources do not settle which is current.
