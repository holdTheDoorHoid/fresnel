Caja de Muertos Light (Faro de la Isla de Caja de Muertos) stands on the highest point of a rocky, desertic island, Caja de Muertos ("Coffin Island"), off Puerto Rico's south coast.[^1][^14] The encyclopedia puts the island about eight miles from Ponce, and the nomination places it about seven miles east of the entrance to Ponce Harbor.[^14][^1] The name, which the National Register nomination says describes the island's "natural coffin shape," has been used there for centuries.[^1][^14] The light is a third-order station built under Spanish rule, and its cross-shaped plan, with the tower at the center, is one the nomination calls unique within the system.[^1]

## The place

The 1901 Light-House Board report says the light "is used as a guide along the southerly coast of Porto Rico," and that it is visible for 18 miles, a figure the report gives without saying whether the miles are nautical.[^2] The sources do not name a particular shoal or reef as the reason for the light, and they do not describe the island before it was built.

## Building the light

Works began in 1882 and were "finished in 1885 by the Spanish, at a cost of 39,412 pesos," according to the 1901 report.[^2] The nomination gives 1887 as the year of construction. The encyclopedia says the light began operating on 15 August 1887, and credits the design to the engineer Manuel Maese Peña, who also built the Culebrita light, opened the year before.[^14] The nomination describes the original lens as a lenticular third-order Sautter, Lemonnier & Cie. lens of 1885.[^1] The encyclopedia dates the original lantern to 1885 as well, while the Society's record gives 1887 as the year the original lens was installed.[^14][^13] None of the sources explains how the 1885 and 1887 dates relate.

The 1901 report describes a cylindrical tower on the center of a one-story, flat-roofed dwelling, and gives the tower as 41 feet (12.5 m) high from its base.[^2] The Society's record gives the same 41 feet, from base to the center of the lantern.[^13] The nomination gives the tower as 62 feet (19 m) high including the lantern, and the encyclopedia gives the same figure.[^1][^14] The sources do not reconcile the two heights.

The sources also disagree on the signal. The 1901 report calls it "fixed white, varied by a white flash every three minutes," and puts it 297 feet (90.5 m) above high water.[^2] The nomination says the original light "showed a white flash every 3 seconds."[^1]

## The keepers

The encyclopedia names Severo del Olmo and César Prieto as the first keepers.[^14] The Lighthouse Service roster lists José R. Parada as principal keeper from at least 1901 to at least 1905, and Alfonso Sanchez Bermudez from at least 1907 to 1926.[^13] The Bulletins and the Commissioner's report name Bermudez repeatedly between 1916 and 1926.[^5][^6][^8][^9][^10][^11][^12]

Antonio D. Jesus moved up from first assistant (1916 to 1926) to principal keeper (1926 to 1942).[^13][^5] Jose Medrano, the assistant keeper named in the 1915 Bulletin, is on the roster from at least 1913 to at least 1915.[^13][^3] The roster also lists Jose P. Castillo as first assistant from 1907 to 1911, and Antonio S. Cuenca from at least 1903.[^13] It lists Juan Ramos Ferran (1942 to 1946) and Pedro P. Roldan (1945 to 1947) as principal keepers, and the two overlap.[^13]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 11 August 1915, during a hurricane, three Puerto Rico keepers were commended. Jose Medrano, the assistant keeper, judged that the station launch would be lost unless it reached a place of safety, so he took it nine miles to Ponce Harbor and beached it there.[^3]

On 22 August 1916 a hurricane struck the ninth lighthouse district. At Muertos Island, the Bulletin reports, "a servant of the keeper was drowned while endeavoring to save the station boat." The Bulletin does not name him.[^4] Ten days earlier, on 12 August, Bermudez and Antonio D. Jesus rescued a man whose boat was discovered sinking near the light station.[^5]

On 14 November, during a storm, Bermudez and Jesus rescued a man whose boat had been disabled nearby, and sheltered him at the station until the storm passed. The 1917 Bulletin gives the day and month only.[^6]

In the hurricane of September 1921, the Bulletin lists Muertos Island among ninth-district stations where damage was done.[^7] On 13 June 1922 a man was brought to the station unconscious, with a knife wound in his arm, and Bermudez gave him first aid.[^9] The Commissioner's annual report gives the same date and calls the injury "a serious wound in the arm."[^8]

The Bulletin of 1924 printed a commendation for Bermudez for helping a disabled fishing boat and its occupants near the light on 10 December. The line gives no year.[^10] In the storm that crossed the southwest of Puerto Rico on the evening of 23 July 1926, Bermudez sheltered seven people from a shipwrecked fishing boat. He also took in 20 more, including 12 children, whose houses near the station had been unroofed.[^12]

## After the keepers

The nomination and the encyclopedia both say the lighting system was changed in 1923.[^1][^14] In 1945 the light was electrified and made automatic and unwatched.[^1][^14] The nomination says the structure was then "abandoned and boarded up," and that the lens was replaced by a 500 mm lens.[^1] The Society's record lists a 500 mm current optic.[^13]

## Today

The lights are under the jurisdiction of the Coast Guard. The island is a nature reserve, and its recreational and operational facilities belong to the Puerto Rico Department of Natural and Environmental Resources (DRNA).[^15] The encyclopedia says the light still operates.[^14] A report published on 21 October 2026 describes a field visit by the mayor of Ponce with representatives of the DRNA, the Coast Guard and the Ports Authority. They reviewed the pier, the lights at Caja de Muertos and Cardona, the gazebos, the museum and the watchmen's houses.[^15]

The pier that once took the weekend ferry has been dismantled, and a March 2026 report says the reserve has had no surveillance station since 2020.[^16] After Hurricane María in 2017 the reserve closed to the public except its beaches, according to a 2021 report, which also says the island had received about 20,000 visitors a year before the storm. FEMA allotted more than $1.2 million for repairs.[^17] None of the 2026 reports says whether the reserve has reopened.

None of these sources describes the tower as open to visitors. The light is under Coast Guard jurisdiction, so do not climb the tower or land on the island without first checking the reserve's current rules.[^15][^16]
