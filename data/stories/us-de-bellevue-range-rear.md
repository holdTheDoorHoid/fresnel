Bellevue Range Rear Light stands in the Christina River near Wilmington, Delaware, a cast-iron skeletal tower built as the rear light of a pair that marked the Delaware River shipping channel.[^1] Its National Register nomination says resident keepers ran it from 1909 until 1934, when it was automated.[^2] A landfill later grew up beside it, its beacon was obstructed, and in 2001 the light was deactivated and replaced by a new tower.[^1][^3]

## The place

The land had come under federal control in 1834, when the War Department obtained a site where the Delaware and Christina rivers meet, for a jetty to help commercial vessels.[^4] Congress authorized the Bellevue Range lights in an act of 20 June 1906. The Lighthouse Board received its first money that June, with a limit of $40,000 for the whole project.[^4][^5]

The river already had range lights. The Deepwater Point and New Castle ranges went into operation in 1876, and the Cherry Island range followed in 1880.[^5] A lighthouse had been established on Cherry Island, at the mouth of Christiana Creek, in 1835. It was called Christiana Light, and it was discontinued when the new pair was lit in 1909.[^2][^6]

Rear range lights usually stood inland. The nomination says that front range lights are generally in the water or at the water's edge, while rear ones are usually further inland.[^5] The Bellevue rear light was an exception: its site was "approximately 200 yards from shore and surrounded by water" (about 180 m).[^5]

## Building the light

The Lighthouse Board's engineers planned a tall skeletal tower for the rear light and a shorter one for the front.[^5] The Board had adopted a standard skeletal plan in the late nineteenth century. The first example, according to the nomination, was Sanibel Light Station in Florida, built in 1884.[^5] The Bellevue rear light and the Reedy Island Range Rear Light are the only two of that type still standing in Delaware.[^5]

The foundation plan was then reconsidered. In a report to the Lighthouse Board on 15 October 1907, the Fourth Lighthouse District Engineer suggested changing the foundation to match ones that had worked for skeletal towers in Florida and Georgia.[^7] To test that idea, the Board drove a test pile at the site on 24 December 1907, to see how the ground would bear a 100-foot (30 m) tower.[^7]

Richmond Iron Works of Richmond, Virginia, won the metalwork, and Tatnall-Brown Company of Wilmington was selected to build the foundation piers.[^7] The erection did not go smoothly. Sherwood Engineering & Company, which had been awarded the contract to erect the tower, failed to complete the work, and a later bid from Edward Fay and Son of Philadelphia was accepted to finish it.[^7]

The finished tower stands on nine concrete piers, under a cast-iron pyramidal frame with nine principal vertical members. The nomination gives its height as 105 feet (32 m) from base to ventilator ball.[^1] The round tower is built of 13 courses of cast-iron plates bolted on the outside.[^8]

In July 1907 the Committee on Lighting recommended an alternative for the rear light: a new fourth-order, eight-panel lens set to flash every 2.5 seconds, each flash lasting half a second.[^6] Barbier, Benard and Turenne of Paris made the lens in 1908 for $1,715.90, and it was shipped to the station on 10 January 1909.[^6] It was fitted with an incandescent oil vapor lamp and "was first lighted on 15 March 1909."[^6]

The Committee on Pay and Allowances recommended $50 a month for the principal keeper and $40 for the assistant.[^6] The old Christiana Lighthouse remained in use as the keeper's dwelling, and a one-story concrete assistant's dwelling was built in 1909.[^6]

## The keepers

The USLHS roster lists three principal keepers and eleven first assistants, in entries from 1909 to 1938.[^9] William E. Spicer was the first principal keeper, from 1909 to 1911.[^9] His son Linwood Spicer followed him, from 1911 to 1919.[^9][^10]

A 1930 Bulletin notice of Linwood Spicer's death says he had served at Fourteen Foot Bank Light Station, Ship John Shoal Light Station and Bellevue Range Rear. He was keeper of Marcus Hook Range Rear when he died on 12 November, after nearly 35 years of service.[^10] The notice also says his father "at the time of his death in 1912 was rounding out his 39th year of service."[^10] The roster ends William E. Spicer's stint at this light in 1911, and the sources do not say where he was in 1912.[^9]

The first assistants changed often. George H. Buchanan's stint ran from 24 March to 8 July 1909, and eleven men held the post between 1909 and 1918.[^9]

William H. Johnson became principal keeper in 1919, and the roster carries him to 1938.[^9] In 1931 the Bulletin listed the station, with Johnson as keeper, among those that had attained the highest general efficiency in their districts during the past year.[^11] The nomination says the light was manned until 1934, when it was automated.[^3] The roster's 1938 and the nomination's 1934 disagree, and the sources do not settle which is right.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Bulletin's report on the September 1928 hurricane includes district notes. In the Fourth District it says the storm cut away the river bank at Bellevue Range Rear Light Station and caused some damage to the dwelling.[^12] The report gives no further detail about the station, and the sources do not record a wreck or rescue there.

## After the keepers

In 1919 the illuminant was changed to acetylene. In 1921 the characteristic was changed to a fixed white light of 20,000 candlepower, and by 1941 the light was electric, at 240,000 candlepower.[^3]

The light was automated in 1934. In 1936 the Commerce Department gave the Dravo Contracting Company of Pittsburgh permission to use the station's unoccupied dwellings as an office and storage site while it built the Edgemoor Dike, which extended Cherry Island over a dredge-spoil area.[^3] The Lighthouse Service was abolished in 1939, and its duties passed to the Coast Guard.[^3] The station's other buildings were later demolished, and only the tower remains.[^3]

The original fourth-order lens was removed and replaced with a modern optic, and the nomination says the location of the original lens is unknown.[^3]

The Cherry Island Landfill opened in 1985 immediately north of the light, and its surface rose until the optic was obscured. The light remained in operation until 2001, when it was deactivated because its beacon had become obstructed. A modern light tower on the unobstructed northeast side of Cherry Island replaced it, and the 1909 light was disestablished.[^3][^1]

## Today

The nomination says that, despite these changes, "the light tower structure is remarkably unchanged from its original appearance."[^3] According to the nomination, the tower is owned by the Coast Guard, and it is no longer an aid to navigation.[^1]

Getting to the tower means crossing the Cherry Island Landfill, where admittance is restricted and permission is required for entry.[^1] A metal catwalk about 50 feet (15 m) long leads from the landfill to a flight of eighteen metal stairs up to the tower.[^8] The sources describe no public visiting arrangement for the light. Do not cross the landfill or climb the catwalk without permission.
