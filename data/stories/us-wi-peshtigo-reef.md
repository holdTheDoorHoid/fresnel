Peshtigo Reef is a rocky shoal in lower Green Bay, about 3.3 miles southeast of Peshtigo Point in Marinette County, Wisconsin.[^1] The nomination does not say whether its distances and ranges are statute or nautical miles, so this story gives them as the nomination does. A steel light vessel marked it from 1906 until 1934. The lighthouse that stands there now, a steel tower on a concrete pier, was finished in 1936, and it was planned from the start to run without resident keepers.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Peshtigo Point stands out on Green Bay's western shore, but by itself it poses little danger to shipping. A broad shoal runs out from it toward the southeast, and its shallow, rocky water was an obstacle for vessels in lower Green Bay.[^2]

In 1865 a Lighthouse Board team came to Green Bay to assess which federal aids to navigation were needed. The team's findings singled out three spots for lights: Chambers Island in Door County, Peshtigo Reef, and Grassy Island at the mouth of the Fox River.[^2] Congress appropriated $25,000 in 1866 for lights at all three.[^2] Chambers Island got its light, but the nomination says funding was pulled in 1867 before the other two could be built.[^2] The Board still marked the reef with four spar buoys, and in 1869 it built a day beacon: a wooden crib with a pyramidal tower and an iron cage on top.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives the money differently. It says $10,000 was left for the reef after Chambers Island was finished, and that the beacon was complete by June 1870.[^5] The two accounts do not agree, and the sources do not settle which is right.

In 1892 the Board asked for $10,000 for a light and fog signal on the reef. Congress endorsed the idea in 1893 but appropriated no money, and nothing was built.[^2] In 1898, as LighthouseFriends reports, the Board judged a lighthouse on the reef a dangerous experiment because of the floating ice, and asked for a light vessel instead.[^2][^5]

## Building the light

Congress finally appropriated $15,000 for the vessel in 1902.[^2][^5] The Johnson Boiler Company of Ferrysburg, Michigan, took the contract to build her, which LighthouseFriends gives as $13,950.[^2][^5] Work began in 1904. The hull reached Sturgeon Bay in the latter part of 1905; LighthouseFriends gives the month as November.[^2][^5]

The vessel was numbered LV 77. The nomination describes a steel scow-type hull of 110 tons, 75 feet (23 m) long, with no engine of her own, so a tender had to tow her to and from the reef.[^2] Three oil lens-lanterns were hoisted on a ring to the top of her mast. Her fog signal was a bell rung by hand. She carried four staterooms and enough food and lamp oil for a full navigation season.[^2] She went out to the reef at the start of the 1906 season, and her lantern was first shown there on 28 April 1906.[^2]

In 1911 she was taken off station for a few weeks in August and September so that an 8-inch compressed-air chime whistle could be fitted. A gas bell buoy marked the reef in the meantime.[^2][^5]

Work on a permanent lighthouse began in September 1934 with a wooden crib, and bad weather then suspended the work. It resumed in the spring of 1935 with a ring of sheet piling, and a concrete pier was built on top of the filled crib.[^3] By August 1935 a temporary light and fog signal stood on the pier. The light vessel was then relieved and towed to Sturgeon Bay, where she awaited another assignment before being decommissioned in 1939.[^3]

Love Construction Company of Muskegon won the building contract with a labor bid of $31,800, and the government supplied the materials and equipment.[^5]

The nomination says the lighthouse was completed in June 1936.[^3] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' 1936 report put the station in full commission on 6 May 1936, LighthouseFriends says, and gives the total cost as $80,710.17.[^5][^6] The two dates do not sit easily together, and this story does not settle them. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1936 says that the Peshtigo Reef stations were placed in operation that season, and gives the cost as slightly over $80,000.[^12][^13]

The finished lighthouse has a crib and pier about 50 feet (15 m) across, a steel service building 25 feet (7.6 m) across on top of the pier, and a four-story steel tower standing on the service building roof. The tower is about 30 feet (9 m) tall, and the whole structure, from the base of the foundation to the top of the lantern, is about 80 feet (24 m) high.[^1]

The lamp was a fourth-order Fresnel lens with an electric lamp.[^3] The nomination gives 20,000 candlepower, and a range of 16 miles in clear weather.[^3] LighthouseFriends gives 26,000 candlepower.[^3][^6] A 1935 Bulletin notice describes a 72-foot-high lighthouse with a 2,000-candlepower light visible for 11 miles.[^11] These figures do not agree, and the story does not choose among them. A generator ran the light.[^3] A 200-millimeter white lens took over as the winter light, switched on each December.[^3][^4] The fog signal was a compressed-air diaphragm horn, blowing for 2 seconds every 20 seconds, with a solenoid-operated bell that struck one blow every 20 seconds as a backup.[^3]

## The keepers

The lighthouse was designed to be run by keepers from another station. The nomination says the light was to run semi-automatically, with no resident keepers, and that its fog signal would be run by radio from Sherwood Point Light Station, a neighboring light to the southeast on the Door Peninsula.[^2][^3] Visiting keepers had rooms in the service building for nights when work or bad weather kept them there.[^1] Keepers from Sherwood Point traveled out only when necessary, in a small wooden launch.[^3]

Before 1936 the people who kept the shoal were the light vessel's crew.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's personnel notices name several of them.

Albert F. Pitman was promoted from mate on the Milwaukee light vessel to master of LV 77 in 1913.[^8] In 1916 he was transferred as master to the North Manitou Shoal light vessel in Michigan.[^9]

Reinhart Larsen was promoted from mate to master of LV 77 in 1916.[^9] A notice in 1917 records that he had resigned as her master.[^10] A notice on the previous page of the same 1917 Bulletin instead moves him to mate of the Grays Reef light vessel; this story follows the notice that records his resignation.[^16]

Thomas Green, master of the Lansing Shoal light vessel, was transferred to the same post on LV 77 in 1917.[^10] Olaf K. Andersen came over from the Lansing Shoal vessel as mate that year, to take the place of H. D. Baxter, who had resigned.[^10]

LighthouseFriends names Conrad Stram as captain of LV 77 from 1908 to 1913, citing Steven Karges's *Keepers of the Lights* (2000).[^6] The same page says that when the lighthouse opened in 1936 Stram was keeper at Sherwood Point. It describes how he used a tuning fork to send a tone to the reef, where a tuned reed tripped a relay that started the fog signal. It says he made the weekly trip out to the reef by boat until he retired in 1945.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends reports that in late November 1914, days after the light vessel had been taken off for the season, the steamer *Jupiter* grounded on the reef in dense fog. She was carrying 6,000 tons of coal from Green Bay to Buffalo.[^5] This account rests on LighthouseFriends alone.

Ice was the reef's seasonal problem. The light vessel could not mark the shoal in early spring or late fall, because of ice.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the Commissioner of Lighthouses' 1923 report put the coal traffic past the reef at 850,000 tons, and called for a fixed station on a crib there to replace the vessel.[^5]

The Commissioner's table of lives saved and property protected for fiscal 1919 lists "Peshtigo Reef Light Vessel No. 77, Wis." among the vessels and employees credited with service that year. The entry is for assistance to the motor boat *Wyandotte*, which had five persons aboard: the light vessel "rendered assistance" and "provided temporary rudder for steering to port." The table gives no date for the service.[^15]

## After the keepers

The light vessel's last season on the reef was 1934.[^2] The lighthouse was run from the shore. A time switch operated the light, and the generators were started and stopped by radio from Sherwood Point.[^6][^3]

Through the 1990s, newer equipment replaced the optic, the fog signal and the generator power supply.[^3][^4] The generator came out after a solar array went in to recharge the batteries, and the fog signal is now an automated unit that runs on that battery power.[^4] The lantern now holds a modern automated 300-millimeter acrylic optic.[^4]

The new light was part of the Bureau of Lighthouses' program of fixed offshore lights to replace Great Lakes lightships.[^2] Looking back at 1936, the 1937 Bulletin counted four new light stations on submerged sites, three of them replacing lightships. Peshtigo Reef was one of the four, and the Bulletin says they "have proven of distinct advantage to navigational interests, and have also brought about substantial savings in maintenance expenses."[^14]

## Today

The U.S. Coast Guard owns the lighthouse and runs it as an automated aid to navigation.[^1] Its light flashes white once every six seconds, with a focal plane 72 feet (22 m) above the lake. The nomination gives its range as 9 miles in clear weather, against the 16 miles it gave for the 1936 lamp.[^1][^3]

The tower is closed to visitors, and access to the lighthouse is by boat.[^1] LighthouseFriends suggests seeing it from a boat or from the air.[^7] The nomination is part of the National Register's *Light Stations of the United States* multiple property listing.[^1]
