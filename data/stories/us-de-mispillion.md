The Mispillion Light marks the mouth of the Mispillion River, where it runs into Delaware Bay about six miles east of Milford, in Sussex County, Delaware.[^10] Lights have stood there since 1831, in a succession of structures. The wooden lighthouse of 1873 was the third, and lightning burned it in 2002; its remains were rebuilt in Lewes.[^1][^2][^12]

## The place

Congress voted $1,500 for a light at the river mouth on March 3, 1831, to guide vessels to Milford, an inland port where more than 400 ships, mostly sloops and schooners, had been built and christened over the years.[^2] The land was bought for a token $5 from Governor Charles Polk and his wife, Mary Elizabeth.[^2] The 2.161-acre station tract, as the 1986 National Register nomination describes it, had Polk among its original owners.[^1]

Erosion shaped the place from the start. The nomination says that the first lighthouse "fell victim to erosion."[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record for the 1873 station, under the name Mispillion New, puts it at the "Mouth of Mispillion Creek," latitude 38°56.49′ N, longitude 75°18.57′ W.[^4]

## Building the light

Winslow Lewis, a prolific builder of lighthouses, signed the contract on August 26, 1831. The work was complete by October 19 that year: a lantern with six lamps and reflectors on top of the keeper's dwelling.[^2] In 1838 an inspector, William D. Porter, found the building poorly built and close to ruin, and he recommended pulling it down and building a new one.[^2] A new light in the same configuration followed in 1839.[^2] The Slaughter Beach Historical Society gives the same 1839 rebuild, after inspections.[^12] The nomination says only that the first lighthouse fell to erosion, so the sources do not agree on how the buildings followed one another.[^1]

In 1855 a fifth-order Fresnel lens replaced the array of lamps and reflectors.[^2] By 1857 the Light-House Board wanted the light gone. Its report that year, as LighthouseFriends reports it, judged the light unnecessary even for the people of that vicinity and recommended that it be discontinued.[^2] The light was deactivated in 1859 and its building sold for $135.[^2] The nomination says the building was moved into Milford and used as a storehouse.[^1]

In 1872 the Board asked Congress for money to re-establish a light near the river entrance.[^2] The 1873 building was a two-story, L-shaped dwelling with a square tower standing where the two wings met, its light 48 feet (15 m) above the bay.[^2] The nomination gives the whole structure as about 65 feet (20 m) tall.[^1] Two dates are given for the first lighting. LighthouseFriends says the sixth-order Fresnel lens was first shown on June 15, 1873.[^2] The Slaughter Beach Historical Society dates the lighting to June 27, 1873.[^12]

During the 1874 canvass for Congress, a letter to the editor printed in the Wilmington Daily Gazette attacked the record of the state's member of Congress. Its writer said that his only effort to get money for the state had been, as the writer understood it, a $5,000 appropriation "for the erection of a dwelling for the keeper of the light-house." The letter attacks him in the middle of a campaign, so its account is the writer's, not a record.[^11] LighthouseFriends says the 1874 money was instead allocated for a brick cistern and four rooms added to the dwelling.[^2] The sources disagree, and this story does not choose between them.

The bay side kept taking ground. The Board's reports, as LighthouseFriends summarizes them, record a four-foot (1.2 m) earth bank in 1877, 203 feet (62 m) of sheet piling in 1883, and about 60 tons of riprap in 1885 against scour and ice.[^2] The annual report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1897, records that a high dike, stone riprap on the bay front and a new water tank had been finished.[^6] The report for the year ended June 30, 1899, says about 12,000 bushels of shells were "placed upon the road which leads across the marsh" to the light station.[^7]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists thirteen spells of service by principal keepers at the station, from Thomas Carlisle, 1831 to 1838, to Walter P. Graham, 1896 to 1911.[^3] Carlisle was the first keeper.[^2]

The post was political. In December 1844 a newspaper, as LighthouseFriends reports it, noted that William Jacobs, a Whig, had been removed as keeper and that Charles Hamm, a Loco Foco (a radical Democrat), had been appointed in his place.[^2] The archive has Hamm there from 1838 to 1841, Jacobs from 1841 to 1844, and Hamm again from 1844 to 1847.[^3] Between 1847 and 1859 the roster lists George M. Davis, Reuben Anderson, Jacobs again, James Scott, Thomas Clark and William R. Jacobs.[^3] No keeper appears on the roster between 1859 and 1873, the years the light was dark.[^2][^3]

James H. Bell, a Civil War veteran, served from June 9, 1873 to April 4, 1881.[^2][^3] Henry B. Spencer followed and died in office on February 21, 1896.[^2][^3] Walter P. Graham kept the light from 1896 until its automation in 1911.[^3] LighthouseFriends says every keeper of the 1873 light was paid $500 a year.[^2]

After 1911 the keeper's post was gone. The nomination says the acetylene system made a keeper unnecessary, but the Lighthouse Service hired a local waterman as caretaker at $60 a year and let him live in the keeper's dwelling.[^1] The archive lists Alex. Still as custodian from December 13, 1911.[^3] LighthouseFriends says Still held the post until 1932.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1891, lists the station as Mispillion Creek and records storm repairs after October 1890: "Damages to the bulkhead and banks, by the storm of October, 1890, were repaired."[^5]

In January 1912 the Milford Chronicle told of the steamer Greensborough, which ran between Milford and Philadelphia. It had lain at the light for nearly two weeks, waiting for a way up the river, which was frozen "solid from shore to shore all that time." The perishable freight, such as oranges, was a total loss because it froze.[^8]

In June 1912 a launch party from Milford set out for Bowers Beach. After leaving the river mouth it went wrong in the bay, and it reached Bowers about dark. On the way home a heavy fog set in and the rest of the party got lost again. Whenever the fog lifted, the paper reported, "they could see the Mispillion light," but the fog settled again before they could get a good sight on it, and they spent the night in the boat.[^9]

On May 2, 2002, lightning struck the top of the tower, and the upper part and much of the interior burned.[^2] The Slaughter Beach Historical Society gives the same date.[^12]

## After the keepers

The wooden light was deactivated on December 29, 1929, and a 60-foot (18 m) steel skeleton tower took over the light.[^1][^2] That steel tower had been built by the Coast Guard at Cape Henlopen about 1924 to replace the Cape Henlopen Light. "After the Henlopen Light collapsed in April, 1926," the nomination says, the steel tower became surplus and was moved to Mispillion.[^1]

In 1932 the federal government sold the lighthouse and most of the station property to two local businessmen for $400.[^1] In May 1933 the Milford Chronicle reported that the lighthouse had been abandoned by the government a few years earlier, and that "the new light does not require the service of a keeper." The two Milford buyers had painted the buildings white and repaired the wharf.[^10] The steel beacon was deactivated by the Coast Guard in 1984.[^1]

The lighthouse changed hands several times after that. In 1991 it came with a restaurant and marina, and by 1998 its owners had put the site up for sale; by then, LighthouseFriends says, the building was little more than a wooden shell.[^2] In 2001 a local lawyer, Merritt "Sam" Burke III, announced plans to buy it and work with a non-profit group to restore it. LighthouseFriends says he announced the purchase that September.[^2]

## Today

After the fire, the remains were moved to an undisclosed place later in 2002.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes Shipcarpenter Square in Lewes as a residential area of moved historic buildings. In July 2005 a new lantern, which cost more than $53,000, went on top of the rebuilt and enlarged structure there.[^2] The Slaughter Beach Historical Society says the remaining structure was moved to Shipcarpenter's Square, where it still stands.[^12]

The house that incorporates parts of the lighthouse stands in Shipcarpenter Square and can be seen from Burton Avenue.[^2] Look at it from the street, and stay off the property.

The old site lies by the DuPont Nature Center, on the marsh where the river meets the bay. The state's event notice says the lighthouse "stood tall above the marsh right by the DuPont Nature Center."[^13] The US Lighthouse Society's record for the 1873 station lists it as not open to the public.[^4] LighthouseFriends reports that the Burke family sold the site to the State of Delaware in 2004 for $1.2 million.[^2]

What became of the steel beacon is not settled. LighthouseFriends says the iron tower was removed in 2016. A note on the same page, which carries no date, says the old skeletal tower still remains near the mouth of the river.[^2] The 1986 nomination says the steel tower and a small plot of land "continue to be owned by the federal government."[^1]
