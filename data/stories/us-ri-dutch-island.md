Dutch Island Light stands on a small rock outcrop at the southern tip of Dutch Island, off Jamestown, where it marked the entrance to Dutch Island Harbor on the west side of the island. Its brick tower was built in 1857, attached to a keeper's dwelling, and the dwelling was torn down after the light was automated in 1947. The station was deactivated in 1979.[^1][^2]

## The place

The nomination says the light "served to guide ships entering Dutch Island Harbor at the west end of Jamestown, including the Jamestown-to-Saunderstown ferry." It was also "an important navigational mark for vessels trying to avoid Dutch Island while travelling up the Bay's west passage."[^1] The Board's report in its 1891–1892 volume describes the danger of that passage. In avoiding Dutch Island, vessels risked "extreme danger of grounding on Plum Beach," the report says, as the recent grounding of the steamer Pequot had shown.[^3]

## Building the light

In 1825 the State of Rhode Island ceded land at the southern tip of the island "for establishing a lighthouse." The nomination says construction began soon after and that the light "was first lit on January 1, 1827."[^1] The Archive's record gives 1826 as the year the station was established, so the two sources differ on the first year.[^2]

By 1844 the light was in poor shape. During an inspection that year, the Lighthouse Service superintendent described its condition as "the worst constructed of any in the state." It was reported in poor condition again in 1855, and Congress then appropriated $4,000 for a new tower and dwelling, both completed in 1857.[^1]

The surviving tower is 13 feet (4 m) square in plan, of brick, and three stories high. The Archive gives its original optic as a fourth-order Fresnel lens, installed in 1857.[^1][^2] The keeper's dwelling was Greek Revival, and the nomination says it closely resembled the dwelling at Lime Rock in Newport Harbor, also completed in 1857.[^1]

The station kept changing. The Board's report in its 1893–1894 volume records that "New boatways were made and placed in position" at Dutch Island.[^4] Its 1900 report says three stone boundary posts were placed to mark the limits of the reservation, and that the fog signal was repaired.[^5] In 1867 a cast-iron gallery deck and railing were fitted at the top of the tower, and in 1878 the station received a machine-operated fog bell.[^1]

## The keepers

The Archive's roster for the light runs from 1826 to 1947, with twenty-two entries. William Dennis was the first principal keeper, from 1826 to 1843. Robert Dennis appears twice, in 1843 and again from 1846 to 1853.[^2]

Later principal keepers include Matthew M. Trundy (1859–1865), Lewis T. King (1885–1901), Albert H. Porter (1901–1915) and John J. Cook (1915–1927). Marion Fife served as first assistant from 1878 to 1883.[^2] The last principal keeper on the roster is Ernest H. Stacey (1936–1947). A Coast Guard name, Olin T. Knight (1946–1947), comes at the end.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A boathouse built in 1867 was destroyed by a storm two years later, according to the nomination.[^1]

On 21 January 1903, the Joy Line steamer Seaboard, on her regular trip from New York to Boston, put into Dutch Island harbor "owing to thick and heavy weather." Trying to make an anchorage, she was driven onto the rocks a few minutes after noon, with her bow about 30 feet from the platform that runs round the light. The Providence News reported the next day that the steamer was "badly damaged" and "will probably be a total loss," and that the lighthouse, "with its keeper and his family," had "certainly had a narrow escape."[^6] The paper does not name the keeper. The roster gives Albert H. Porter as principal keeper from 1901 to 1915, which covers that date.[^2]

## After the keepers

The light was unmanned and automated in 1947, and the keeper's dwelling was torn down shortly thereafter.[^1] In 1979, "after 152 years of service, the station was deactivated," and its property passed to the Rhode Island Department of Environmental Management.[^1] The National Register asset record gives 25 February 1988 as its publication date.[^7] The nomination says the surviving buildings, and Dutch Island itself, were included in the "recently created Bay Islands State Park."[^1]

## Today

The National Register record, published 25 February 1988, describes the tower and its outbuilding as deteriorated. "The tower is unsecured," the nomination says, and some of the lantern glass was broken.[^1] The state's tourism listing says the lighthouse is owned by the Rhode Island Department of Environmental Management and "is not open to the public and is best seen by boat."[^8] The Archive's record says the station is open to the public, and the nomination called the site "accessible - yes, unrestricted." The sources differ on access.[^2][^1][^8]
