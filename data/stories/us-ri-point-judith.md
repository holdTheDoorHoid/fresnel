Point Judith Light stands on the western side of the entrance to Narragansett Bay, at the southern tip of Point Judith in Narragansett, Rhode Island. A light has been shown there since 1810, and the tower standing now dates from 1857.[^1] It stands beside an active Coast Guard station, and its grounds are closed to visitors.[^1][^2][^7]

## The place

The nomination calls the point one of the most exposed and dangerous spots on the east coast.[^1] LighthouseFriends adds that heavy seas and dense fog frequent the point, and that shoals such as Squid Ledge lie nearby.[^2]

An act of 10 February 1808 provided $5,000 for a light-house on Point Judith, "at the expense of the United States."[^2] The site was acquired in 1809, and the light was established the following year.[^1] The nomination says it has "served continually since 1810 as the primary navigational aid for all shipping entering Narragansett Bay."[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that lighthouse personnel counted 22,860 ships passing by during daylight hours in 1907.[^2] It also says there may have been earlier beacons at the site.[^2]

## Building the light

The first tower was completed in 1810.[^2] LighthouseFriends says it was a wooden tower built under contract by Daniel S. Way, with a spider lamp that burned whale oil.[^2]

The second tower, which replaced the first, was built in 1816.[^1] The nomination describes it as 35 feet (10.7 m) high and made of rough stone coated with cement.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Congress provided $7,500 in April 1816, and that the tower went into operation on 16 November 1816. It also says Winslow Lewis designed the first optic, ten lamps with reflectors, which burned whale oil.[^2] A stone arch from that tower is set in the east wall of the present one. It is inscribed with the name of the second tower's founder, William Ellery, and the year 1816.[^1]

In 1857 the present tower replaced the second, along with a separate brick keeper's dwelling joined to the tower by an enclosed walkway.[^1] The nomination describes the new tower as an octagonal granite-block structure that "stands 51 feet tall" (15.5 m).[^1] LighthouseFriends calls it an octagonal brownstone tower rising sixty-five feet (20 m) above the water.[^2] The nomination also gives a light 65 feet (20 m) above sea level. The archive gives brownstone, and 46 feet (14 m) from the base to the center of the lantern.[^3] The sources do not agree on the material or on what the heights measure.

The archive's record also gives a fourth-order Fresnel lens installed in 1857.[^3] The nomination describes the light as a 24,000-candle-power, fourth-order light with a range of 16 miles, showing three occulting eclipses every fifteen seconds.[^1]

The lantern now on the tower was added in 1869 to replace an earlier one.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive's roster has nineteen principal-keeper entries. The first is John P. Whitford (1809 to 1835), and the last open-ended entry is Robert A. Bauer (from 1961).[^3] LighthouseFriends calls Whitford the first keeper, and says he survived the 1815 hurricane that destroyed the first tower.[^2]

Joseph Whaley kept the light from 1862 to 1889, according to the archive.[^3] In August 1889 the Narragansett Herald reported that Whaley, "for over 27 years in charge of the lighthouse at Point Judith," had resigned, and that his son, Henry A. Whaley, had been appointed in his place.[^8] LighthouseFriends says Whaley's retirement came after twenty-seven years and three months of service. It says that on the Sunday before he left, more than 275 people and 70 wagons had visited the lighthouse and fog sirens, and that 375 hours of fog in May and June, not the visitors, prompted his decision.[^2] Henry A. Whaley had been serving as assistant keeper for thirteen years, according to LighthouseFriends. The archive lists him as first assistant from 1876 to 1889 and principal keeper from 1889 to 1910.[^2][^3]

The archive's roster includes later keepers: Elmer J. Rathbun (1911 to 1927) and Rudolph Iten (1927 to 1944).[^3]

The nomination says the fog signal needed an assistant keeper, and that one was hired in 1874 to operate and maintain it.[^1] LighthouseFriends says an assistant was hired when the station's first fog signal went in, in 1867.[^2] The archive lists first assistants from 1867, including Edwin B. Tucker in 1867 and Henry W. Clark from 1867 to 1873.[^3] The sources do not settle the date of that hire.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Great Gale of September 1815 destroyed the first tower.[^1][^2] The nomination dates the storm to 17 September 1815, and says the wooden tower "stood for only five years."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the storm toppled the tower, and that the storm surge funneled by the bay destroyed some 500 houses and flooded Providence.[^2]

The fog signal ran for long hours. The first trumpet went in during 1867, and in 1872 the nomination says two first-class steam whistles replaced it.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the fog signal ran about 600 hours a year. For fiscal 1895, the year ended 30 June 1895, the Board's report says the first-class steam siren, in duplicate, "was in operation about 934 hours and consumed some 63 tons of coal."[^2][^4]

LighthouseFriends says at least sixteen ships went aground or were wrecked within sight of the light in 1855 alone.[^2] Its partial list of wrecks on the shoals includes the Normandy (1864), American Eagle (1870), Mars (1892), Blue Jay (1896) and Amanda E. (1902).[^2] The sources cited here record no rescue by the light's keepers.

In September 1938 a hurricane hit New England.[^6][^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1938 says that serious damage was experienced at a number of stations in Rhode Island, among them Point Judith.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the storm destroyed 250 feet (76 m) of the seawall around the station, but that the lighthouse survived unscathed.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1899 the top half of the tower was painted brown, to give it a distinctive daymark, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The Board's report records a change of tower color on 15 May 1899.[^9]

A new fixed fourth-order Fresnel lens went into operation on 2 January 1907.[^2] In 1906 a bill was introduced in Congress to station a lightship off Point Judith, in addition to the lighthouse.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the proposal was dropped in 1908 after the Lighthouse Board objected that the lighthouse was sufficient.[^2]

The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1916 says that, at the close of the fiscal year, a concrete oil house with an asbestos shingle roof was under construction at Point Judith Light Station.[^5] The nomination describes a brick oil house built in 1917, and a brick fog-signal building built in 1923.[^1] The sources do not say whether the 1916 building is the brick oil house of 1917.

The first radio beacon at a Rhode Island light was established at Point Judith on 11 November 1931.[^1] Its two 60-foot (18 m) radio towers were taken down in 1974, because they were unsafe.[^1]

In 1954 the light was automated, and the keeper's dwelling was torn down soon afterwards.[^1][^2] After that, the nomination says, personnel from the Coast Guard station maintained the light and fog signal.[^1] The archive's roster and LighthouseFriends still list keepers after that date, up to Robert A. Bauer from 1961. The sources do not explain those entries.[^2][^3]

The lantern room and lens were taken out for a restoration in 2000 that lasted four months and cost $200,000, LighthouseFriends reports.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is Coast Guard property. The nomination lists its owner as the U.S. Government, Department of Transportation, and classes its access as "yes, restricted."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the grounds and tower are closed, and that as of 2013 the grounds appeared to be no longer accessible.[^2] A visitor guide says the lighthouse grounds are no longer open to the public, but that the lighthouse and the Coast Guard station can be seen from a small park before the gate.[^7] The archive's record still says the light is open to the public.[^3]

The light remains an active aid to navigation and still uses a fourth-order Fresnel lens, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] It is listed on the National Register of Historic Places.[^1] Visitors should view it from outside the Coast Guard gate, not from the station grounds.
