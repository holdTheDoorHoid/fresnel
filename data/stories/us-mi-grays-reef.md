Grays Reef Light Station stands on a square concrete-and-timber crib in the Grays Reef Passage, the deep-water gap near the western end of the Straits of Mackinac, in Emmet County, Michigan. For its first forty-odd years the reef was marked by a small steam-powered lightship, and for the four decades after 1937 by the Art Deco tower that is still there.[^1]

## The place

Grays Reef Passage is a narrow channel running between Grays Reef on the west and Vienna Shoal on the east. According to LighthouseFriends it is the only navigable opening for deep-draft vessels between the Straits of Mackinac and the waters east of Beaver Island and the Manitou Islands.[^1] The Light-House Board's 1869 annual report, as quoted by that site, described the reefs as rocky patches with only 5 feet (1.5 m) of water on them, and said they were "much dreaded, and probably have been the cause of a greater number of disasters than any other of the many dangers in this locality."[^1] The Board proposed a first-class iron buoy, and a buoy was laid on the reef.[^1]

## Building the light

On 2 March 1889 Congress appropriated $60,000 for a light and fog signal on the reef, with the same sum for Simmons Reef nearby. LighthouseFriends reports that the Board judged both too little for a permanent tower.[^1] The Board's report for the year ended June 1891 records that an act of 3 March 1891 let the $60,000 already voted be spent instead on three light-ships for Simmons Reef, White Shoal and Grays Reef.[^2] They were of special design, with wooden hulls 100 feet (30 m) long, 18 feet in beam and 14 feet deep, steam power for moving and for the fog signal, and a cost of $15,000 each.[^2] The Board said it was trying inexpensive light-ships "at the earnest request of the lake vessel-men."[^3]

Light-ship No. 57 took Grays Reef. The Board's list of new fog signals gives a 6-inch steam whistle there from 22 October 1891,[^4] while LighthouseFriends says the three ships were towed to their stations by the tender *Dahlia* on 24 October.[^1] The first season went badly. The Board reported "some trouble" with the new keepers and engineers: each of the three vessels left for winter quarters without orders before navigation closed, were sent back by the lighthouse inspector, and all the officers and crew but one were discharged, replaced by men who had "shown themselves more trustworthy."[^5] LighthouseFriends puts No. 57's early departure on 17 November 1891, when it went into Cheboygan.[^1]

The ship was a seasonal station. In the winter of 1892-93 she left on 7 December 1892, wintered at Cheboygan and was back on 23 April 1893, now with a fog bell as well.[^6] In the report for 1899 she is described as a wooden steam screw vessel of about 101 tons, which left on 14 December 1898 and went to Cheboygan, where she was fitted with hardwood floors in the cabin and staterooms and had her boiler covered with asbestos; she returned to her moorings on 28 April 1899.[^7] In the year ended June 1893 the steam whistle sounded for about 221 hours, burning about 7 tons of coal and a cord of wood.[^8]

LighthouseFriends reports that No. 57 served until 1923, when she was retired and replaced by a ship numbered 103, that a radiobeacon was added in 1930 and the light changed to electricity, and that the permanent lighthouse replaced the last lightship, No. 99, when it was commissioned.[^1] The sources do not agree on when the lamp went electric: the Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1925 lists Grays Reef lightship among the stations changing from acetylene to electric incandescent, five years after LighthouseFriends' date.[^9] A Milwaukee newspaper reported in May 1923 that the Gray's Reef lightship, "one of the oldest in service on the Great Lakes," had come into Milwaukee for repairs, with the relief ship stationed at the northeast end of Lake Michigan meanwhile.[^10]

The permanent station began in 1934. The site chosen lay well south and a little east of the lightship, where it could mark a new channel 3,000 feet (910 m) wide to be dredged through the reef in 1936.[^1] A timber crib 60 feet (18 m) square and 24 feet (7.3 m) deep was built at St. Ignace for $6,900 and sunk on 3 September 1934. Nine of its 24 outer pockets were filled with concrete at once and the rest weighted with temporary stone through the winter.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1936 describes the Grays Reef, North Manitou Shoal and Minneapolis Shoal stations as similar structures on timber cribs approximately 64 to 74 feet (20 to 23 m) square, built from Public Works Administration allotments, as part of a programme in which four of five new Lake Michigan stations replaced lightships.[^11] That article says the tower was nearing completion in 1936 but would probably not be commissioned until the next season, when the dredged channel opened.[^11] The Bulletin gives a height of 82 feet (25 m) for the structure, which the archive's place record also gives as the height of the light above mean high water.[^11][^14] LighthouseFriends gives the cost as about $220,000 and the builder as Greiling Brothers of Green Bay, Wisconsin, and reports that the station was commissioned on 1 April 1937.[^1] The living quarters sit in the upper part of the 29-foot (8.8 m) square base, with a steel tower rising from its centre to the lantern.[^1] The lens was a third-and-a-half-order Fresnel showing a red light, and the fog signal was a compressed-air diaphragm horn.[^1]

## The keepers

The lightship years are thinly recorded by name. A Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1916 notes Robert Bruce moving from engineer on Grays Reef Light Vessel No. 57 to a relief ship, and Angus H. Phillips promoted from North Manitou Light Vessel No. 56 to engineer on No. 57.[^12] In 1922 the master was P. D. Ance.[^13]

For the lighthouse, the US Lighthouse Society's archive lists keepers beginning with Ron Benjamin, 1936 to 1939, then Harry L. McRae in 1939 and Angus H. Phillips from 1940, with assistants including Peter O. Gallagher, Benjamin F. Aldrich and Homer R. Meverden.[^14] The archive's list starts in 1936 although the station was commissioned in 1937; the difference is unexplained. From 1960 the archive lists a rotating Coast Guard crew, and its head keepers in the 1960s include Elwood R. Wade, Robert J. Smith and others.[^14]

LighthouseFriends, which lists a 1966 newspaper article among its references, describes the routine in the 1960s. Elwood Wade was in charge in 1966, with a crew of four who ordinarily served three weeks on and then had a week off. The crew shared a single room for cooking, eating and relaxing, with two small bunk rooms opening off it. Someone was on watch in the radio room around the clock, in six-hour turns; each man washed his own clothes in the basement, the cooking rotated, and television took up most of the free time.[^1] Leon F. DeRosia, listed as head keeper in the mid-1950s, recalled in 1985 that "The monotony was terrible," and that the men were kept busy cleaning.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1913 reports that in a storm Grays Reef Light Vessel No. 57 was dragged a short distance from her station.[^15] On 28 August 1922, in a strong southwest gale, master P. D. Ance and the crew sighted a motor boat drifting toward White Shoal Reef; the Bulletin published his letter, and Secretary Hoover officially commended him for rescuing two men from the disabled launch *McTavish*.[^13][^16] On 9 July 1924 the passenger steamer *North American*, with 350 passengers, went aground on Grays Reef; the Chicago, Duluth and Georgian Bay Transit Company said she was floated the next day without damage.[^17] In May 1929 relief lightship No. 103, stationed temporarily at Grays Reef, was struck by lightning; no one was hurt, but "Streaks of fire went through the entire ship."[^18] (Calling No. 103 a relief ship in 1929 sits oddly with LighthouseFriends' statement that she replaced No. 57 in 1923; the sources do not reconcile the two.)

On the night of 6 May 1965 the crew were watching television when the 504-foot (154 m) freighter *J. E. Upson* struck the lighthouse in fog, with visibility between zero and 250 yards. The light, fog signal and radiobeacon were all running. The crib lost some concrete and a few handrails were crushed; the freighter, northbound, had her bow stem ruptured, backed away and made for Mackinaw City.[^1]

## After the keepers

In the first week of October 1976 the Coast Guard cutter *Buckhorn* took off the last crew, after an electronic fog detector and other equipment had been installed to run the station automatically. LighthouseFriends calls it the last manned offshore station in the northeast part of Lake Michigan.[^1] The radiobeacon tower on the roof was removed in August 2010.[^1]

On 1 May 2013 the General Services Administration declared the lighthouse excess to Coast Guard needs and offered it free to eligible public and non-profit bodies. When none took it, an online auction opened on 15 July 2016. It closed on 13 October 2016 with a high bid of $52,777 from Bobby Sager, who also owns lighthouses in Maine and Massachusetts.[^1]

## Today

The light station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2005 (reference 05001210).[^19] The lighthouse is privately owned and the tower is closed to visitors; the US Lighthouse Society archive also lists it as not open to the public.[^1][^14] It is best seen from the water or the air, and the archive lists it as an active aid to navigation.[^14] The station's Fresnel lens is on display at the Harsha House Museum in Charlevoix.[^1]
