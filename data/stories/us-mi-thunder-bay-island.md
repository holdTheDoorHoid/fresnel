Thunder Bay Island is a 215-acre limestone island on Lake Huron, about thirteen miles out from the harbor at Alpena, Michigan, and the outermost of a chain of islets that runs off the north point of Thunder Bay.[^1] A light has marked it since 1832, and the first tower on the site fell down.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends calls the surviving tower the second oldest on Lake Huron, while Terry Pepper describes the station as what would become the lake's third lighthouse.[^2][^1]

## The place

The island sits three miles east-northeast of the north point of Thunder Bay. Because it is the last of a group of islands joined to the shore by a shallow, rocky shoal, it was both a landmark for vessels turning north toward the Straits of Mackinac and a place that could wreck those that strayed too close.[^1] Congress heard memorials from shipping interests around the lakes asking for a light here and ordered its Commerce Committee to look into it on 6 January 1831.[^1] The Thunder Bay Island Preservation Society dates the appropriation of $5,000 to 1830; a history of the light by Lighthouse Friends gives 3 March 1831.[^3][^2]

The keepers were not the island's only residents. The Preservation Society says the island was made a federal reservation in 1842, and that by 1846 there were 160 settlers living there, with a fishing industry on the west side (the Alpena visitors' bureau dates the 160 to 1845); the Alpena visitors' bureau adds that they caught some twelve thousand barrels of fish a year from thirty-one boats, and that the people who had settled on federal land moved to nearby Sugar Island when the government moved against them.[^3][^4]

## Building the light

The contract went to Jeremiah Moors, a Detroit builder, and the crew worked through the 1831 season to raise a 40-foot (12 m) rubble-limestone tower and a one-and-a-half-story dwelling.[^1] What happened next is told two ways. Lighthouse Friends says the first tower, on a site picked by the government's agent, was nearly finished when a gale sent a heavy sea over that part of the island and it toppled, and that Moors then built a second tower farther from the lake in 1832 from the same stone.[^2] Terry Pepper's history, drawn from the congressional journals and the Light-House Board's reports, has the first keeper taking office in February 1832 and reads the evidence as showing that the tower did not last much longer than that keeper: Moors' petition of 26 January 1834, presented by Congressman Austin Wing, asked to be repaid for the cost of rebuilding after the tower "fell in consequence of being located on a site unfit for the same."[^1] Moors was still petitioning in 1855, when Senator Lewis Cass took up his cause.[^1]

Lieutenant James T. Homans, inspecting the Great Lakes lights in November 1838, found the lake lapping at the foundation and called the tower "a critical situation, and may, if not soon protected by some barrier, share the fate of one formerly near this site." He asked for $800 for stone-filled timber cribs and suggested rebuilding on higher ground behind.[^1] Pepper takes it that the cribs were built and worked, since an 1850 inspection reported only that "Everything here is in good order"; Lighthouse Friends says no record of protective work survives.[^1][^2]

A fog bell was paid for in March 1853 ($2,500) and went into service in March 1855.[^1] In 1857 the tower was raised to a height of 50 feet (15 m) by encasing its upper 14 feet (4 m) in brick and carrying the brick above the old top, and a fourth-order Fresnel lens by Sautter of Paris with six bull's-eye panels was set in a new iron lantern.[^1] Lighthouse Friends measures the refurbished tower at 57 feet (17 m) to the top of the ventilator ball.[^2] It showed a fixed white light varied by flashes, which Pepper gives a range of 14 miles.[^1] Congress appropriated $8,000 in March 1867 for a new keeper's dwelling, and a crew built it in 1868: a two-story brick house, 43 by 28 feet (13 by 8.5 m), joined to the tower by a covered passage.[^1][^2]

Steam arrived in 1871. Following a decision in 1870 to replace the bell, a work crew built an iron-sheathed frame building for the boilers and the ten-inch steam whistles (Lighthouse Friends mentions a single boiler), which sounded a pattern of an 8-second blast, 10 seconds of silence, a 2-second blast and 40 seconds of silence.[^1][^2] Pepper adds that keepers sounded it in fog, snow squalls and the smoke of forest fires.[^1]

## The keepers

The first keeper is disputed. The Preservation Society and Pepper both say Joseph Duchene was named first and left within days (the society) or after three months, on 1 May 1832 (Pepper), and was replaced by Jesse Muncey.[^3][^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive, which draws on the keeper registers, lists instead "Israel Noble (1832-1833)" and then "Jesse Muncey (1833-1843)."[^5] Muncey was followed by William Terry (1843-1845), who died on the island; the society says his wife kept the light until a replacement came.[^5][^3] Joseph J. Malden then served twice, 1845 to 1851 and 1853 to 1860.[^5]

For most of the early years the station had a head keeper alone. An assistant was added in 1860 to help with the fog bell, according to Lighthouse Friends, and the archive lists Peter Panquin as the first.[^2][^5] Alonzo E. Persons took charge in 1861 and stayed until 1873, with John D. Persons as first assistant from 1867 to 1874; the Preservation Society says John was Alonzo's son and afterwards became captain of the life-saving crew on the island.[^5][^3] Patrick H. McGuire was head keeper from 1874 to 1882, with Catharine McGuire listed as first assistant over the same years.[^5]

The archive then shows no head keeper until Michael M. Cooney in 1894. Alpena's weekly newspaper fills part of the gap: it named "Captain Sinclair" of the Thunder Bay Island light in 1887, 1888 and 1891, and noted that William H. Holmes, the assistant, would "have charge of the light hous" while Sinclair went to Saginaw for the winter of 1887.[^6][^7][^8] The papers give no first name, but Lighthouse Friends' keeper list has John Sinclair, Jr. as head keeper from 1882 to 1894, which fits the gap.[^2]

Lighthouse Friends calls William R. Bennetts the longest-serving keeper of the station, though the sources differ on his start: 1901 in the archive, 1902 in Lighthouse Friends, both ending in 1919.[^5][^2] Lighthouse Friends adds that he had been an assistant at Manitou Island and Whitefish Point on Lake Superior before this, and went on to be the first head keeper of Sand Hills Light.[^2] A second assistant was added in 1906.[^2] Paul J. Klebba held the light from 1919 to 1929, and the index to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1924 to 1929 lists an efficiency flag awarded to him and to the station.[^5][^9] Peter Romes was second assistant from 1934 to 1937.[^5] When the Coast Guard took over aids to navigation in 1939, Pepper names William De Rusha as keeper, with assistants James MacDonald and Russell Perry, who appear to have left within a year; the archive instead has Mathew Storback as head keeper from 1937 to 1942.[^1][^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sea could sweep over the low island. Pepper reports that on 27 November 1889 waves scoured the island and carried away the tramway and its cart; the Preservation Society places a storm that destroyed docks, buildings and the boat basin in 1890, which may be the same one.[^1][^3] The Light-House Board's report for the year to June 1891 records that a working party landed on 20 September of that year (so 1890) and rebuilt part of the tramway "washed away during the fall of 1889," also putting the fog signal in order and deepening the well that fed its boilers; Pepper dates this visit to 1891.[^10][^1] In the year to June 1892 a new landing dock 100 feet (30 m) long was built half a mile north of the old wharf.[^11]

The fog signal ran hard. The Board counted 280 hours of steam whistle in the year to June 1892, 266 hours in the year to June 1894 and 306 in 1895; in the year to June 1896 it ran 408 hours and burned about 24 tons of coal, and in the next year 224 hours and about 19 tons.[^11][^12][^13][^14] Pepper says the signals ran a station-record 444 hours in 1907, and that the keepers had to shovel the 25 tons of coal this needed.[^1]

The Life-Saving Service opened a station three-eighths of a mile west of the light in 1876, which Pepper puts down to the wrecks in the area.[^1][^3] Its reports record what the island's seas did. On the night of 20 November 1880 the mercury stood at ten degrees above zero, a gale was blowing from the west, and ice banks seven to ten feet high had formed along the shore, when a distress whistle sounded: the tug *Aimee* of Alpena was ashore about fifty yards from the station. Keeper John D. Persons dragged his skiff over the ice to her and found her "a complete mass of ice, in which the pilot-house particularly was so imbedded that it had to be chopped out to extricate Captain Howe."[^15] On 4 October 1883, at ten at night, the crew heard steamer whistles off the east side of the island and found the steam barge *James Davidson* of St Clair, with eighteen aboard and a coal cargo, stranded and sunk on the southeast end, with her tow, the schooner *Middlesex*, aground beside her. They shifted the schooner's cargo aft and hauled her off.[^16]

In March 1914, at the start of the shipping season, the fishing tug *Fannie A.* took Captain Bennett, "llghtkeeper on Thunder Bay island," out to his post, and on the way back was caught in ice.[^17][^2] The L'Anse newspaper of 4 April 1914 has the four men forced to abandon the tug in Thunder Bay and take to a ten-foot yawl; they forced their way "through three miles of heavy ice" to a small uninhabited island, where they lit a bonfire and huddled around it until a tug rescued them.[^17] A November 1913 storm, the Preservation Society adds, destroyed the rail system at the south dock, and rebuilding it took nearly two years.[^3]

## After the keepers

The light's machinery was changed again and again. Pepper has the lens converted to incandescent oil vapor on 21 May 1913 and gives the new strength as 5,600 candlepower; Lighthouse Friends says it rose from 5,600 to 50,000.[^1][^2] Pepper has diaphones replacing the steam whistles in 1921; Lighthouse Friends gives 1 June 1923 for the switch to an air diaphone.[^1][^2] The Bulletin's 1924-29 index lists the station as "to have radio beacon," and Lighthouse Friends gives the start of the radiobeacon as 1 December 1924.[^9][^2] The tower was painted white in 1938, hiding the cream-colored brick.[^1] The Coast Guard took over in 1939.[^1] A Coast Guard station built on the west side in 1930 was burned down along with the old life-saving building in 1962 to prevent vandalism.[^3] A Coast Guardsman's account on the Preservation Society's site describes four men assigned to the island, each on duty three weeks and off one, and, on 10 April 1963, the buoy tender taking them in as far as it could through ice that surrounded the island, after which they were put out on the ice with their sea bags and supplies to walk to the island.[^3]

The archive records the station as automated in 1980; Pepper and Lighthouse Friends say 1983, and the Preservation Society's history gives both, saying the station was decommissioned in 1980 and the beacon automated in 1983.[^5][^1][^2][^3] It went on the National Register of Historic Places in 1984 according to the Preservation Society; Lighthouse Friends puts it four years after the 1983 closing.[^3][^2] Pepper says the empty station stood for almost fifteen years, damaged by weather and vandals.[^1] Local people formed the Thunder Bay Island Preservation Society in 1996, took a ten-year lease from the Coast Guard in 1997 (later extended), repaired the tower in 2005 (Lighthouse Friends describes masonry restoration in the summer of 2004) and replaced the keeper's quarters roof in 2006.[^3][^2]

## Today

The U.S. Lighthouse Society archive lists the light as an active aid to navigation with a 190 mm optic as its current optic; the original was a fourth-order Fresnel.[^5] The Preservation Society says the island stopped being a federal reservation in 2014 on a property transfer, and the Fish and Wildlife Service oversees it.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says Alpena Township took ownership of the lighthouse at a ceremony on 25 October 2014, with the Preservation Society managing it.[^2] Access is limited to the Coast Guard, the Fish and Wildlife Service and Preservation Society members; the archive lists it as not open to the public, and it lies some fourteen miles off Alpena's harbor, reachable only by boat or plane.[^4][^5][^18][^2] Lighthouse Friends lists the grounds as open and the dwelling and tower as closed.[^2] A camera tower installed in 2014 gives a view of the island that anyone can open online.[^18]
