Cox Reef Light stands on a reef off the east side of Berens Island, in the northern basin of Lake Winnipeg, where the Berens River reaches the lake.[^1] The Department of Marine built a wooden tower and keeper's dwelling there in 1905. That building is no longer standing. The present light is on a skeletal tower that can be reached only by boat.[^27][^28][^30]

## The place

The Berens River rises in Ontario, flows west through eastern Manitoba, and enters the northern basin of the lake. Berens Island lies about sixteen kilometres offshore from the river's mouth.[^1] The Hudson's Bay Company used the river as a trade route.[^2]

The first light in the area stood on Swampy Island, the popular but unofficial name for Berens Island.[^4] It went into service in June 1884 as Manitoba's first lighthouse.[^3] Manitoba History argues that the first tower almost certainly stood on a small island about a mile to the south, which later became known as Plunkett Island.[^4] Harry E. Plunkett was hired as the first keeper, at $350 a year.[^5]

A Toronto newspaper reported in April 1888 that the Swampy Island light had been destroyed on 12 March.[^6] Manitoba History says no official record of a rebuild has come to light, but that there is no doubt one took place.[^7] A handwritten note in the Department's 1904 list of lights said the tower on Plunkett Island had burned and would be replaced by a new structure on Cox Reef.[^8]

## Building the light

The Department's report describes the 1905 light as a combined lighthouse tower and keepers' dwelling: a wooden building with a kitchen annex, square in plan, topped by a square wooden lantern, about 12.5 m (41 ft) from its base to the lantern ventilator.[^9] John W. Scott of Selkirk built it under contract for $4,100.[^10] LighthouseFriends gives 20 September 1905 as the day it was placed in operation, and says the Plunkett light was discontinued then.[^11] The Department's next report says the Plunkett light had been permanently discontinued, replaced by the new light on Cox Reef.[^12] The Department's list printed in 1912 includes a hand fog horn at Cox Reef.[^13]

## The keepers

Harry Plunkett, keeper of the earlier light, died suddenly at his post in April 1906. Manitoba History gives that date and quotes a missionary's later recollection of the death.[^14] The same article says a John Doggie of Rabbit Point was put in temporary charge of Plunkett's duties, but it does not name him at Cox Reef.[^15] LighthouseFriends lists Doggie as a keeper at Cox Reef in 1906.[^15]

The Department's 1912 list names William Dore as keeper at Cox Reef, appointed on 6 August 1908.[^16] Dore died on 13 December 1909. A. A. T. McKay, who had been posted to George Island, was appointed to succeed him at an initial salary of $460.[^17]

LighthouseFriends also lists John Thomas (1906 to 1908), A. J. Anderson (1913 to 1922), A. M. Anderson (1923 to 1925), Donald Sayer (1926 to at least 1940) and Charles Settee (1957 to 1967).[^18][^19][^21] The 1935 newspapers spell the keeper's name Donald Sayre and call him the "chief lighthouse tender at Cox's Reef."[^20]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In November 1935 a blizzard hid the fate of ten men stranded on Lake Winnipeg. The United Press reported on 14 November that two of them were the lighthouse tenders on "lonely Cox's Reef", 225 km (140 miles) north of the mouth of the Red River.[^22] They were Donald Sayre and an assistant. LighthouseFriends identifies the keeper as Donald Sayer, and says he and his assistant had a "harrowing experience" at the close of the 1935 navigation season.[^19] The others were Capt. Roy Purvis and the crew of the ice-bound steamer Lu-Berc, who had left more than two weeks earlier to bring the tenders out.[^22]

On 15 November the Washington Daily News reported that an airplane had flown north with food and fuel for the two keepers, and that reports from it were awaited. Its pilot was ready to drop the supplies if the ice proved too thin to hold his ski-equipped plane. Marine Department officials said Sayre might be alone at his outpost, and that an assistant first reported with him may have left before heavy snow cut off communication.[^23]

On 16 November a United Press story from Gypsumville reported that Capt. Purvis and his crew had reached the settlement after a fourteen-day ordeal. Ice had jammed the lake and ripped the hull of the Lu-Berc, and the crew abandoned her and then their dog team. The last stretch was made in an open sleigh.[^24] How the two men at Cox's Reef fared is not recorded in these reports.

The 1935 wire report also repeated a fishing-colony legend that the light was haunted by the ghost of a wooden-legged man named Plunkett, who had first tended it.[^25] Manitoba History says the legend wrongly connected Harry Plunkett with another lighthouse.[^26]

## After the keepers

Rowlett's directory says the original house was demolished after the light was deactivated, and that its foundations can be seen on satellite images.[^27] LighthouseFriends reports that in 2020 flashing white lights on skeletal towers marked both Cox Reef and the north-east end of Berens Island.[^29]

## Today

Rowlett describes the present light as a skeletal tower about 14 m (46 ft) high, flashing white every four seconds. It stands on a small island in the channel between Berens Island and the north-east shore of the lake.[^28] The Canadian Coast Guard owns and manages the station. It is reached only by boat. The site is open, and the tower is closed to visitors.[^30] LighthouseFriends says the modern light is best seen from a boat.[^31]
