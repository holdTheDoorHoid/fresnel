Little Cumberland Island Light is a brick tower, 60 feet (18.3 m) high, at the north end of Little Cumberland Island on the Georgia coast. It was built in 1838 to mark the entrance to St. Andrew Sound, and its light was dimmed in 1915. The tower now stands on private land owned by an island association. The National Register nomination counts it as one of five remaining lighthouses in Georgia.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Little Cumberland lies just north of Cumberland Island, and the two are separated by Christmas Creek.[^4] Congress recognized the need for a light at the entrance to St. Andrew Sound as early as 1802.[^5] The nomination says that over the next 35 years more than $55,000 was appropriated for a light.[^6] In 1817 John Floyd sold six acres on the north end of the island to the government for $500.[^7]

## Building the light

During 1838 a lighthouse complex went up on the six acres, with the tower, a keeper's cottage, a warehouse and a privy, at a cost of $8,025.[^8] The builder was Joseph Hastings of Boston.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the light began operation on 26 June 1838.[^10] It was a fixed white light of the third order, 78 feet (24 m) above the sea, and the nomination says it could be seen for 14½ nautical miles all around the horizon.[^11][^12] LighthouseFriends gives the tower as 61 feet, one foot taller than the nomination's 60.[^10]

Shortly after the 1867 repairs, the nomination says, encroachment by the sea from the northwest was serious enough to call for a large brick wall, built two feet deep below the tower.[^13] A brick retaining wall dates from 1876. In 1879 the tower's old timber framing began to show need for repair, and iron structural members were installed.[^14][^15] A replacement keeper's cottage was built in 1881.[^16] The Light-House Board's report for 1896–97 records the station's new works: "A new boathouse, 185 feet of picket fence, 125 feet of low plank walk, and 200 feet of sand fence were built."[^17]

## The keepers

The first keeper, David Thompson, served from 1838 to 1849 at $400 a year, LighthouseFriends says.[^18][^19] Not long after William F. Kelley took over in 1849, someone entered the tower at night with false keys, turned the cocks on the oil casks, and let the oil run out onto the brick floor. Kelley recovered forty-seven gallons, LighthouseFriends says.[^20]

The archive's roster lists 42 stints on the station from 1838 to 1915: 22 head keepers and 20 first assistants. By the archive's own dates, 18 of the 22 head keeper stints lasted less than five years.[^19] James A. Clubb, a pilot, became keeper in 1859 and served until 1861, according to the roster.[^21][^22][^32] John Robertson was principal keeper from 1906 until the light closed in 1915. Marvin B. Wilder was his first assistant from 1910.[^24][^25] The nomination says the last keeper was removed in 1915, when the government closed the station.[^26]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In November 1858 the Wanderer, a slave ship, reached the Georgia coast with captives from Africa.[^27] "The Wanderer was the last ship to bring enslaved people from Africa to Georgia," the New Georgia Encyclopedia says, and it arrived at Jekyll Island on 28 November.[^28]

Accounts of the pilotage differ. The nomination says that keeper James Clubb, "a retired pilot," guided the ship through St. Andrew Sound on 29 November.[^29] The nomination thus calls Clubb the keeper at the time of the voyage, while the roster starts his keepership in 1859.[^21] LighthouseFriends says Clubb was talked into piloting the ship and became keeper the next year.[^23] The historical marker on Jekyll Island gives another account. Its captain, William Corrie, rowed to Cumberland Island and found Horatio Harris, the island's assistant light keeper. The pilot he needed was James Clubb, and the two men rowed across the sound to Jekyll to find him.[^30][^31][^32]

In 1862, during the Civil War, Union ships were in St. Andrew Sound and the light was still intact. In a letter to the Light-House Board of 5 March 1862, Flag Officer S. F. Du Pont wrote that "the light-house on Little Cumberland is in a perfect state of preservation," and that the lens had been "carefully taken down by the light-house keeper, who came to me in St. Andrew Sound."[^33][^34] LighthouseFriends says Clubb rowed out and told them he had taken the lens out and sent it to Brunswick, as the Confederacy had ordered.[^35] The light was repaired after the war damage and came back on 1 September 1867.[^36][^37]

In 1912 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin commended John Robertson, keeper, for his efforts in trying to save an abandoned boathouse from fire.[^38]

On 5 January 1915 the little tug Rambler was wrecked off Little Cumberland.[^39] The Brunswick News reported on 7 February that four men and two deck hands lost their lives in the wreck, and that the bodies of the two deck hands were found on Little Cumberland a day after it.[^39][^40][^41] A body found on Cumberland beach the Friday before was identified on 9 February as one of the crew.[^42] LighthouseFriends gives a different account: four men aboard, all of whom perished, and only one body recovered.[^43] Keeper Robertson and his assistant Wilder sheltered the searching parties, LighthouseFriends says.[^44] The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1915 lists the two men against "Parties searching bodies of crew of Rambler," and gives the nature of their assistance as shelter for those parties.[^45]

## After the keepers

Congress decommissioned the light in 1913 and ordered the structure sold, but the light was not dimmed until 15 March 1915.[^46][^47] A 1937 Lighthouse Service Bulletin article says the lighthouses at Bull Bay and Little Cumberland Island had been discontinued over 20 years earlier, and that at Little Cumberland an unwatched flashing acetylene light on a marine site not far distant replaced it.[^48][^49]

In 1923–24 the R. L. Phillips Company of Brunswick bought the tower and six acres at auction for $800.[^50] In 1961 the Little Cumberland Island Association, with sixty-five members, bought the island, lighthouse included.[^51] The association's July 1967 newsletter announced that the two derelict buildings at the lighthouse were to come down.[^52] The keeper's residence was torn down in 1968.[^53] By early 1969 the association had restored the tower. Sand had drifted twenty feet (6 m) on one side and blocked the entrance when work began. Water had damaged the lower spiral stair, which was replaced with a replica, and the top was restored with eight jalousie windows.[^54][^55][^56] LighthouseFriends says the tower was placed on the National Register in 1989.[^57]

## Today

The Little Cumberland Island Association owns the island and the lighthouse. Access is restricted to homeowners and their guests, LighthouseFriends says, and the grounds and tower are closed.[^3][^58][^59] LighthouseFriends photographed the tower from the water, on a boat trip from Jekyll Island.[^60] The association has no plans to relight the tower, the nomination says, because a light could confuse mariners and become a death trap for migrating birds.[^61]

The site lies behind a sand dune, and the foundation of the keeper's cottage can still be seen in part.[^62][^63] The tower has no surviving mechanical systems.[^64] From the top, the nomination says, one can see St. Andrew Sound to the north and the Atlantic to the east.[^65]
