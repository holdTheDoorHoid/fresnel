Sturgeon Point Light stands on a point about four miles north of Harrisville, on the Lake Huron shore of Alcona County, Michigan.[^1][^2] Its brick tower was finished in November 1870, to mark a reef that runs out from the point into the lake.[^1][^2][^3] The light is still operational. The Alcona Historical Society runs a museum in the keeper's house, open on weekends from Memorial Day to Labor Day, and the tower can be climbed periodically through the summer.[^3]

## The place

Two earlier lights left a gap on this stretch of Lake Huron. Thunder Bay Island had a light from 1832, and Tawas Point from 1857, but LighthouseFriends says neither could fully light the stretch of roughly fifty-five miles between them.[^1] In 1866 the Lighthouse Board proposed a light near the middle of that gap, at Sturgeon Point.[^1] The Board's argument was that vessels often left the direct course, and that a light would let them take a new departure and shorten the crossing at the mouth of Saginaw Bay.[^1]

The hazard was the reef. The Alcona Historical Society says it extends a mile and a half into Lake Huron from the point.[^3]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated the $15,000 the Board had asked for on 2 March 1867.[^1] Plans were approved on 6 July 1868, and a deed for just over sixty acres on the point was formalized on 22 September 1868. Work began in the spring of 1869.[^1][^3]

The tower is circular and brick. It is 16 feet (4.9 m) across at the base and 70 feet 9 inches (21.6 m) tall, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] Its lantern room is decagonal and cast iron, and LighthouseFriends says it came from a lighthouse in Oswego, New York.[^1] The lamp was a third-and-a-half-order Henry-Lepaute Fresnel lens, mounted on a cast-iron pedestal.[^1] The archive's place record gives the original optic as sixth order, which disagrees with LighthouseFriends.[^2] A covered passage linked the tower to a two-story brick dwelling of eight rooms.[^1]

The tower was completed in early November 1870. LighthouseFriends says the first keeper, Perley Silverthorn, arrived late, so the first lighting was put off until the opening of navigation the following spring.[^1] The Alcona Historical Society says the light became operational in the spring of 1870.[^3] The two accounts differ by a year, and this story reports both.

The lake was the continuing problem. LighthouseFriends says that in 1885 five cribs were built along the shore, because the lake had crept to within forty feet (12 m) of the tower.[^1] It also says the lens was damaged in 1887, and that the old lens was shipped to the general lighthouse depot on Staten Island for repair.[^1] A brick-lined circular iron oil house was built in 1893, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] The Light-House Board's report, in a volume labelled 1891-1892, records that the metal work for a circular iron oil house for the station had been delivered.[^6]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists Perley Silverthorn as keeper from 1869 to 1873, Noah T. Farr from 1873 to 1876, John Pasque from 1876 to 1882, and Louis J. Cardy Sr. from 1882 to 1913.[^2]

Silverthorn was removed as keeper in 1873.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he returned to the point three years later as the first keeper of the Life-Saving Station set up beside the lighthouse.[^1] Farr stayed on for almost three years and then resigned.[^1]

John Pasque took charge in 1876. LighthouseFriends, quoting a local newspaper of 14 September 1877, reports that a cartridge exploded in his hand and shattered several of his fingers.[^1] In 1882 he moved on to head the newly finished Stannard Rock Lighthouse, and Louis Cardy was hired in his place.[^1]

Cardy kept the light from 1882 to 1913. The archive gives those dates, and LighthouseFriends says he served thirty-two years.[^2][^1] He died in November 1913, aged eighty-one, a few weeks after leaving the station.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The steamer Marine City burned in late August 1880 on this shore. The sources differ on whether she burned off Alcona or off Sturgeon Point. The Life-Saving Service's report for 1881 dates the fire to 29 August and says the ship took fire off Alcona, about two miles from land.[^4] The report says a portion of the crew of Station No. 5, Tenth District, which it places at Sturgeon Point, rowed out in the surf-boat and picked up five people. It adds that this was "not within the scope of the Service," and it gives nine people lost.[^4]

The Cheboygan *Northern Tribune* of 4 September 1880 reported that a later dispatch had said "fifty or sixty lives had been lost," and then said that this report "proved untrue." A first-person account printed in the same issue said the Sturgeon Point life-saving crew "reached us soon after the Vulcan," and that eight were missing.[^5] LighthouseFriends dates the fire to 28 August, places it off Sturgeon Point, and gives twenty people lost out of 160 on board.[^1] The sources disagree on the date, on how many people were aboard, and on how many died.[^1][^4][^5]

On 31 May 1913 the tender Amaranth, placing buoys to mark the reef, went hard aground off Sturgeon Point in fog. LighthouseFriends says the lifesaving crew from the point went out to help, and a tug from Alpena was sent to free the tender.[^1]

## After the keepers

On 15 April 1913 the light began showing a white flash every three seconds instead of a fixed white, using an automated acetylene apparatus.[^1] The Bulletin of 1913 records the change from oil to acetylene at Sturgeon Point.[^7] Cardy was not replaced. LighthouseFriends says the coastguardsmen at the neighboring station were assigned to keep watch on the light.[^1]

The Alcona Historical Society says the light was electrified and automated in 1939, and that the last personnel left in 1941. That conflicts with the 1913 automation described above, and the story gives both dates.[^3][^2][^1] The society also says the Coast Guard buildings were later destroyed and the lighthouse itself was severely vandalized.[^3]

By the time the Alcona Historical Society took a lease in 1982, LighthouseFriends says, the station was badly run down. Volunteers restored the buildings in a three-year project.[^1] The lighthouse was awarded to the Michigan Department of Natural Resources in 2005 under federal transfer rules set by the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000, and the Alcona Historical Society still manages and interprets the site.[^1]

## Today

The Alcona Historical Society's visitor page invites people to tour the 1870-built lighthouse. The page gives its 2026 season hours as Friday, Saturday and Sunday, 11 a.m. to 4 p.m., and says the tower climb costs $5.00 for adults and closes at 3:30 p.m.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the keeper's house, now a maritime museum, and the tower are open to the public during the summer, and that the grounds are open all year.[^1]

The society says the lens is still in the tower but no longer in operation.[^3] LighthouseFriends says it is not certain whether the original lens returned after the 1887 repair, but a lens of that description was in the lantern room in 1910 and remains there today.[^1] Because the schedule is seasonal, visitors should check the society's current hours before they go.[^3]
