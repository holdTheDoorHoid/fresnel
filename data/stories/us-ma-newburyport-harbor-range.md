Newburyport's two range lights were lit in June 1873 to guide ships up the Merrimac River to the town.[^3] The front light stood on Bayley's Wharf until 1964, when it was moved onto Coast Guard grounds.[^2] The rear light, a brick tower on Water Street, still stands.[^1][^3] Both lights were decommissioned in 1961.[^3] From 1886 to 1933, with one break, the keeper's post was held by the Barrett family, father and son.[^3][^4]

## The place

A range is a pair of lights, one higher than the other, that a mariner lines up to find the middle of a channel.[^3] LighthouseFriends describes range lights as pairs meant "to line one above the other to indicate the center of a channel."[^3] The nomination says the pair helped mariners "to avoid the dangerous" reef at Goose Rocks.[^2]

Before the town's own pair, the two lighthouses on Plum Island at the river mouth were the harbour's formal lights. LighthouseFriends dates them to 1788, and Weiss's 1926 history gives the same year.[^3][^9] Putnam's 1917 history dates the Plum Island lights to 1789, and says they were built by Massachusetts.[^8] The sources disagree by a year. Putnam also says that the small range lights are "at present on the north side of the river." He does not say whether those are the same lights as the 1789 pair.[^8] Putnam's "Newburyport Harbor Light" is "a single tower, last rebuilt in 1898."[^8] That light is not one of the range lights, and it is not covered here.

In the town itself the range lights were privately maintained for several years before the government took them over. LighthouseFriends reports that in 1871 the citizens of Newburyport petitioned for federal charge of the lights. In June 1872 Congress appropriated $10,000 "for re-establishing and setting up two small beacon lights in harbor of Newburyport, the site of one of which has been washed away by a storm."[^3] The land came from Robert Bayley, who owned Bayley's Wharf. He sold two parcels to the government in 1873 to erect the range lights, and the nomination says, "Both were erected the same year."[^1][^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends prints the Board's 1873 description. The front light was "on an iron tower, conical in form, 14 feet 6 inches high." The rear light was "on a brick tower, pyramidal in form, 32 feet high."[^3] In 1901 the front light's height was raised eleven feet (3.4 m) with a wooden top on the iron base, and the rear tower was extended nine feet (2.7 m).[^3] The National Register nominations, both recorded in 1981, give the front light as a 35-foot (10.7 m) frame of steel plate lined with brick, and the rear tower as 53 feet (16.2 m) high.[^2][^1] The 1873 and nomination figures for the rear tower do not agree, and the sources do not settle which is right.

The lights burned gas from the city supply, as the 1910 inspector wrote.[^3] The Commissioner of Lighthouses listed the "Newburyport Upper Harbor Range" as two lights on coal gas in 1915 and again in 1916.[^10][^11] The 1889 history by Arnold Burges Johnson counts Newburyport among the few stations then lit by gas: gas was "now used in but three stations, namely, Cleveland, Ohio, Alexandria, Va., and Newburyport, Mass."[^7] The rear light's 1933 entry in the nomination says "Candlepower was changed from 2000 to 690," and that the illuminant changed from coal gas to electricity in the same year.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the front light always showed a fixed red light, and the rear showed green until 15 March 1907, and red after that.[^3] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1891 lists a "reflector light" for the "Newburyport Harbor Range (front), entrance to the Merrimac River," in September 1890.[^13]

## The keepers

Local caretakers looked after the lights first.[^3] The first keeper, George Stickney, "started at an annual salary of $250 and served until 1886."[^3][^4] The archive lists him from 1873.[^4]

Matthew F. Barrett followed him, serving from 1886 to 1889, and then again from 1893 "to 1908, when he died." Edwin F. Hunt held the post between the two Barrett stints, from 1889 to 1893. The archive's record for the front light lists the same five men, which suggests one keeper tended both lights, though the sources do not say so.[^3][^4][^5]

Barrett's son, Bernard W. Barrett, "replaced his father as keeper of the range lights in 1908."[^3] His pay was "reduced in 1910" after an inspection by Wilson W. Buchanan, who wrote that the two lights were "burning gas from the city supply" and that "there is little work attached to them." Buchanan recommended that the keeper's post be abolished in favour of a laborer in charge at $20 a month. LighthouseFriends quotes his remark on the keeper: "I am told that the present keeper is a bank clerk."[^3] Bernard Barrett's tenure "was discontinued on April 15, 1933, after the range lights had been electrified."[^3][^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

No wreck or rescue at the range lights appears in the sources cited here.

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1895 describes a "Salisbury Beach range (front) light, entrance to Newburyport Harbor, Massachusetts" that was "Carried away in the storm October 1, and reestablished October 7, 1894."[^6] The report does not name the Newburyport range lights, and neither National Register nomination mentions a storm.[^1][^2] Whether this light was one of the range lights is not settled by the sources.

## After the keepers

The Board decommissioned both range lights in 1961.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the rear light was sold to a private party soon after, and the nomination dates the sale of the brick tower to 1964.[^3][^1] The nomination dates the front light's move from Bayley's Wharf onto Coast Guard grounds to 1964. Its 1964 entry describes photographs of "what appears to be the removal of the range from Bayley's Wharf to Coast Guard property," and says the front light's foundation was built when the tower was moved.[^2] The Merrimac River Coast Guard Station next to it was built in 1972.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says the front light "suffered severe fire damage in the 1990s" and was later "restored to its original form with a replica lantern room."[^3] In 2009 the rear tower "received a new exterior paint job and a complete interior makeover of the lantern room," and a new heat pump was installed so that dining could be offered year-round.[^3]

The Lighthouse Preservation Society, a non-profit that runs the dinners, says its mission is to "preserve historic lighthouse structures for future generations."[^12]

## Today

The front light is Coast Guard property.[^3] LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds and tower are closed, and that the light "can be seen through a fence from the parking lot north of the rear range light."[^3] Access to the Coast Guard station is restricted.[^2]

The rear light is privately owned and not open to the public.[^4] Its top room is used for dinners that must be booked in advance through the Lighthouse Preservation Society,[^3][^12] which describes "A totally unique dining experience at the top of the Newburyport Rear Range Lighthouse."[^12] Neither tower is open for casual visits.[^3][^4]
