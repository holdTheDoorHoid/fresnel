The Pointe-des-Monts lighthouse stands on a small rocky islet at the north shore of the St. Lawrence, where the river's estuary gives way to the Gulf, in the municipality of Baie-Trinité on the Côte-Nord.[^1][^2][^3] Its stone tower was built in 1829 and 1830, and it was the second light on the river after the one on Île Verte.[^1][^3] The tower is now a museum that visitors can climb.[^6]

## The place

Early geographers, going back to Samuel de Champlain himself, treated this point as the dividing line between the river and the gulf, the official history of the lighthouse says (translation).[^5] The Quebec heritage record gives the reason it was chosen. The point was selected in 1826 for a second lighthouse, because navigation there was "particulièrement difficile" (particularly difficult), and because sheltered bays gave ships a place to take refuge in bad weather (translation).[^1]

The first light on the river had been set up on Île Verte between 1806 and 1809, the register says.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that John Lambly, harbour master for Quebec, told a committee of the House of Assembly in 1828 that lights were needed at three places, including this one.[^3]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says construction began in July 1829 under Charles Touchette, and the register dates the work to 1829 and 1830.[^3][^1] The work drew complaints. William Lampson held a lease giving him hunting, fishing and trading rights over the King's Posts territory, which included this point. He petitioned for compensation, saying the work had already scared off seals and affected the natives, as LighthouseFriends puts it.[^3] A Royal Navy cartographer, Captain Bayfield, argued that the site lay too far east for eastbound ships to see the light. Lambly agreed, but pointed out that westbound ships, the more urgent case, could see it, and the site stayed where it was.[^3]

The contractor first tried to build in local granite, which proved too hard to work. Limestone was brought from Montreal, with white fire brick for the outer layer.[^3] Lambly left Quebec at the end of July 1830 with the lantern room and the lighting apparatus, which had come from England. At the site he found the top of the tower had to be altered to take the lantern, so the light was not first shown until 20 September 1830.[^3][^1] LighthouseFriends gives the total cost as £4,727.[^3]

The tower is conical and built of stone, with walls 1.8 m (six feet) thick at the base. LighthouseFriends gives its height as 27 m (90 ft), tapering from 12 m (40 ft) across at the base to 6 m (20 ft) at the lantern.[^3] The register describes a stone tower of seven levels, covered in cedar shingles, painted white, with two red bands and an octagonal metal lantern.[^1] The first lamps were seventeen Argand lamps, each with a parabolic reflector 53 cm (21 inches) across.[^3] In 1852 a Mr. Poitras clapboarded the tower to keep out water, LighthouseFriends says, and two red stripes were painted on the day-mark in 1881.[^3] When a triple-flashing catoptric apparatus replaced the original in 1914, a hole was cut through the floors so that a weight could fall down the tower and turn the clockwork.[^3]

The keeper's house was built in 1911 and 1912 and stands against the tower.[^1][^4] A powder magazine dating from 1867 stands at a distance from the tower.[^1]

## The keepers

The Corporation's keepers' page lists the keepers in order, and LighthouseFriends adds detail. James Wallace was the first keeper, from 1830 to 1844.[^4][^3]

Zoël Bédard followed from 1844 until his death in 1867. LighthouseFriends says the ground was frozen, so his body stayed at the station for six months until the shipping channels opened and it could be taken to Quebec City for burial. His widow asked to replace him, and was turned down because of her age and because the work needed a more capable man.[^3][^4]

Paul Peuliot, whom the official site spells Pouliot, kept the light from 1867 to 1872. He was a former sea captain. LighthouseFriends says the Montagnais kept away from the station, believing the tower was haunted, and that Peuliot encouraged the story by claiming to have seen a man walking around the turret at night. When an inspector visited in 1872, LighthouseFriends says, he judged the keeper too unwell to stay on and had him taken to Quebec.[^3][^4]

Louis-Ferdinand Fafard took charge in 1872 and kept the light until 1889. The Corporation's keepers' page calls him the first of the Fafard keepers, the start of a family dynasty at the light.[^4] In his first autumn the family fell ill with typhoid. LighthouseFriends prints his spring 1873 note that seven of them were confined to bed and that he alone had stayed up. Later, after five years in the damp, cold tower, he suffered serious health problems and was given six months' leave for treatment in Quebec City. His two eldest sons tended the light in his absence.[^3]

Victor Fafard kept the light from 1889 until his death in February 1926.[^4][^7] The keepers' table in the Department's report printed in 1916 lists "V. Faffard" against the "light and explosive fog signal" at Pointe des Monts, appointed 10 July 1889 at a salary of $880. The printed name has a double f, and the link to Victor Fafard rests on the station and the 1889 date.[^7] The Corporation's keepers' page says he and Marie-Louise Montreuil had twelve children, and that he was the first resident of the present keeper's house, overseeing its construction in 1911.[^4]

Georges Fafard, Victor's son, kept the light from 1926 to 1954. The Corporation's page counts the Fafard keepers as covering 82 years and three generations, from 1872 to 1954; LighthouseFriends says 83 years.[^4][^3] Georges Fafard left in 1954 for a post at Mitis-sur-Mer.[^4][^3]

Sauveur Duguay replaced him, keeping the light until 1959. The Corporation's page says his family had been keepers on the south-west point of Anticosti for several decades.[^4] Jacques Landry kept the light from 1959 to 1978. The Corporation's page credits his efforts, together with those of a historical society, chambers of commerce and the municipality, with saving the old tower from demolition.[^4] Roland Boudreault was keeper from 1978 to 1983.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The station also held food for people wrecked on the coast. The Quebec register says a store of provisions for shipwrecked sailors was added the year after the light first shone, in 1831. In 1850 a new building next to the tower replaced the store and could also house survivors.[^1]

In November 1873 a schooner foundered at the point. Nobody died, but the four-man crew sheltered at the station until the spring opened the channel, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] The register says a cannon was installed here in 1867 as the first sound signal at the point.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it was fired every hour in fog and snowstorms. It was replaced by a 32-pound gun in 1882, and in 1894 a device for exploding cotton-powder cartridges was set up at the station.[^3]

The Department's report printed in 1904 records the British barque *Sardhana*: "This British barque was stranded at Point des Monts, Gulf of St. Lawrence, on July 19, 1903", and its causes were formally investigated at Quebec City. The court found the fog signal at the point "inefficient", and witnesses said it was impossible to hear "within a mile and a-half of the lighthouse", even after the vessel stranded.[^8]

The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1919 lists a vessel, not named in the entry, which "stranded near Pointe des Monts" on 9 May. A formal investigation was held at Montreal on 29 May. As this is a fiscal-year report, the stranding took place in May 1918.[^9]

The report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1920 lists a second stranding "near Pointe des Monts" on 17 November, with a preliminary inquiry at Quebec on 20 November. The year is 1919.[^10]

On 14 October 1944 the frigate HMCS *Magog* was torpedoed off Pointe-des-Monts by the German submarine U-1223, while escorting convoy GONS.33. The Navy's history says she lost 18.3 m (60 feet) of her stern and was towed to Quebec, where she was declared a constructive total loss. LighthouseFriends gives 65 feet (about 20 m) and says three sailors were killed.[^12][^3] Alphonsia Fafard, wife of Keeper Georges Fafard, described the convoy's escort as "a splendid maritime ballet", in the account LighthouseFriends prints.[^3]

LighthouseFriends dates a new fog-signal building to 1918, with a three-inch diaphone foghorn driven by an oil engine, 1.9 km (1.2 miles) south-west of the lighthouse.[^3] The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1919 lists new fog-alarm plants established at Pointe des Monts that year.[^11]

## After the keepers

A telegraph and a flag-signal system reached the station in 1883, so passing ships could report to Quebec City.[^3] With the telegraph in place, the old provision house beside the tower was no longer needed, and in 1889 the keepers moved out of the tower into it, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] Wireless came in 1928.[^3] The flags and telegraph were dropped in 1947, when telephone service reached the station.[^3]

The fog-signal building was deactivated in 1972, LighthouseFriends says. In the 1950s new keepers' houses were built at the fog-signal site.[^3] The historic tower's light was last shown on 4 December 1964, and the register says the function passed to a new installation from 1964.[^3][^1] The Ministry of Transport had slated the disused limestone tower for demolition.[^3]

The sources differ on when the tower was protected. The Corporation's keepers' page says the Ministry of Cultural Affairs agreed to classify the tower and powder magazine as historic monuments in 1964.[^4] The Corporation's history page says the tower was classed as a historic monument in 1965.[^5] The Quebec register gives the classification as 8 September 1965.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the Government of Quebec bought the lighthouse in 1965.[^3] Since 1987 a non-profit corporation has run the site, drawn from the local community.[^5]

The light on the skeletal tower at the second site was automated in 1985, and the last keeper was transferred. That light was deactivated in 2000, and its tower removed.[^3] The buildings were restored in 2011, and the exhibits in the tower refreshed, LighthouseFriends says.[^3]

## Today

The lighthouse is open to visitors for tours of its exhibits.[^6] The official site says the tower has seven floors of exhibits and a panoramic view from a point 21 m (69 ft) above the ground, and that the season opens on 23 June.[^6] LighthouseFriends gives the whole tower as about 27 m (90 ft) and says the site has a wooden footbridge of about 200 m. At high tide the tower stands on an island.[^3]

The keeper's house has a restaurant and four guest rooms, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] The Quebec register classes the tower, the house and the outbuildings as protected heritage property, both inside and out, and also the islets around it.[^1]
