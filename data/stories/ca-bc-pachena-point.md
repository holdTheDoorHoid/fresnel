Pachena Point is a cliff-edged headland on the outer coast of Vancouver Island, British Columbia, and the light on it is, by its federal heritage statement, the sole remaining wooden lighthouse of its type on the west coast. It was built after one of the worst wrecks on that coast, and LighthouseFriends counts 117 years of keepers there.[^1][^2]

## The place

The name is a version of a Nitinaht word that LighthouseFriends renders as "foam on the rocks"; the point was earlier called Beghadoss Point, after the *Becherdass Ambiadass*, wrecked there in 1879, and took its present name in 1907 while the lighthouse was going up.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes the seas around the point as littered with the wreckage of scores of ships. In 1896 the barque *Janet Cowan* struck the rocks near the point on New Year's Day, and five of the men who got ashore died before a tug found the survivors on 11 January.[^1]

The disaster that brought the lighthouse came on 22 January 1906, when the passenger steamer *Valencia* went onto the rocks near the point. LighthouseFriends puts the toll at 133 of 160 aboard.[^1] Parks Canada's heritage statement says the lighttower was built in response to the 1906 sinking of an American passenger ship, one of a series put up along the west coast of Vancouver Island to reduce shipwrecks.[^2] Marine Agent William P. Anderson, arguing for a life-saving trail along the shore, described a coast whose "rugged front" "almost defies description", with approach from land or sea "practically impossible", as LighthouseFriends quotes him.[^1]

## Building the light

The Department of Marine and Fisheries reported the work in stages. Its report that covers the 1906 season listed at Pachena "A new lighthouse and fog alarm are being erected."[^3] The next report called it "A first-class light and fog-alarm", under construction and "now nearing completion".[^4] The report after that said the tower, fog-alarm building and double dwelling were finished and that the "5-inch diaphone plant" had been installed.[^5]

LighthouseFriends, a secondary source that lists the Department's reports among its references, adds that George H. Frost superintended the work, most materials came by ship, an aerial tramway landed them, and a landslide in February 1907 carried away the partly built tower, so the crew started again.[^1] It describes the tower as octagonal and wooden, 11.8 m (38 ft 9 in) from base to lantern deck, with a double dwelling and a fog-alarm building on a ledge below.[^1]

The dates of first lighting differ by source. LighthouseFriends says the first keeper lit a temporary light, "a lantern on a gas tank", on 1 September 1907.[^1] A later Department report says a first-order dioptric double-flashing light and lantern "has been erected, replacing the temporary light shown from a lantern on a gas tank."[^6] LighthouseFriends names the lens as a Chance Brothers first-order Fresnel lens installed in 1909.[^1] The reports above show a temporary light first and the permanent light only later, so no single "first lit" year can be given from them.

A life-saving trail was cut from Bamfield at the same time. LighthouseFriends says work started on 21 June 1907; the Department's report says about 22 miles had been completed "beyond Pachena light station" when work stopped for the year.[^1][^5] A life-saving crew, with a surf boat, a Lyle gun (a small line-throwing cannon) and rocket apparatus, was stationed at Pachena Point in December 1910, working with the Banfield crew.[^7]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends lists the keepers, from John S. Richardson (appointed 1907) through William Robinson Pillar (1908), Richard Clark (1913), G. Allan Couldrey (1919) and James W. Davis (1925).[^1] John Alfred Hunting served from 1931, through 1958 by that list, the longest stay on it.[^1] The same source says a wireless station was built on the point in 1907, that the station was also home to linemen of the telegraph system, and that the community used the base of the lighthouse as a schoolroom on weekdays and a dance hall at weekends.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In January 1909, LighthouseFriends says, the *Soquel* struck Seabird Rocks just west of the point; the keeper telegraphed for help after seeing a fire the crew had lit on an offshore ledge, and all aboard were saved but the captain's wife and baby.[^1] On 2 January 1923 the steamer *Alaskan* of Victoria was lost near Pachena Point with all 13 men aboard, according to the Department's report.[^8]

In the Second World War, after the shelling of Estevan Point in June 1942, LighthouseFriends says the west coast lights were put out and the towers painted in camouflage. On 1 April 1943 the Russian ship *Uzbekistan* ran onto the rocks two miles east of the point; the same source says no one died and the lights returned eight days later.[^1]

## After the keepers

A radio direction-finding station at Pachena Point began work in 1923; the Department counted 1,000 bearings given to ships in its first year, and noted the dwelling had been completely resheathed.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the radio staff moved to Spring Island in 1950 and that the Fresnel lens was retired in 1997 for a four-metre pole with a dimmer light.[^1] The Coast Guard, for its part, says automated lights have been in use at both Pachena Point and Carmanah Point since 2003; the two sources do not give the same date.[^1][^10]

In July 2024 the Canadian Coast Guard announced it would end light keeper functions at Pachena Point and Carmanah Point because a geotechnical study found many station buildings on ground too unstable to use, with added risk of slope failure in a large earthquake.[^10] LighthouseFriends reports that keeper Lance McNichol filed the last weather report on 24 October 2024.[^1] The towers and heritage buildings stay under Fisheries and Oceans Canada.[^10]

## Today

The tower is a recognized federal heritage building and the only surviving wooden lighthouse of its type on the west coast, according to its heritage statement.[^2] It stands near the West Coast Trail. LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse belongs to the Canadian Coast Guard, with the grounds open and the tower closed, and that the trailhead near Bamfield is a little over 10 km (6.3 miles) from the light.[^1] The station is now unstaffed and the site is subject to ground-stability concerns, so check current Parks Canada notices before planning a visit.[^10]
