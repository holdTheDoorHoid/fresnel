A square limestone tower, joined to its keeper's house, stands in Golden Hill State Park near Barker, Niagara County, New York, on the south shore of Lake Ontario. The light shone from that tower from 1876 until 1958, when the Coast Guard left the station and the light moved to a skeletal tower beside the fog-signal house. It returned to the historic tower in 1998.[^1][^6][^7][^8]

## The place

Thirty Mile Point is the northernmost point on Lake Ontario's south shore, thirty miles east of the mouth of the Niagara River.[^6] The Light-House Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1891 gives the place as "Thirty-Mile Point, 30 miles east of the mouth of the Niagara River, Lake Ontario, New York."[^2]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' estimates for 1921 call the station "a turning point for vessels bound to or from the Welland Canal," and say that a fog signal was needed to guide them.[^4] In 1872 the first light east of the Niagara River was a newly erected fourth-order pier light at Oak Orchard, forty-five miles away. Lighthouse Friends says it was of little service to ships making for the canal, so the Lighthouse Board asked for a light at this point.[^6]

## Building the light

The Board asked for $30,000 for a third-order light, and Congress appropriated that sum on 3 March 1873.[^6] Lighthouse Friends says two acres and a right of way were bought that year from a local farmer for $500. A further $5,000 was obtained on 3 March 1875. Ground was broken on 28 April 1875, and bad weather that spring delayed the tower and dwelling until the following January.[^6]

The archive records the tower as limestone, with a third-order Fresnel lens installed in 1876.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the six-panel lens cost just over $3,000, was made in France by L. Sautter and Company, and was first exhibited on 27 April 1876.[^6]

The heights in the sources do not agree. The archive gives the focal height as 71.5 feet (21.8 m) and the tower as 53.5 feet (16.3 m).[^1] Lighthouse Friends calls the tower seventy feet (21.3 m) tall,[^6] and Rowlett gives the historic tower as 61 feet (18.5 m).[^7]

The light burned lard oil until 1881, when it was changed to kerosene.[^6] The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1891 notes that the metalwork for a circular oil house of 225 gallons' capacity had been delivered to the station by the tender, and says that "The oil house will be erected at an early day."[^2] The report for the year ended 30 June 1892 says that "A circular iron oil house of 226 gallons' capacity was erected."[^3]

Soon after completion, erosion became a serious concern. Lighthouse Friends says the Board reported in 1877 that waves were wearing away the lighthouse lot, and that it recommended a series of jetties. The same source says Congress appropriated $5,000 in 1878 and 1880 for protecting the site.[^6]

## The keepers

The archive lists Edwin D. Pratt as the first head keeper, from 1876 to 1878.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says his assistant, Hiram A. Vaughn, made charges against him that led to his dismissal, and that Samuel A. Penfield took charge of the light in 1878.[^6]

The appointment became a local quarrel. Lighthouse Friends says that four years after Penfield took charge, local military veterans had a resolution printed in a few papers, complaining that Penfield had been chosen over Vaughn, a Civil War veteran. The collector who made the appointment replied that Vaughn had said he did not want the head keeper's post if Pratt were removed.[^6] Penfield kept the light until 1888, when he was transferred to the Ogdensburg Lighthouse. Vaughn left the lighthouse service in 1884.[^6]

Robert J. Bannerman served as an assistant from 1885 and was head keeper in 1887 and again from 1888 to 1907.[^1]

Glenn R. Seeley came to the station as a laborer in 1900 and as an assistant in 1903. He was head keeper from 1907 until he retired in 1945.[^1][^6] Lighthouse Friends says four of his children were born at the lighthouse.[^6]

Lawrence Bernard was first assistant from 1909 to 1928, the longest term of any first assistant on the archive's roster.[^1]

After Seeley, five more head keepers served in thirteen years: Carl A. Sprague, Harry L. Klein, Osgar K. Elmer, Frank Kubiak and, last, Richard Prange in 1958.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Lighthouse Friends says at least four ships had sunk near the point before the light was built. It names H.M.S. Ontario, which it calls an eighty-foot schooner, and says she foundered off the point in a Halloween blizzard.[^6] The New York Almanack, reporting the 2008 discovery, calls her an "80-foot British sloop of war." It says she went down on 31 October 1780, in a gale, and lies in "nearly 500 feet waters of Lake Ontario."[^10] That account does not place her off this point, so the location Lighthouse Friends gives is not confirmed here.

The Life-Saving Service's table of places where vessels stranded, in its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900, lists "Thirty-Mile Point N Y" among the Lake Ontario entries. The table gives counts of stranded vessels for the years up to 1900, not an account of any wreck there.[^11]

The fog signal came in 1935. Shipping interests had asked for one as early as 1908, and Lighthouse Friends says the Lighthouse Service did not request funds until 1921.[^6] The Lighthouse Service's Bulletin for 1935 says the signal fulfilled "a plan which had been under consideration for some years," and describes it: "The fog signal is a type "F" diaphone." It adds: "In addition to the building of a brick fog-signal house, extensive alterations were made to the keeper's dwelling."[^5][^1]

Lighthouse Friends says the work was contracted in 1934 to a Buffalo firm, that municipal electricity was brought in, and that a 500-watt bulb replaced the kerosene lamp inside the lens. It also says concrete shore protection was added at the same time.[^6]

## After the keepers

In 1958 the Coast Guard withdrew its crew.[^6][^8] Lighthouse Friends says an automated beacon was then set on a metal skeletal tower near the fog-signal building.[^6] Rowlett describes that tower as a 62-foot (19 m) square skeletal tower, painted black, and says the light was moved to it "when the original lighthouse was destaffed."[^7] New York State Parks says the Coast Guard "decommissioned" the lighthouse "in 1958."[^8] The archive gives the years of discontinuance as 1959 to 1998.[^1]

The lens came out at about the same time. Lighthouse Friends dates its removal to forty years before the 1998 relighting, which is 1958.[^6] The sources used here do not say where the lens went.

New York State acquired the land in 1962, and it became Golden Hill State Park.[^8] Lighthouse Friends says the Coast Guard transferred the lighthouse to the state's parks office in 1984. The property went onto the National Register the same year, in a multiple listing of Coast Guard lights on the Great Lakes. NPGallery gives 19 July 1984 as the date published.[^6][^9]

On 11 July 1998 a new beacon was activated in the lantern room.[^6] Rowlett says the light was returned to the historic tower then, and that the skeletal tower has been inactive since 1998.[^7] Lighthouse Friends says a non-profit Friends group was formed in 1995 and that, with help from the park's manager and a grant or two, the slate roof, the windows and the interior wiring were replaced.[^6] The second floor of the keeper's house has been rented for stays since 2001.[^6][^8]

## Today

New York State Parks runs Golden Hill State Park and the lighthouse within it.[^8] The park lists the historic lighthouse for tours from mid-May to mid-October, weather permitting, and says visitors can climb the tower on a tour.[^8] The keeper's house is a cottage for rent, sleeping six, with a two-night minimum.[^8]

In May 2017 there was concern that high lake levels were eroding the bank beneath the lighthouse.[^7]
