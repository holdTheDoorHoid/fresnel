Long Point Lighthouse stands at the east end of Long Point, a sand spit that runs roughly 40 km into Lake Erie in Norfolk County, Ontario.[^2][^3] Its concrete tower, finished in 1916, is the third light on the site. The first, a stone tower at the tip, was taken down in 1838 or 1839 after its foundation was undermined.[^2][^3] The second, a wooden tower of 1843, gave way to the present one.[^1][^2] A separate light at the west end of the point was described by the Department of Marine in 1879, and it remained in operation until 1919.[^3]

## The place

The spit is a danger to mariners and, on its northern side, a protected bay.[^2] In 1828 Elisha Whittlesey, a U.S. representative from Ohio, wrote to Henry Clay, the U.S. Secretary of State, that in violent storms vessels "are unavoidably driven on to it in the night season". He asked Clay to press the British for a lighthouse there, or else to let the United States build one.[^2]

## Building the light

In March 1829 the government of Upper Canada passed an act providing money for a lighthouse on the point and appointing three commissioners to see it built.[^2] The lowest bidders, the brothers Joseph, Benjamin and Whitney Van Norman, contracted to build a round stone tower 15 m (50 ft) high, with walls thinning from 1.5 m (5 ft) at the ground to 0.6 m (2 ft) at the lantern.[^2]

The light was first shown on the evening of 3 November 1830, and the tower was not finished until the following summer. Thomas Price, who tended it in 1830, was its first official keeper.[^2]

The shore did not hold. By 1832 waves were beating at the base, and the north side of the point had lost more than 27 m (30 yards) since the tower was built.[^2] Erosion had claimed the brick dwelling by 1836. In 1838 the inspector general warned that the tower was in danger of collapse, and it was taken down in 1838 or 1839 and moved inland.[^2]

A second tower, octagonal and of wood, went up near the tip in 1843. A description from 1876 gives it as 18 m (60 ft) high, with six lamps and six reflectors.[^2] In 1890 a contract was let for a steam foghorn at the east end of the point. The fog alarm building was finished in 1891, but delays in receiving the horns held up the foghorn until 1 May 1892.[^2]

In 1905 the station received a new keeper's house, coal shed, barn and boathouse. The Department's report for that year says they "were erected" as part of the improvements.[^6] In 1906 a new fog alarm building housed a diaphone run by an oil engine.[^2]

The present tower went up in 1916. The 1987 heritage statement calls the lightstation "the third on the site" and says the Department of Marine built it in 1916.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the contractor as Charles Mattaini, at $8,490, and places the new tower about 520 m (1,700 ft) south-east of its predecessor.[^2] It stands 31 m (102 ft) to the vane and originally used a revolving third-order Fresnel lens showing a white flash every eight seconds.[^2] The same statement calls the tower "a sleek column based on the three classical elements; base, shaft and capital", and gives it a "Recognized" designation for its importance to the historical theme of shipping on the Great Lakes.[^1]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends lists nine head keepers. Thomas Price served from 1830 to at least 1831. Moses Newkirk is recorded from at least 1846 to 1856, then came Henry H. Clarke (1856 to 1869), Henry H. Woodward (1869 to 1893), Walter H. Stalker (1893 to 1897) and Sheldon B. Cook (1897 to 1914).[^2] LighthouseFriends names no keeper from 1914 until Lorne Brown (1928 to 1955), who was followed by Bill Ansley (1955 to 1984) and Bob Nelder (1984 to 1989).[^2] The Department's keeper lists for 1906 and 1907 name Sheldon B. Cook for the "Long Point Light and Fog Alarm".[^7][^8]

In early September 1929 Keeper Lorne Brown found hundreds of dead or injured birds at the base of the tower and telephoned William E. Saunders, an avid naturalist, who came the next day. Saunders and a companion counted 823 birds killed in the two nights before, 583 of them warblers and 137 vireos. Brown later packed birds killed in late September and sent them to Saunders by express.[^2]

Saunders published the paper in *The Auk* in 1930.[^2] LighthouseFriends, which summarises that paper, gives his total for four nights as 1,237 birds of fifty-five kinds.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Department also ran a life-saving station at Long Point, a separate establishment from the light. A new station was built there in 1902, near Port Rowan, with quarters for the crew and a tramway to the water.[^5] That September its crew went to the steamer W. H. Stevens, of Buffalo, burned at Clear Creek. By the time they reached her, her crew had already landed safely in their own boats.[^5]

The life-saving station had two calls in 1913. On 7 July its crew went to the barge Mary McLaughlin, which became a total wreck, and the five crew were rescued.[^9] On 21 October, "after a long hard pull", the lifeboat reached the steamer C. W. Elphicke, ashore off the point, and rescued her crew of eighteen.[^9] The report says the Long Point crew was permanent for the season of navigation.[^9]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1989. A solar-powered beacon replaced its third-order lens, with beams much more focused and at half the intensity.[^2] The lens went to the Port Burwell Marine Museum, where it is on display.[^2] Between 1960 and 1989 the kill averaged about 200 birds a spring and 393 a fall. After automation the averages fell to about 18.5 in spring and 9.6 in fall, according to a 2003 study that LighthouseFriends cites.[^2]

In 1978 much of the point became the Long Point National Wildlife Area, and UNESCO named it a biosphere reserve in 1986.[^2] The Long Point Bird Observatory was founded in 1960 to monitor birds on the point.[^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard operates the light; the property belongs to the Ministry of Transport.[^1] The Coast Guard's list gives a white octagonal tower with a red upper portion, 31.0 m high, and a light with a 2-second flash and 6-second eclipse (LFl W 8s), focal height 29.6 m and a nominal range of 15 nautical miles. The list marks the light "Seasonal (in place year round)".[^4]

LighthouseFriends reports the grounds open and the tower closed. It says the light is best viewed from a boat, that summer boat trips to the tip have been offered, and that most of the point is part of a biosphere reserve.[^2]
