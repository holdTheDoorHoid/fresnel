Great Captain Island Light stands on a seventeen-acre island about two miles south of the Greenwich, Connecticut, mainland, facing the open water of Long Island Sound.[^2] (Lighthouse Friends says both "about a mile offshore" and "just over two miles offshore" from Greenwich Harbor; the National Register nomination says two miles.)[^1] The granite dwelling with an iron lantern on its roof dates from 1868, and it replaced an earlier stone tower, built in 1829 or 1830 depending on the source, whose mortar and walls were failing almost from the start.[^1][^2][^3] The island now belongs to the Town of Greenwich.[^1]

## The place

The island is the largest of a group of three, with Little Captain and Wee Captain, which is joined to Great Captain by a sandbar.[^1] Local legend ties the name to Captain Kidd's buried treasure, but the more reliable accounts, according to Lighthouse Friends, credit Captain Daniel Patrick, an early settler of Greenwich.[^1] Ownership was disputed for generations: the king granted the islands to one John Anderson in 1763, a Greenwich family sued him for cutting trees, and New York claimed the island as part of Rye in 1788. A commission later settled it in Connecticut's favour.[^1]

The National Register nomination describes the island as the outermost of the islands and shoals off Connecticut's coast, with smaller islands and rocks to the east and Bluefish Shoal to the west, and as one of seven major lights marking the main ship channel through the Sound.[^2] Vessels leaving New York used those lights to mark their progress eastward.[^2] The light was also a landmark for approaching Greenwich, Cos Cob and Stamford harbours.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress set aside $5,000 for a light here in 1829, and about three and a half acres on the southeast side of the island were sold to the federal government, with both Connecticut and New York releasing their claims.[^1] Charles H. Smith was paid $2,950 to build the lighthouse and a dwelling and to fit the tower with patent lamps and fourteen-inch reflectors.[^1] The Lighthouse Society's archive lists the station as established in 1829 and its first keeper as starting in 1830; the National Register nomination dates the first light to 1830.[^2][^4] That first tower was a thirty-foot (9 m) stone tower, separate from a five-room keeper's house.[^1] A fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the old lamps in 1858.[^1]

The tower did not wear well. The nomination says its mortar deteriorated and its walls cracked "almost from the start", which made it a priority for replacement by the late 1860s.[^2] Congress allocated $12,000 in 1867 for a new building.[^1] The Light-House Board used one standard design for six lights in the Long Island Sound area, and Great Captain Island's was among them.[^2]

The result, finished in 1868, is a 2½-story dwelling of rough-hewn gray granite with a white-painted cast-iron light tower straddling the roof at the south end.[^2] The nomination gives the overall height as 51 feet (15.5 m), and the date appears in large numerals on the cast iron below the tower's cornice.[^2] Putting the tower on the house was part of the design's purpose: earlier lights had separate keepers' houses, and the nomination notes that the trip to the light was hardest in stormy weather, just when it was needed.[^2] The focal plane was 74 feet (22.6 m) above the water by the nomination's figure, and the Lighthouse Society's archive gives 73 feet.[^2][^4]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society's archive, drawing on the national keeper registers, lists James Merritt as head keeper in three separate stretches between 1834 and 1863, and Eliakim Ferris Worden in charge from 1871 to 1890.[^4] Worden's seventeen-year-old son Dean was awarded a silver life-saving medal in 1885 for saving a New York lawyer, Charles P. Cowles, who had fallen through the ice while skating.[^1] Eugene Mulligan kept the light from 1890 to 1907, and Charles W. Oliver from 1907 to 1916, after which Georgia A. Oliver appears as head keeper for 1916.[^4]

Ferdinand Heizman was head keeper from 1919 to 1926.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says he received the highest marks for general efficiency among 150 light stations in 1924, which entitled him to fly the efficiency flag, and that he earned an efficiency star three years running.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1924 to 1929 lists the award of the flag at Great Captain Island under his name, spelled "Heisman" there.[^5] The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported in fiscal 1921 that he "rescued occupant of a motor boat during storm and recovered the boat".[^6]

Adam L. Kohlman, head keeper from 1928, was the last civilian keeper. He moved to Throgs Neck Light in 1944, and Coast Guard crews took over the station.[^1][^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The 1890s annual reports record how often the fog signal ran. The station's 10-inch steam whistle, in duplicate, was in operation about 343 hours in the year covered by the Light-House Board's 1893 report, and about 428 hours, burning some 47 tons of coal, in the 1900 report.[^7][^8] A steam whistle was added in 1889 by Lighthouse Friends' account and in 1890 by the nomination's.[^1][^2] In 1905 a compressed-air siren, driven by oil engines, went into service on 10 June. Lighthouse Friends reports that residents of Greenwich complained almost at once that the noise kept them awake, and that the signal was adjusted afterward.[^1]

Rescues turn up regularly in the Lighthouse Service's own bulletin. On 21 April 1913 Keeper Charles W. Oliver and Assistant Emil M. Usinger rescued two men from a disabled power boat that was drifting out to sea, and fed them.[^9] That September Usinger gave dry clothing, food and a night's lodging to six people from a boat that had run on the rocks of the island.[^9] In the Bulletin's account for 1926, Keeper Rudolph Iten gave assistance on 31 March to the crew of a ferryboat that had parted its towline and drifted near the station.[^10] Lighthouse Friends names the vessel as the *Thomas D. Brennan* and quotes the New York Evening Post's report that Iten "imperiled his life to take a rowboat across 100 feet of torn and angry water".[^1] Keeper Kohlman rescued two small boys from drowning near the station on 2 September 1929.[^11]

During Prohibition, the Coast Guard came to the island in 1925 on a tip about bootlegging and found seventy-five empty whiskey cases too far inland to have washed ashore. The keeper, who had himself reported illegal liquor activity before, was held and questioned as a possible accomplice and then cleared, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1]

Coast Guard crews had a fatal accident on 30 November 1945. Frederick W. Priabe and Americo J. Rametti, both twenty-four, left the island in a small outboard boat to take the station's radio ashore for repair, and the boat overturned in the Sound. Both men died.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the two men left behind could not launch another boat, and tried to send distress signals with the light, and that word did not reach the mainland until a supply boat came the next day.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard moved the working light to a skeleton tower in 1970, ending the stone house's life as an operating light.[^1][^2] In 1966 the Town of Greenwich bought the island, apart from the lighthouse grounds, from Aerotech Corporation for $90,000, and it received the remaining three and a half acres from the federal government in 1973.[^1] Before that, proposals for private use of the island included a country club and a casino.[^1] The lighthouse was listed in the National Register of Historic Places in 1991; when the nomination was prepared in 1990, the house was occupied by a caretaker, and the interior was not inspected because the island was hard to reach.[^2][^1]

Caretakers lived in the house until 2003, when it was judged too dilapidated.[^1] A campaign called "Return the Light", launched by the Greenwich Chamber of Commerce and the Indian Harbor Yacht Club in the late 1990s, raised about $305,000 in private gifts, many in the name of Ben Fisher, a campaign supporter who died in the 2001 World Trade Center attack.[^1] The town contributed as well, and in May 2008 awarded a $1.13 million restoration contract to Tomlinson Hawley Patterson of Trumbull.[^1] A bronze plaque with the names of Greenwich victims of the 11 September attacks was placed on a boulder near the lighthouse.[^1] A caretaker moved into the ground-floor apartment in May 2009, and an ornamental four-second flashing green light was lit in the lighthouse in November 2011.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse belongs to the Town of Greenwich, which also owns the island as a recreation area.[^1][^2] According to Lighthouse Friends the grounds are open but the dwelling and tower are closed, and the island is reached by a ferry that runs in summer, formerly limited to residents and their guests and reportedly open to everyone now.[^1] The navigation light that mariners use is the Coast Guard's red-and-white light on a skeleton tower about 300 feet from the old house, not the green ornamental light in the lantern.[^1] The Coast Guard's parcel, with two brick outbuildings from before 1891 and probably 1905, is fenced off.[^2]
