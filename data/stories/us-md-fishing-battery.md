Fishing Battery Light stands on a man-made island at the entrance to the Susquehanna River, just over three miles south of Havre de Grace, in Harford County, Maryland.[^1][^2] Its first light was lit in 1853, in a brick house with a lantern on its roof. In the early 1920s the light moved into a steel tower beside that house.[^3][^1][^7] The island was made in the early 1800s to serve the shad fishery, and by 2011 little high ground was left on it.[^1][^16]

## The place

Robert Gale and John Donahoo created the island off Havre de Grace in the early 1800s to profit from the shad runs.[^1] Paddle boats could not get into the shallow port at Havre de Grace. They stopped at the island instead, where fish netted over the Susquehanna Flats were loaded for the run to Baltimore.[^1] The island was first known as Donahoo's Battery and was later called Fishing Battery or Shad Battery.[^1]

Congress provided $5,000 on 3 March 1851 for a light there.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls the land purchase a curiosity. Records show $600 paid to a Mr. Otho Scott for a forty-five-foot (14 m) square parcel, and two weeks later $10 paid to John Donahoo for the same land.[^1] LighthouseFriends suggests the $10 may have been payment for his work brokering the sale.[^1]

## Building the light

John Donahoo built the light in 1853. LighthouseFriends counts it as the final one of about a dozen lights he put up around the bay.[^1] The sources do not agree on what the house looked like. LighthouseFriends describes a one-and-a-half-story brick dwelling with its lantern room on the pitched roof.[^1] The Chesapeake Chapter describes a one-story brick house with a wooden lantern on the roof.[^2] The state's survey file calls it a three-story lighthouse.[^17]

The first light used five lamps with 14-inch (36 cm) reflectors.[^3] A sixth-order Fresnel lens replaced them two years after the light was built, in 1855.[^1][^3]

An inspection in 1864 found the lantern in poor condition, and a new one went up in 1867.[^1] The Chesapeake Chapter records a major renovation in 1887 that replaced the foundation and extended the building.[^2] The Light-House Board's report for 1896 to 1897 records a new porch with platforms leading to the walks, and "various repairs."[^4]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists twelve keepers, from 1853 to 1917.[^3] It begins with John Levy, listed for 1853 alone, and Sarah Levy, listed from 1853 to 1855.[^3] Nicholas Sutor kept the light from 1856 to 1861, and William Youse from 1861 to 1864. Several keepers of the 1860s served only a few years. William Canell, for one, is listed for 1864 alone.[^3]

George W. Mitchell kept the light for thirty years, from 1881 to 1911, the longest stint on the roster.[^3] The 1887 renovation and the 1891 work on the island came in his time.[^2][^1]

Patrick Reedy is listed for 1911 alone. LighthouseFriends says William Schoenfelder became head keeper that year, after three years in the same post at Thomas Point Shoal.[^1][^3] The roster ends with Schoenfelder in 1917.[^3]

The name is not consistent across the sources. The Service Bulletin's index for 1912 to 1917 gives "Robert R." Schoenfelder.[^18] The Bulletin's text for 1914 gives William R., and the Commissioner's report for fiscal 1915 gives W. R.[^8][^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In January 1899 the Cecil Whig reported that ice on the river between the light and Havre de Grace "has grounded across the channel," forming "hummocks in some places as high as ten feet."[^12]

In late January 1902 the Aegis & Intelligencer of Bel Air reported that ice had "gorged across the channel at Fishing Battery lighthouse." It warned that, if the gorge did not break, Port Deposit and Havre de Grace would be in "a perilous condition when the break-up occurs."[^13]

In February 1912 the Midland Journal reported that, because of the ice, "the lights have been discontinued at Turkey Point and Fishing Battery Light station."[^11]

In October 1914 the Service's Bulletin reported that the keeper, William R. Schoenfelder, "assisted in saving from drowning an injured man" who had gone overboard from a dredge working near the light.[^8] LighthouseFriends says he was commended for it in 1915.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1917 the Service changed the light. A Bulletin list records the Fishing Battery light going from fixed white, oil, to flashing white, acetylene.[^9] The Commissioner's report for that year lists it among the lights changed from fixed to flashing.[^6] The Midland Journal had given the date in May: the light would change on 14 June to a flashing white every five seconds.[^10]

LighthouseFriends says the acetylene apparatus, which cost $1,159 in 1917, made a resident keeper unnecessary.[^1] The Chesapeake Chapter says keepers continued to live in the house until 1939, when the light was automated.[^2] The sources disagree, and the roster ends with Schoenfelder in 1917.[^3]

The light went onto a steel tower in 1921. LighthouseFriends describes a thirty-eight-foot (11.6 m) steel tower.[^1][^2] The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1922 lists the work on the skeleton tower among the construction completed that year, at a cost of $1,545.[^7]

A red sector was to be added to the light in 1910, according to the Cecil Whig.[^14] In October 1937 the Midland Journal reported that an additional red sector had been approved.[^15]

In 1942 Fishing Battery Island was deeded to the Department of the Interior, to be used as part of a migratory bird and wildlife refuge.[^1] Hurricane Agnes in 1972 destroyed what remained of the island's waterfowl vegetation.[^1] In 1992, LighthouseFriends reports, the wooden parts of the lighthouse were torn out and burned. A metal plaque about the lighthouse's history was stolen in 1994.[^1] A plan in the 1990s to make the island a public park, and to turn the lighthouse into a historic bed-and-breakfast, had fizzled before an official lease agreement was ready.[^1] A Lighthouse Society trip in 1999 found the island to be "a patch of dry land only inches above lapping high-tide waves."[^1]

In 2013 a federal restoration enclosed what was left of the island in sand. The work was done by the Army Corps of Engineers and the Fish and Wildlife Service, and it planted 60,000 plants.[^16] The report says local interest in saving the lighthouse came together with the need to dredge the navigation channel beside the island.[^16]

## Today

The island is part of the Susquehanna National Wildlife Refuge.[^16] The Chesapeake Chapter instead places the island in the Blackwater refuge.[^2]

The Chesapeake Chapter says the light still operates as an aid to navigation, but the Lighthouse Society's place record marks it as not an active aid.[^2][^3] The site and tower are closed to the public, and the Chapter says the light is best seen from a boat.[^2] The island can be reached only by boat.[^2]

The state's survey file recommends the light as eligible for the National Register under Criteria A and C.[^17] The Lighthouse Digest has put the abandoned lighthouse on its Doomsday List of critically endangered lighthouses.[^2] The Chesapeake Heritage Conservancy and the Havre de Grace Maritime Museum were seeking funds to save and restore it.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the whereabouts of the Fresnel lens are officially unknown.[^1]
