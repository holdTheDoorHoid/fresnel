Vermilion Light stands at the end of the pier where the Vermilion River meets Lake Erie, on the Ohio shore. The tower there now is a replica of an 1877 iron tower, dedicated in 1992. The light it stands for began as a beacon on the pier in 1847.[^1][^2]

## The place

Vermilion sits where a river of the same name empties into Lake Erie.[^1] The village was incorporated in 1837 and became known as the "Village of Lake Captains."[^5] Before Congress paid for a light, townspeople marked the harbor entrance themselves, with wooden stakes topped by oil-burning beacons.[^1] Government reports spelled the name differently. The Board's 1893 report lists the light as "Vermillion, on the outer end of the west pier."[^3]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $3,000 in 1847 for a beacon light and the head of the pier.[^1][^2] An inspection in 1858 described the light as "a gallows frame standing on a completely wrecked pier." In 1859 the beacon and pier were rebuilt for $5,000, with a whale-oil lamp and a sixth-order lens.[^1]

In 1870 the Lighthouse Board asked for $4,000 to buy a lot and build a keeper's dwelling. The lot the government found already had a house on it, which was bought and altered to store oil.[^1]

In 1877 the Board reported that the old wooden tower was decayed and in danger of falling, and a new iron tower was ordered. Ironworkers in Buffalo cast it in tapering octagonal sections, and LighthouseFriends says the iron came from obsolete smoothbore cannon.[^1] In September 1877 the lighthouse tender *Haze* carried the parts from Oswego, through the Welland Canal and onto Lake Erie. At Vermilion the crew raised a tower thirty-four feet (10 m) high at the end of the pier.[^1] A 400-foot (122 m) catwalk connected it to shore, so the keeper could cross when waves broke over the pier.[^1]

In 1880 the light's characteristic changed from fixed white to fixed red.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive's roster for the pier light begins in 1849 with William B. Andrews, who served twice, from 1849 to 1851 and from 1861 to 1869. Miles Harris kept the light from 1879 to 1901, and John H. Burns from 1904 to at least 1917.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says Captain John H. Burns lived in the dwelling the government bought in 1871, and walked to the pier each night to light the lantern.[^1] After the light was converted to acetylene in 1919, LighthouseFriends says it was put under the care of the keepers at Lorain. Olin W. Stevens, keeper at Lorain from 1920 to 1937, was responsible for the automated Vermilion light while he served there.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 27 April 1893 the schooner M. S. Bacon, being towed out of the harbor by the tug J. P. Devney, struck the light-tower on the west pier.[^1][^3] The Board's report says two angle-iron plates in the second section of the tower were so badly injured that new plates had to be made.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the repair bill of $161.61 went to the tug's owners, who refused to pay, and the matter went to the Justice Department.[^1]

In early 1929 an icy storm undermined the lighthouse pier. Two local teenagers noticed the tower leaning toward the river and told their father, a commodore, who alerted the Lighthouse Service. LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse was taken down that March.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1906 an oil shed was built south of the lighthouse, and a 500-foot (152 m) elevated iron walk was built on the pier.[^1]

The lamp was converted to acetylene in 1919. LighthouseFriends gives 2 September 1919 as the date.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's list of 1919 lights whose illuminant was changed includes Vermilion, Ohio, which changed from oil to acetylene and from a fixed red light to a flashing white one.[^4] The Bulletin's index for 1918 to 1923 also lists a change in illuminant at Vermillion.[^6] In the early 1920s the keeper's house was sold to the local Masonic lodge.[^1]

In 1929 the old iron tower was taken down, and a skeletal steel tower went up to mark the pier. LighthouseFriends says that tower was moved to the outer end of the west pier that October.[^1] In 1934 electric range lights were set up on the pier, using the skeletal tower as the front light.[^1]

In 1935, according to LighthouseFriends, the old iron tower was renovated and set on a cement crib off Cape Vincent on Lake Ontario, renamed East Charity Shoal Light.[^1]

The replica began with local fundraising. Ted Wakefield, one of the two boys who had reported the lean in 1929, spearheaded the campaign, and by 1991 it had collected $55,000.[^1] Mayor Alex Angney broke ground on 24 July 1991. The tower was raised on 23 October 1991, and the new Vermilion Lighthouse was dedicated on 6 June 1992.[^1] The light was relit in September 2016 with a fifth-order Fresnel lens made for the purpose. The city had taken over the site in 2014, after the museum that held it closed.[^1][^5]

## Today

The city of Vermilion cares for the lighthouse, along with its propeller and anchor.[^5] The interior is closed to visitors, but visitors may come to the site at any time of day.[^5] LighthouseFriends notes that the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1] The light has been an active navigational aid since 2016, and a lighthouse keeper was appointed that year to look after it.[^5]

The keeper's dwelling on the corner of Liberty and Grand Streets, bought by the government in 1871, is now in commercial use, LighthouseFriends says.[^1]
