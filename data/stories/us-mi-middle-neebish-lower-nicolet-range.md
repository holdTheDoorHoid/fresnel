The Lower Nicolet range lights stand at the north end of Neebish Island, in the St. Marys River, where ships bound between Lake Superior and Lake Huron pass through the lower part of Lake Nicolet, which LighthouseFriends also calls Hay Lake.[^1][^2] The front tower is a steel cylinder built in 1907 at Windmill Point, Detroit, and moved here in 1931.[^2] It is the only example of that 1907 construction left on the site.[^2] The men who kept these lights lived in a duplex nearby.[^1]

## The place

The St. Marys River runs about 75 miles (120 km) between Whitefish Bay on Lake Superior and Detour Reef at the north end of Lake Huron, and Neebish and Sugar islands divide it into channels.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the channel that runs around the north and east sides of Neebish Island "was completed in 1894," and that in the following year "a set of range lights, known as Lower Hay Lake Range (now Lower Nicolet Range), was established on the northern end of Neebish Island."[^1] The Light-House Board's report for 1896 lists a Lower Hay Lake range with two lens-lantern lights, established 5 October 1895.[^4] The same report gives the pair as Stations 10 and 11 in the Hay Lake channel.[^4]

The keepers' duplex stood roughly a mile east of the range lights.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes it as a two-story duplex with six rooms in each half, a schoolroom shared in the middle, two brick outhouses, a boathouse and a barn. It was built to house "a head keeper and assistant keeper who were responsible for not only Lower Nicolet Range Lights but also numerous other fixed and floating lights in the area."[^1]

## Building the light

The 1907 tower was built at Windmill Point, Detroit, to serve as a range on the Detroit River.[^1] HAER No. MI-68 says it was "originally built at Windmill Point, Detroit, Michigan, 1907."[^2] LighthouseFriends says the Windmill Point range was discontinued when that reservation was given to the Public Health Service in the late 1920s so a marine hospital could be built.[^1]

In 1919 the front tower was enlarged with a straight section added to the top. HAER gives the original cylinder as 35 feet 4 inches (11 m) high, with a straight section 14 feet 8 inches (4.5 m) high added that year, and says the tower totals 50 feet (15 m) to the focal plane of the lens.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives the original cylinder as "forty-two feet tall," so the two accounts disagree about its height before 1919.[^1][^2]

In 1931 the U.S. Corps of Engineers undertook a large-scale dredging project to revise the channel system around the St. Marys River islands. HAER ties the work to the need "to accommodate the increasing number of larger ore ships."[^2] The Lighthouse Service moved two of the discontinued Windmill Point towers to Neebish Island instead of building new ones, "rather than to build new skeleton towers from more recent designs."[^2] LighthouseFriends says the two sets of range lights came to be known as the Lower Nicolet East Range and the Lower Nicolet West Range.[^1] HAER quotes the Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for fiscal 1931: "Lower Nicolet West Range Lights have been started and the concrete bases finished."[^2] The report for fiscal 1932, as HAER quotes it, lists the Lower Nicolet West Range among the new range sets.[^2]

## The keepers

The USLHS roster lists the principal keepers and first assistants for this station from 1895.[^3] Walter H. Anning, the first principal keeper, served from 1895 to 1902, and George B. Peterson was his first assistant until 1904.[^3] John M. Sweet followed Anning as principal keeper and served until 1918.[^3]

Roger W. Campbell became principal keeper in 1918.[^3] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for fiscal 1921 names a Roger W. Campbell as "keeper of Middle Neebish Cut Light Station."[^5] The USLHS keeps a separate record for Middle Neebish Cut, which lists Campbell there in 1918.[^6] The sources used here do not say whether that station and these ranges are the same place, and the 1921 events are reported below with that caveat.

Hall was first assistant on the USLHS roster from 1920 to 1922, and became principal keeper in 1922. The USLHS gives his term as principal keeper as 1922 to 1931, and LighthouseFriends as 1922 to 1932.[^3][^1]

Other keepers came and went. William H. Rice served as first assistant from 1907 to 1911, Robert Eldridge from 1911 to at least 1915, and William A. Brooks from at least 1917 to at least 1919.[^3] Thomas P. Brander was principal keeper from 1933 to 1941, and William R. Small was principal keeper from 1932 to 1933.[^3] LighthouseFriends also places Small as assistant from 1925 to 1928. The USLHS roster for this station does not list that stint, but its separate Middle Neebish Cut record does.[^1][^3][^6]

Hugh A. M. Cook was first assistant from 1929 to 1943 on the USLHS roster.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he was born in Canada, grew up on a homestead on Neebish Island, enlisted in the Coast Guard in 1906 and left it in 1928. It says he "was appointed assistant keeper on Neebish Island in 1929."[^1] LighthouseFriends also says that during Prohibition Cook reportedly captured smugglers, and that, according to his grandson, he confiscated their liquor, released the rumrunners and told them to bring more the next time.[^1] It further says that in 1941 Cook "was promoted to head keeper and assigned to Little Rapids Lighthouse near Sault Ste. Marie," which the USLHS roster does not show.[^1][^3]

The last names in the sources are David S. Barclay, whom LighthouseFriends lists as head keeper in 1943, and Eugene I. Marshall, whom the USLHS roster lists as principal keeper over a span marked "at least 1950."[^1][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for fiscal 1921 describes two rescues by the keepers of Middle Neebish Cut Light Station. Roger W. Campbell towed a disabled motor boat ashore in a northeast gale. Campbell and his assistant, W. S. Hall, pulled the sloop T-631 off the rocks and made temporary repairs.[^5] Whether these events happened at these ranges is not settled by the sources. The sources used here tie no other storm, wreck or rescue to these range lights.

## After the keepers

When the towers were moved, an acetylene lantern replaced the locomotive headlight. LighthouseFriends says this "allowed Lower Nicolet West Range to be unattended and visited only occasionally when the acetylene-gas cylinders needed to be serviced."[^1] By 1958 the rear range tower had been moved 2,000 feet (610 m) further back and replaced by a steel skeleton tower, HAER says.[^2] LighthouseFriends says daymark boards were added to the towers in 1975.[^1]

In 1993 HAER No. MI-68 was prepared "as mitigative documentation prior to demolition of the tower," and the 1907 front tower was scheduled for demolition and replacement.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that in the 1990s the front light and its daymark were moved to a modern skeletal tower, and the historic tower was left standing.[^1]

## Today

The historic front tower stands on the site in poor condition.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it "will hopefully be left in place or transferred to a museum, where it could be restored."[^1] The Coast Guard owns the light.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says "Grounds open, tower closed," and that the lighthouse is "best viewed from the water."[^1] The USLHS archive lists the station as not open to the public, which does not match LighthouseFriends' "Grounds open."[^3][^1] The keepers' duplex is now a private residence.[^1] In 1993 HAER recorded the Coast Guard as owner and the tower as unmanned.[^2]
