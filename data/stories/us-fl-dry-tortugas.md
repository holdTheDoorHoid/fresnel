Dry Tortugas Light stands on Loggerhead Key, the most westerly key of the Florida Reefs, at the far end of the Dry Tortugas group.[^3] It is a brick tower that Putnam's 1917 history calls "a tall brick lighthouse, one hundred and fifty-seven feet in height" (47.9 m), and it was first lit on 1 July 1858.[^3][^2] The light still shows, and the station is now part of Dry Tortugas National Park.[^1][^2]

## The place

The reefs and shoals around the Dry Tortugas wrecked many ships, and LighthouseFriends says wreckers still made a living salvaging them after the first light went up.[^2] William Marvin sat as judge of the admiralty court at Key West from 1839 to 1863, and it was his job to set the reward for each salvage.[^2] The first light stood on Garden Key, about three miles east.[^4] Fort Jefferson was later built on that key, and in 1856 Captain Woodbury was overseeing its construction.[^2] Putnam's history says that "The Dry Tortugas Light of 1826 was on Garden Key, about three miles east, where a harbor light is still maintained."[^4]

## Building the light

In August 1856 Congress appropriated $35,000 for a second, more powerful light, to stand on Loggerhead Key, three miles west of Garden Key. Captain Daniel P. Woodbury, who was overseeing the construction of Fort Jefferson, was put in charge of building it.[^2] The conical brick tower carried a first-order Fresnel lens.[^1] The lens, supplied by L. Sautter & Company of Paris, France, was first lit on 1 July 1858.[^2] The USLHS record gives 1858 as the year the tower was established and the lens was installed.[^1]

The sources give the tower's height differently. Putnam gives 157 feet (47.9 m). The USLHS record gives the light as 150.67 feet (45.9 m) above mean high water.[^3][^1]

A hurricane in 1873 badly damaged the towers on both Loggerhead and Garden keys.[^2] Congress appropriated $75,000 on 3 March 1875 to rebuild the Loggerhead Key tower. LighthouseFriends says the Board rebuilt the upper eight or nine feet (about 2.4 to 2.7 m) of masonry instead, and that the plans for a replacement tower were abandoned.[^2]

On 30 April 1893 the Board's report records a change in the characteristic of the light, with a red sector added to warn of a reef just offshore.[^5][^2] In 1899 the iron work of the tower was scraped and given two coats of paint, and new storm doors were made.[^6] In 1909 the Board bought a second-order bivalve lens for the tower. It produced a flash every twenty seconds, and its secondary flashes had to be corrected with copper screens.[^2][^10]

A one-story brick bungalow was built for the head keeper in 1922 and 1923 at a cost of $6,498.[^2] The Bulletin had reported a keeper's dwelling under construction at the station in 1922.[^9]

## The keepers

Benjamin H. Kerr was the first head keeper. LighthouseFriends says he had served nine years at Garden Key before he was moved to Loggerhead Key, and the roster lists him from 1858 to 1861.[^2][^1]

LighthouseFriends describes an 1860 report by G. Phillips of Fort Jefferson, which says the two assistants, and Kerr's wife and eldest daughter, "made an attempt on the keeper's life." Kerr defended himself with a carving knife and fled by boat to Garden Key.[^2]

Later keepers included Edgar J. Russell (1907 to 1917), Charles Johnson (1917 to 1926) and Andrew M. Albury (1928 to 1950). Antonio Canalejo was first assistant from 1939 to 1950 and head keeper from 1950 to 1954.[^1]

During the Civil War, roughly two thousand people were living at Fort Jefferson. LighthouseFriends says the turtle hunts gave the fort's families a chance to visit the light, and quotes an officer's wife on one such evening: "We took three boats, with music for dancing and supper."[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 17 March 1913 keeper E. J. Russell, first assistant B. M. Roberts and second assistant C. Johnson brought the captain and crew of the wrecked schooner Pendleton Brothers to the light station, the Bulletin reports.[^7] The Bulletin's 1912–1917 index names the first assistant Holton M. Roberts, and the USLHS roster lists a Holton M. Roberts as first assistant at the station from 1911 to 1917, so the index entry is probably the same man.[^14][^1]

In July 1916 a storm destroyed the station's old wharf. The Commissioner of Lighthouses reports a new wharf, 140 feet (43 m) long, built on the eastern side of the reservation on Loggerhead Key to replace it.[^13]

On the night of 8 May 1919 keeper Charles Johnson and assistant Robert J. Fine went to the aid of a fishing schooner that had run ashore on the rocks on the west side of Bird Key. They pulled her off and towed her away.[^8]

On 2 November 1923 second assistant C. C. Turner "rendered assistance" to a disabled vessel and its crew, which later listed and sank.[^11]

## After the keepers

In 1927 a radiobeacon was established at the station on 21 December. LighthouseFriends calls it the first radiobeacon in Florida.[^12][^2]

In 1931 generators were set up in a frame addition to the old oil house to power the light.[^2] The keepers' dwelling burned in 1945 and was razed.[^2] In 1986 the mercury float of the revolving lens was damaged and the lens stopped. The Coast Guard later fitted a DCB-24 lens and then a VRB-25.[^2] The light was automated in 1987, and Dry Tortugas National Park was created in 1992.[^2]

## Today

The National Park Service owns the lighthouse. LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower closed; the USLHS record says the light is open to the public.[^2][^1] Visitors reach the park by seaplane or by ferry, and the ferry stops only at Garden Key.[^2] Getting ashore on Loggerhead Key takes your own boat or a guide holding a park permit, because the pier is closed to the public.[^2] The light is still active, with a solar-powered VRB-25 lens.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says volunteers now stay in the original building that served as a kitchen and dwelling, and keep an eye on the property. Check current arrangements before you plan a visit.[^2] Do not climb the tower, and do not land on Loggerhead Key without a permit.
