On Harbor Island, just inside the pass that carries ships from the Gulf of Mexico toward Corpus Christi Bay, an octagonal brick tower has stood since the 1850s. It is called the Aransas Pass light, or the Lydia Ann light after the channel that now runs beside it, and nobody agrees for certain whose name that is.[^1][^2]

## The place

Aransas Pass is one of the few openings in the barrier islands of the Texas coast, between Saint Joseph Island to the north and Mustang Island to the south, and it gives access toward the port of Corpus Christi, roughly twenty miles to the west.[^2] The pass did not stay put. Sand was piling up at the south end of Saint Joseph Island while the north tip of Mustang Island eroded, and the opening was slowly creeping south.[^2] Congress authorized $12,500 for a lighthouse here on 3 March 1851, and a Coast Survey officer, Lieutenant Commander T. A. Craven, recommended a lightship instead. A second survey in 1853 favoured a screwpile light inside the pass, and a district inspector proposed a prefabricated cast-iron tower. The committee that weighed the options chose brick.[^2] The government bought 25 acres on Harbor Island, and Texas ceded jurisdiction on 20 June 1855.[^2] By 1920 the Lighthouse Service described the station as lying on the bayou at the east end of Harbor Island, about 6 miles from the town of Aransas Pass.[^3]

## Building the light

The first load of bricks never arrived. The schooner carrying them foundered on the sandbar at the mouth of the pass in heavy seas in late December 1855; the crew was saved, but the vessel and its cargo were a total loss.[^2] New bricks came in 1856, followed by the lantern and a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^2] The tower and keeper's dwelling were finished early in 1857, and the light was first shown later that year, according to LighthouseFriends; the Texas Historical Commission's marker gives a starting date of 15 July 1857.[^2][^10] The US Lighthouse Society archive gives 1855 as the year of establishment and lists a first keeper from 1856, so published dates for the first lighting differ by about a year.[^1] The archive records a red-brick octagonal tower, 55 feet (17 m) from base to the centre of the lantern, with the light 65 feet (20 m) above mean high water; the Texas marker describes a 67-foot tower.[^1][^10]

The war interrupted the light. The lens was removed for safekeeping, and the tower passed between Confederate and Union hands. LighthouseFriends says General John B. Magruder ordered it blown up on Christmas Day 1862, with two kegs of powder wrecking the upper twenty feet of brickwork and most of the stairway; the Texas marker places the damage in 1863.[^2][^10] A repair crew arrived early in 1867 and met one of the severest northers the Lighthouse Service engineer had known on the Texas coast, with frozen fish thrown ashore by the hundreds.[^2] It was the last principal light on the Texas coast to return to service, in spring 1867 by LighthouseFriends' account and on 15 July 1867 by the marker.[^2][^10]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society archive, drawing on the keeper registers, lists seventeen head-keeper entries between 1856 and 1952 (Parry Humphreys appears in three of them), beginning with Elmanson Lewis (1856 to 1859) and William R. Roberts (1859 to 1861).[^1] Parry W. Humphreys, appointed on 3 June 1869, stayed more than sixteen years, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The family kept a vegetable garden on Mustang Island to add to what the service supplied. In 1885 the district inspector judged Humphreys, then about seventy-four, unfit, and a superintendent urged his dismissal for age and for "drunkenness, disobedience of orders and refusing to pay his debts", as LighthouseFriends reports. The Humphreyses left in March 1886, and Jane Humphreys had served as her husband's assistant for about ten years.[^2][^1]

Frank Stephenson was head keeper from 1897 to 1917, after five years as first assistant. His daughter Lydia Ann married his assistant, Thomas P. Roberts, who succeeded him as head keeper in 1917 and held the post until 1923.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends reports that a historical marker near the tower says the channel was named for the first keeper's daughter, but notes that Stephenson was the ninth keeper, and that local historians have instead proposed Lydia Ann Wells, wife of a Texas Revolution veteran who ranched on Saint Joseph Island.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The hurricane of 18 August 1916, which the Commissioner of Lighthouses placed near Aransas Pass, did great damage here.[^4] The Lighthouse Service put its losses at about $15,000, the greatest at this station: the keepers' dwelling, oil house, walks and outbuildings were destroyed, and no one in the service lost his life.[^6] The bulletin singled out Thomas P. Roberts, then assistant keeper, for showing the light without a break "when the station was almost a complete wreck" and for protecting government property.[^7] Congress appropriated $20,000 on 6 October 1917 for the rebuilding.[^5]

The new double dwelling of hollow tile went up on cast-iron columns above the marsh, about 30 feet southwest of the tower, which had not been destroyed. Work began in May 1918 and was practically complete in September 1919.[^3] LighthouseFriends records that a second hurricane struck on 13 September 1919, two days after the work was practically finished; the workmen sheltered in the new dwelling, but the old assistant's house was swept away.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin credits Roberts, by then keeper, with helping the keeper of the Coast Guard station at Port Aransas with relief work in the Coast Guard boat from 13 to 19 September.[^8]

On 11 April 1935, a dust storm cut visibility to about a quarter of a mile, and the radiobeacon ran from 8:15 a.m. on the 11th to 7:20 p.m. on the 12th. The keeper wrote that dwelling, tower and radio room were covered, inside and out, "with a coating of dust that is as fine as chalk."[^9]

## After the keepers

Electricity came in 1928 with diesel generators for a new radiobeacon.[^2] When the Coast Guard took over lighthouses in 1939, a civilian keeper and three Coast Guard assistants staffed the station and also looked after forty minor beacons marking channels in the area.[^2] The pass kept moving south until it lay more than a mile away. A new light at the Port Aransas Coast Guard station replaced this one in 1952, and the tower was deactivated.[^2][^1] After no agency wanted it, the property was sold in 1955 to Everett Bohls of Austin for $25,151, and Charles Butt, president of the H-E-B grocery chain, bought it in 1973 and had the tower and outbuildings restored.[^2] Live-in caretakers followed, and the light returned to service as a private aid to navigation in 1988 according to LighthouseFriends (which gives 3 July) and the Texas marker, or on 4 July 1989 according to the Texas Maritime Museum.[^2][^10][^11]

## Today

The station is privately owned, on Butt family land, and the tower and grounds are closed to the public.[^2][^11] LighthouseFriends says the tower can be seen in the distance up the Intracoastal Waterway from Port Aransas, that boat tours from the area may include it, and that a public waterway runs close to the property.[^2] The fourth-order lens is reported on display at the Port Aransas Museum.[^2] The station was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1977, after a state marker was placed in 1973.[^12]
