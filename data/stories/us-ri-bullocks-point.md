Bullock's Point Light was a small lighted beacon on a granite pier at the point of a shoal in the Providence River, off Barrington, Rhode Island. It was lit in 1872, its wooden house and tower were finished in 1876, and the hurricane of September 1938 left the dwelling uninhabitable and the tower unsafe.[^1][^15][^16]

## The place

Bullock's Point juts south toward the mouth of the Providence River. D'Entremont says shoals surrounded the point and made the growing shipping traffic to and from Providence hazardous in the nineteenth century, and that an unlighted day beacon had been set offshore in the mid-1800s.[^16] The Lighthouse Board's 1894 report still places the light "at the point of the shoal off Bullock Point."[^6] D'Entremont traces the name to Richard Bullock, who farmed the neck of land in 1666.[^16]

In July 1872 the steamer Ida ran "Daily Excursions" from a wharf "opposite 41 Dyer street" in Providence to Field's Point, Ocean Cottage, Silver Spring, Cedar Grove and Bullock's Point.[^3] That August the Morning Star reported a steamer bringing "the lantern for the new lighthouse at Sabin's Point", the light to the north.[^4][^16] D'Entremont says the care of the new Bullock's Point beacon went first to Joseph Bowes, keeper at Sabin Point.[^16]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $1,000 in 1872, D'Entremont says, and a small lighted beacon was set on a granite pier. Its fixed red light, shown from a sixth-order Fresnel lens, went into service on 4 November 1872.[^16] The Archive's entry gives the same year for the light and its lens.[^1]

A further $15,000 was appropriated on 23 June 1874 for a combined lighthouse and dwelling.[^16] Work began in August 1875 with the enlargement of the pier.[^16] Bad weather and ice on the river held the building up over the winter, and D'Entremont says the station was finished in early spring 1876.[^16] The Archive dates the tower to 1876 and describes it as wood, square and built from the roof of the dwelling, 35 feet (11 m) from base to lantern, with the light 48 feet (15 m) above mean high water and the foundation a granite pier.[^1] D'Entremont calls the whole a Victorian dwelling on a rectangular granite pier, with a lantern on its roof, and says a fog bell was added in 1907.[^16]

The annual reports record repeated work at the station. In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 new boat davits were fitted on the west side of the pier.[^5] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 riprap was placed and repairs were made.[^6] In the year ended 30 June 1895 a platform was built and a contract was let for 200 tons of riprap "around the station."[^7] In the year ended 30 June 1900 an oil house was built.[^8] In its report for fiscal 1918 the Commissioner of Lighthouses asked $3,600 for riprap at Bullocks Point, one of the third-district stations. The estimate noted that ice that winter had damaged the foundations of many of these stations.[^10]

## The keepers

The Archive's roster names seventeen people for the station, from Joseph Bowes in 1872 to Andrew Zuius, Sr., the keeper until 1939.[^1]

Bowes was the first keeper, from 1872 to 1875.[^1] In October 1875 the Morning Star reported that John F. Weeden had been appointed principal keeper of the Sabin's and Bullock's Point lights, in place of Bowes, who had resigned and removed to Florida.[^2] Weeden kept the station until 1877.[^1] The Archive lists four first assistants: George H. Burroughs, Joseph Sarle and F. B. Smith in 1873, and George W. Doane, who served as first assistant until 1877 and was principal keeper for a single year in 1877.[^1]

Stephen A. Hopkins served from 1877 to 1885 and Charles Lough from 1885 to 1886.[^1] Joseph B. Eddy followed from 1886 to 1892. D'Entremont, who gives him as Joseph P. Eddy, says the four Eddy children made the daily row to a school in Drownville, in Barrington.[^16] John J. Card kept the light from 1892 to 1901.[^1]

William Tengren is the keeper with the longest record here, eighteen years in all across two stints.[^1] D'Entremont says he was born in Sweden and went to work on ships at nine.[^16] The Archive has him keeper from 1901 to 1909.[^1] In July 1913 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin names William Tengren as keeper of the Mussel Bed Shoal light, where he and his son rescued two women and a man from an overturned power boat.[^11] The Archive has him back at Bullock's Point from 1916. D'Entremont puts his return in 1918, and the Commissioner's fiscal 1918 list of saving-of-life services names "W. Tengren, keeper, Bullock Light Station."[^1][^9][^16]

Between Tengren's two stints, John F. Anderson, Arthur J. Baldwin, Willis A. Green, Charles C. Fletcher and Julius Johansen each kept the light for one to three years, from 1909 to 1916.[^1] D'Entremont's dates run later for some of them: he gives Baldwin 1911 to 1914 and Johansen 1916 to 1918, which overlaps the year he gives for Tengren's return.[^16]

Andrew Zuius, Sr., is the Archive's last keeper, from 1926 to 1939. D'Entremont gives 1927 to 1938.[^1][^16] In May 1930 the Bulletin reports that a keeper named Andrew Zuius rescued two people from a sailboat that capsized in a squall.[^14]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

D'Entremont says the Eddy family endured a severe winter in 1892, when a steamer bound for New York City became stuck in the ice.[^16]

On 30 June 1918 Tengren rescued "two men and two women" from drowning when their boat overturned about half a mile (0.8 km) from the light.[^12] In the winter of 1918-19 the river froze over. D'Entremont says the Tengren family recorded that it was possible to walk to shore.[^16]

On 16 August 1925 Tengren rescued a man who had become exhausted while trying to swim from Gaspee Point to the lighthouse.[^13]

The hurricane that struck the North Atlantic coast on September 21, 1938, is the event the Archive gives as the station's disposition: "Damaged in 1938 Hurricane."[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports that the dwelling at Bullock's Point was not habitable and the tower was unsafe.[^15] D'Entremont says the storm undermined the pier beneath the light and badly damaged the building. He says Zuius kept the light burning through the storm, and that in the morning Zuius found the wall facing the wind had been ripped away.[^16]

## After the keepers

The Archive records the light as discontinued in 1938.[^1] D'Entremont says it was discontinued soon after the storm and that the structure was torn down a few years later.[^16]

## Today

An automated light on a skeleton tower now stands on the base of the old lighthouse, and a small lighted beacon on the old foundation marks the spot.[^16] The Archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^1] Do not land on the pier or climb the skeleton tower.
