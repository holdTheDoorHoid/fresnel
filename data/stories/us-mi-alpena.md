Alpena Light stands on the north breakwater where the Thunder Bay River meets Lake Huron, in the city of Alpena, Michigan. Its third tower, a black skeletal iron frame with a lantern on a concrete pier, went up in 1914.[^1][^8] The Coast Guard declared the lighthouse excess in 2011, and in 2013 it was awarded to the Michigan Lighthouse Conservancy.[^1]

## The place

Alpena grew at the river mouth.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the city "straddles Thunder Bay River where it empties into the protected waters of Thunder Bay," and that the river "was a natural conduit for the lumber industry."[^1]

Terry Pepper's history of the light says the lumber barons petitioned for a pier and light at the river mouth. In 1867 Congress followed the Lighthouse Board and voted $10,000 for a light on Trowbridge Point, about a mile and a quarter (2 km) from the river.[^2] The Board's 1867 report then recommended the river mouth instead. Pepper quotes it: "subsequent examination has shown that the proper site is at or near the mouth of the river."[^2] Congress approved the site change on 20 July 1868.[^2]

The piers that reached into the bay were built by a private company, which charged tolls on vessels using them.[^1] In June 1874 Congress provided $20,000 for the light, on condition that "all tolls on vessels entering or leaving the river shall be abolished after the light-house is completed."[^1]

The first light was temporary. LighthouseFriends says the Board set a light on a cluster of piles at the river mouth in September 1875.[^1] Pepper says Enoch G. Howard was appointed its keeper, and that it was first shown on the night of 25 October 1875.[^2] Two years later the Board's notice described it as "the light heretofore exhibited on a cluster of piles at the mouth of Thunder Bay river."[^3]

## Building the light

Work on the permanent light began in 1877 with a timber crib on the north pier.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says the open frame tower on the crib was finished in July, and that its lantern held a sixth-order Fresnel lens, with the focal plane about 44½ feet (13.6 m) above the base.[^1]

The Board's notice to mariners, printed in the *Alpena Weekly Argus* of 22 August 1877, gave 18 August 1877 as the date the new light would show: "a fixed red light will, on and after August 18, 1877, be shown from an open frame tower, painted white."[^3]

Keeper Howard had no house of his own at first. Pepper says he rented a room in town.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the city lot for a six-room frame dwelling was bought in June 1877, and that Howard, who had worked as a boot and shoe maker before he came to the light, was the first keeper to live in it.[^1]

A fog bell followed. LighthouseFriends says a 1,500-pound bell was cast in 1890.[^1] The Board's annual report for 1891 says the new fog bell "was sounded for the first time" on 20 March 1891.[^5]

## The keepers

The USLHS roster gives Enoch G. Howard as keeper from 1875 to 1889, and John C. Wallace from 1889 to 1915.[^8] Howard was keeper through the fire of 1888, described below. Pepper gives a later end for Wallace: he says Wallace resigned on 7 June 1920, after 31 years at the light.[^2] The roster has no keeper between Wallace's 1915 and the next entry, which begins in 1920.[^8]

George H. A. Burzlaff had served at lights in Wisconsin and Michigan before Alpena.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he began as second assistant at Raspberry Island in June 1907, and that on 20 June 1920 he "was placed in charge of Alpena Lighthouse and remained there until his retirement in 1946."[^1][^8]

The roster's later entries run from the late 1950s into the 1970s. The Coast Guard crew who served here are listed under their own heading.[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 12 July 1888 a fire broke out at a sawmill on the river and swept across the town.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse was destroyed, along with its sixth-order lens, its lantern room and the supplies stored in the tower.[^1] Pepper says the fire was "fueled by high winds and the huge stacks of lumber along the river bank," and that it traveled along the wooden pier to the crib.[^2]

Pepper says Howard could do nothing to stop the fire from consuming the lighthouse. He and his wife fought the fire at their own dwelling all night, with buckets of water carried from the river.[^2] LighthouseFriends says Howard saved the dwelling, along with the oil and supplies kept there.[^1]

The *Alpena Weekly Argus* of 18 July 1888 gave so much room to "the report of the fire" that it held over the city council's proceedings.[^4] On page three it printed a notice from Commander Elmer, the inspector for the Eleventh District, that a temporary red light from a lens lantern hoisted on a pole would be shown "at the end of the crib on which the light house stood, until a new lighthouse is erected."[^4]

The replacement stood on a new crib. It was 18 feet (5.5 m) square at the base and rose 50 feet (15.2 m). Its lantern came from the abandoned lighthouse at North Point, Milwaukee, and a fourth-order lens intended for Two Harbors, Minnesota, was put into use on 1 October 1888.[^1] A sixth-order lens replaced it in April 1889.[^1]

## After the keepers

The third light was first exhibited on 26 June 1914, with an electric incandescent lamp in place of a kerosene lamp, LighthouseFriends says.[^1] On 22 April 1918 its characteristic changed from fixed red to a white light that was on for one second and off for one second.[^1] Pepper dates the change to occulting white to 28 June 1918, and describes alternating one-second periods of light and darkness.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1918 list of changed lights records a change to "occulting white" at Alpena.[^6]

The sources disagree on when the fog bell gave way to a siren. LighthouseFriends dates an electric siren to 28 June 1902.[^1] The USLHS record lists an electric siren in 1920, and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports a fog signal "established" at Alpena that year, with an electric siren.[^8][^7] Pepper also places the siren in 1920, three weeks after George Burzlaff's arrival.[^2] LighthouseFriends says a compressed-air fog signal replaced the siren in 1932, and that the tower was painted red in 1950.[^1]

The fourth-order lens came out of the tower around 1987, LighthouseFriends says, and is now at the Grand Traverse Lighthouse.[^1] The USLHS record dates a fourth-order lens to 1914.[^8] Pepper says the lens went back to Detroit for storage when the sixth-order lens was installed in 1889, and was later installed in the 1914 tower. He says the Coast Guard later removed it and replaced it with a 250 mm acrylic optic.[^2]

In June 2011 the Coast Guard declared the station excess and offered it to eligible groups under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000. In August 2013 it was awarded to the Michigan Lighthouse Conservancy, whose president estimated $60,000 to $80,000 for restoration.[^1]

## Today

LighthouseFriends says the Michigan Lighthouse Conservancy owns the light and the tower is closed. Visitors can walk the paved breakwater path from downtown for a view of the lighthouse.[^1] The USLHS record marks the station as not open to the public, and lists its current optic as a VRB-25.[^8] Pepper describes a 250 mm acrylic optic.[^2] The light has an official website with a webcam view of the harbor, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1]
