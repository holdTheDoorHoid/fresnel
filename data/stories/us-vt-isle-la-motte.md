Isle La Motte Light stands at the northern end of Isle La Motte, an island in Lake Champlain in Grand Isle County, Vermont. Its cast-iron tower was first lit in 1881.[^1][^3] In 1933 the Lighthouse Service put a steel skeleton tower beside it, and the old tower stood dark until 2002, when the light went back into it.[^1][^3] Today it is a working aid to navigation on private property, and visitors are asked to stay off the grounds.[^2][^3]

## The place

In 1666 the French Captain Pierre de La Motte established the first European outpost on Lake Champlain, near what is now Saint Anne's Shrine.[^1] The northern end of the lake was dangerous water. Shoals and reefs near the Canadian line were hard on any vessel that left its course, most of all at night or in haze.[^2] The Champlain Canal, opened in 1823, brought more cargo and passengers onto the lake.[^2]

The Coast Guard's date for the station is 1829, when a lantern was shown from an upper window of a house at the island's north end.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the first lantern hung from a pine tree east of the old fort in 1829, and was later moved to the upper window of a nearby stone house.[^1] The two accounts differ on where the first light hung.

## Building the light

In 1853 the collector of customs at Plattsburgh recommended that the Lighthouse Board spend $5,000 on a lighthouse at the northern end of the island.[^1] William D. Fraser of the Corps of Engineers reported that a small light on the island would cost only $500.[^1]

In 1856 the federal government bought land on the island for $50.[^1] Vermonter says a lantern went on top of a small stone pyramid in 1857, and that a local farmer was paid to light it.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes a limestone tower with a large lens lantern, 18.5 feet (5.6 m) high.[^1] The farmer lived elsewhere, and that was the difficulty.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that on stormy nights the lamp often went out.[^1]

Authorization for a permanent lighthouse came in 1879. LighthouseFriends says the result was a 25-foot (7.6 m) tower of curved cast-iron plates, with a detached keeper's cottage. The tower held a sixth-order Fresnel lens, with a focal plane of forty-six feet (14 m), and Keeper Wilbur F. Hill first exhibited the light in 1881.[^1][^3] A brick oil house was added in 1906.[^1]

When work began on the iron tower in 1880, a local landowner claimed the lot.[^1] The Board's reply, as LighthouseFriends prints it, was that the claim was "undoubtedly made to extort money from the United States."[^1]

In 1891 the Light-House Board recorded steps built "leading from the shore to the top of the hill," along with various repairs.[^5]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists four principal keepers: John D. Reynolds (1857 to 1862), Ezra Pike (1862 to 1871), Wilbur Fisk Hill (1871 to 1919) and William W. Grant (1919 to 1933).[^3]

Hill began tending the old pyramid light in 1871.[^2] LighthouseFriends says his service ran for forty-eight years, until he retired in 1919 at the age of seventy-six.[^1] He also kept a 100-acre farm nearby.[^1] Hill is named in the Service's table of life and property saved in fiscal 1915 as "W. F. Hill, keeper, Isle La Motte Light Station, Vt."[^4]

Hill retired at the end of January 1919. He died on 19 March 1919, before a replacement was appointed, and LighthouseFriends says his daughter Gladys looked after the station until Grant arrived on 18 April.[^1] Grant had been an engineer on lighthouse tenders for more than twenty years, but had not kept a light before.[^1]

LighthouseFriends prints a description Grant gave in 1928 of his routine. It begins: "In the morning, I turn out at sunrise and go out to the lighthouse."[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Light-House Board's report for 1868 said of the light, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, that "In stormy nights it is not to be relied on, though it is one of considerable importance."[^1] Vermonter describes the same problem: on stormy nights the lamp could go out before anyone reached it to relight it.[^2]

In fiscal 1915 the Commissioner of Lighthouses listed Hill in the table of vessels and employees who had rendered assistance.[^4] LighthouseFriends says he was commended that year for towing a disabled boat with three people in it to shore.[^1]

## After the keepers

In the 1930s, LighthouseFriends says, the Lighthouse Service replaced many of the lights on Lake Champlain with steel skeleton towers. At Isle La Motte the steel tower, with an automatic beacon on top, went up in 1933.[^1][^3] Grant was told the light would be automated at the end of June 1933. He served a month at Block Island Southeast before retiring on disability, and was allowed to stay on at Isle La Motte until the station was sold.[^1]

The old tower went dark, and LighthouseFriends counts about seven decades of darkness before 2002. A private family acquired the lighthouse in 1949.[^1]

In 2001 the Coast Guard weighed the cost of replacing the deteriorating steel tower and decided that returning the light to the original tower might be the cheaper course.[^1] The light went back into the old tower in 2002, and Isle La Motte Lighthouse was in active service again on 5 October 2002.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says a crowd of over 300 watched it come back.[^1]

## Today

The tower was painted bright red when new. It has faded to a light pink that the locals call "Nantucket red."[^1] The Lighthouse Society's record lists it as an active aid to navigation, and as not open to the public.[^3] Vermonter describes both island lights as private homes that are closed to the public, and asks readers not to enter the grounds or approach either tower.[^2]
