The Nootka Lighthouse stands on San Rafael Island, off the entrance to Friendly Cove (Yuquot) at Nootka Sound on the west coast of Vancouver Island, in an area First Nations peoples had occupied for thousands of years before any light was built.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The Canadian Register of Historic Places' statement of significance says the area was occupied for over 4,300 years by First Nations peoples of the Nootka Sound region, and that in the 18th century the Sound became a centre of early contact, trade (notably furs) and diplomacy between First Nations and European explorers.[^1] The light station sits on the summit of San Rafael Island, and the same statement notes extensive archaeological remains on it, mostly related to Mowachaht-Muchalaht history.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends recounts the lead-up. The *King David* was wrecked on Bajo Reef near Nootka Sound in December 1905, and her crew were marooned for thirty-three days, about eight miles from Friendly Cove, before being sighted. In January 1906, the month of their rescue, Herbert Smith, the storekeeper at Friendly Cove, established a light at the entrance to the Sound. Three years later a petition went to Victoria, signed by settlers and others in the West Coast shipping trade, asking for a light to mark the Sound.[^4] The account does not say that the wreck prompted either step. The Heritage Lighthouse statement adds that the Department of Marine and Fisheries "yielded to local demands" in building one as part of a wider expansion of British Columbia's lights.[^1]

## Building the light

The Commissioner of Lights' report lists "Friendly Cove, Nootka Sound" among the year's new lights: a lighthouse "established on the summit of the middle and largest island of the St. Miguel group" off the entrance to the cove, with a fourth-order lens.[^3] The same report records the department's steamer spending 19 October to 1 November landing construction materials for the Nootka lighthouse.[^5]

The first Nootka lighthouse was a square wooden dwelling with a lantern on top.[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives its height as 11 m (37 ft) and its focal plane as 33 m (108 ft), and says Herbert Smith, by then keeper, first showed the light on 15 March 1911.[^4] The heritage statements give only the year, 1911.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

Smith had emigrated from England with his eldest son; in 1912 he asked for leave to bring back his wife and the rest of his family, and the 22-year-old son was left in charge of the light meanwhile. Lighthouse Friends says the cold, clammy weather told on Smith's wife, who within a few years was an invalid with rheumatism, and that Smith took an appointment at Cape Mudge, where his daughter could attend school. His friend William Taylor succeeded him.[^4]

Taylor took over on 18 October 1918. A magnitude 6.9 earthquake struck just off the coast at midnight on 6 December 1918, and the light sustained only minor damage; a month later Taylor resigned. In his resignation, citing the damp and the storms, he wrote that "it has been necessary to mop the bedroom floor during the night" (as quoted by Lighthouse Friends).[^4] After weeks without a replacement, Patrick Foley, formerly of St. Mary's Lighthouse in Newfoundland, took the post but resigned a few months later after a month-long illness; Arthur Marks, a local man, followed and kept the light until he retired in 1927.[^4]

Thomas Fish arrived in January 1929 and lost two fingers in the derrick winch gear five months later. In August 1935 his wife Annie found him unconscious after a stroke; she and their son Gilbert kept the light for a month before taking him to Point Atkinson, where he died on 29 May 1936.[^4] Annie wrote to the Marine Agent that she was taking his ashes back to Nootka to scatter them in the sea off the home he loved.[^4]

Later keepers listed by Lighthouse Friends include Howard Frazer Chamberlain (1936-1941), Abe Price (1965-1970), and Edward Kidder, whose tenure ran from 1970 to 2003. Lighthouse Friends reports that on 10 January 1989, after a Mayday call, Ed Kidder and his son Dean found a family of six suffering severe carbon monoxide poisoning aboard a clam boat and helped them ashore, from where a Coast Guard helicopter evacuated them.[^4] The artist Emily Carr visited in 1929.[^4]

## After the keepers

A diaphone fog alarm replaced a hand horn in 1927.[^4] The present tower was built in 1958 to replace the original lighthouse.[^1][^2] It is a square galvanized steel tower inside a steel skeleton;[^2] the heritage statement notes its aluminum lantern and enclosed metal staircase.[^1] Lighthouse Friends describes the new towers at Nootka and Cape Beale, built to the same plans, as 32-foot (about 10 m) steel skeletal towers.[^4] The keepers' dwellings, engine room and fog alarm building and boathouse were added during the modernization of the 1950s and 1960s.[^1] The lighthouse was designated a heritage lighthouse under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act in 2014.[^2]

## Today

Lighthouse Friends reports that the lighthouse is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard, with the grounds open and the tower closed. Friendly Cove, next to the lighthouse, is reached in summer by the *Uchuck III* from Gold River, and Air Nootka flies between Gold River and Friendly Cove.[^4]
