Fort Point Light is a lighthouse on top of Fort Point, the brick army fort under the south end of the Golden Gate Bridge in San Francisco.[^2][^20] Its nine-sided iron tower is the third light to stand at the point, and its lamp went out for good on 1 September 1934.[^2][^20][^13]

## The place

The Golden Gate is the narrowest part of the entrance to San Francisco Bay.[^20] Fort Point and Lime Point mark its southern and northern flanks.[^20] The Spanish fortified the cliff, building the Castillo de San Joaquin in 1794, a small adobe fort that housed nine to thirteen cannon.[^4] The Army Corps of Engineers began work on the brick fort in 1853, according to the park's archive; LighthouseFriends gives 1854.[^4][^20] Work took seven years.[^20] The fort "never saw action," as the park's own archive puts it.[^4]

The water was the hazard. Off Fort Point a shoal runs out to the northeast, and the Lighthouse Board called it Fort Point Shoal in 1901.[^23][^22] In summer, the Gate can be "socked in day after day for weeks on end," the National Park Service tells visitors.[^3]

## Building the light

The first tower came out of a contract scandal. A Treasury official named John McGinnis took the contract for the eight West Coast lighthouses, including the one at Fort Point, and passed the work to two Baltimore builders.[^6] The Fort Point structure was to be one of the "Cape Cod dwellings with a light tower" rising through the center.[^6] A congressional committee later concluded that the charges were true, although the two builders were cleared.[^7]

The crew began erecting the tower in December 1852.[^7] It was never lit.[^20] In September 1853 the Army tore down the new, unlighted tower, because it stood where the fort's batteries were to go: "the Army had decided to place batteries at Fort Point, and the lighthouse was in the way."[^8] The lens bought for it was later used at Point Pinos.[^8]

LighthouseFriends says the second tower stood on a narrow ledge between the fort and the water.[^20] The sources give its first light in three ways. The 1972 Historic Structure Report says it was "exhibited on March 21, 1855."[^9] The National Park Service's web page says it was "Lit on February 1,1855."[^3] LighthouseFriends says it was first exhibited in March 1855.[^20] The 1972 report says that little is known about this tower.[^10] Its 1859 Light List entry gave the focal plane as 36 feet (11 m) above the base of the tower and 52 feet (16 m) above the water, with a fifth-order lens.[^10] A magazine description printed in the late 1850s and quoted in the 1972 report describes the fog bell as "a Fog Bell, weighing 1,100 pounds, and worked by machinery, that strikes every ten seconds, for five taps."[^10]

The light stayed in service until 1863, when additions to the fortifications required that it be moved, because a sea wall was being built, and the tower stood in its way.[^10] In July 1863 the Board decided "to place the light over a stairway at the fort."[^11] The District Engineer, R. S. Williamson, was given "carte blanche" to build the new tower.[^12] The National Park Service's tour credits Williamson with the design.[^2] The tower was nine-sided, with a watchroom "of boiler iron." The Board figured it cost slightly less than $4,000.[^12] The 1972 report says the light was "apparently exhibited in January, 1864".[^12] LighthouseFriends describes a twenty-eight-foot (8.5 m) iron skeletal tower, with a nine-sided white tower capped by a black lantern room, and a light 83 feet (25 m) above the water.[^20] The 1972 report describes cast iron resting on a granite curb.[^17]

The sources do not agree on the height of the light. The United States Lighthouse Service's archive record gives 81 feet (25 m) above mean high water, LighthouseFriends gives 83 feet (25 m), and the 1972 report quotes the 1871 Light List at 166 feet (51 m) above the water.[^1][^20][^13]

The lens changed more than once. In 1878 the light went from a fifth-order fixed white to a fourth-order fixed white, varied each minute by a red flash.[^20] In 1903 a new fourth-order lens gave alternating red and white flashes, five seconds apart.[^20] In April 1912 a new lens produced a white light, on for one second and off for one second.[^20] The archive gives the original optic as a fourth-order Fresnel lens installed in 1855.[^1] The 1972 report and LighthouseFriends both say the first lens was fifth order.[^10][^20]

Access to the light was by stairs.[^20] The keepers went down steep stairs on the bluff, through the fort, and up a granite spiral stair to reach the lantern.[^20] A wooden bridge across the gap was built in 1874, according to the 1972 report.[^15] LighthouseFriends puts the bridge in 1876.[^20] The keepers' dwellings went up on the bluff in 1884, with a tramway for supplies in 1897, and a new head keeper's house at the base of the bluff in 1908.[^20]

The fog signal changed from bell to trumpet.[^20][^14] In 1880 the fort's bell was exchanged for the larger bell at Yerba Buena Island, which LighthouseFriends describes as a 3,000-pound bell.[^20][^30] In 1899 the Board said the bell had "long been complained of as inadequate, both in position and power," and proposed a second-class Daboll trumpet.[^21] Congress provided $7,000 in 1902. LighthouseFriends dates the start of the Daboll trumpet, atop the fort, to 30 September 1904; the 1972 report says it was first used on 1 October 1904.[^20][^14][^29]

## The keepers

The archive lists 71 keeper stints at Fort Point, from 1853 to 1934.[^1] A 1972 National Park Service table also names an assistant, Ephrain Sohn, from April 1856. The archive does not list him.[^18]

The archive's first entries are for 1853 and 1854, when James Rogan was principal keeper and Sarah Rogan first assistant.[^1] That is before the second tower was lit in 1855, and neither the archive nor the 1972 report explains those years. The 1972 keepers table starts in July 1855 with B. F. Deane.[^18]

Several women kept the light as assistants, and the archive names their roles.[^1] Sarah Rogan was first assistant in 1853 and 1854.[^1] Ann Blanchard served as first assistant from 1865 to 1866. Rachel L. Jones was first assistant from 1867 to 1868, and Theresa Welch briefly in 1868. Mary Thompson was first assistant from 1869 to 1871, and the 1972 table calls her "Mrs. Mary Thompson" and says her post was abolished.[^1][^19] Sophie Huie was first assistant from 1874 to 1878, and the table says she was transferred.[^19] Edna N. Rankin was an acting assistant in 1903.[^1]

John T. Huie was principal keeper from 1871 to 1878.[^1] The 1972 table says he was transferred on 7 August 1878, and that James Rankin was appointed the same day.[^18]

James Rankin served as head keeper from 1878 to 1919, which LighthouseFriends calls an unusually long stay for one lighthouse.[^20] About 1915, LighthouseFriends reports, he told a San Francisco Call reporter that the fort offered little to see, and that it "grows monotonous."[^20] He painted landscapes of the fort in his spare time.[^20] He retired in February 1919 at the age of 78, and died in 1921.[^20] The Coast Guard named a keeper-class buoy tender after him in 1999.[^20] The archive also lists Edna N. Rankin, an acting assistant in 1903, and Arthur Rankin, a laborer in 1908. The sources do not say how they were related to James Rankin.[^1]

George D. Cobb was principal keeper from 1919 to 1934, the last head keeper.[^1] Ralph H. Jordan was first assistant in the same years.[^1] General context, not local history: the 1881 *Instructions to Light-Keepers* made the keeper responsible for the care of the light and for "the station in general." The Instructions were written for every light station, and they do not describe Fort Point.[^28]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1867 a Fourth of July salute fired from the fort badly damaged the fog-signal structure and its machinery.[^20]

On 22 February 1901 the steamer *City of Rio de Janeiro* struck a rock ledge just offshore from the fort in fog, according to LighthouseFriends, and "with a terrific sound, the City of Rio de Janeiro sank," the *San Francisco Call* reported.[^20][^23] The *Call* gave the loss as "one hundred and twenty-two" lives. The Lighthouse Board reported "a loss of over 100 lives."[^23][^22] The Board wrote that the old bell "can be heard only over a very limited area," and that the disaster "might not have occurred if an efficient signal had been in operation here."[^22] George Putnam's 1917 history also records the 1901 wreck in fog near Fort Point, with "a loss of one hundred lives."[^24]

The 1906 earthquake did little harm to the tower itself, the 1972 report says.[^13] It did pull the 92-foot (28 m) bridge southward; the 1972 report, quoting an inspection, says the south end was carried "nearly off its supports."[^16] LighthouseFriends says the bluff dwellings lost their chimneys and that the bridge was only slightly displaced.[^20] The two accounts of the bridge differ.

Rankin was also a rescuer. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin, in its March 1913 issue, reports that on 15 June he swam out to help a man from an overturned boat. The item gives no year; the item printed beside it is dated 1912, so the rescue was probably in 1912.[^25] On 29 December 1918 he rescued a boy who had fallen from the sea wall, and on 6 January 1919 he rescued another.[^26] LighthouseFriends credits him with saving eighteen people during his service at the fort.[^20] The 1919 Bulletin gives sixteen other persons.[^26]

## After the keepers

Work on the Golden Gate Bridge began in 1933.[^20] The fort's light was soon obscured by its new neighbor.[^20] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin explains the closure: the bridge's north pylon, built outside the fog signal and light, was "cutting them off from vessels passing through the Golden Gate."[^27] The light was discontinued on 1 September 1934.[^27] LighthouseFriends says that a fog signal and a navigational light were placed at the base of the bridge's south tower.[^20] The Bulletin says the headland and its shoal were marked by "a light and fog signal maintained jointly by the State highway commission and the contractor."[^27]

Fort Point became a National Historic Site on 16 October 1970, according to LighthouseFriends. The 1972 report dates President Nixon's signing of the authorizing act to 19 October 1970.[^20][^5] In 1972 the National Park Service published a historic structure report on the light, prepared by "A. Lewis Koue, Architect," with "F. Ross Holland, Historian," at its Denver Service Center.[^5]

## Today

Fort Point is now a National Park Service site, part of Golden Gate National Recreation Area.[^2] The park's own tour shows the lighthouse on top of the fort, with "bridge ironwork above."[^2] The light is no longer an aid to navigation, and the lens has been removed.[^1]

LighthouseFriends gives the fort's opening days as Friday to Sunday, 10 a.m. to 5 p.m., with Mondays and Thursdays added in summer, and advises checking the park's website.[^20] LighthouseFriends says visitors can reach the top of the fort, where the lighthouse is mounted, but are not allowed inside the lighthouse; the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^20] Do not climb the tower or the iron stair, and check the park's website before a visit.
