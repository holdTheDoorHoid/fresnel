Black Rock Point is a headland on the eastern shore of Cape Breton Island, Nova Scotia, where a light has marked the entrance to the Great Bras d'Or channel since 1868.[^1][^3][^4] The tower standing there now is the third on the point, a wooden tower of 1978 that the Canadian Coast Guard runs.[^1][^4]

## The place

The light stands near the shore, at the entrance to the Great Bras d'Or from St. Ann's Bay.[^2] The Coast Guard list places it on the south side of the entrance, but the Lighthouse Directory says the east side.[^4][^5] The register says the region "was known for its severe weather and many shipwrecks," and that the lighthouses on the site guided coal and other cargoes from Sydney down through the calmer waters of the Bras d'Or Lakes.[^1] The register names no particular wreck.

In its report for the year ended 30 June 1901, the Department of Marine and Fisheries wrote that once new lights at the entrance to the Great Bras d'Or were established, "the lights at Black Rock point and Careys beach will be of little use."[^7]

## Building the light

Lighthouse Friends says the Board of Works of Nova Scotia awarded a $1,750 contract on 20 July 1867 to Robert Purves to build the first light, a one-storey building with a three-sided window projecting from its hipped roof.[^2] The register starts the story earlier. It says the light was established in 1866, when the Committee on Navigation Securities recommended one to serve the Great Bras d'Or, and it describes the first building as a "square tapered, wooden tower with an attached dwelling."[^1] The two accounts differ on the shape of that building and on when the work began. Both date the first tower to 1868.[^1][^3]

The government bought three acres for the light from the estate of the late Roderick Morrison, for $84.[^2] That land had been part of 100 acres granted to Roderick Morrison in 1842.[^2] Donald Morrison, the first keeper, was responsible for four lights shining from the building: two seaward, one across the channel and one toward the harbour.[^2]

A two-storey lighthouse with a square lantern room was built in 1937 to replace the first.[^2] The Lighthouse Directory also dates it to 1937.[^5] The register instead dates a second two-storey lighthouse to 1925.[^1] The register says that building was moved around 1980 because the shoreline was eroding.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says it was bought in 1979 and moved a couple of hundred metres to the south to serve as a private house. The Directory says it was sold in 1979 and moved about a kilometre to the southwest of the present light.[^2][^5]

The present tower was built in 1978.[^1] The register describes it as a square wooden tower built into one corner of an engine building, and gives the building as 10.8 m high.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates the rectangular engine building to 1977, with a square tower built on one of its corners to carry a rotating airport beacon.[^2] The Directory gives the tower's height as 10.5 m (35 ft), and the Coast Guard list gives 12.4 m.[^5][^4] An octagonal metal lantern was installed in 1987.[^1] The Directory says the 1978 light first carried a rotating aerobeacon without a lantern.[^1][^5]

## The keepers

Donald Morrison kept the light from 1868 until he died in 1888.[^2] His son Alexander looked after it for a few years, and then a second son, Malcolm, took charge.[^2] Lighthouse Friends' keeper list prints A.F. Morrison from 1888 to 1892 and Malcolm D. Morrison from 1892 to 1929. Its text calls the first of these Alexander, but the page does not say that the two are the same man.[^2] The Department of Marine's list of lightkeepers for the year ended 31 March 1911 gives M. D. Morrison at Black Rock Point.[^2][^6]

After Morrison came W. Fahie, from 1929 to 1930, and T. T. Stephenson, from 1930 to at least 1937.[^2] Lighthouse Friends lists Frank Kozera as keeper from 1977 to 1990. He left in 1990, after the government refused to let him keep living in the dwelling under a lease agreement.[^2] The register describes the present tower as fully automated, without giving a date for that.[^1]

## After the keepers

The old keeper's house was sold in 1979 and moved, and it became a private residence.[^2][^5] The register gives 20 October 2017 as the date the lighthouse was recognized as a heritage lighthouse.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the designation is there to protect it.[^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard is listed as owner of the present light, and the Lighthouse Directory says the site is open and the tower closed.[^2][^5] Visitors can drive part of the way down the access road, but must park at a gate and walk the rest of the way, Lighthouse Friends says.[^2] Lighthouse Friends' history section also says the lighthouse "is reportedly now owned by Island Lights Inc."; the sources do not make clear which building that refers to.[^2] The Directory names Island Lights Inc. as the owner of the former keeper's house, which it says can be viewed from the road.[^5]

The Coast Guard list gives the present light a long flash of white every six seconds, with a focal height of 25.8 m and a nominal range of 13 nautical miles.[^4] It lists the light as operating all year round, 24 hours a day.[^4] The Directory gives a lower focal height of 23.5 m, and describes a fog horn that sounds a three-second blast every thirty seconds.[^5] The register notes a seasonal fog horn on the grounds.[^1]
