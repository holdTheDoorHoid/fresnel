Cape Disappointment is the headland on the north side of the Columbia River's mouth, in Pacific County, Washington. Its lighthouse, lit in 1856, is the oldest in the state,[^1] and it is still an active aid to navigation.[^2] From 1856 to 1898 the tower carried a first-order Fresnel lens, the largest size, before that lens was moved to a newer tower two miles north.[^3]

## The place

The Columbia flows into the Pacific across a bar that mariners have long dreaded. LighthouseFriends counts 234 ships stranded, sunk or burned near the river's mouth between 1725 and 1961.[^2] Before there was a light, ships steering for the river used the cape itself as a landmark: the same source says three spruce trees on the summit were topped so that a ship could line them up from five miles offshore, and Hudson's Bay Company employees are said to have set a white flag and burning trees on the cape in 1812 to help their ship *Beaver* in.[^2][^3]

Congress first provided for a light here in 1848, but the $15,000 it voted for this light, for New Dungeness and for buoys from the Columbia's mouth to Astoria was, in the words of George R. Putnam's 1917 history of the service, "totally inadequate," and nothing was built.[^4] In 1850 the Coast Survey's Lieutenant William P. McArthur argued that the river's trade could not wait, writing that "The greatly increasing commerce of Oregon demands that these improvements be made immediately."[^3] A Coast Survey sub-assistant, A. M. Harrison, recommended a site near the southern edge of the cape, about 250 feet (76 m) above high water, low enough to stay under the fog banks that covered the summit and with a landing at Baker's Bay below.[^3]

## Building the light

A contract for the tower was signed on 30 April 1852 for $31,000, later increased by $7,500 for changes to the design.[^2] The total of $38,500 matches the cost HistoryLink gives for the station.[^3] The builders' first attempt ended in disaster. The contractors' supply ship *Oriole* waited eight days offshore, tried the bar on 18 September 1853 and was wrecked directly below the cape; the 32 men aboard escaped, but the ship and every building supply were lost.[^2] The National Register nomination, which dates the wreck only to September 1853, calls the tower "the most sorely needed light tower in [the] continental United States."[^1]

The sources differ on the building timeline. The National Register nomination says the tower was completed in 1854 and stood without a light until a first-order Fresnel lens arrived in 1856.[^1] LighthouseFriends says construction began again about two years after the wreck and was held up when the upper part of the tower proved too narrow for the lantern room, so that the whole tower was taken down brick by brick and rebuilt.[^2] HistoryLink gives a third version: a second shipment of materials arrived in 1854 and building followed, with the lens installed two years later.[^3] The accounts do not fit together, and the sources cited here do not settle it.

The light was first lit on 15 October 1856,[^2][^3] showing a fixed white light.[^3] The tower is of brick and about 53 feet (16 m) tall, with the light 220 feet (67 m) above the sea.[^2] One account of the lens says it was made in 1822 and had already served at a New England lighthouse;[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends instead says it was a first-order lens made by Louis Sautter & Co. of Paris.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's archive record describes the tower as dressed stone, against the brick of the other sources.[^5] No dwelling fitted at the tower's foot: the keepers lived in a separate house downhill, a considerable distance away.[^2] The station also had a 1,600-pound (730 kg) fog bell, which struck nine times a minute in fog.[^3] Mariners found it of little use against the roar of the surf, and it was discontinued in 1881.[^2]

The light's weakness was its view to the north. The Light-House Board wrote, in a report bound in the volume for 1891 and 1892, that the existing light was "inadequate for the purposes of commerce and navigation," proposed a first-order light at North Head, and said that once it was built the cape's own light could be reduced "to a light of the fourth order."[^6] Congress did not fund the full cost. North Head's tower was finished in 1898, and because only half the requested money had come, the Board moved the first-order lens there and put a fourth-order lens, built by Barbier & Benard in 1896, in the old tower.[^3][^2] HistoryLink dates the first-order lens's debut at North Head to 16 May 1898; the new lens at Cape Disappointment flashed alternately red and white.[^3] The first-order lens is now displayed at the Lewis and Clark Interpretive Center in the state park.[^2][^3]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists John Boyd as principal keeper from 1854 to 1865, then Joel W. Munson (1865 to 1877), James Anderson (1877 to 1894), Adam J. Hartman (1894 to 1898), Isaac L. Smith (1898 to 1909) and Lars C. Hansen (1909 to 1924), with four more principal keepers before the roster ends in 1932.[^5]

Boyd, the first keeper, died on duty on 26 October 1865; the roster dates his term from 1854, two years before the light was lit.[^2][^5] Surviving letters, as LighthouseFriends summarises them, show a station that was hard to live at: the dwelling stood so far from the tower that whoever had the night watch had to sleep up there, and Boyd asked his superior in San Francisco for a stove; in 1858 a letter (LighthouseFriends prints the writer's name as Boyle) reported about a foot of water in the cellar of the dwelling; and for five quarters he and his assistants went unpaid and lived on credit.[^2] In May 1865 he complained that when a carpenter came to build an addition to the dwelling, one assistant, C. Flores, dug the post holes but the other, Henry Brown, refused to.[^2]

Munson was keeper for twelve years. After the bark *Industry* was wrecked near the cape on 15 March 1865 with only seven of 24 people surviving, he found a battered longboat on the beach and set out to turn it into a lifeboat.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he was a fiddler who organised two dances in Astoria, at $2.50 a person, and raised over $200 for the repairs.[^2] The roster also lists a Sarah S. Munson as second assistant from 1867 to 1871; none of the sources cited says how she was related to the keeper.[^5]

James Anderson came to the station as first assistant in 1865, left for a time to be head keeper at Willapa Bay, and came back in 1877 to replace Munson.[^2][^5] The station's logbook entries, as LighthouseFriends prints them, give the family's life in plain lines. On 4 August 1881 there was a wedding in the keeper's dwelling, between Anderson, then fifty-four, and Henrietta Sorenson, twenty-four. On 15 December 1883 the log praised a sunset: "A more beautiful sunset could never have been witnessed as last night." Two days later a daughter was born at 3 a.m. She died on 13 October 1884, aged nine months and twenty-six days, and the log records the death of another infant child of the Andersons on 26 July 1885.[^2]

The longest single stint on the roster is Martinus Larsen's, as first assistant from 1896 to 1919.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The station's own people were not safe. On 25 October 1858 Second Assistant Keeper Harrington was crossing to Astoria in the station boat when it capsized; a man ashore saw him climb onto the hull and sent three men in a canoe, but the boat drifted into the breakers and Harrington was not seen again.[^2]

When the Civil War fortifications went up around the light, the guns became a hazard to it. A 15-inch gun fired in 1865 broke eleven panes in the lantern room, and Boyd's suggestion to move the light was answered with orders to open all the windows in the station buildings before firing.[^2] In 1871 a new double dwelling with eleven rooms on each side was built 1,300 feet (400 m) north of the tower, and a new bell house was needed because a gun blast had shattered the old one.[^2] HistoryLink says instead that the blast shattered the bell itself.[^3] LighthouseFriends sends the discontinued bell to West Point on Puget Sound; HistoryLink sends it to Warrior Rock on the Columbia.[^2][^3]

Munson's boat saved lives. On 5 May 1866 the *W. B. Scranton*, carrying 800 tons of freight from San Francisco, was driven onto the middle sands of the bar, and Munson launched the boat and rescued her whole crew; her master, Captain Paul Corno, had been one of the seven who survived the *Industry*.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates a life-saving station on the cape to 1871 and credits Munson's efforts;[^2] the National Register nomination instead says the Life Saving Station at Fort Canby was established in 1877 by Captain J. H. White of the Revenue Service, and that the lighthouse served as its lookout.[^1]

Captains also complained that ships coming from the north could not see the light, and HistoryLink cites the wrecks of the *Whistler* in 1883 and the *Grace Roberts* in 1887 on beaches north of the cape.[^3]

In November 1899 the Columbia River light-vessel No. 50, moored off the bar, broke her chain in a 74-mile gale. After three tow lines parted she was beached on the sand between McKenzie Head and the rocks of the cape, and her crew was brought ashore by breeches buoy by the Cape Disappointment life-saving crew with a detachment of soldiers and local citizens.[^7] Putnam adds that she lay on the beach for sixteen months before being hauled 700 yards (640 m) overland and launched in the Columbia.[^4]

On 29 January 1921 a record gale struck the Washington and Oregon coast. At Cape Disappointment a window in the assistant keeper's dwelling was blown in and many trees fell across the road to North Head and Ilwaco.[^8]

## After the keepers

Under a 1917 appropriation, the Lighthouse Service built an electric power station at the Cape Disappointment light station, supplying current to four lights nearby.[^9] A radiobeacon was set up in 1936 and the light was electrified in 1937.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says the black band around the tower was added about 1930 to tell it apart from North Head.[^2] The Coast Guard planned to discontinue the light in 1965, calling the lightship and entrance range lights sufficient, but protests from the Columbia River Bar Pilots kept it burning, and it was automated in 1973.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's keeper roster ends in 1932, so the people who tended the light between then and automation are not covered here.[^5]

## Today

The light is still an active aid to navigation with its fourth-order lens, according to LighthouseFriends and the US Lighthouse Society.[^2][^5] It is on Coast Guard property. In July 2020 the Coast Guard and Cape Disappointment State Park announced that the trail from the Lewis and Clark Interpretive Center to Deadman's Cove and the lighthouse was closed to the public for safety, and said Station Cape Disappointment would look at dedicated tours of the lighthouse.[^10] Treat the tower as closed unless the Coast Guard announces otherwise. The park's other lighthouse, North Head, is open to visitors in season (May to 30 September, per the state parks site),[^11] and the first-order lens that served here until 1898 is on display at the interpretive center.[^2][^3]
