Two Harbors Light stands on a rocky point between Agate Bay and Burlington Bay, on Lake Superior's North Shore in the town of Two Harbors, Minnesota.[^2][^3] It was first lit in April 1892 to help ore boats reach Agate Bay.[^2][^10] Minnesota Public Radio calls it "the oldest continuously operating light on the North Shore of Lake Superior."[^11] The red brick tower and keeper's house still stand.[^9] The Coast Guard ran the light until 2001, when the Lake County Historical Society, which owns it, took over its maintenance and operation, with volunteer keepers.[^9][^2]

## The place

"At the time the Two Harbors Light Station was completed in 1892, this was the major shipping point for the iron ores of the Mesabi Range, far surpassing Duluth in importance."[^3] A railroad to link Agate Bay with the Vermilion Range was begun in 1883.[^2] LighthouseFriends says "The first load of iron ore from the Vermilion Range was loaded on the steamer Hecla and consort-barge Ironton on August 1, 1884."[^2]

Congress appropriated $10,000 for a light at Two Harbors on 4 August 1886.[^2] The Lighthouse Board first decided to put the light at the outer end of the harbour pier, but the pier was judged "so narrow and so exposed to the sea that the station, if built upon it, would be insecure."[^2] The Board therefore wanted the headland, and buying the land took years.[^2] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 says that negotiations for the site had been carried on since 1886 without satisfactory result.[^4] In July 1890 proceedings to condemn the land were begun, "pending which the owner deeded the site required to the United States."[^4]

## Building the light

Plans were drawn and the work let in June 1891.[^2] LighthouseFriends says work on the headland began on 15 July 1891, and that blasting for the foundation went on through August and September.[^2] The historical society says "Nearly 600 tons of bedrock were blasted out of the point to form the foundation for the new light tower and head keeper's quarters."[^9] The lighthouse and its fog signal were finished in November 1891.[^2]

LighthouseFriends describes the complex as "a two-story, square, redbrick dwelling with gables facing the south and west," with a twelve-foot-square light tower between the gables.[^2] The HAER inventory of 1979 gives the keeper's house as 35 feet (11 m) square, and the tower as 12 feet 9 inches (3.9 m) square and 49 feet 6 inches (15.1 m) tall, to the top of the ventilator ball. The two figures for the tower's width differ slightly. "The focal plane of the lens is 78 feet above the mean low water level of Lake Superior," or about 24 m.[^3] The historical society adds that "Both structures are constructed of red brick and are connected with a shared wall, 5 bricks thick."[^9]

The light was a fixed fourth-order Fresnel lens in an octagonal lantern room.[^2] The fog signal sat in a separate frame building that "housed duplicate machinery and boilers for ten-inch steam whistles."[^2] In its first five years the signal ran "an average of 576 hours each season."[^2] The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1894 says the whistle ran "some 613 hours, consuming about 42 tons of coal," and the report for 1895 says "some 964 hours, and consumed about 50 tons of coal."[^5][^6]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1896 records that the station's barn was made over into an "assistant keeper's dwelling by contract in July, 1895."[^7] The same report notes that on 14 December 1895 "a post light was established on the west end of the breakwater." That was the breakwater light, a separate station.[^7] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1897 records the Two Harbors light itself: "Color of light changed from red to white, April 10, 1897."[^8]

Small improvements followed. A parabolic deflector went on the fog-signal house in 1898, "to direct more of the sound seaward."[^2] The lamp changed from kerosene to incandescent oil vapor in 1909, and the light was electrified in 1921.[^2][^10]

The sources give different dates for the lens. LighthouseFriends says a revolving four-sided lens replaced the original fixed one in 1907, and that the revolving lens "was removed in 1969 and transferred to the Inland Seas Maritime Museum."[^2] HAER says the original Fresnel lens "was replaced in 1970 with a pair of rotating airport-type electric beacons."[^3]

## The keepers

Charles Lederle was the first head keeper, and the roster has him in the post from 1892 to 1910.[^1][^2] Henry Spurbeck was his first assistant for 1892 and 1893.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a second assistant's post was added in 1908, and that the bottom room of the tower "originally served as the bedroom for the second assistant keeper."[^2]

After Lederle came Otto Redman (1910 to 1919), Alexander McLean (1919 to 1930) and Lawrence T. Pedersen (1930 to 1941).[^1] Herbert R. Driver began as first assistant in 1935, and the roster has him as principal keeper from 1941 to 1951.[^1] No woman appears on the roster for this station.[^1]

The roster and the 1912 Bulletin spell one second assistant's name differently.[^1][^13] The Bulletin for 1912 says Hans F. Christianson, second assistant keeper of the Two Harbors Light Station, "has been commended for assisting" in the rescue of two fishermen.[^13] The roster lists a second assistant named Hans F. Christensen for 1912 and 1913.[^1] The spelling differs, and the sources do not say whether these are the same man.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The one dated rescue in the sources is from 1912.[^13] The second assistant keeper, Hans F. Christianson, was commended for assisting in "the rescue of two fishermen" from drowning.[^13] No shipwreck tied to the light is recorded in the sources this story draws on.

## After the keepers

In 1921, LighthouseFriends says, electricity "increased the light's power from 30,000 candlepower to 230,000 candlepower," and the fog signal was changed to an air diaphone.[^2] The fog signal was discontinued in 1973, and in 1981 the light was automated, which allowed its Coast Guard personnel to be stationed elsewhere.[^2]

The Lake County Historical Society "began to provide tours of the Light Station in 1988 when it was granted a lease by the US Coast Guard."[^9] LighthouseFriends dates the lease to 1987.[^2] Its page says the ownership of the lighthouse "was transferred to the Lake County Historical Society" under a bill passed in Congress, and dates the transfer to 1999. LighthouseFriends gives 1998.[^9][^2] A bed and breakfast opened in 1999, and the society took over the light's maintenance and operation from the Coast Guard in 2001.[^9][^2] The lighthouse complex "has been listed on the National Register of historic places since 1984."[^9]

The original lens came back to the station in February 2015 and went into an exhibit case.[^2] The revolving DCB-224 aerobeacon, which the society says "dated to 1969" and had come from another lighthouse, failed in November 2019. "On November 25th, 2019 the beacon had a failure that could not be repaired."[^9] On 11 July 2020 the volunteer keepers removed it. A flashing Maxiluna-300 beacon, on a five-year loan, was lit on 2 June 2021.[^9][^10]

Minnesota Public Radio reported in early July 2025 that the society had raised $50,000 for a new LED beacon "that mimics the rotational sweeping pattern of the original lighthouse," and had planned to light it on 4 July 2025.[^11][^12]

## Today

The Lake County Historical Society owns the lighthouse and runs it as a museum and a year-round bed and breakfast.[^9][^10] The society says the light "remains a private aid to navigation."[^9][^10] Tours are offered in season. LighthouseFriends gives the hours as "Monday - Saturday, 9 a.m. - 5 p.m. and Sundays from 10 a.m. - 4 p.m.," running "Memorial Day weekend through mid-October," but that page may be out of date, so check before you go.[^2]

The beacon's status is the thing to check before a visit. The society's calendar listed a "Centennial Celebration & Beacon Relighting" for 4 July 2025, when, it said, "the iconic sweep and signature of the Two Harbors Lighthouse will return to the shoreline of Agate Bay."[^12] Minnesota Public Radio, reporting in early July 2025, describes that lighting as planned for 4 July.[^11] No source read for this story confirms whether the beacon was lit on that date, so check before a visit.
