Cape Race is the south-eastern corner of Newfoundland, a headland that was often the first landfall for vessels bound to North America from England. A light has stood on it since 1856, and its present concrete tower, lit in 1907, then held the largest lighting apparatus in North or South America.[^1][^2]

## The place

Cape Race stands on the Avalon Peninsula. In the 1850s Chandler White, vice-president of the New York, Newfoundland, and London Telegraph Company, described it to the colonial secretary as the point towards which every mariner bound between Europe and America "looks as to a place of departure".[^1] In 1847, after Newfoundland's governor asked for a light on Cape Pine, the naval surveyor Henry Bayfield examined Cape Race too and concluded that a light on each headland would have its own advantages.[^1] Cape Pine's light came first, and the money for a wooden beacon on Cape Race was included in its appropriation. That beacon, a six-sided timber tower painted in alternate red and white, was finished in 1852.[^1]

A wreck helped to change the plan. In September 1854 the new iron steamer *City of Philadelphia* struck a rock off the cape in thick fog on her maiden voyage; her captain ran her ashore in Chance Cove, and all aboard were landed safely.[^1] White cited the loss in asking for a first-class light in place of the beacon.[^1] In April 1855 the Colonial Secretary told Newfoundland's governor that Parliament would be asked for £5,000 for a lighthouse at Cape Race.[^3]

## Building the light

The contract went to Alexander Gordon, the engineer of the Cape Pine tower, for an iron tower with stone dwellings at its base. The plates came from England to Trepassey in autumn 1855, and in summer 1856 twenty workmen hoisted the panels and bolted them together.[^1] Mariners were told that the light would be shown from 15 December 1856, fixed and white, so that it could not be confused with the flashing lights of Cape Spear and Cape Pine. Lighthouse Friends gives the total cost as £7,358.[^1]

Paying for it was a question of empire. Lighthouse Friends says Newfoundland's commissioners refused to carry the cost alone.[^1] Ingenium describes a July 1856 Order in Council that would levy a toll on ships crossing between British North America, the United Kingdom and New York, with the light estimated to cost about £5,160 a year to run.[^4] By that autumn the Crown's law officers had decided that the colonial legislatures had to consent first; apart from Prince Edward Island, which assented in 1857, the colonies did not rush to do so.[^4] In 1866, after complaints that the light was too feeble for so important a headland, it was changed from fixed to flashing.[^1]

The arrangement ended in 1886. A British Act of that year recites that the cost of the lighthouse had been repaid from the dues, that Newfoundland's government "have declined to undertake the maintenance of the said lighthouse", and that Canada was willing to take it on, without dues, for all time.[^5] The land involved was about three hundred acres.[^5] Lighthouse Friends gives the transfer date as 1 July 1886.[^1]

Canada built the present tower in 1906 and 1907. The department's report for the year ended 30 June 1906 says a reinforced concrete tower had been built to carry a hyper-radial flashing light and a 17-foot lantern, and that the lantern was at the site but the apparatus had not yet been installed because the season was too late.[^6] The Steel Concrete Company of Montreal built the tower for a contract price of $4,800.[^7][^8] The next report records the light going into operation on 1 October 1907, and calls it "the largest light apparatus in either North or South America".[^2] A later report, for 1908-09, gives the tower as cylindrical, painted in broad red and white vertical stripes, 30.5 m (100 ft) from base to the vane on the lantern, with the light 50 m (165 ft) above high water and visible nineteen miles.[^8] Lighthouse Friends also gives 30.5 m, while the Mistaken Point heritage centre says 29 m.[^1][^10] The Canadian Register of Historic Places calls it the first reinforced concrete lighthouse in Canada, and possibly among the first in the world.[^9]

The 1856 iron tower was taken down. Sources disagree about when: the Mistaken Point heritage centre says it was moved to Cape North in 1907, Ingenium says 1908, and the department's tables of works describe the old Cape Race tower being taken down in sections and shipped for re-erection in 1909-10.[^10][^4][^18] Ingenium reports that it was decommissioned at Cape North in 1980 and has stood at the Canada Science and Technology Museum in Ottawa since that year; Lighthouse Friends says it was used at Cape North until 1978.[^4][^3][^1]

## The keepers

William Hally, a retired captain, was the first head keeper.[^1] On Christmas 1856, ten days after the light was lit, the timber ship *Welsford* went ashore at the cape; Lighthouse Friends says Hally and his assistant lowered lines down the cliff and saved four crew while twenty-two died.[^1] Michael J. Hally appears as keeper from 1866 to at least 1878.[^1]

The Myrick family, for whom the station's interpretation centre is now named, is tied to the fog whistle. Patrick Myrick had charge of the steam whistle set up in 1873, and Lighthouse Friends says the Myrick family served as light keepers, fog alarm engineers, telegraphers and wireless operators at Cape Race from 1874 to 2007; the Mistaken Point heritage centre says 1872 to 2007.[^1][^10] Lighthouse Friends lists Patrick Myrick as head keeper from 1886 to 1897, then John Myrick from 1897 to 1922, Francis Myrick (1922), Jeremiah Myrick (1942), Patrick Myrick (1955) and James Myrick (1957 to 1973), then Tom Ryan from 1973.[^1] The department's list of lightkeepers in its report for 1907-08 names "Myrick, John" beside Cape Race, Newfoundland, which supports John Myrick's place, though that is read from the position of the names in the scan text.[^11]

Lighthouse Friends reports two 50-horsepower boilers installed in 1923 that burned 500 tons of coal a year, and an electric lighting apparatus in the lighthouse in 1926, when around ten families lived at the cape and a two-room school was built. A concrete shell was put around the tower in 1937, and in 1969 a four-person shift system let keepers' families live in Trepassey and other nearby communities.[^1] The 1909 fog alarm building is among the six buildings the Canadian Register lists as contributing to the lighthouse's heritage character.[^20]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the spring of 1909, the Department of Marine noted "the great number of bergs reported by the Cape Race station", with "as many as two hundred" counted in sight at the same time, first seen in early April and passing until mid-August.[^12]

The fog signal began in 1873 as a ten-inch steam whistle, which Lighthouse Friends says could be heard about 32 km in calm weather.[^1] On 1 December 1901 the department changed its blast to five seconds with alternating intervals of 15 and 35 seconds.[^13] Ships still struck. The steamer *Assyrian* went ashore in dense fog about a mile north of Cape Race on 5 June 1901; the report of the wreck inquiry notes that the fog signal had been heard sounding for an hour before she grounded but had been taken for the signal of a steamer.[^14] On 1 April 1907 a five-inch diaphone replaced the steam whistle.[^8]

A Marconi wireless station was built at the cape and, according to Community Stories, began service on 17 November 1904.[^15] On the night of 14 April 1912 its operators Walter Gray, Jack Goodwin and Robert Hunston handled the *Titanic*'s calls.[^15] Hunston's log, transcribed by The Rooms, shows J.C.R. Goodwin on watch hearing the *Titanic* call CQD at 10:25 p.m. Eastern Standard Time, the time used at Cape Race, with a position of 41.44 N 50.24 W, and at 11:25 p.m. has an entry to establish communication with the *Virginian* and give her all the information.[^16] The station burned on 5 May 1913 and a temporary one was installed within two days.[^15] Sources differ on its end: Community Stories says the station closed in 1930 and was dismantled in 1965, while Lighthouse Friends says a marine radio station was active until 1965.[^15][^1]

## Today

Lighthouse Friends reports the lighthouse designated a national historic site in 1974 and a Recognized Federal Heritage Building in 1990.[^1][^17] It describes the lighthouse as owned by the Canadian Coast Guard, with the grounds open and the tower closed.[^1] The fog alarm was discontinued in 1991.[^1] The Myrick Wireless Interpretation Centre, a replica building holding exhibits on early telegraphy and wireless, listed a 2025 season from 1 July to 15 September.[^10] The cape is reached by a gravel road of about 20 km from Route 10.[^1]
