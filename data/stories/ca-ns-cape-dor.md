Cape d'Or is a headland on the Bay of Fundy coast of Nova Scotia, near Advocate, where it marks the northern entrance into Minas Basin.[^1] A steam fog whistle began to sound on the cape in 1874 or 1875, depending on the source, and the cape got its first light in 1922.[^1][^2][^3] The cape is low and green, and on the flood tide a heavy tide rip forms just south of it.[^2]

## The place

The land came to the Minister of Marine and Fisheries by sale.[^3] In 1880 George Knowlton Morris sold a piece of land with a coal shed at Horseshoe Cove, and a right of way to the whistle, to the minister. In 1891 James Gilbert sold 15 acres at the end of the cape to the same minister.[^3]

## Building the light

Lighthouse Friends quotes the Department's annual report on the whistle's first year. The machinery came from Allen Brothers of St. John, New Brunswick, for $3,000. The buildings and reservoir were built by contract for $4,850, and a coal shed and a tramway up the bank added $590. The whistle went into operation in December 1874.[^1]

It sounded two blasts of six seconds in each minute, with 24 seconds of silence after each. The Department expected it to be heard up to 20 miles in calm weather or with the wind, and five to eight miles in stormy weather or against the wind.[^1][^3]

In the fall of 1877 a storm swept away the tramway that had hauled coal up from the beach to the station, and destroyed the station's boat.[^1] The local history also says the storm destroyed the tramway.[^3] A road to Horseshoe Cove was then built to supply coal to the fog alarm.[^1]

In the year ended 31 March 1908 the Department changed the signal to one blast of six seconds every minute. The work, done by day labour, cost $695.87 and included repairs to the fog-alarm machinery.[^4]

The first light came from elsewhere. In the year ended 31 March 1909 the Department erected a small wooden lighthouse near the government wharf at Eatonville. The tower was square, 6.7 m (22 ft) high, with a fixed red dioptric light of the 5th order, 7.3 m (24 ft) above high water, visible six miles. A contractor from Parrsboro, A. H. Dyas, built it for $675.[^5] Lighthouse Friends and the local history both give 1908 for its erection.[^1][^3]

In the year ended 31 March 1923 the Department moved that old tower to Cape d'Or and fitted a fifth-order lens in it.[^6] It stood on a red wooden trestle near the fog-signal building.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends, the Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society and the local history all give 1922 for the first light.[^1][^2][^3] The Nova Scotia society gives another origin: it says the tower is believed to have been brought to the cape by water from Apple River. The Department's report and the local history name Eatonville.[^2][^6][^3]

## The keepers

The whistle was run by fog-alarm engineers as well as keepers. John E. Short was the first, from 1874 to 1877. Allen H. Rand followed from 1877 to 1887, and James C. Kirkpatrick from 1887 to 1898.[^1][^3]

Fred H. P. Dewis held the post from 1898 to 1913. The Department's keepers' table in the report labelled 1909 gives his appointment as 13 April 1898, and the report labelled 1903 calls him "engineer".[^7][^8]

Daniel P. McAleese followed from 1913 to 1917, according to both the local history and Lighthouse Friends.[^1][^3]

Malcome McLeod came next. Lighthouse Friends says he had been fog-alarm engineer since 1917, took on the light in 1922 and was listed at least as late as 1937.[^1] The local history gives his service as 1917 to 1919.[^3] The two accounts do not agree on how long he stayed.

Vance Frederick Barteaux kept the light from 1939 to 1958.[^1][^3] The Nova Scotia society quotes Walter Barteaux, who grew up at the cape, recalling that the roof of the house the family lived in sometimes lifted in storms, and that rocks blew up from the cliffs.[^2] The society does not name Walter's father, whom it says kept the light for 19 years from 1939. Those years match Vance Barteaux's service, but the source does not link the two.[^2]

Phil Morris is next. Lighthouse Friends gives his years as 1950 to 1981, and the local history gives 1958 to 1981.[^1][^3] Lawrence Morris (1981 to 1985) and Ernie Morris (1985 to 1989) followed, and the Nova Scotia society calls Ernie Morris the last keeper.[^3][^2]

## After the keepers

The light was automated and de-staffed in 1989.[^1][^2][^3] The two keeper's houses, built in 1958/59, sat vacant until 1995.[^2][^1] In that year the Advocate District Development Association obtained a lease through the County of Cumberland and converted them into a tearoom and a hostel.[^1][^2][^3]

The present concrete tower was built in 1965.[^1][^2] The Nova Scotia society's timeline dates the replacement of the 1922 tower to between 1966 and 1970.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard's current list gives the light as a long flash of white every six seconds, showing 2 seconds of light and 4 of dark. It is 20.3 m (67 ft) above the water, visible 8 nautical miles, and operates day and night. The tower is square and white with a red top, joined to a white building, and it stands 10.7 m (35 ft) high.[^9]

The sources disagree on who owns the site. The Nova Scotia society says the Municipality of the County of Cumberland owns the light, and the local history says the houses were leased from the county. Lighthouse Friends says the Canadian Coast Guard owns it.[^2][^3][^1]

The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1] From Route 209 just west of Advocate Harbour, the way in is Back Street and then Cape d'Or Road, a dirt road that runs 5.6 km (3.5 miles) to the lighthouse. Reaching the light from the parking area is a short hike down and up a steep road.[^1] One of the keeper's houses is a restaurant. The other has four rooms for overnight guests.[^1]
