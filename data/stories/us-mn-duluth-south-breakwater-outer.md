At the lake end of the south breakwater of the Duluth Ship Canal, about 1,000 feet (300 m) from shore, a one-and-a-half-story brick building, painted white with a red roof, stands on a concrete pier. A square tower is built into one corner of it, and the building holds both a light and a fog signal.[^1] With the taller Inner Light nearer shore on the same breakwater, it makes a range that guides ships into the canal at Duluth, in St. Louis County, Minnesota.[^2] A light has marked the end of this breakwater since 2 June 1874, and the present building was first lit on 1 September 1901.[^3]

## The place

Duluth's harbor lies behind Minnesota Point, the northern strand of what the National Register nomination calls the world's longest freshwater sand spit.[^3] The point runs some seven miles (11 km) south from the adjoining uplands and, with Wisconsin Point beyond it, shuts the harbor off from Lake Superior; ships once had to go around its far end, through a natural channel called the Superior Entry.[^3][^4] Business interests in Duluth, competing with the port of Superior, Wisconsin, dug a canal across the point in 1870 and 1871, and it opened in April 1871.[^3][^1] The canal is 300 feet (91 m) wide and about 0.3 mile (0.5 km) long, counting its north pier and south breakwater.[^1]

The first federal lighthouse in the Duluth area was the Minnesota Point Lighthouse, about six miles (10 km) to the southeast, which operated from 1858 to 1885 and marked the Superior Entry.[^2] According to LighthouseFriends, the Light-House Board asked Congress for $10,000 in 1870, after receiving six petitions; the petitioners said, in words that site reprints, that the harbor was easy by day but "difficult and dangerous at night when the weather is thick."[^5] Fog is a second hazard. The nomination says, in the present tense: "Foggy conditions on western Lake Superior sometimes makes it difficult for approaching vessels to discern the Duluth Harbor Ship Canal's narrow entrance."[^3]

## Building the light

Terry Pepper's history of the Duluth lights says that, at the Light-House Board's request, Congress set aside $10,000 in March 1870 for a light at the entrance to the canal.[^4] The chosen site was the outer end of the south pier, which still had to be built, so construction waited until 1872.[^4] Work began late that summer. The crew had built a keeper's house on shore at the inner end of the pier and moved on to the elevated walk when a violent storm broke up much of the wooden breakwater and ended the season's work.[^4] LighthouseFriends says that by May 1873 a seven-room dwelling and part of the walkway were done, but that the storm of the previous fall had damaged the south pier, so a temporary light went on the end of the north pier while the south pier was repaired.[^5]

The tower that rose on the repaired pier was a wooden pyramid at the offshore end of the south breakwater, and a raised wooden walkway along the breakwater linked it to shore so that the keepers could reach it in bad weather.[^3] Pepper says the walk kept them above waves that often broke across the top of the breakwater.[^4] (As general background, D. P. Heap wrote in 1889 that the end of one pier in a Great Lakes harbor was usually marked by a small fourth- or fifth-order light on a frame tower, and that such towers could be moved out to the end when a pier was extended.[^6]) LighthouseFriends says the tower was finished in January 1874 but not commissioned until 2 June.[^5] Its fifth-order Fresnel lens showed a fixed red light from a focal plane 40 feet (12 m) above the water, and the nomination says it could be seen for 12.5 miles in clear weather.[^3]

Sources disagree about the lens that followed. The nomination says a fourth-order lens, made in Paris by Barbier and Fenestre, replaced the fifth-order lens in 1877.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the light was changed to alternating red and white flashes in 1885 and to a fixed red fourth-order light in 1886.[^5] Pepper dates the lens to 1877 but calls the date of the upgrade unidentified.[^4] Both the nomination and Pepper give 1877 as the year the fourth-order lens was made, and the nomination says this lens was the one retired in 2014.[^3][^4][^7]

The fog signal came in stages. The nomination says an automated bell went in at the south breakwater light in 1880, and that in 1885 a wooden fog-signal building was built on the breakwater near the lighthouse with a pair of steam-powered whistles.[^3] LighthouseFriends dates the bell to 1879 and says it had been used on South Manitou Island.[^5]

A second light turned the station into a range. The deficiency act that Congress approved on 2 March 1889 included an item for Duluth: "For establishing range-lights at Duluth Harbor, three thousand two hundred and eighty-four dollars and twelve cents."[^8] The new Inner Light, a wooden pyramidal tower taller than the 1874 light, began operating that year, and the older light was renamed the South Breakwater Outer Light.[^3] A ship coming in keeps the taller light directly above the shorter one.[^2] The Light-House Board's reports of the 1890s call the station "Duluth range" and this light its "front" light; the nomination's own list of sources includes a 1910 description of the "Duluth Range Front Light Station."[^9][^10]

The nomination says that in 1895 Congress appropriated three million dollars to improve the ports of Duluth and Superior (Pepper gives 1896).[^3][^4] The canal gained a concrete south breakwater and a concrete north pier, finished in 1900, and the wooden Inner and Outer Lights were pulled down and replaced with temporary lights.[^3] The Light-House Board's report for 1899 records that drawings were made for "a combined light tower and fog signal building" to stand on the pierhead of the new concrete work.[^11] Its report for 1901 lists the light and fog signal as moved to temporary structures on 1 November 1900, and describes them: a temporary structure on the new south pier took over one of the fog-signal plants; a pyramidal tower 25 feet (7.6 m) high went up at the outer end of the pier and a mast 37 feet (11 m) long at the inner end; and the old elevated walk, 700 feet (210 m) long, was taken off the old pier, repaired and set up again on the new one.[^12]

The permanent building was put up by the Eleventh Lighthouse District and was lit on 1 September 1901, with the 1877 lens from the old tower and the 1885 steam whistles and plant.[^3][^7] It stands on a concrete pier carried by a timber crib filled with rock, in 21 feet (6.4 m) of water.[^1] The nomination says the original pier was narrower than it is now and was strengthened in the late twentieth century with a sheet-pile ring and poured concrete; today it is about 100 feet (30 m) long and 36 feet (11 m) wide and rises about 18 feet (5.5 m) above the lake.[^1][^7] The walls are buff Cream City brick on a stone base.[^1] The main block is 45 feet (14 m) long and, by the nomination, 20 feet (6 m) wide (Pepper gives 22 feet).[^1][^4] The tower built into its southeast corner is 35 feet (11 m) tall and eight feet (2.4 m) on a side, with a cast-iron spiral stair up to a trapdoor in the platform that carries the lantern.[^1] A basement under the building has an opening to a tunnel that runs to shore beneath the deck of the breakwater, built for sheltered movement between shore and light in bad weather.[^1] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' table of lights for 30 June 1915 lists "Duluth Range Front" (printed there with the state given as Michigan) among 51 electric incandescent lights.[^13]

## The keepers

For its first twelve years the station had one keeper.[^5] An assistant was added in 1885, to help with the new steam whistles, and a second in 1889, when the rear range light was lit.[^5] The names below come from the keeper registers as compiled in the U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive, which files the Duluth canal keepers under a single record, Duluth Harbor North Breakwater. The archive dates that record to 1910 although its roster begins in 1873, so the list, and the roster table for this page, covers the canal lights together rather than this light alone.[^14] LighthouseFriends prints almost the same list under this Outer Light.[^5] They served the canal lights as one station: the Inner and Outer Lights needed three resident keepers,[^3] and an Army Corps of Engineers park ranger quoted in a Destination Duluth article describes the three canal lights as run by one head keeper and two assistants.[^15]

Ernest R. Jefferson was the station's first keeper.[^14] He kept the light for fifteen years, from 1873 to 1888, when he resigned, and for the first twelve of them he had no help.[^16][^5] He was born in 1844 and died in 1911, and his pay ran from $540 to $560 a year.[^16] His years cover the first fog bell and the first steam whistles.[^3] James Prior followed him in 1888 and stayed twenty years, to 1908.[^14] Prior came from Stannard Rock, where the same archive shows him as head keeper from 1883 to 1888, and he was the brother of two other keepers, William H. Prior and George Prior.[^17] His twenty years took in the sound reflector of 1895, the temporary lights of 1900 and the move into the new building in 1901.[^18][^3]

Under Prior the register shows assistants promoted from second to first assistant. Alexander Shaw was second assistant in 1890 and first assistant from 1891 to 1893, then left to be head keeper at Passage Island until 1907; he came back to Duluth as head keeper in 1908 and left in 1910.[^19] Thomas Doody and John Irvine each served as second assistant and then as first, and Thomas White was second assistant from 1894 to 1898 and first assistant for the next ten years.[^14] John Woods was second assistant from 1898 to 1902, went on to be head keeper at Grand Marais, Minnesota, and later at Superior Entry, Wisconsin, and returned to Duluth as head keeper from 1934 to 1940.[^20]

Charles Lederle, head keeper from 1910 to 1919, had earlier kept Sand Island, Wisconsin (1881 to 1892), and Two Harbors, Minnesota (1892 to 1910), and he retired from Duluth.[^21] Edwin C. Bishop followed him in 1919 and stayed until he retired in 1934.[^22] LighthouseFriends names him the head keeper who served longest at the Duluth breakwater lights, but the register dates give him fifteen years against James Prior's twenty.[^5][^14] Bishop had begun in 1897 as a third assistant at Stannard Rock, rose to head keeper at Spectacle Reef, and kept Wisconsin Point from 1913.[^22] In a 1937 interview that LighthouseFriends quotes, he remembered that first station, 45 miles out in Lake Superior: "they always put the youngsters in the isolated places."[^5] After Bishop the registers show John Woods, then Hobart J. Beyschlag from 1946 and Thomas Daly into the 1950s.[^14] From 1960 the roster lists a succession of head keepers who each stayed a year or two, and a longer list of Coast Guard personnel, to 1970.[^14]

The head keeper lived in the frame dwelling built for the station, which the nomination dates to 1874 and LighthouseFriends to 1873.[^3][^5] The two assistants rented housing in the city from 1901 until 1913.[^3] In 1908 that cost them $10 and $15 a month, and the duplex the Light-House Board asked for was not finished until February 1913, at a cost of $7,178.81.[^5] Pepper says Congress first provided money for land only, on 4 March 1907.[^4] Both houses still stand across the street from each other near the south end of the Aerial Lift Bridge.[^3]

The Board's report for 1897 lists the station's upkeep: a sewer was laid; the fence at the back of the keeper's dwelling and some 225 feet (69 m) of the elevated walk were renewed; two smokestacks were bought to replace worn-out stacks on the fog-signal building; and the fog-signal machinery was overhauled.[^23] The same report says both range lights were then red lights of the fourth order, the rear one flashing, and that they should be raised to at least the second order and made white flashing lights "to distinguish them from the city lights, which lie back of them."[^23]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Light-House Board counted the hours the steam whistle at the "Duluth (front range)" station ran and the coal it burned.[^9] In the year ending 30 June 1893 it ran some 334 hours and burned about 21 tons of coal; in 1894, some 442 hours and 19 tons.[^9] In 1895 the figure was some 1,048 hours and 45 tons, more than twice the year before.[^18] The Board gives 466 hours and 24 tons for 1896, then 692 hours and 33 tons for 1897, 655 hours and 32 tons for 1899 and 576 hours and 29 tons for 1900.[^23][^11][^24] (As general background, Arnold Burges Johnson's 1889 book on the service described the steam fog-whistle of the day as the same instrument used on steamboats and locomotives, worked by an automatic engine that took steam from the same boiler and turned the steam to the whistle on and off at set times.[^8])

LighthouseFriends says residents of Duluth complained about the noisy whistle.[^5] The Light-House Board's report for 1895 records the remedy, a parabolic reflector built "to deaden the sound of the fog signals on the landward side." The position of one whistle was changed where it passes through the roof, and a platform built just above the ridge of the house carried the reflector, which was framed in arches, covered with heavy sheet iron and enclosed in a box filled with sawdust.[^18] The Board tested it by sounding the two whistles in turn while a boat left Duluth, and concluded that "the open whistle was inaudible over 2 miles away and the one with reflector could be heard 4 miles away."[^18] It added that no further complaints had come from the residents of Duluth.[^18]

The complaints came back with new equipment. Pepper writes that Duluth residents objected almost at once to the diaphones that replaced the locomotive whistles in October 1923, writing to the local newspaper, the Chamber of Commerce, the Bureau of Lighthouses and even Herbert Hoover, then Secretary of Commerce, and that the Bureau installed a sound deflector.[^4][^5]

The east side of the fog-signal building faces the open lake and, the nomination says, is more exposed than the other sides.[^7] It was rebuilt in solid brick during the first half of the twentieth century, which removed its two large windows and its decorative cornice.[^7] The nomination lists the whaleback freighter Thomas A. Wilson, launched in 1892, among the National Register vessels near the canal; she sank in 1902 about a mile from it after a collision. The nomination does not tie that wreck to this light.[^2]

## After the keepers

The fog signal changed three more times after 1885. In 1915 the steam whistles gave way to a pair of locomotive whistles, also steam-powered; in 1923 these were replaced with a Type "F" diaphone worked by compressed air, with compressors and air tanks in place of the steam plant; and in 1968 the diaphone was replaced by an electric signal.[^7][^3] The nomination says resident keepers kept the Outer Light until 1976, when it was automated, and the Destination Duluth article agrees.[^3][^15] The Duluth Preservation Alliance gives 1970 as the year the station was automated, and the archive roster also stops in 1970.[^25][^14]

Later, some Duluth residents came to miss the old sound.[^4] In 1976 a group calling itself TOOT, for reTurn Our Old Tone, formed to bring a diaphone back; it later obtained diaphones from the lighthouse at Kewaunee, Wisconsin, and they went back into service on 1 April 1995, with a large crowd on the piers.[^5][^4] Hotel owners complained that guests were being disturbed, and the diaphones were restricted to the hours between 9 a.m. and 9 p.m. The power that ran them failed in 2005, and in 2006 TOOT members took the horns down; LighthouseFriends says they were the last diaphone in official use in North America.[^5]

In 2014 the Coast Guard retired the fourth-order lens, which had been in service since 1877, and put an LED beacon in its place.[^7] LighthouseFriends reports that this cut the light's range from about 16 nautical miles to 13.[^5] The lens is on display at the Lake Superior Maritime Visitor Center in Canal Park.[^26] The light was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 2016.[^5] In 2018 the Coast Guard declared the lighthouse excess and offered it to eligible public and non-profit bodies.[^5]

## Today

The Lake Superior Marine Museum Association, a Duluth non-profit, took ownership.[^26][^15] The transaction closed in March 2025, more than six years in the making, and the Coast Guard keeps the right to maintain its aids to navigation there.[^15] The Coast Guard continues to operate the light and the fog signal in the building.[^26] According to LighthouseFriends, the association commissioned a historic structures report and a clean-up of pigeon waste, lead paint and asbestos tile, and it hopes to offer tours in 2027.[^5]

For now the building is closed: the nomination says it is not open to public visitation,[^1] and LighthouseFriends lists the grounds as open but the tower closed.[^5] The light stands about 1,000 feet (300 m) out on the Army Corps of Engineers' breakwater, and LighthouseFriends says it can be viewed from the northern breakwater.[^1][^5] The nomination describes a large sign above the door that reads "Danger - Loud Fog Signal."[^1] The light is fixed green, 44 feet (13 m) above the water; the nomination and a 2025 report give its visibility as 17 miles in clear weather (neither says statute or nautical miles), while LighthouseFriends puts its range at 13 nautical miles since the LED went in.[^1][^26][^5] The nomination says the fog signal sounds two three-second blasts every minute from April to December.[^1] A 2025 report on the sale says the station welcomes about 700 vessels a year into the port.[^26]
