Conover Beacon is a small metal tower on Leonard Avenue, on the shore of Sandy Hook Bay in the Leonardo section of Middletown Township, Monmouth County, New Jersey. It was the front light of the Chapel Hill range, which marked a channel into the bay. The first tower went up in 1856, the present one in 1941, and the beacon has shone a green light since its rededication in March 2024.[^1][^2][^8][^9]

## The place

The Chapel Hill Channel runs from just off the tip of Sandy Hook south into Sandy Hook Bay, toward Leonardo.[^1] To enter it, heavy-draft vessels plying to the port of New York had to make a detour.[^3] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1892 describes such a vessel making a detour past the northern part of Sandy Hook, then bringing her round "gradually" to "the exact entrance of Chapel Hill Channel."[^3] The Board wrote that "The range lights of Conover Beacon and Chapel Hill Beacon in a right line indicate this entrance," and that these lights "are at times more or less obscured by mist or smoke."[^3] It called the night passage, between buoys about 900 feet (270 m) apart, "an exceedingly delicate and dextrous piece of seamanship."[^3]

The rear light, Chapel Hill Rear Range Lighthouse, stands about a mile and a half to the south on a hill, and it is now on private property.[^1][^9]

## Building the light

Congress approved funds in 1852.[^1] The next year the beachfront was bought from Rulif Conover, whose name the beacon took.[^1][^9] Richard Calrow Jr. built a hexagonal wooden tower and a wooden keeper's dwelling there in 1856.[^1] The Middletown township page gives the first tower as 55 feet (16.8 m) tall.[^8] LighthouseFriends says the original daymark, a central red band on a white tower, was the same as the one used today.[^1]

LighthouseFriends quotes the Board's 1868 report: "The tower is very frail and shaky."[^1] The report said the bottom timbers had decayed and the tower had settled, and that some three years before it had become necessary to stay it with iron guys.[^1] The Board proposed removing ten feet (3 m) from the top.[^1] Funds for altering the tower were authorized the following year, but the money reverted to the Treasury before the work was carried out, and the tower was finally rebuilt in 1876.[^1]

In 1874 black screens, 25 feet (7.6 m) high and 20 feet (6.1 m) wide, were set on either side of the tower.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the screens helped mariners pick out the tower when snow lay along the bay shore.[^1] It also quotes Gustav Kobbe's 1889 description of the tower as "this graceful red and white tower, 55 feet high, on a low sandy point surrounded by a ledge."[^1]

In the fiscal year ended June 1894 a wire fence was built near the high-water line, and 162 feet (49 m) of plank drain was built through the reservation.[^4] In the fiscal year ended June 1895 the Board recorded that an oil house was built at the front beacon.[^5] In 1901 the screens were rebuilt at 30 feet (9.1 m) and painted white with a black diagonal cross.[^1] On 20 January 1907 the illuminant was changed from mineral oil to incandescent oil vapor, which increased the light's intensity.[^1]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster for the station runs from 1855 to 1923.[^2] Mark L. Mount was first, from 1855 to 1861.[^2] LighthouseFriends calls him Marsh L. Mount, says he was the first keeper placed in charge, and gives his salary as $400 a year.[^1][^2] He was followed by Taber Chadwick (1861 to 1869), Charles Grossinger (1869 to 1872), Samuel V. Bartleson (1872) and John Biddle Swan (1872 to 1888).[^2]

Samuel A. Foster kept the beacon from 1888 to 1907.[^2] In January 1894 the Monmouth Inquirer reported that "A wild goose struck the lantern of Conover's Beacon, near Atlantic Highlands, and broke its neck and was served at keeper Samuel Foster's New Year's dinner."[^6] In December 1900 the same paper named him again as the keeper of the beacon.[^7] After Foster came John Dahlman (1907 to 1910), Olof Olsson (1910 to 1915) and Ernest Bloom (1915 to 1923).[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The light was discontinued between 28 April and 1 August 1898 as a safety measure during the Spanish-American War, LighthouseFriends says.[^1]

In July 1923 the range lights were discontinued, when gas-lighted buoys were used to mark the channel they had served.[^1] Four months later the ranges were reactivated, after tugboat captains complained that they were "absolutely necessary to counteract the side drift of the current."[^1]

Hurricane Sandy damaged the base of the tower in 2012.[^1]

## After the keepers

In November 1931 a concrete-block saw-tooth jetty was placed along the shore to protect the reservation.[^1]

The current tower replaced the hexagonal wooden one in 1941. It had been the front light of the Waackaack range and was moved from Point Comfort at Keansburg, just over four miles west.[^1] The township calls it "the current metal structure," built in 1941 and 45 feet (13.7 m) tall.[^8]

The sources date the end of the light differently. The township says the beacon "had been deactivated since 1988 until 2024."[^8] The Two River Times says the tower was officially used until its decommissioning in 1957, and that a lightkeeper maintained and lit it until 1988.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the beacon has been inactive since 1957.[^1]

In 2004 the tower was transferred to Monmouth County for management by Middletown Township.[^1] The Friends of Conover Beacon Society formed in 2007 to help restore it.[^1]

The township began restoring the beacon in March 2022.[^8] Its public works crews replaced "rusted structural members and metal decking," a New Jersey firm painted the tower, and a plaque was set on a jetty stone donated by a local business.[^8] The beacon was rededicated on 12 March 2024.[^8][^9]

## Today

The beacon is restored and stands on Leonard Avenue, on the beach.[^9] Since the rededication it has shone a green light after sunset, because the lighthouse is decommissioned.[^8][^9] The township administrator explained that the township had to install a green light instead of a clear one, per Coast Guard regulations.[^9]

At the rededication the mayor said the stairs had been unsafe three years earlier: "Just three years ago, that center structure beam was almost fully deteriorated and the stairs were unsafe." He added, "Now it's completely safe to walk to the top."[^9]

The sources do not say whether the public may now climb the tower. The Society's place record lists the site as not open to the public,[^2] and LighthouseFriends, before the restoration, listed the grounds as open and the tower as closed.[^1] Do not climb the tower unless the township confirms it is open to the public.

LighthouseFriends says Monmouth County owns the tower, and Middletown Township manages it.[^1]
