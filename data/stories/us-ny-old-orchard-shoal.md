Old Orchard Shoal Light was a cast-iron "spark plug" lighthouse that stood in the open water of Lower New York Bay off Staten Island, built in 1893 on a sandy shoal so that ships could pass it safely in the dark and in fog. Hurricane Sandy swept it away on 29 October 2012, and today only rubble and concrete landings mark the spot.[^1][^17]

## The place

The shoal lies roughly two miles southeast of Crooke's Point, the tip of Great Kills Harbor on the eastern shore of Staten Island, in Richmond County, New York. LighthouseFriends says that when winter ice closed Staten Island Sound, an estimated 15,000 tons of shipping a day were forced into the channel along Staten Island's eastern shore, passing close to the shoal, and that a bell buoy and a lighted buoy that first marked it were thought grossly inadequate.[^16] The Lighthouse Board's own report for 1891-92 places the light at "or near Old Orchard Shoal, Princess Bay, New York."[^2]

## Building the light

Congress made an appropriation by the act of 3 March 1891 for a lighthouse and fog signal at the shoal, and the Board reported that the site had been chosen, soundings made and plans adopted.[^2] LighthouseFriends puts the sum at $60,000, which it says was also to rebuild the Waakcaack tower in New Jersey as a rear range light.[^16] The result was a cast-iron "spark plug" tower on a caisson foundation: the lower part was filled with concrete, while the upper part flared like a trumpet to a diameter of 33 feet (10 m) and held cisterns and a basement, with a three-storey, brick-lined tower on top housing the keepers and their supplies.[^16] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive records the height of the structure from base to the centre of the lantern as 35 feet (11 m), the light as 50.5 feet (15.4 m) above mean high water, and the original lens as a fourth-order Fresnel installed in 1893.[^1] LighthouseFriends instead calls it a fifty-one-foot (15.5 m) tower, so the two sources do not use the same measure, and adds that the Variety Iron Works Company of Cleveland supplied the metalwork.[^16]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1893 says the station "was completed and lighted April 25, 1893."[^3] Temporary lights had shown from the site on 23 March.[^3]

A fog signal followed. A blower siren worked by an oil engine was established on 20 August 1896, and the Board's report for fiscal 1897 also records about 1,000 tons of riprap stone placed to protect the site.[^4] In the report volumes labelled 1899, 1900 and 1901, the siren is credited with about 782, 746 and 731 hours of operation a year.[^5][^6][^7]

## The keepers

The first head keeper was Alfred L. Carlow, listed from 1893 to 1902, and Thomas Burke was the first assistant.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Carlow had served on the Sandy Hook and Scotland lightships before coming to the new tower.[^16] The New-York Tribune reported in October 1902 that he had been "the first man to light the lamp there," that he had retired because of ill health, and that he died by his own hand at Fort Tompkins Lighthouse, where his daughter's husband was keeper.[^15] Other sources date his leaving the station to 1902, so the exact timing of his departure is uncertain.[^1][^16]

The U.S. Lighthouse Society archive lists sixteen head keepers between 1893 and 1943, among them Adolph Nordstrom, William J. Epkens (1913 to 1921), Andrew Zuius, Jr. (1924 to 1927), Christopher Connolly and Ralph W. Rexinger (1933 to 1943), with dozens of assistants, many of whom served a year or less, especially around 1910 and 1924.[^1] Epkens told a reporter in 1916 how he handled the bundles of newspapers that reached the station, sometimes a month's worth at once, reading them in order, one day at a time; "I never skip a day no matter how much I may want to," he said, in LighthouseFriends' account of the interview.[^16]

Frank P. Schubert appears in the roster as an assistant from 1940 to 1942; Lighthouse Digest says he took up marquetry while at Old Orchard Shoal and later became well known as the keeper at Coney Island.[^1][^17]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The New-York Tribune of 2 December 1895 reported that the bark Nellie Smith, bound from Mobile to New York with lumber, had run aground on the shoal at high water the previous morning, and that her captain said he "mistook Old Orchard Shoal light for the Bluff light on Fort Tompkins."[^14]

On 4 and 5 October 1918 an explosion at a munitions plant at Morgan, New Jersey, damaged lights around the lower bay. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that at Old Orchard Shoal "the tower vibrated and shook considerably" but no perceptible damage resulted.[^8]

Ice floes in the winter of 1918 cracked the foundation, according to LighthouseFriends, which says about 7,000 tons of riprap were added.[^16] The Bulletin shows riprap work in progress there in 1921 and finished by 1922, with a landing platform for the station boat authorised.[^9][^10] LighthouseFriends says the extra stone made it impossible to launch the boat from the davits, so a concrete landing with a crane was built beyond it.[^16]

The keepers' rescue work was recorded in the Bulletin. On 21 September 1924 first assistant Pearl S. King saw a motorboat adrift about half a mile off with its engine broken and five men aboard; King and second assistant Andrew Zuius, Jr. brought them to the station for the night and towed the boat to Great Kills next day.[^11] Zuius, by then keeper, was officially commended for a rescue on 26 June 1927, when four men's boat sprang a leak in heavy sea: he brought them in with the station lifeboat, put them up overnight and towed their boat ashore.[^12] The Bulletin also reports that on 7 September (in its 1925 volume) he rescued three men whose boat capsized near the station.[^13]

In the war year of 1898 the light was darkened as a war measure on 28 April and relit on 1 August.[^5]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends reports that the station was automated in 1955.[^16] In May 2007 the Coast Guard declared the property excess; no eligible group came forward, and the General Services Administration auctioned it, with the first winning bid in 2008 later contested and the sale repeated.[^16]

## Today

Hurricane Sandy destroyed the lighthouse on 29 October 2012, leaving little but rubble and part of the dock.[^16][^17] The U.S. Lighthouse Society archive records the station as not open to the public.[^1] In December 2012 the National Lighthouse Museum's director announced a plan to recover and rebuild the tower on the museum's grounds, subject to salvage rights from the owner; this story has found no source on whether it happened.[^16]
