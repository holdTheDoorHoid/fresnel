Prospect Harbor Point Light stands on a shallow rocky peninsula on the east side of the entrance to Prospect Harbor, in Gouldsboro, Maine.[^2][^1] Its first tower dates from 1849 or 1850, depending on the source. The light was switched off in 1859 and relit in 1870, and the wooden tower that stands now dates from 1891.[^2][^3][^1] The light was automated in 1934.[^3][^2]

## The place

The National Register nomination says the station was authorized in 1847 but not established until 1850, and that it marks the eastern entrance to the inner part of a harbor of refuge.[^2] Throughout the nineteenth century the harbor was home to a large fleet of fishing vessels, and the nomination says the beacon and fog signal greatly facilitated their safe passage in and around the harbor.[^2]

In 1859 the Light-House Board deactivated the light, the nomination says, claiming that the harbor was not used as an anchorage during storms.[^2] LighthouseFriends says Congress decided to discontinue the light after being told that the harbor was "not used as a harbor of refuge."[^1] New England Lighthouses gives the same reasons and adds that the village "has only a small coasting trade."[^9]

LighthouseFriends says the Board reversed the decision about a decade later.[^1] The nomination says only that in 1870, "for some as yet unknown reason, the light was relit and the buildings renovated."[^2] In 1870 the Lighthouse Board announced that a light alternating red and white flashes would be shown from 15 May, "to serve as a guide to the harbor of refuge which it marks."[^1][^9]

## Building the light

The first station had a granite conical tower attached to a stone dwelling.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends gives the date of establishment as 2 April 1849, while the nomination, the Archive and New England Lighthouses give 1850.[^1][^2][^3][^9]

By 1889 the stone dwelling and tower were in poor condition, according to a Board report as LighthouseFriends quotes it.[^1]

The Board's annual report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1892 records that the light was moved to a new tower about 25 feet (7.6 m) southerly, on 25 July 1891.[^5] The new tower was a conical wooden tower 38 feet (11.6 m) tall, and New England Lighthouses says it carried a fifth-order Fresnel lens.[^1][^9] The nomination says the earlier buildings were pulled down in 1891 and replaced.[^2]

The Board's annual report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1891 had called the old buildings "old and dilapidated" and said they would be rebuilt.[^4] A later entry in the report for fiscal 1892 lists a new frame dwelling, 19 by 20 feet (5.8 by 6.1 m) in plan, built on the foundation of the old stone dwelling.[^6]

LighthouseFriends says a fifth-order lens was installed in 1857, the year after reflecting apparatus was fitted in 1856.[^1] The Archive gives 1870 as the year its original fifth-order lens was installed.[^3] In 1906 the lens was replaced with a fourth-order Sautter lens, and the light was changed to a "fixt red with two white sectors."[^1]

The Board's annual report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1897 records that a site had been cleared and preparations made to build a boathouse.[^7] LighthouseFriends says the boathouse and boat slip were added in 1897.[^1] The Board's annual report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1900 records a new boat winch.[^8] An oil house of stone went up in 1905.[^2][^9]

## The keepers

The Archive lists ten stints by nine keepers between 1849 and 1934, beginning with Nathaniel Noyes.[^3] New England Lighthouses names Pillsbury Stevens as the first keeper, and says S. H. Sargent succeeded him in April 1853.[^9] The Archive and LighthouseFriends give Sargent a stint in 1849 and 1850, and Stevens 1850 to 1853.[^3][^1]

The longest service was that of Ambrose H. Wasgatt, a Civil War veteran, from 1885 to 1918.[^3][^9] LighthouseFriends says he took over in 1891, but its own keeper list and its statement that he served thirty-three years both fit 1885.[^1] His two daughters remembered playing croquet with guests, and annual open houses that included "boathouse dinners," with long tables of food set up inside the boathouse for visitors.[^1][^9] Wasgatt retired in 1918, after thirty-three years at the lighthouse.[^1]

William C. Gott kept the light from 1918 to 1930.[^3] Albion T. Faulkingham was the last keeper, from 1930 until the light was automated in 1934.[^3][^1] New England Lighthouses dates his service from about 1928 to 1935.[^9]

John Workman then became caretaker of the property and lived in the dwelling until 1953, for $25 a month and a rent of $12 a year.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Fresnel lens was replaced in 1951.[^9] New England Lighthouses says the caretaker stayed until the mid-1950s, although its own keeper list ends Workman's service in 1953.[^9]

The keeper's house was offered at auction in 1955. The high bid was rejected, and the property was transferred to the U.S. Navy instead.[^9]

A Coast Guard crew reshingled and repainted the tower in April 2000.[^9] In May 2000 the American Lighthouse Foundation took over responsibility for the light from the Coast Guard.[^1] The foundation began renovating the stairs in 2001, and in August 2004 had the lantern removed by crane for repainting.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the lantern room was restored in 2004, and the windows were replaced in spring 2006 with a grant from the New England Lighthouse Lovers.[^1]

The keeper's house, known as Gull Cottage, was available for overnight stays by active and retired military families until October 2021.[^9] New England Lighthouses says the program ended then, when the property was put under a different military branch for oversight.[^9]

## Today

The light stands on the grounds of a Navy satellite operations installation, and the grounds are closed to the public for security reasons.[^9][^1] The keeper's dwelling belongs to the Navy, while the Coast Guard owns the tower and has leased it to the American Lighthouse Foundation.[^1][^3] The light can be seen from Route 186 across the harbor, and from the entrance to the Navy installation.[^9][^1]

In the early morning of 27 June 2022, a family of four staying in the keeper's dwelling was woken by smoke detectors.[^1] Fire departments kept the fire from spreading to the adjacent wooden lighthouse.[^1] The Gouldsboro fire chief said that between fire and water damage the dwelling would be a complete loss, but restoration work on it started later that year.[^1]
