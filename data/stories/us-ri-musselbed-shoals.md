Musselbed Shoals Light was a small wooden light on a stone pier, in the channel between Narragansett Bay and Mount Hope Bay, opposite Bristol Ferry in Portsmouth, Rhode Island. It was lit in 1873, and it was demolished in 1939, a year after the hurricane of September 1938 damaged it, to make way for an automatic light on a skeleton tower.[^1][^2][^6]

## The place

The shoal was a hazard well before the light. D'Entremont describes it as an obstruction in the channel from Narragansett Bay toward Mount Hope Bay, on the approach to Fall River, Massachusetts, and says a stone daybeacon marked it.[^6] The Lighthouse Board recommended a light and fog bell in 1871.[^6] The station's place in the channel is recorded in the Board's 1894 report, which puts it "on southeast side of the channel, opposite to Bristol Ferry Light."[^2]

## Building the light

D'Entremont says a congressional appropriation of $3,000 was obtained in March 1873, and that the light was established on 1 August of that year.[^6] The Archive's record agrees on the year and gives the original optic as a sixth-order Fresnel lens, installed in 1873.[^1] D'Entremont describes the first structure as a hexagonal tower attached to the keeper's house, with a sixth-order Fresnel lens showing a fixed red light.[^6] The Archive gives the tower as wood, 19 feet (6 m) from base to lantern, with a red daymark, on a granite breakwater foundation; it gives the light as 33 feet (10 m) above water.[^1] D'Entremont gives the height as 31 feet (9.4 m) above sea level.[^6] The two figures differ, and neither source gives the method of measurement.

D'Entremont says the first structure did not last long. Ice floes moved the entire station four feet (1.2 m) in the winter of 1874 to 1875.[^6] He says $6,000 that had been requested for a rebuild was obtained in March 1877. The stone pier was enlarged, the lighthouse and dwelling were rebuilt, and the fog bell was set on the roof beside a short tower and lantern.[^6]

## The keepers

The Archive's roster gives fifteen principal keepers from 1873 to 1939.[^1] Dennis Shea, who kept the light in 1873, was followed by William Dunwell, Thomas S. Smith and Andrew T. Smith. D'Entremont says Andrew Smith, who served from 1881 to 1891, stayed about a decade, and that James D. Leonard held the post for fourteen years, from 1891 to 1905, the longest stint.[^6] The dates for the early keepers differ between the two sources, and the Archive's roster is the one used here. D'Entremont spells the second keeper William Dunnell, dates him 1874 to 1875, and gives Thomas Smith's stint as 1876 to 1881.[^1][^6]

Several keepers followed in quick succession after 1905. George E. Hansen, Lucius E. Chadwick and Evard Jansen each served for a short time.[^1] D'Entremont calls the last of them Edward Jansen. He says Jansen stayed only a few months, from 1 July to 1 October 1908, before taking over Lime Rock Light from Ida Lewis.[^6] John F. Anderson served at Mussel Bed Shoals before going to Bullock's Point, where D'Entremont places him from 1909 to 1910.[^1][^6][^7]

William Tengren kept the light from 1909 to 1916.[^1] In July 1913 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports William Tengren, keeper of the Mussel Bed Shoal light, rescuing two women and a man from an overturned power boat with his son.[^3] A 1912 to 1917 index entry names him keeper of the same station.[^8] Otis L. Barstow, Ellsworth J. Smith, David N. Hanson and Henry R. McCarthy followed. The Archive prints McCarthy's stint as 11 to 31 October 1920.[^1]

Patrick J. Brides kept the light from 1924 to 1939, the Archive's last keeper here.[^1] The Bulletin's 1935 report names "P. J. Brides, keeper of Musselbed Light Station, N. Y." When he saw a stir on the deck of the steamer Plymouth, he lowered the station boat, rowed several hundred yards out and picked up a man who had jumped or fallen overboard.[^5] The Bulletin gives the state as New York, while the Archive places Brides at Mussel Bed Shoals, Rhode Island. No other Musselbed light appears in the sources read, so the rescue may belong to this station, but the state does not match and the rescue is not confirmed here.[^1][^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The winter of 1919 to 1920 brought heavy ice. The Bulletin reports that in early February 1920 the ice "dislodged the riprap around the station, as well as one of the large foundation stones."[^4] D'Entremont says the structure was soon rebuilt.[^6]

For the year ended 30 June 1894 the Board's report says the boat landings were cleared of large stone and riprap stone was placed around the pier.[^2]

In summer 1938 a large section of the ceiling in the new four-room dwelling caved in, according to D'Entremont, who says the dwelling had been plagued with leaks. Later that year the hurricane of 21 September 1938 devastated the lighthouse.[^6]

## After the keepers

The Archive gives the year discontinued as 1939, and the disposition as "Demolished in 1939 After Hurricane Damage, Replaced with Skeletal Tower W/fixed Acetylene Light."[^1] D'Entremont says the building was dismantled the following year and an automatic light on a skeleton tower was installed.[^6]

## Today

A light still operates on the spot, and D'Entremont says it flashes red every six seconds.[^6] The Archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^1] Do not land on the shoal or the pier, and do not climb the skeleton tower.
