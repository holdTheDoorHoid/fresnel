The Key West Lighthouse stands on Whitehead Point, at the southern end of Key West, Florida.[^3] Its first tower was lit in January 1826. By one account, members of the Mabrity family, by birth and by marriage, kept the light for more than eighty-five years, and the Coast Guard did not decommission it until 1969.[^1][^2] The brick tower, with 88 steps to the top, now stands beside a museum in the old keepers' quarters.[^2]

## The place

Ships bound for Key West and the Gulf of Mexico had to pass a field of reefs.[^1] In 1822 Commander Matthew C. Perry surveyed the Florida reefs and recommended four lights to help vessels avoid them.[^1] The first plan put a light on the Sambo Keys, about seven miles offshore from Key West. Those islands were at times completely under water, so the builders chose Whitehead Point, the southernmost point of Key West, instead.[^1]

## Building the light

Samuel B. Lincoln, who had helped secure the contract for the lighthouses, sailed from Boston on 29 August 1824 with part of the materials. When he failed to arrive after several months, LHF concludes that his ship and everyone aboard had been lost at sea.[^1] The tower that was finished was a brick cone, 65 feet (20 m) from its foundation to the base of its lantern, and it carried fifteen whale-oil lamps.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive gives 1825 as the year the station was established and lists the original optic as thirteen Argand lamps with 21-inch reflectors.[^3]

The first tower did not survive the hurricane of 1846, described below. On 3 March 1847 Congress provided $12,000 for a new light about half a mile (0.8 km) inland. Work began that November, and the lighthouse was completed by 15 January 1848 at a cost of $7,247.77. The new tower was fifty feet (15 m) tall, fifteen feet (5 m) shorter than its predecessor, but it stood on higher ground.[^1] The archive gives 1847 as the year the present tower was established.[^3] The Key West Art & Historical Society says the current lighthouse opened in 1848.[^2]

In 1872 the tower was raised five feet (1.5 m) with a new lantern room, according to LHF.[^1] By the early 1890s the Light-House Board judged the tower too low to be conspicuous. Its annual report in the volume dated 1891 to 1892 says the tower was "not high enough to make it as conspicuous as it should" be, and that "Tall trees obstruct the view of the light from the northwest."[^4] Under an act of 3 March 1893, the Board's report in the volume dated 1893 to 1894 says, "plans and specifications were prepared for increasing the height of the tower."[^5] On 30 November 1894 the third-order light was taken out of service. A lens lantern without red sectors replaced it while the work went on.[^6] The Board's report in the volume dated 1895 says the tower was raised 20 feet (6 m). It says that "The work of increasing the height of the tower 20 feet was completed February 8, 1895," but also that the regular light was reestablished on February 5, 1895, three days before the work was finished.[^7] LHF gives 5 February as the day the third-order light resumed.[^1]

The archive dates the present keepers' dwelling, a wood house in the West Indian vernacular style, to 1887.[^3] LHF says it was built to replace the first keepers' quarters at the new site.[^1]

The archive gives 1847 as the year the original lens was installed. LHF dates the third-order Fresnel lens, which arrived from Paris, to 1858.[^1] The archive's entry describes its original optic as Argand lamps, so the two sources may be describing different things.[^3]

## The keepers

Michael Mabrity was the first principal keeper, from 1826 to 1832.[^3] LHF says he hired a man to live in the keepers' quarters and tend the light while he lived in town. The Treasury Department frowned on this, and he was ordered back to the lighthouse. LHF says his salary was later raised from $400 to $600 a year, which it suggests may have been meant to keep him at his post.[^1] Michael died of yellow fever in 1832, and his wife Barbara became head keeper.[^1][^3]

The archive's record for Barbara Mabrity gives her birth year as 1782 and her death year as 1867. It lists her as principal keeper from 1832 to 1864, and her cause of leaving as "Dismissed or Removed."[^8] LHF says she was appointed assistant keeper in 1826 and, counting from then, gives her thirty-eight years of service when she was let go. The archive's span starts six years later, in 1832.[^1][^3] While raising six children, LHF says, she weathered the hurricanes of 1835, 1841 and 1842.[^1] In 1843, Key West citizens wrote to Stephen Pleasonton, head of U.S. lighthouse operations, that she had "performed the duties of her office with fidelity," and asked for help in her old age, as LHF quotes the letter.[^1] In 1864 LHF says she was urged to retire for being "disloyal to the Union." When she refused, at eighty-two, she was officially fired.[^1]

Her granddaughter, Mary Amanda Fletcher, married John J. Carroll. He was appointed assistant keeper in 1868 and became head keeper in 1870.[^1] Mary Amanda Carroll was his assistant from 1876. He died of typhoid on 14 March 1889, and she was made keeper, a post she held for only a few weeks before she died of the same disease on 21 May 1889.[^1][^3]

The Mabritys' daughter Nicolosa married Captain Joseph Bethel in 1831. Their son William A. Bethel replaced Mary Carroll as head keeper in 1889 and served until 1908.[^1][^3] His wife, Mary E. Bethel, had been his assistant from 1891 and became head keeper in 1908. Their son Merrill A. Bethel served as assistant from 1908 to 1914.[^1][^3] LHF gives Mary E. Bethel's term as head keeper as ending in 1915, while the archive ends it in 1914.[^1][^3]

Three women appear in the archive's roster as head keeper or first assistant: Barbara Mabrity, Mary Amanda Carroll and Mary E. Bethel. The archive's roster runs from 1826 to 1915.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LHF dates the hurricane that destroyed the first tower to 10 October 1846. It describes a stifling day, waves striking the shore by late afternoon, and then a storm surge that covered Key West in five feet of water.[^1] Press accounts from the weeks after the storm describe the damage. The Richmond Enquirer reported that the light-house at the southwest point "has been swept away, leaving not a vestige to mark the spot where it stood." It gave the number of people who perished there as 14, and said the Sand Key light was gone as well, leaving "only a shoal" where it stood.[^9] Another passage in the same issue gives 15 people lost at the Key West light and six at Sand Key.[^9] A letter giving an account of the Key West hurricane, printed in the New Orleans Bulletin and reprinted by the Mississippi Creole, also gives 14: "Fourteen persons were either killed or drowned at Key West lighthouse, and not a soul escaped."[^10] The newspaper counts disagree, and the sources do not settle the number. LHF says that Barbara Mabrity survived, although some of her family died.[^1]

Later storms also touched the station. In 1907, LHF says, Keeper William Bethel climbed onto the dwelling's roof to clear the gutters before a hurricane and fell, injuring his back. When another hurricane struck in 1908 he insisted on leaving his bed to check the light, though his family told him it was still burning. He died six months later, and his wife took charge of the light.[^1]

In 1917, during the First World War, William Demeritt, whom LHF calls the lighthouse superintendent for southern Florida, recommended that he and his family live in the keepers' dwelling "so that the lighthouse property be under observation at all times during the war due to storage of kerosene in the oil house." LHF says he stayed on after the war.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1913 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that William W. Demeritt had been promoted from superintendent to inspector and "assigned to the seventh district."[^11] The Bulletin does not say whether this is the superintendent LHF names as William Demeritt, and the sources do not settle it. In 1915 the Bulletin's list of changes to navigation says the Key West light changed from incandescent oil vapor to acetylene, and from fixed to occulting.[^12] LHF says the lamp no longer needed a keeper's constant care and was soon automated, and the archive gives 1915 as the year the station was discontinued.[^1][^3]

Demeritt stayed in the keepers' dwelling after the war until the Coast Guard took control of the lighthouse in 1939, according to LHF.[^1] In 1966 the Key West Art and Historical Society was granted control of the dwelling, and turned it and the grounds into a military museum.[^1] The Coast Guard decommissioned the light in 1969, because technology had removed the need for a full-time keeper.[^2] LHF says the society was then allowed to open the tower for tours.[^1] A $265,000 renovation of the tower began in 1987, and in 1990 the keepers' quarters opened as a lighthouse museum.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by Monroe County and managed by the Key West Art & Historical Society.[^1] The museum is open every day from 10:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m., with the last admission at 4:30 p.m., and visitors can climb the 88 steps to the top of the tower.[^2] The grounds and the tower are open daily.[^1] Displayed at the lighthouse are the first-order Fresnel lens from Sombrero Key, a fourth-order flashing lens and two fifth-order fixed lenses.[^1] The archive lists the station as open to the public.[^3]
