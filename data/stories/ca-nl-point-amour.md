Point Amour Lighthouse is a 33-metre limestone tower on the Labrador side of the Strait of Belle Isle, built in the 1850s for the trans-Atlantic shipping that used the strait.[^3] The station's website gives its height as 33 metres, and Parks Canada's heritage lighthouse statement calls it the second tallest in Canada.[^18][^1] In August 1922 the British cruiser HMS Raleigh ran aground close to it.[^2]

## The place

By the middle of the 1800s the Strait of Belle Isle had become part of an increasingly important trans-Atlantic shipping route, but Parks Canada's heritage statement notes that its narrow form, ice and strong currents made it a dangerous passage.[^3] The Province of Canada built Point Amour as one of four coastal lighthouses put up in the 1850s around the Gulf and the Strait, and the statement adds that the light served the communities from L'Anse au Clair to Red Bay.[^3] Parks Canada places it on the southeast side of Forteau Bay.[^1]

The department's own ice notes show what the strait could do. For the winter of 1900-01, the department's signal-service ice notes for Point Amour put the first ice along the shore on 6 December, and from 1 to 25 January "the strait was full of large heavy sheet ice, as far as could be seen." In late March it filled again with very heavy ice, and in early June the station still counted large strings of ice and numerous icebergs.[^4]

## Building the light

Lighthouse Friends reports that the engineer John Page chose the site in June 1854, and that stone was quarried locally for the work.[^5] The statement of significance describes a tower of locally quarried limestone, with walls six feet thick at the base and foundations carried down to solid rock. It tapers from 7.5 m (24.6 ft) across at the base to 2.7 m (8.9 ft) at the cornice,[^3] and the station's website gives the tower's height as 33 m.[^18]

The sources differ on dates. The provincial historic sites page says the lighthouse was completed in 1857 and first illuminated in 1858.[^6] Lighthouse Friends gives the notice to mariners, which set the first showing of the light for 1 April 1858.[^5] The lens was a second-order Fresnel lens, supplied from Paris.[^5] The tower was later covered with clapboards, which Lighthouse Friends dates to 1861.[^5] Parks Canada's description records the present outer skin as firebrick covered with vertical wood battens and wood shingles.[^1]

Fog signals changed several times. The station's own history says the first keeper fired a nine-pounder signal gun once every hour in fog, a method it calls ineffective, and that a 10-inch steam whistle later ran from a boiler fed by a small brook and burning 130 tons of coal a year shipped from Montreal.[^7] Lighthouse Friends dates the whistle's start to 8 August 1878 and a compressed-air horn to 1885.[^5] In its report for the year to June 1905 the department described the new fog alarm station "entirely completed, with the exception of the construction of a coal shed," at a cost of $3,158.32.[^8] The report for the following year says the whistle had been replaced by a diaphone worked by compressed air, with the horn 40 feet above high water.[^9]

In 1901 the department's inspector surveyed the station and a lake behind it, to see whether water power could drive an electric light plant. He feared the supply would not be enough.[^10] On 15 September 1903 the fixed white light became an occulting white light, showing 16 seconds and eclipsed for 4.[^11]

## The keepers

The station's history lists seven keepers across its first 138 years, and gives their dates as John Blampied 1857-1869, Pierre Godier 1869-1879, Matthew Wyatt 1879-1889, Thomas Wyatt 1889-1919, Jeff Wyatt 1919-1963, Milton Elliott 1963-1969 and Max Sheppard 1969-1995. Lighthouse Friends gives slightly different dates for several of them, and the roster below follows the station's own.[^7][^5] Blampied was the first keeper, and Lighthouse Friends adds that Godier died before his replacement arrived.[^5]

Matthew Wyatt "brought his family from Quebec in 1879," and the station's history says Wyatts then kept the light for the next 83 years.[^7] The department's keeper table for 1904-05 lists a Thomas M. Wyatt at "Amour Point, Forteau Bay, Light and Fog" station; the same line gives his appointment as 18 October 1889.[^12] The station's history credits Thomas Wyatt with a part in saving four lives from the wreck of HMS Lily, and with maintaining the Marconi wireless station.[^7] Of Jeff Wyatt it says he was a wireless operator who sent word of a German aircraft that came down on nearby Greenly Island, and that he was estimated to have climbed the tower about 10,000 times.[^7]

In the 1960s, under Milton Elliott, the station had three assistants, and its history says it was "like a small town" with 25 to 30 people living there.[^7] Max Sheppard had been first assistant for five years before he became head keeper in 1969.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A government telegraph station was established at the lighthouse in the early 1900s and was added to the marine signal stations that reported shipping to Quebec and Montreal.[^13] The department's report says the Marconi wireless station there was completed on 10 August and spoke to an outward-bound steamer the next day; Lighthouse Friends dates the station to 1904.[^14][^5] It was linked with the Belle Isle station about 66 miles to the east.[^14]

The best-known event is the loss of HMS Raleigh. The department's wireless report says that at 3.40 p.m. on 8 August 1922 the cruiser called Point Amour to say she had grounded badly somewhere in Forteau Bay. The station staff knew the situation was serious only when they heard guns firing to the west. A messenger then landed from the ship asking that an S.O.S. be sent, which was done, and several ships replied. By about 7 p.m. the last of the crew was ashore, and the commander judged that no help was possible because the sea was pounding the ship.[^15] Parks Canada says eleven men died.[^3] The Shipwreck Preservation Society of Newfoundland and Labrador records that survivors were taken into the homes and outbuildings of the keepers and local families, and that the captain and navigator were later found responsible at a court martial.[^2] The station's history says Jeff Wyatt helped settle about 700 men.[^7]

The same report records that on 22 November 1922 Point Amour received a distress message from the steamer Nordfjeld, reporting that she was sinking near Flowers Cove, and two steamers that answered it reported that the Nordfjeld told them by flag signal she needed no help.[^16]

## After the keepers

The heritage statement lists the former assistant keeper's dwelling of about 1954 and the keeper's dwelling of about 1967 among the site's buildings, with a former fog alarm and radio beacon building and an active foghorn equipment building.[^17] Lighthouse Friends says the light was electrified in 1964 and that Sheppard retired in 1996, while the station's history says 1995.[^5][^7] The station is no longer staffed by keepers.[^7]

## Today

Point Amour is a Provincial Historic Site on L'Anse Amour Road, off Route 510, about 4.8 km from the highway. It is open to visitors in season, with guided tours and a climb up the tower; the site page lists it as closed for the season and due to reopen on 22 May 2027.[^6] A coastal trail of about 30 minutes leads to the Raleigh wreck site.[^6] The heritage statement says about 6,000 people visit the light station each summer.[^17]
