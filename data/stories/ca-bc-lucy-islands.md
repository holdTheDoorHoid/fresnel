Lucy Islands Light stands on the north-east side of the easternmost of the Lucy Islands, about 15 km (10 mi) west of Prince Rupert, in Chatham Sound, British Columbia.[^6] The Department of Marine put the light into operation on 1 January 1907.[^3] The octagonal concrete tower that stands there now dates from 1960.[^1][^6] The station is closed to visitors and can be reached only by boat.[^6]

## The place

The easternmost and highest of the islands was named by Captain George Henry Richards of HMS Hecate, after Lucy McNeill, the daughter of a Hudson's Bay Company captain.[^1] The light marks the start of the Metlaketla Channel that leads to Prince Rupert harbour.[^6]

A light was asked for in 1904 by Mr. Macpherson, a Member of Parliament, and the following year the Department's chief engineer, William P. Anderson, endorsed the project.[^1] LighthouseFriends puts the islands roughly 17 km (11 mi) west of Prince Rupert, where the Lighthouse Directory gives 15 km (10 mi).[^1][^6]

## Building the light

Lumber and materials for the lighthouse were shipped north from Victoria aboard the steamer Cascade. G. H. Frost, the supervisor of the project, set out on 11 October 1906 with a few carpenters, his wife and their daughter.[^1] The Department's annual report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1906, printed in 1907, lists a dwelling and a lighthouse among the works then under way at Lucy Island.[^2]

The Department's report for the nine months ended 31 March 1907, printed in 1908, describes the light as a rectangular wooden dwelling, painted white with a red roof, carrying a white square wooden lantern. The work was done by day labour under the supervision of G. H. Frost, and it cost $7,059.04.[^3] LighthouseFriends says it took just a couple of months to build.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says a fourth-order lens was installed in 1924, and that an air diaphone housed in a frame building next to the light was placed in operation in 1927.[^1]

The present concrete tower was built in 1960.[^6] LighthouseFriends says Stange Construction was hired to erect the lighthouse, along with a new single dwelling.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory gives 1960 as the date of the present tower.[^6]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends names the first keeper as L. Lindblow, also spelled Linbloom or Lindbloom. The Department's list of lightkeepers printed in 1908 has a Lindblow at Lucy Island, with no appointment date.[^1][^4]

About nine months after he took up the post, boats passing the station reported that something was wrong, and the light seemed to be out. Those sent to help found the keeper ill, with a paralytic shock that left him barely able to move. He was lifted into a boat and taken to hospital at Port Simpson.[^1]

From 1910 the station was run by Octave Ouellette. LighthouseFriends says the island struck the Ouellettes on a June 1910 picnic from Prince Rupert. When the resident keeper expressed a longing for civilization, they saw a chance to move, and about a month later they were settling in on Lucy Island.[^1] The Department's list printed in 1912 places J. O. Ouellette at Lucy Island, with an appointment date of 6 July 1910.[^5]

In August 1913 a fourth child was born at the lighthouse, and the couple named her Lucy after their island home.[^1] Octave's wife, Therese, died in hospital in 1919, after catching influenza there. Octave resigned that year and left the island.[^1]

Frank Glinn was head keeper from 1945 until 1963, and LighthouseFriends says he was on the island when the present concrete tower was built in 1960.[^1] LighthouseFriends lists nineteen head keepers between 1907 and 1988, from L. Lindblow to Ike Pronk.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1987 the Canadian Coast Guard backed away from a plan to drastically reduce the number of staffed west-coast lights. Five lights still lost their keepers the next year, and Lucy Island was one of them.[^1] Ike Pronk, the station's last keeper, was transferred to McInnes Lighthouse in July 1988. LighthouseFriends says a contractor burned the keeper's dwelling the next month. The Lighthouse Directory says the house was demolished in 1988, so the two accounts differ on how it went.[^1][^6]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard's list gives the light as a red flash every six seconds, with a focal height of 21.6 m (71 ft) and a nominal range of 14 nautical miles. The Lighthouse Directory gives the focal plane as 21.5 m (71 ft).[^7][^6]

The Coast Guard owns the light. The site and tower are closed, and the island can be reached only by boat, although the light is easily seen from passing ships.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the islands are now home to a thriving colony of rhinoceros auklets, and that the concrete tower now seems to be the only reminder of the people who once lived and worked there.[^1] Because the site is closed, visitors should not try to land on the islands.
