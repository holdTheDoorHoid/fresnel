Point Conception is the corner where the California coast stops running north and south and turns east into the Santa Barbara Channel, and a lighthouse has stood on the headland since 1856. The present white tower, built low on the bluff in 1881 and 1882, replaced a first lighthouse that sat on the cliff above it and had begun to crack.[^1][^2]

## The place

The headland was a landmark long before the government put a light on it. The National Register nomination says Spanish navigators returning from the Philippines looked for Point Conception, and it describes a "ships graveyard" in the water between Point Conception and Point Arguello.[^1] The same nomination records that the point had religious significance to the Chumash people. It gives the name Humqaq and passes on an account, recorded by the ethnographer John P. Harrington from his Ynezeno informant Maria Solares, that the souls of the dead go first to this "wild and stormy place".[^1] The nomination adds that modern Chumash still attach great religious significance to the point.[^1]

The station stands on land that belonged to the Rancho Punta de la Concepción, patented by the United States to Anastacio Carrillo in 1880. The nomination describes the light station as a complex of buildings on 29.6 acres, bordered by wind- and sea-eroded cliffs on the ocean side.[^1]

## Building the light

The first lighthouse went up in a place with no road. Supplies came down the coast from San Francisco and were landed through the surf at Cojo Landing, just west of the point, then hauled to the site over sand deep enough that, in the words of the supervising officer Major Hartman Bache as quoted by LighthouseFriends, four mule trains were needed "to draw even a fourth the usual load."[^3] The National Register nomination names the contractors as two Baltimore men, Francis A. Gibbons and Francis X. Kelly, who it says finished the story-and-a-half dwelling, with its tower, by midsummer 1854.[^1]

Then the inspector arrived. The nomination says Bache objected to what he called "inferior workmanship" and found the tower too small for the first-order lens that had been ordered, so the contractors were made to take it down and rebuild it wider before they were paid.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes the rebuilt tower as moved back a few inches from the centre of the dwelling so that the front door would open, and says the house stood 215 feet above the ocean.[^3]

The keeper, George Parkinson, arrived before there was a light to keep. The nomination says he found people using the unfinished building as a base for hunting, persuaded them to leave, and then waited without a light and without pay, writing to his congressman about both.[^1] The lens came ashore from the schooner *General Pierce* on 11 September 1855, in an unusual spell of calm water; the Christmas Day target for lighting was missed, and the *Alta California* announced that the light would be postponed to 1 February 1856.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives that date as the day Parkinson was finally able to activate the light.[^3] The nomination reports that within a fortnight the skipper of the steamer *Golden Gate* said he had seen the beam 42 miles out at sea.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive gives 1855 as the year the station was established and lists Parkinson from 1854, while the lighting itself was in 1856.[^2]

By 1875 the first building was failing. The nomination says a section of the lighthouse settled and large cracks opened, and that inspectors grew alarmed.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes the Light-House Board's 1875 recommendation to pull the old dwelling down and leave the tower standing, and says the Board reported in 1880 that the tower needed wooden props.[^3] The nomination says seven years after 1875 funds were appropriated for an entirely different lighthouse seaward of the first and almost 100 feet lower, and adds that the old high light was often hidden by low cloud.[^1] LighthouseFriends puts the appropriation at $38,000 and says work began in 1881.[^3]

The first-order lens was lit in the new tower on 20 June 1882, according to LighthouseFriends, after a fourth-order lens had served from a temporary platform on the old building.[^3] Sources differ on the year the tower is counted: the nomination dates the keepers' dwelling and attached light to 1881, and the Lighthouse Society archive gives 1882 for the tower.[^1][^2] The nomination describes the present building as a white, one-story stuccoed brick dwelling with an attached circular light structure 52 feet (16 m) above the ground.[^1] The light was at first fed on sperm oil, then colza and lard, and finally kerosene in 1888, and weights wound by hand turned the lens.[^1] Later buildings included a double dwelling for the keepers, which LighthouseFriends dates to 1905 and the Lompoc Valley history to 1906, and a reinforced-concrete dwelling of 1911 (or 1912) that replaced the old lighthouse.[^3][^6]

## The keepers

Parkinson's time was short and unhappy. In a letter that LighthouseFriends quotes, he wrote that "the freight on goods amounts to more than my pay" and that "My pay has not been forthcoming in over four months."[^3] The nomination says he often called the place "this dreadful promontory desolation."[^1] The sources disagree on how long he stayed. The Lighthouse Society roster lists him as principal keeper from 1854 to 1856, with James P. Meacham following, and LighthouseFriends says he served the lit light for only about six months before being dismissed. The nomination, by contrast, says he stayed on until 1875.[^2][^3][^1]

The archive's roster shows a station that, for its first decades, carried a head keeper and as many as three assistants. Thomas L. Perry, who appears first as an assistant in 1868, is listed as principal keeper from 1872 to 1895, the longest term on the list.[^2] Harley A. Weeks, an assistant in the late 1880s and early 1890s, returned as principal keeper from 1895 to 1913.[^2] Women appear on the roster as assistants too, among them Rachel P. Bates (1869 to 1872), Mary A. Perry (1873 to 1875) and Carrie R. Weeks (1904).[^2] The roster does not say whether any of these people were related to the men with the same surnames.

Charles E. Hellwig is listed as first assistant from 1939 and principal keeper from 1946 to 1955.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he was in charge in 1948, when the station was converted to electricity, and quotes him as calling the change a good thing because it meant "A lot less work."[^3] Coast Guard crews are listed from the mid-1950s.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Two of the station's early troubles were with the ground and the water. LighthouseFriends says an earthquake in 1868 left the cistern unable to hold water, and that a steam fog signal replaced a bell in 1872, set on a flat shelf more than 100 feet down the bluff from the first lighthouse.[^3]

The water problem continued. The Board's report for fiscal 1895 called the supply "a source of trouble for some time," and said a pipe from the spring was being run along the face of the cliff so that the whole spring could feed the fog signal.[^4] In the autumn of 1894 the signal's sounding pattern had to be changed "owing to scarcity of water," and restored on 13 December.[^4] That same report says the twelve-inch steam whistles ran some 518 hours in the year and burned about 41 tons of coal.[^4] The next volume records that falling rock broke the pipe line, so the overhanging cliff was blasted away for 150 feet back from the edge; a later year's entry records about 2,500 cubic yards of rock removed from the bluff.[^5]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the fog signal was converted from steam to a diaphone in 1917, and that the station was the last on the West Coast of the United States to receive electricity, in 1948. It gives 1973 as the year the station was automated.[^3] The Lompoc history repeats the automation date.[^6] The nomination notes that in its day, hand-cranked weights turned the lens.[^1] According to LighthouseFriends, the first-order lens was still turning in 1999; it was secured in March 2000 to prevent damage, and a modern beacon replaced it.[^3]

The lens itself survives. The Santa Barbara Maritime Museum says it is 18 feet tall, was built in Paris in 1854, has 624 glass prisms and weighs nearly 6,000 pounds, and that the Coast Guard approached the museum in 2012 to take it.[^7] Lampists began removing it from the tower in June 2013 and it went on exhibit that September.[^3]

## Today

The lighthouse is on the Jack and Laura Dangermond Preserve, which The Nature Conservancy bought in December 2017.[^3] The preserve says the lighthouse is owned by the U.S. military and that only authorized personnel may go there, despite what hiking apps show, and asks the public not to trespass.[^8] The light itself is an LED beacon mounted in the lantern room.[^3]
