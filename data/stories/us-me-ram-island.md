Ram Island Light stands on an offshore ledge beside a small island at the eastern entrance to Boothbay Harbor, Maine. Its brick tower on a granite base was first lit in 1883 and automated in 1965. It remains an active aid to navigation, marking the entrance to Fisherman's Island Passage, the eastern gateway to Boothbay Harbor, and to the Damariscotta River.[^1][^2][^9]

## The place

Boothbay Harbor was a working port long before the light went up. The National Register nomination for the station says the need for a light "was clearly evident by the nature of the commerce at this busy port."[^1] Vessels bound east or west made for the harbor when headwinds or bad weather met them.[^9] On 2 June 1837 Captain Joseph Smith of the U.S. Navy submitted a report in which he wrote that "more than 300 sail have been seen anchored there at one time," as LighthouseFriends prints it.[^9]

Smith thought Ram Island the point best suited to a light at the entrance to the eastern passage into the harbor.[^9] He also counted four lighthouses in view from the island and seven lights within twelve or fifteen miles.[^9] He was inclined to think some of those existing lights were badly placed, and he judged that, while they were kept up, it would not be "judicious" to build another on Ram Island.[^9]

Congress had already appropriated $5,000 for a light on Ram Island on 3 March 1837, before Smith's report.[^9] After the report, the Board of Navy Commissioners recommended suspending that spending until a more general examination of the area's lighthouses was made.[^9] LighthouseFriends reports that local fishermen then took the lighting of the passage into their own hands. After the first volunteer keeper left the area, Aldis McKay, a young lobsterman, took charge of the private light. He enclosed it in a glassed-in box that he weighted with rocks.[^9]

Congress approved $25,000 for an official station on 7 August 1882, forty-five years after Smith's report.[^9]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says construction could start right away in the spring of 1883.[^9] The National Register nomination says "the entire complex, with the exception of the oil house, was built in 1883."[^1] The tower is cylindrical, with a granite base, a brick shaft and a cast-iron lantern.[^1][^9] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record gives its height from base to lantern center as 35 feet (11 m), and the height of the light above mean high water as 36 feet (11 m).[^2] The tower stands on a rock outcropping and was first linked to the island by an elevated walkway.[^1]

The society's record gives a fourth-order Fresnel lens with a "Year Original Lens Installed" of 1881, two years before the station was lit.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes a fourth-order Fresnel lens in the lantern of the 1883 station.[^9] Neither source read here settles which is right, so the date stays open.

The Portland Daily Press reported in October 1883 that "Samuel Cavanor has been appointed keeper of Ram Island light—new station."[^3] LighthouseFriends says he was paid $540 a year, had served as an assistant at Seguin Island, and first showed the light on 5 November 1883.[^9] The light was first fixed white, with two red sectors marking the clear passages.[^9] On 1 October 1889 the colors were reversed, so that white sectors marked the safe approaches and red warned of danger.[^9]

Later work is recorded in the Light-House Board's annual reports. For the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892, the report says plank walks were built "from the dwelling to the bridge," and on to the fuel house and the barn.[^7] For the fiscal year ended 30 June 1897, it says "a thousand-pound fog bell, struck by machinery, was established" at the station.[^6] For the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901, it records repairs to the tower's lightning rod and an overhaul of the striking machine.[^8]

## The keepers

Samuel John Cavanor kept the light from 1883 until his death in 1913.[^2] The Boothbay Register, reprinted in the *Daily Kennebec Journal*, said he was born in Halifax, Nova Scotia, had been an assistant on Seguin "for a short time," and had then come to Ram Island, where he was "a faithful servant."[^5] The same notice called him "a very honorable and upright man." LighthouseFriends says a buoy being hauled aboard the tender *Iris* crushed his leg, that the leg had to be amputated, and that he kept working on a peg leg.[^9] The Register's notice says he had "lost his right leg."[^5]

He died of heart disease on Thursday, 10 April 1913, in a wagon near the Congregational church in Boothbay Harbor, while driving.[^5][^9] The Register's notice says he had decided "to give up his position at the light station" because of heart trouble, and had come to the harbor to live.[^5] LighthouseFriends prints a later Register article on the couple. It says no family in the vicinity was closer, and that they had lived for years together on the little island.[^9]

Captain O. G. Reed, one of the harbor's old salts, took temporary charge until Almon Mitchell was made keeper on 10 May 1913.[^9] Mitchell had earlier served as an assistant at Great Duck Island from 1898, then at Libby Island and Lubec Channel, and as head keeper at Petit Manan and at Franklin Island.[^9] He kept Ram Island until he died of heart failure in 1925, at sixty-eight.[^9]

The roster names fifteen keepers, from Cavanor to Terry E. Stanley, whose listed term is 1965.[^2] Between Mitchell and automation the roster lists Alonzo A. Connors (1925 to 1933), Charles N. Robinson (1933 to 1936), Benjamin Stockbridge (1936 to at least 1942), Ralph C. Norwood (1945 to 1950) and Wendell Reece (1953 to 1958).[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A driving hailstorm, not long after the light had been established, smashed the lantern glass and put out the light. Cavanor plugged the broken panes with newspaper to keep the light burning.[^9] Another storm swept away the station's boat in 1885, and in 1898 another storm knocked the weight box of the fog bell off the tower.[^9]

Cavanor wrote on 6 December 1884, as LighthouseFriends quotes him, that the schooner *Mineola*, aground on nearby Squirrel Island, was a "totle rack [sic]."[^9]

One rescue from November 1891 is recorded in detail. Allen Conary of Fisherman's Island left Boothbay Harbor in the afternoon with the wind blowing very fresh. Cavanor and another keeper, James Burke, started for their respective stations in Cavanor's boat. Off Mouse Island Cavanor turned and saw the peak of Conary's sail disappearing beneath the water off Spruce Point, about a mile away. Burke, at the wheel, put about. The Register's account says the keepers "quickly reached" Conary, who was "clinging to a board," with his head just above water that was "icy cold." They got him into the boat and made for Fisherman's Island.[^4]

On 18 January 1903 the schooner *Harriet W. Babson* grounded on Ram Island while returning from Newfoundland with a load of salt herring. The walkway was damaged, and the light went dark for the next few nights, because the keeper could not reach the tower to refuel the lamp until the walkway was repaired.[^9]

On 7 July 1939, with war looming, the Coast Guard took over the Lighthouse Service.[^9] At Ram Island, when the civilian keeper went on leave, a coastguardsman would step in for him.[^9] Not long after Ralph Norwood transferred to the station in early 1945, he rowed to the mainland with his two sons and was met by men armed with loaded shotguns. A skittish neighbor had called the police and the FBI to report that German spies were coming ashore.[^9]

LighthouseFriends also repeats local stories of a ghostly "White Woman" who appeared with a light to warn ships of danger. One captain reported hearing a fog whistle during a blizzard, though, as LighthouseFriends notes, there has never been such an aid on Ram Island. These are stories, not station records.[^9]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1965.[^1][^2][^9] A modern foghorn replaced the fog bell in 1972.[^9] A decade later, the fourth-order lens was stolen from the unattended lighthouse. It was recovered, and it is now on display at the Boothbay Regional Historical Society.[^9]

The station was offered to the town of Boothbay, but the town expected large maintenance costs and did not take it on.[^9] With no resident keeper, the station fell victim to vandalism and neglect.[^9] In 1977 the Coast Guard completed repairs at the station, which totaled $44,000, and the badly deteriorated walkway to the tower was removed.[^9] When the Coast Guard announced its decision to demolish the keeper's house in 1983, the Grand Banks Schooner Museum Trust stepped forward and leased the station. The Ram Island Preservation Society was formed and repairs began.[^9]

The trust was granted ownership of the site in 1998 through the Maine Lights Program. The Coast Guard retains responsibility for the active navigational light itself. The trust installed a new walkway to the tower in 2002.[^9]

## Today

The Grand Banks Schooner Museum Trust owns the station.[^2][^9][^10] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record lists it as not open to the public.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open, with the dwelling and tower closed.[^9] The New England Lighthouses guide says the grounds are open by appointment.[^10] Public cruises that pass the light are offered from Bath by the Maine Maritime Museum and from Boothbay Harbor, and LighthouseFriends notes that a distant view can be had from land, including from the road that hugs the shore at Ocean Point.[^9]
