Faro de Los Morrillos de Cabo Rojo, known in English as Cabo Rojo Light, stands on the southwest corner of Puerto Rico.[^1] Its hexagonal stone tower is attached to the keepers' dwelling.[^1][^3][^8] It also signals the southeast point of the entrance from the Caribbean, through the Mona Passage, into the Atlantic.[^1] The Coast Guard's list dates the present tower's first lighting to 1882, and the light was automated in 1967.[^3]

## The place

The point is a white limestone headland. The National Register nomination calls it "an isolated point in a deserted white lime rocky cliff surrounded by marshes and lagoons," with an open command of sea and land.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it is believed that Columbus named the cape for its limestone cliffs, tinged red by minerals.[^7]

The Lighthouse Board's annual report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 says the light "is used to guide along the southerly coast of Porto Rico and to the Mona Passage."[^2] The nomination says the light signals the southeast point of the entrance "from the Caribbean Sea through Mona Passage, into the Atlantic Ocean."[^1]

## Building the light

The Spanish built the tower. The 1901 report says it "was commenced in 1878 and was finished in 1882 by the Spanish, at a cost of 14,900 pesos."[^2] The report describes a third-order light that flashes white every minute, 121 feet (37 m) above high water and visible 18 miles.[^2] It gives the tower as 40 feet (12 m) high from its base.[^2]

The nomination gives the hexagonal tower as 60 feet (18.3 m) high including the lantern.[^1] It says the dwelling was designed for first- and third-class keepers.[^1] The 1901 report describes the dwelling as one-story and flat-roofed.[^2] The archive's record gives the tower's height from base to the centre of the lantern as 40 feet.[^8] The two heights do not agree, and this story does not choose between them.

The nomination dates the original lens to 1881, a year before the report's completion date for the tower, and says the lantern "still houses" it when the nomination was written.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the lantern "originally housed" a third-order lens, and the archive lists the current optic as a DCB-24.[^7][^8] The sources do not say whether the 1881 lens is still in the lantern today.

The archive's place record gives 1868 for the station's establishment and for its original lens.[^8] The Coast Guard list gives 1882 for both the station and the first lighting of the present tower.[^3] Nothing in the sources cited here describes an earlier light on this point, so the story does not date one. LighthouseFriends notes a cistern just east of the light, used to store water for the keepers.[^7]

## The keepers

The archive's roster runs from 1901 to 1967 and lists twelve principal keepers, with first assistants, laborers, and a group of names under a "USCG" heading from 1959 to 1966.[^8] The first name is Mariano Lopez, principal until 1901. Simeon Martin followed for 1901 to 1902, and Domingo Suarez-Rosa is listed from at least 1903 to at least 1909.[^8] The archive lists Jose Escalera Velez as principal keeper from at least 1915 to 1916, and the 1915 Bulletin names him in its account of a rescue below.[^8][^4]

Pedro Albino Gonzalez served as first assistant in three separate spells: 1916 to 1917, 1921 to 1922, and 1936 to at least 1941.[^8]

The light's equipment was in poor shape in 1901.[^2] In February 1901 the lamp gave out and was replaced temporarily with a table-stand lamp, "which is still in position, awaiting a new lamp," and the report said the lamp and revolving machinery were "still in bad condition."[^2]

The archive lists Arturo M. Ferrer as first assistant from at least 1915. The 1915 Bulletin names Arturo Machicote Ferrer as assistant keeper at Cape Rojo, and LighthouseFriends gives the same name. The archive's entry matches the first name, middle initial and surname, but no source cited here connects it with the 1915 rescue, so this story does not treat the two as one man.[^8][^4][^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends dates a hurricane that passed just off Puerto Rico's southern coast to 11 August 1915.[^7] The Bulletin's own account, in the same year's volume, refers to "the hurricane of August 11."[^4] It names keeper Jose Escalera Velez and assistant keeper Arturo Machicote Ferrer as the rescuers of three men "who were out in a small boat about 1 mile from the station," and says they "cared for them until the storm was over."[^4] The sources cited here name no wreck at this light.

In September 1921 a hurricane passed over Puerto Rico.[^5] The Bulletin of 1 October 1921 lists Cape Rojo among the ninth-district stations damaged, with Cardona Island and Mona Island.[^5] For the whole group it reports that four boats were lost and many station buildings damaged, and it gives no separate account for Cape Rojo.[^5]

On the evening of 23 July 1926 a storm damaged lighthouse property at Ponce Range, Guanica Harbor, Cape Rojo and Mona Island.[^6] The Bulletin says the principal damage was at Mona Island, where "the dwelling was partly unroofed."[^6]

## After the keepers

In 1959 the living quarters were altered, and the nomination says the original structure was altered that year.[^1][^7] In 1960 the weight-driven clock that turned the lens was replaced by an electric motor.[^1] The nomination lists changes to the light's characteristics in 1918, 1962, 1967 and 1974.[^1]

In 1967 the light was automated and left unmanned, and the lighthouse was boarded up.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends says the empty lighthouse fell into disrepair until a three-million-dollar renovation was begun in 2002.[^7] When the nomination was written it classed the station "Unoccupied. Unrestricted access."[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and the Municipality of Cabo Rojo manages it, according to LighthouseFriends.[^7] The lighthouse was returned to near its original condition and opened to the public in March 2007.[^7] Visitors may climb the staircase to the roof.[^7] An artist decorated the interior with Homer's Odyssey as the theme, and LighthouseFriends says the place is more of an art museum than the usual history museum.[^7] The archive's place record still reads "Open to Public: No."[^8]

LighthouseFriends gives the opening hours as Thursday to Tuesday, 9 a.m. to 4:30 p.m., and says reaching the lighthouse takes a 10-to-15-minute walk, or a trolley ride.[^7] The light stands at the end of Route 301, in the Cabo Rojo National Wildlife Refuge.[^7] Check the hours before a visit.
