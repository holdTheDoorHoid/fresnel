Dumpling Rock Light is a light on a rock in Buzzards Bay, a few hundred yards off Round Hill Point in Dartmouth, Massachusetts, at the approach to New Bedford Harbor.[^1][^2][^4] It was first lit in 1828. Since the 1940s a skeleton tower with a flashing green light has stood on the rock where a wooden keepers' house once stood.[^1][^2]

## The place

Dartmouth grew as a centre for shipbuilding and whaling in the early nineteenth century, and its shipping needed a light to guide it. Dumpling Rock, a few hundred yards offshore from Round Hill Point, was the place chosen.[^2] On 23 May 1828 a sum of $4,000 was appropriated for the lighthouse.[^2]

In the early nineteenth century D'Entremont describes a kind of semaphore system worked by the keeper. When a ship was approaching New Bedford Harbor, the keeper raised a signal arm on a post, and New Bedford's merchants, seeing it, would get their goods ready for the incoming mariners.[^2]

## Building the light

The first station was a two-story stone dwelling with a lantern on its roof.[^2] Levi Smith, the first keeper, was paid $400 a year. The light was first lit on 19 October 1828 from ten lamps with 14-inch (36 cm) reflectors, a fixed white light 43 feet (13 m) above the sea, visible for 10 nautical miles.[^2]

The sea often reached the station until the government built a wall around the rock.[^2] A new lantern and deck were installed in 1851.[^2]

In 1888 the Lighthouse Board announced that the dwelling was in such poor condition that further repair would be a waste of money. A new wood-frame dwelling, 26 by 34 feet (7.9 by 10.4 m), was built on the old foundation, with a square wooden tower attached.[^2] The sources differ on the date. The Archive gives 1889 for the tower, and D'Entremont gives 1890 for the new dwelling.[^1][^2] The Archive's record gives the original optic as a fifth-order Fresnel lens, with no date. D'Entremont says the lantern on the new tower held a fourth-order lens.[^1][^2] The two may describe different periods, and the sources do not say whether the optic was changed.

On 22 December 1893 the light was changed from fixed white to fixed white with a fixed red sector, and the red sector covers Mishaum Ledge.[^3]

A Daboll trumpet, a fog signal worked by an oil steam engine, was put into service on 12 October 1897.[^4][^2] In the year ended 30 June 1899 the trumpet was in operation for 862 hours and used 998 gallons of mineral oil.[^6] The next year it ran for some 707 hours.[^7] In the year ended 30 June 1901 it ran for some 657 hours.[^9]

In the year ended 30 June 1899 a cable was laid to connect the station with the telephone line on the mainland.[^5] In the year ended 30 June 1901 the engine house was enlarged, a brick oil house was built, and two 2-horsepower steam oil engines were replaced by two 4-horsepower Hornsby-Akroyd oil engines.[^8]

## The keepers

The Archive's roster lists 45 lines of keepers and assistants for Dumpling Rock, from 1828 to 1942.[^1] Levi Smith was head keeper from 1828 to 1856, the longest head keeper's term on the list.[^1] His successor, Charles C. Smith, kept the light until 1873. George B. Sistare, first assistant from 1870, was head keeper from 1873 to 1883.[^1]

Life at the station was hard, by D'Entremont's account.[^2] In 1838 a Navy lieutenant on a survey found the keeper absent on the main, and the reflectors looked as if they had not been polished for some time.[^2] In 1842 Smith reported that the reflectors' silvering had worn away, and that the cistern often held salt water rather than fresh.[^2] D'Entremont adds that the condition of the reflectors was probably not the keeper's fault.[^2]

Later keepers appear in the Board's and the Commissioner's reports. The Archive dates John E. Rogers as head keeper from 1916 to 1933, and Charles A. Ellis as first assistant from 1912 to 1920.[^1] D'Entremont gives Rogers's start as 1927, not 1916.[^2] The Commissioner's table of lifesaving assistance for the fiscal year 1920 (July 1919 to June 1920) names J. E. Rogers as keeper and C. E. Ellis as assistant keeper of the station together.[^10] Rogers is named at the station in 1919 and again in 1921, which is earlier than D'Entremont's date.[^10][^12][^14] Octave J. Ponsart was head keeper from 1937 to 1942, and he was keeper in September 1938, when D'Entremont says the worst hurricane in New England history struck.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1842 the engineer I. W. P. Lewis, inspecting the station, reported that the lantern had been "shaken nearly to pieces in storms."[^2]

On 16 November 1919 the Bulletin records that the keeper and his assistant towed a disabled motor boat, the *Nistia*, with six men aboard, to shore from Dumpling Rock Light Station.[^10][^12][^13]

On 31 July 1921 the power boat *Kyra*, with five men aboard, drifted onto Dumpling Rock. The Department commended Rogers for the help he rendered that day.[^14] The Commissioner's report says he helped pull the boat off the rocks on the southwest side of the light and anchor it in safe water.[^11]

The September 1938 hurricane did extensive damage at the station. The Bulletin for 1938 says that in Massachusetts extensive damage was also done at Dumpling Rock.[^15] D'Entremont's account adds detail. Ponsart and his family were about to leave on their first trip away in several years, and had left the assistant keeper in charge, when the wind picked up. Water came almost to the ceiling downstairs, and a large piece of the rock broke through the living-room wall. It stayed partly inside the house and anchored the dwelling to the rock.[^2] Emma Ponsart later said that the hymn "Rock of Ages, Cleft for Me" held new meaning for her after that day.[^2] The Ponsarts and Fontineaus lost everything they owned and did the repairs to the station themselves.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Archive's record dates the steel skeletal tower to 1940 and says the old wooden tower was damaged in the 1938 hurricane.[^1] D'Entremont dates the removal of the house and the erection of the skeleton tower to 1942.[^2]

## Today

The tower on the rock shows a flashing green light.[^2] D'Entremont says the automatic light still counts as an active aid to navigation, but the Archive's record lists the station as not an active aid and not open to the public.[^1][^2] The sources cited here do not name an owner. The rock is closed to the public, and visitors should not land on it or climb the tower.[^1]
