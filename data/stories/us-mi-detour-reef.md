DeTour Reef Light stands on a concrete pier about a mile off the shore of Michigan's Upper Peninsula, where Lake Huron narrows into the St. Marys River and vessels bound for Lake Superior pass close by. It replaced a lighthouse on the point of land to the west, and the keepers moved out to it from the shore when it was put into service on 10 November 1931.[^1][^3]

## The place

The station guards the mouth of the St. Marys River, the strait connecting Lake Huron and Lake Superior. The Lighthouse Service wrote in 1932 that all vessel traffic to or from Lake Superior passes the new station, and quoted an average of 77 vessels a day for one earlier season.[^1] Before it, the light stood on the point of land at the west side of the river entrance. The sources disagree on the date: the Lighthouse Service's own account says that old lighthouse was built in 1848, while the village of DeTour's website says the station was established on 13 April 1847 and the buildings put up that year, but that the light was not shown until 1848, "apparently" because the work finished too late in the year to fit the light and employ a keeper.[^1][^5] Lighthousefriends, citing a congressional appropriation of $5,000 in 1847, describes a 57-foot (17 m) stone tower and a keeper's dwelling there; the village site gives the stone tower as about 65 feet (20 m).[^3][^5] Both say the point was later marked by an iron tower finished in 1861.[^3][^5]

## Building the light

The Lighthouse Service described the shore station as having a dangerous shoal between it and the track of passing vessels.[^2] The shipping interests had asked for a major aid nearer the channel.[^1] The crib, a timber box 60 feet (18 m) square and, in the 1932 Bulletin, 21 feet deep (the 1931 Bulletin and Lighthousefriends say 20 feet high), was built on the shore and finished early in the summer of 1930; it was floated off the shore like a small vessel, towed to a prepared site in 24 feet (7 m) of water at the outer end of the reef and sunk by filling its compartments with rock.[^1][^2][^3] When the working party left for the winter the station was about half complete, and the Bulletin said the superstructure would go up when navigation opened in the spring of 1931.[^2]

The finished pier rises 20 feet (6 m) above the lake, flared outward by 2 feet to keep seas off the deck, and a steel band 8 feet 6 inches wide was fitted round it as ice protection.[^1] On top stands a steel-and-cast-iron building of two storeys, 30 feet square, whose second floor was living quarters for three keepers, with a square tower carrying a 3½-order revolving lens 74 feet (23 m) above the water.[^1] The lens, lantern, watch room and fog-signal engines were taken over from the shore station.[^1] The Lighthouse Service put the station in commission on 10 November 1931, after one and a half years of construction, of which only about 327 working days were actually spent at the site.[^1] The village and the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation give the height of the structure as 83 feet (25 m) above the water.[^5][^6] On cost, the 1931 Bulletin forecast about $140,000 and the 1932 Bulletin gave approximately $140,000 as the total, while Lighthousefriends gives $144,976.[^1][^2][^3]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists a head keeper and a first and second assistant for the station from 1931.[^4] William S. Hall is listed as the first head keeper, followed by John M. Sweet from 1935. Charles R. Jones then held the post from 1940 until 1962; Lighthousefriends says he retired after twenty-two years, and that although the Coast Guard took charge of all lighthouses in 1939, it kept a civilian head keeper until Jones retired in 1962.[^3][^4] The first assistants listed include James Brander (1931-1936), James A. Beloungea (1937-1943) and David S. Barclay (1951-1957).[^4] From 1951 the archive lists Coast Guard men in short stints, with many names in the 1960s.[^4]

The one first-hand account in the sources is from Marvin Kurkierewicz of Drummond Island, who served two stints. He remembered a late-season storm that threw a chunk of ice onto the deck and wrecked the crane used to lift supplies and boats; the station had a second crane at the opposite corner. Living with three other men, he said, "You had to get along with the crew you had."[^3] The Lighthouse Society's roster lists him in 1963-1964 and again in 1969-1970, where Lighthousefriends has the second stint as 1971 to 1973.[^3][^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

No wreck or rescue at the reef station itself is documented in the sources used here. One earlier rescue belongs to the old point station, not the reef. The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1921, so an event between July 1920 and June 1921, lists keepers' acts of assistance, and says that the keeper and first assistant keeper (D. McRae) of "Detour Light Station" "pulled the naval radio service launch off rocks to place of safety." The scanned text prints the keeper's name as "F. G. Sominer"; the Lighthouse Society's archive lists Frank G. Sommer as head keeper at DeTour Point from 1911 to 1925.[^7][^10]

## After the keepers

The station was automated in 1974, and in 1978 the Fresnel lens was replaced with a modern optic, according to Lighthousefriends; the Drummond Island Tourism Association also gives 1974 for automation and says the Coast Guard declared the light excess in 1997.[^3][^8] The DeTour Reef Light Preservation Society formed in January 1998; the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation says its volunteers restored the light to its 1931 appearance in 2003 and 2004, and that the Society became the owner on 17 June 2010.[^6][^8] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2005.[^8] The old diaphone fog signal was restored and sounded again on 27 August 2010.[^3]

## Today

The light is owned by the DeTour Reef Light Preservation Society, which offers tours and a weekend keeper program in which volunteers stay at the light and host visitors.[^6][^9] The light is offshore and reached by boat, and Lighthousefriends reports that the tower is open during the Society's tours and that visitors must be able to climb a 20-foot ladder to the deck; the road to the nearest shore point is gated.[^3] The Fresnel lens is on display at the DeTour Passage Historical Museum in DeTour Village.[^3]
