Faro de Punta Higüero (in English, Punta Higüero Light) stands on the westernmost point of Puerto Rico, in Rincón, between Mayagüez and Aguadilla bays.[^1][^9] Spain finished a brick lighthouse here in 1892, a stuccoed dwelling built around an octagonal tower that the station's National Register nomination says had a "castle" look.[^2][^1] The earthquake of 11 October 1918 left it, in the Commissioner of Lighthouses' words, "practically wrecked"; the concrete tower that replaced it, put into commission in January 1922, still carries the lantern from the Spanish building.[^3][^4][^5]

## The place

The light was built to guide ships using the Mona Passage, the channel off the island's western side.[^5] In 1885, the National Register nomination for Puerto Rico's lighthouse system reports, two Spanish officials working for the Lighthouse Commission called the passage "the natural trading course" of any European vessel going to South America and the "future Panama Canal."[^6]

Punta Higuero was on Spain's lists long before it was built: a plan approved in 1869 named it among fourteen lighthouses for the island, but no money was provided until 1875, and then for other sites.[^6] The nomination lists it among nine more locations "under consideration" between 1876 and 1885, and a revision of the plan in 1890 ranked it tenth in order of construction, after Punta de la Tuna.[^6]

Its job was to fill the gap between the lights of Punta Borinquen and Cabo Rojo.[^1] The Puerto Rico historic-preservation office puts it as "como punto intermedio entre los faros de Punta Borinquen (Aguadilla) y de los Morrillos (Cabo Rojo)" ("as an intermediate point between the lights of Punta Borinquen (Aguadilla) and Los Morrillos (Cabo Rojo)," my translation).[^7] The United States Light-House Board, describing the light in 1901, said it "serves as a guide through the Mona Passage and to the approach to Mayaguez Bay."[^2]

A railway ran past the site: the 2.46 acres (1 hectare) nominated to the National Register are bounded on one side by the right-of-way of the old American Railroad Company of Porto Rico.[^1]

## Building the light

Work began in 1891, according to the Light-House Board, which had been directed on 1 May 1900 to take charge of the Porto Rican lights after the war of 1898[^10] and recorded in 1901 that the tower was "commenced in 1891 and finished in 1892" by the Spanish "at a cost of 12,361 pesos."[^2] José A. Mari Mut's survey of Puerto Rico's lighthouses names the designer as José María Sainz and says the government built it itself, on a high point at the island's western tip.[^5] In his account the dwelling measured 50 feet (15 m) by 28 feet (8.5 m), with an octagonal tower 32 feet (10 m) high, and over the entrance sat the arms of the Corps of Engineers of Roads, Canals and Ports.[^5]

The station's National Register nomination describes a dwelling "for one 2nd class keeper" with a Mediterranean look, which it credits to brickwork both exposed and recessed around the doors, windows and corners, to reddish stucco made to look like stone on every face, and to an "elaborate exposed brick cornice topped by a parapet built in lace-like brick-work."[^1] The colour is disputed: the Board called the dwelling stone-coloured with red trimmings,[^2] and Mari Mut writes of white stucco that matched the stones of the base.[^5]

The Board's 1901 report described a sixth-order fixed white light, 69 feet (21 m) above high water and visible 8 miles (the report does not say nautical or statute).[^2] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive likewise lists a sixth-order Fresnel lens installed in 1892.[^8] The nomination also calls the light a sixth-order station, yet a few lines later describes a fourth-order lens by Bernard & Barbier, 50 cm across, and I could not reconcile the two.[^1] The day the light first showed is also unsettled: Mari Mut gives 17 January 1893, Lighthousefriends 13 January 1893, and the nomination only the year 1892.[^5][^9][^1]

The Board's report for fiscal 1900 counted fifteen lights in Porto Rico and said preliminary measures were being taken to assimilate the island's service to the general Light-House Service.[^10] A chartered schooner made the rounds of the Porto Rican lights from late October 1900 to about 27 January 1901 and made minor repairs "at all the lights except Jiguero and Arecibo"; of Point Jiguero, as the Board spelled it, the 1901 report adds that "No repairs were made at this station."[^2]

In August 1913 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin listed Point Jiguero among the lights changed from fixed to flashing or occulting, with the illuminant changing from oil to the incandescent oil-vapor kind.[^11][^9] As general background, not a description of this station's own lamp, George R. Putnam wrote in 1917 of the incandescent oil-vapor lamp: "the kerosene, forced into the vaporizer by air pressure, is heated and vaporized, and is burned mixed with air under a mantle, which is thus brought to a brilliant incandescence."[^12] The mantle and the vaporizer both reappear in the earthquake reports below. Lighthousefriends dates a fireproof oil house for storing the kerosene, standing apart from the other buildings, to 1914.[^9]

The earthquake of 1918, told below, set off the rebuilding: Congress appropriated $24,000 for it on 19 July 1919.[^4][^25] The superintendent of lighthouses at San Juan advertised in El Mundo of 16 December 1919 for sealed proposals, to be opened at 2 p.m. on 2 February 1920, for building a tower and dwelling at Point Jiguero Light Station, Rincón.[^13] Bids came in above the appropriation, so the Lighthouse Service did the work with its own district force, hired labor and purchased materials; by the Commissioner's report for fiscal 1921 a dwelling for two keepers was finished and the materials for a reinforced-concrete tower were being assembled.[^4][^14][^25] The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1922 describes the reinforced-concrete tower as a "cylindrical shaft 12 feet in diameter, with walls 1 foot thick, having a stepped base" (3.7 m across, with walls 0.3 m thick), with "a spiral stairway of precast concrete steps" running from base to top around a hollow concrete column; its focal plane was 55 feet (17 m) above the ground and 93 feet (28 m) above high water.[^4] The plan was to top it with a 7-foot (2.1 m) helical-bar lantern holding the lens apparatus taken from the old station, but "for the present the old lantern is in use."[^4] The dwelling was a frame building covered with asbestos shingles, 63 feet (19 m) long and 26 feet (8 m) wide.[^4] The Commissioner gives 12 January 1922 as the day the light was placed in commission in the new tower, and the Bulletin's construction column says 9 January; the report shows $23,432.19 spent on the project by 30 June 1922.[^4][^15]

## The keepers

No source I found names the Spanish keepers who tended the lamp from 1893 until the change of flags. The one trace of a Spanish-era keeper that I found is a complaint in La Correspondencia de Puerto Rico of 21 September 1893:[^16] the newspaper told the administrator of the central communications office that "constantemente estamos recibiendo reclamaciones del torrero del faro de punta Higüero por faltas del periódico que le remitimos con toda puntualidad diariamente" ("we are constantly receiving complaints from the keeper of the Punta Higüero lighthouse about missing copies of the paper we send him punctually every day," my translation), and it blamed the communications office at Rincón.[^16] What governed a keeper's work is known in outline: the nomination for Puerto Rico's lighthouse system says two sets of Spanish regulations, 102 articles in all, reached down to the polishing of the floors in the keepers' dwellings and to the "chamois or buckskin with which the optics are to be polished"; that describes the island's Spanish lights in general, not Punta Higuero in particular.[^6]

The first names come from the National Archives' keeper registers, as compiled in the U.S. Lighthouse Society's Digital Archive, which lists Jose Prieto as principal keeper from at least 1901 to 1914.[^8] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of January 1915 listed the station in each district that showed "the highest general efficiency during the fiscal year 1914"; for the ninth district, which included Puerto Rico, it was Point Jiguero, with Jose Prieto named as keeper.[^17][^14]

The Spanish dwelling had been built for a single second-class keeper,[^1] but the registers show an assistant keeper at the station from 1914,[^8] and the frame dwelling finished in 1921 had "a double set of quarters of four rooms each, with porches at each end."[^4]

Rufino Flores Graniel, whom the registers show in charge from at least 1921 to at least 1930, was named in the Lighthouse Service Bulletin three times for helping people.[^8] The issue of 2 February 1925 reported that on 20 November (1924, judging by the issue date) he had assisted a workman who had hurt his knee working on the railroad track near the station.[^18] The issue of 1 October 1925 reported that on 9 August he had helped four people whose boat, after drifting for four days, had reached the station.[^19] And after the hurricane of July 1926 the Bulletin recorded that he had sheltered four families, 24 people including 15 children, whose homes were destroyed.[^20]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The records I found for this station are dominated by earthquakes.

At 10:20 on the morning of 11 October 1918 an earthquake struck the area; Lighthousefriends adds that the lighthouse, on its bluff, was not affected by the tsunami that came with it.[^9] The Lighthouse Service's Bulletin of 1 November reported that nearly every light station on the island sent in an earthquake report but that the damage was inconsequential except at Point Borinquen and Point Jiguero, where each station was a large one-story brick dwelling with the light tower on top of it, and where the Bulletin says both were damaged "beyond repair."[^21] At Point Jiguero the keepers had been obliged to abandon the dwelling.[^21] The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported for the year that both stations were "practically wrecked."[^3] Even so, a local notice to mariners, dated San Juan, 6 November 1918, and printed in El Imparcial on 9 November, said the Point Jiguero light had been repaired and restored to its former characteristic, a white light flashing in groups of three every fifteen seconds.[^22] The USLHS roster lists Julio L. Rengel as principal keeper from at least 1917 to at least 1919, a span that brackets that day, but none of the reports I read names the keeper who was there.[^8]

The shocks did not stop: a second Bulletin note, in the issue of 2 December 1918, recorded a severe earthquake on 24 October, a slight one felt at about 10:30 a.m. on 3 November during a visit by a Lighthouse Service representative to the station, a lesser one on 11 November and a "very severe" one on 12 November; as a result of these quakes, it said, more serious cracks appeared in the station.[^23] Lighthousefriends, summarising an inspection made after the first quake and several aftershocks, says the inspector found two serious cracks that went all the way through the tower's brickwork and a great deal of fallen plaster, that the keepers could not safely live in the dwelling, that $30,000 was requested for a concrete replacement, and that a reinforced-concrete "yoke" was put around the tower as a temporary support.[^9] Mari Mut adds that a new wooden house was built nearby for the keeper and that the strengthened tower kept working until 1921.[^5]

At 4:39 a.m. on 22 August 1919 the keeper reported an earthquake that lasted four and a half minutes; it broke the lamp's mantle and put the light out temporarily, but caused no additional damage.[^24]

In early 1920 the Bulletin printed the keeper's reports to the superintendent of lighthouses at San Juan.[^25] The first, dated 27 January, says that the day before, "three earthquakes were felt, lasting 4, 6, and 7 seconds, respectively, breaking the mantles in each case."[^25] The last damaged the vaporizer and connecting tubes, which had to be replaced: "The light did not fail because we immediately installed the spare outfit."[^25] The second, dated 11 February, reports an earthquake of 10 February lasting 11 to 12 seconds that broke the mantle and shook plaster off the building.[^25] At 9:05 p.m. the keeper judged it necessary to put the light out and change the vaporizer, suspecting that fragments of the broken mantle had interfered with the nozzle: "It took 42 minutes to effect the change."[^25]

Shocks went on being reported, without damage to the station: one on 18 December 1922,[^26] three on the night of 24-25 February 1923, at 9:50 p.m. and 5:48 and 5:50 a.m.,[^27] and one at about 4:58 a.m. on 14 June 1927.[^28]

The hurricane of July 1926 passed over the southwest of the island on the evening of 23 July, the Bulletin of 1 September said; it listed damage to lighthouse property at Ponce Range, Guánica Harbor, Cape Rojo and Mona Island, and Point Jiguero was not among them.[^20] Lighthousefriends dates the passage of the storm's eye to 24 July.[^9]

## After the keepers

In 1933, the National Register nomination says, the light was made automatic and left unattended.[^1] Mari Mut adds that the keepers' house and the support buildings were abandoned and destroyed years later by a fire.[^5]

The first, Spanish building took a longer road out: Mari Mut writes that its tower was removed after the lantern moved to the new light, that the building was turned into a school (a photograph captioned 1925 in his book shows it without the tower), and that it was demolished in 1931; its footing lasted until the mid-1990s, when it was removed during construction of the park.[^5] The National Register nomination and the Puerto Rico preservation office say instead that the old structure was demolished in 1922.[^1][^7] Rincón's municipal page offers a third version, that the lighthouse was destroyed again in 1931 and rebuilt again; the only other source I read that mentions 1931 is Mari Mut, and he describes a demolition, not a second rebuilding.[^29] The nomination also says the evidence suggests the old lens was destroyed in the 1918 earthquake,[^1] while Mari Mut writes that a 1922 report suggests it survived and went into the new tower, and that a buoy lens installed later was used until at least 1978 and is now in the Coast Guard museum in San Juan.[^5]

Rincón's municipal page says the name was changed in 1953 from Punta Higüero to Punta Higüera, and that by 1959 the light ran on batteries.[^29] The lighthouse was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 22 October 1981.[^7] The Library of Congress holds the Historic American Engineering Record's written data on the lighthouse, prepared in 1984 from the 1979 nomination.[^30] In 1993, the preservation office says, the Municipality of Rincón entered a lease agreement with the Coast Guard to administer, maintain and restore the lighthouse, and has since built sidewalks, huts, lookouts and other facilities in the park.[^7]

## Today

The lighthouse stands in the El Faro park, reached by Route 4413 off Route 413 north of Rincón.[^7][^9] The Coast Guard is the custodian of the lighthouse; the passive park around it is developed and run by the Municipality of Rincón.[^7] The park is open daily, but the tower is closed to the public, according to Mari Mut's survey and to Lighthousefriends.[^5][^9]

The concrete tower is 70 feet (21 m) tall.[^5] Mari Mut's book (copyright 2009-2013) says the light then came from a small lamp powered by solar panels, and that the lantern was still the one from the first lighthouse: "La linterna es el único remanente del faro original español" ("The lantern is the only remnant of the original Spanish lighthouse," my translation).[^5]

Rincón's municipal page says the lighthouse was restored in 1993 and turned into a passive park with a maritime museum, café, huts and a lookout for watching whales and Isla Desecheo, and that it is well frequented by tourists between December and March.[^29] Just north of the lighthouse stands the dome of a former nuclear reactor.[^9] Rincón's tourism page names it Planta Bonus and says it was a nuclear plant built in 1960 that operated from 1962 and was retired in 1968;[^31] Lighthousefriends says the reactor was built between 1960 and 1964 and decommissioned in 1970.[^9]
