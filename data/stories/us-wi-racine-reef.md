Racine Reef Light stood on a reef about two miles offshore from Racine harbor, in Lake Michigan.[^1] Its first light was a gas beacon on a crib, lit on 31 August 1899.[^2] In 1906 a staffed station went up on the reef: an octagonal lighthouse with a steel frame, faced in brick.[^1] The lighthouse was demolished in 1961, and a steel tower took its place.[^3][^1]

## The place

The reef lies in the track of ships running between Milwaukee and Chicago.[^1] In its 1902 report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, the Lighthouse Board called the reef "a dangerous stretch of solid rock," and said the route stayed open all winter, so a light there had to run without a break.[^1]

Before the light, the reef was marked from around it. LighthouseFriends gives two dates for buoys on the reef: a buoy in 1863, and a first-class iron can buoy, painted red, in 1869.[^1] A pierhead light at Racine, established in 1872, ranged with the harbor light. From 1880 a red light at Wind Point marked the reef, and LighthouseFriends says these aids "proved insufficient at marking the reef."[^1]

An 1868 survey estimated that a lighthouse on the reef could be built for $175,000. The engineer in charge judged that the outlay, together with the yearly cost of upkeep, could not be justified.[^1]

Ships still went onto the reef. On 23 August 1883 the steam barge *Edward Smith*, of Marine City, Michigan, bound from Buffalo to Racine with a cargo of coal and a crew of fifteen, stranded there at half past three in the afternoon, about two miles east by south of the Life-Saving Service's Racine station.[^4]

## Building the light

In August 1898 a crib was towed to the reef, sunk, and filled to the water level.[^5] The Board's report gives it as "9 feet 2 inches high, 40 feet by 10" in plan.[^5] LighthouseFriends describes a forty-foot (12 m) square crib a little over nine feet (2.7 m) high.[^1] The two accounts agree on the height and do not agree on the plan.

A metal tower carrying gas tanks went up on the crib.[^1] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1900 says the construction was finished in July 1899, ready for the tanks and burners to be fitted.[^6] The beacon was first lit on 31 August 1899.[^2]

The gas light was hard to keep burning.[^1] The Board's 1902 report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says the beacon was "frequently extinguished by the heavy winter waves."[^1] On 24 December 1900 it went out, came back the next day, and went out again on the night of 5 March 1901.[^7] In 1901 the tower was raised twenty feet (6 m) on a pyramidal skeleton frame.[^8][^1]

In October 1901 the Board concluded that the reef needed a light tower, a fog signal and keepers' quarters. Congress appropriated $75,000 on 3 March 1903.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes a newspaper story of 5 April 1903. Visitors to the Hotel Racine, where Congressman Cooper was staying, demanded that he work against the appropriation for the lighthouse and the steam foghorn. They claimed the foghorn would be a nuisance: "in foggy weather none will be able to enjoy a night's rest."[^1]

The permanent work went up in stages. A second crib, sixty-foot (18 m) square, was sunk on the eastern point of the reef, and an octagonal concrete pier was built atop it. The work was completed in October 1905.[^1]

On the pier stood an octagonal lighthouse with a steel frame faced in brick, and seven rooms for the keepers. The lantern held a fourth-order Fresnel lens. The fog signal building was embedded in the pier, and its two steam boilers powered a ten-inch (25 cm) whistle on the east gable of the lighthouse.[^1] The Lighthouse Society's record gives the height from the base of the structure to the center of the lantern as 72 feet (22 m), and the lens's installation as 1906.[^3] The light and fog signal went into service on 6 October 1906, with George Cornell as head keeper.[^1]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society's roster for the station runs to 84 entries, from 1906 to 1961, one for each person in each stint. It lists head keepers, first, second and third assistants, acting assistants, laborers and the Coast Guard crew of the station's last years.[^3]

George J. Cornell was head keeper from 1906 to 1910. The roster names Marion L. Brown as first assistant and Conrad A. Stram as second assistant in that first year.[^3]

Edward Skilling, a second assistant, was feared dead in early December 1908 after he failed to reach the reef in a storm.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he had spent four hours on the lake in a naphtha launch, in winds of about forty miles an hour, before a steamer picked him up. He telephoned Racine from Chicago, went back to the reef, and served there until 1910.[^1]

Edward W. Knudsen was head keeper from 1911 to 1918.[^3] The Bulletin prints the keeper's report of the storm of February 1915 with his name in its heading.[^10] In 1918 the Bulletin listed the station among the lighthouse efficiency flags awarded for 1917, with Edward W. Knudsen as keeper.[^12][^14]

Charlie E. Tesnow was head keeper from 1933 to 1946.[^3] In February 1936 the assistant keepers, Plumer Layman and Marvin Cornell, were on the reef when bad weather prevented the regular supply run from reaching them. After seventeen days they were out of lard, butter and coffee. Tesnow and five men from the Coast Guard station at Racine decided to risk a supply run. They placed provisions in a small wooden boat and pushed and pulled it toward the light across the ice, and Tesnow then relieved Cornell at the lighthouse.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the winter of 1911 to 1912 the water around the reef froze solid, and LighthouseFriends says the keepers could walk the two miles to shore.[^1] In early March 1912 two steamers were stranded in the ice near the light, and around 500 curious residents walked out to look at them. The spectacle ended on 7 March, when dynamite freed the steamers.[^1]

In December 1900 the steam barge *George Dunbar*, laden with lumber, ran aground on the reef around midnight in a blinding snowstorm.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the light was not burning that night, and the crew spent the night jettisoning lumber from the decks. At daylight residents at Racine spied the wreck, and a volunteer crew went out to the reef on a fishing tug. The barge was freed and steamed into harbor, though leaky.[^1] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1901 records the light out from 24 December 1900 until the next day, but does not mention the barge.[^7]

The Bulletin's 1913 volume reports a rescue on 24 July. "Edward R. Knudson, keeper of Racine Reef Light Station, Wis.," towed a disabled motor boat with four people on board into Racine Harbor.[^9] The Bulletin's middle initial differs from the roster's, and no source read shows whether the two names belong to the same man.

The keeper's report of the storm of 1 February 1915 survives in the Bulletin. Dated 6 February, it begins by saying that "this station experienced the most severe storm of the season." The wind came from the east and east-northeast, and the station "was covered with one continuous cloud of spray," which flooded all the north and east rooms. Lantern glass was iced eight inches (20 cm) thick on the east and northeast sides. The building vibrated so much that furniture was moved across the rooms, and books and other articles were thrown off the shelves.[^10]

Afterwards the Service tried to protect the tower from the sea. Early in the summer of 1915 about 1,365 tons of riprap were deposited about the pier. The stone was laid with a deliberately uneven top, so that the seas would break before they reached the tower.[^11] The Bulletin's 1916 note says the vibration from heavy seas "was excessive," and that in past winters the station "was covered with ice from top to bottom." The riprap, it reports, cut the vibration and kept the tower "more free from ice."[^11]

## After the keepers

An air diaphone replaced the steam fog signal in 1925, the same year the light was electrified.[^1] In 1933 the tender *Hyacinth* laid 18,000 feet (5,500 m) of telephone and control cable between the reef light and the breakwater light at Racine Harbor, to consolidate the two stations.[^1]

The Society's record gives 1954 as the year the light was automated.[^3] LighthouseFriends describes a crew still on the reef until 8 August 1961, when the last crew, Lewis Lamb, Jerry Schober and Jerome Ziemba, left aboard a Coast Guard boat.[^1] The two dates do not agree, and no source here settles which is right.

As the lighthouse was to be demolished, Coast Guard personnel removed the station's lens and fog signal equipment. From the first week of September, over about two months, the Army Corps of Engineers demolished the lighthouse. On 30 October 1961 a steel tower of about 45 feet (14 m) was barged out and set on the old pier. Coast Guard personnel at Racine ran the new light by remote control.[^1]

In 2001 the Coast Guard repaired the pier, which had started to list. The tower was removed from the pier to be sandblasted and repainted in Milwaukee, and a temporary light was shown until it went back on the pier.[^1]

## Today

LighthouseFriends reports the light inactive since 2022.[^1] The Society's record lists the station's disposition as "Destroyed."[^3] The steel tower of 1961 is the only structure on the pier that the sources describe, but no source read confirms whether it still stands.[^1]

According to LighthouseFriends, the Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and its grounds and tower are closed. The light is best seen by boat, with a distant view from Racine Harbor.[^1] The Society's record does not list the station as open to the public.[^3] Do not land on the reef or try to climb the tower.

The Fresnel lens removed in 1961 is on display at the Racine Heritage Museum, which the Society's record places at 701 Main Street.[^13][^1]
