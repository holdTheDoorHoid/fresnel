Mount Desert Light stands on Mount Desert Rock, a ledge about 600 yards long off the coast of Maine, south of Mount Desert Island.[^1][^2] The first light station was established in 1830, and the granite tower that stands there today was built in 1847. The light was automated in 1977, and the College of the Atlantic now owns the station, which it uses for marine research.[^1][^2][^9]

## The place

Its highest point is about twenty feet above the sea at low tide, and during storms waves can cover the whole rock, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The sources do not agree on how far out it lies. LighthouseFriends gives twenty-six miles from the nearest harbor.[^2] I.W.P. Lewis, who visited in 1842, put it twelve miles from the nearest land, as LighthouseFriends quotes his report.[^2] The 1934 *Bulletin* gives another figure, 2½ miles from the mainland, and calls Mount Desert Rock the "farthest out" lighthouse on the coast of Maine.[^7]

Lewis called the location "the most dangerous of the whole light-house establishment," because the rock lay in the open ocean and was swept by surf in storms.[^2] In 1853 mariners asked for a fog bell there, since the rock sat in the track of ships running between Boston and the Bay of Fundy. A bell worked by machinery was soon placed on the rock, and an assistant keeper was added in 1855 to tend it.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress allocated $5,000 in 1829 for a lighthouse on the rock, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] A contractor, Gamaliel E. Smith, made a low bid, and his crewmen, working without proper tools, had dug only part of the cellar when he was dismissed. Joseph Berry, who had just finished the lighthouse at Hendricks Head, took over for $2,770 and completed it in the summer of 1830.[^2] The first lighthouse was a stone dwelling with a wooden tower and a birdcage lantern. Its light came from ten Argand lamps with fourteen-inch reflectors.[^2]

A year after it was built, a Captain Derby reported that it was "in a state of rapid decay," that its mortar had been made with salt-water sand, and that the dwelling leaked and smoked.[^2]

In 1847 Congress authorized $15,000 for "rebuilding" the lighthouse. The new granite tower stood apart from the old dwelling, and an 1850 report called it "a beautiful tower, built of heavy granite stone."[^2] The keepers went on living in the old stone dwelling until a new house replaced it in 1892. The 1847 tower is the one that stands today.[^1][^2]

LighthouseFriends dates the new lantern room and its third-order Fresnel lens to 1857. The US Lighthouse Society's record gives the lens as installed in 1858.[^2][^1] The sources differ, and this story does not choose between them.

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1893 says that "At this isolated station very little work in the way of repairs was done for a number of years," and that the station was in great need of overhauling when operations began there in August 1892.[^5]

Later work came in stages. A frame dwelling went up in 1876.[^2] In 1880 the leaking stone parapet was removed and a cast-iron lantern deck and gallery were fitted.[^2] A forty-five-foot bell tower was built in 1887.[^2] In November 1891 a third-class Daboll trumpet replaced the fog bell, which the Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1892 records as changed "from a bell struck by machinery."[^2][^3] A prefabricated wooden duplex replaced the stone dwelling in 1892. The Board reported that work on the "double dwelling for the keeper" was done at the shop in Portland, and that the dwelling would probably be ready for occupancy in October.[^4][^2] A revolving fourth-order Fresnel lens went in during 1898, changing the light from fixed white to a white flash every fifteen seconds.[^2]

In 1932 a radiobeacon was installed at the station. The *Bulletin* says the power plant built for it was used to electrify the whole station, raising the light's candlepower from 24,000 to 70,000.[^6][^2]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists 43 principal keepers at Mount Desert Rock, with dates running from 1830 to at least 1970. It also lists 42 first assistants and 49 second assistants, and 51 other names, including three contractors, a superintendent of construction and Coast Guard personnel.[^1]

Esaias Preble was the first principal keeper, from 1830 to 1839.[^1] Benjamin Ward kept the light from 1840 to 1848. When Lewis visited in 1842, Ward told him that a gale in February had broken the sea into the house and flooded the cellar, and that a gale in October 1841 had stove in the boathouse and carried off a boat.[^2]

Rufus King served from 1853 to 1859, nearly six years, and was terminated for an unknown reason, LighthouseFriends says.[^1][^2] Keepers did not stay long. George Booth resigned after seventeen months, and his replacement lasted under eight months.[^2] LighthouseFriends says keepers here were paid $840 a year at one point, against about $600 at most other lights.[^2]

Thomas R. Milan kept the light from 1882 to 1902. LighthouseFriends says he lasted almost twenty years, which was rare, since few keepers lasted long at this station.[^1][^2] Fred M. Robbins came after him and was head keeper until 1911.[^1]

Vinal O. Beal was head keeper from 1911 to 1918, and Wilbert F. Lurvey was first assistant from 1911 to 1914.[^1] Henry C. Ray was an assistant keeper in 1920, when he drowned during an attempted landing from the lighthouse dory. Harry E. Freeman rescued Maurice R. Beal, the other assistant, from the same accident. LighthouseFriends gives Ray's age as forty-three. The roster lists Ray both as a first assistant from 1918 to 1920 and as head keeper for 1920.[^1][^2]

In 1934 the *Bulletin* reprinted a newspaper article about the rock's yearly garden. Fishermen from Bar Harbor, Southwest Harbor and Bass Harbor, on Mount Desert Island, brought grain bags of dirt each spring, and seeds were sown in the crevices.[^7] The article says the bloom made "a strange and pleasant sensation" for the landsman who first sees it, and that winter storms washed the soil away.[^7] George W. York, head keeper from 1930 to 1936, was one of four Maine offshore keepers who reported bird kills in 1934, LighthouseFriends says. He left the service in 1936.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1842 Lewis wrote that the rock "has been the scene of many fearful disasters," and that the brig *Billow*, of Boston, had been wrecked there a few years before.[^2]

LighthouseFriends describes two other rescues by keepers. In October 1912 First Assistant Wilbert F. Lurvey rescued Lafayette Carter from his disabled powerboat in a gale, with help from a son of Keeper Vinal O. Beal. Carter later wrote that he had had nothing to eat for six days, and LighthouseFriends quotes his account.[^2] In 1923 Arthur E. Ginn towed a disabled powerboat with two fishermen aboard to the station.[^2]

The wreck of the tug *Astral* in December 1902 is told in two accounts: LighthouseFriends, and a newspaper dispatch. LighthouseFriends says the tug, with eighteen aboard and towing a barge, crashed into the northeast ledge of the rock at 5 a.m. on December 9, 1902. It says the keepers rescued seventeen men, and that one was frozen to death.[^2] The Worcester *Spy* printed a Rockland dispatch of 15 December that tells a different story. The *Astral* struck the rock at five on the Tuesday morning, and the crew got ashore except the mess boy, Frank Cummings, who was washed into the sea and lost. The crew were stormbound for several days and took supplies to the keeper's store. Then, on 15 December, Captain Sorensen of the Boston tug *Clara Clarita* went out to the rock in a row boat and brought off a party the dispatch gives as sixteen men.[^8] The two accounts disagree on who made the rescue, how many were saved, and how the one death came about, and this story cannot resolve that.

## After the keepers

The station was automated in 1977. LighthouseFriends says a helicopter landed on the rock just before Christmas that year and took away the last two keepers.[^2] The Coast Guard tried to make life bearable on the rock with books, radios and other comforts, but crews still went ashore for eight days' leave after about twenty-two days on duty.[^2]

Not long after the station was destaffed, the College of the Atlantic began using it as a whale research outpost. It was transferred to the college in 1998 under the Maine Lights Program.[^2] The keeper's house became the Edward McC. Blair Marine Research Station. The college replaced its roof in 2000, and the next year added a second floor to the generator house.[^2] In August 2009 Hurricane Bill damaged several buildings and nearly destroyed the boathouse, but the lighthouse was not damaged.[^2] In 2014 Jacomien and Forrest Mars donated $425,000 to upgrade the station's facilities, LighthouseFriends says.[^2]

## Today

The College of the Atlantic owns and runs the station, and the light is still an active aid to navigation. Its optic is an LED beacon, solar powered, installed in August 2018.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society lists the station as not open to the public.[^1] New England Lighthouses says it is used as a whale research station and is best seen from a boat.[^9] The lighthouse is on the National Register of Historic Places.[^2] The rock is a research base, not a public site, and visitors should not try to land on it.
