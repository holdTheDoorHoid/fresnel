Newport Harbor Light stands at the north end of Goat Island, at the entrance to Newport Harbor, Rhode Island.[^1] The first tower on the island went up in 1823, and the National Register nomination calls the light the first of three built within the harbor itself.[^1] The tower standing there now is dated by the sources to 1842, 1856, 1864 or 1865.[^1][^2][^8][^10]

## The place

Newport had been a commercial port since the early 1700s, and Goat Island sat at the mouth of its harbor.[^2] Beavertail, at the south end of Conanicut Island, was the first lighthouse ships passed on their way to Newport's agricultural markets, the nomination says.[^1] Newport Harbor Light was the first light placed within the harbor itself.[^1]

The first tower did not mark the reef well. LighthouseFriends says it did not mark the shallow reef just north of Goat Island well enough, and many vessels ran aground there.[^2] In 1799 Newport transferred the island to the federal government, for Fort Wolcott.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress provided $2,500 for the first tower in March 1823, LighthouseFriends says. It was lit on New Year's Day 1824, the same account says, with a multi-lamp reflector apparatus showing a fixed white light.[^2] The Lighthouse Board's report for 1894 says the light "was established in 1823, but it was rebuilt in 1865."[^4]

In 1838 Lieutenant Edward W. Carpender inspected the station, and LighthouseFriends quotes his report: "I found this light, late in the afternoon, in the filthiest condition of any light in the district."[^2]

The second tower stood on a breakwater off the north end of the island.[^1][^2] The nomination says it was built in 1838 by the Army Corps of Engineers under Lt. George W. Cullum.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives $67,700 appropriated between 1834 and 1842 for the dike and a new 29-foot (8.8 m) tower, and it dates the move of the light to the new tower to 1842.[^2] Rowlett also gives 1842.[^8] The nomination disagrees: it says the 1838 light "remained active until 1865."[^1]

Both the nomination and LighthouseFriends say the old tower was taken down and moved to Prudence Island in 1851.[^1][^2] The nomination says "the original tower was moved to Prudence Island in 1851."[^1]

A fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps in 1857, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The archive lists the original optic as a fourth-order Fresnel.[^3]

The date of the present tower is disputed. The nomination says the present tower "dates from 1865," replacing the 1838 light, which was "taken down" three years later.[^1] The nomination's own description gives another year, saying the original lighthouse "was replaced by the present structure in 1856."[^1] The 1894 report describes a white stone tower, 29 feet (8.8 m) high, with an attached keeper's dwelling at the end of the breakwater.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the keeper's dwelling on the pier went up in 1864 and that the old keeper's house on Goat Island came down in 1868.[^2] Visit Newport gives the current structure's date as 1864.[^10]

In 1891 the light changed from a fixed white light to an occulting one, fixed white for fifteen seconds and then dark for five, from May 20, 1891, the Lighthouse Board's report says.[^5] The report, in the author's own words, says he "modified an old clock, formerly used for pumping lard oil to a lamp," so that it raised and lowered a brass cylinder around the chimney.[^12]

## The keepers

The archive lists Samuel Watson as the first keeper, from 1823 to 1841.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he was hired for the post.[^2] Caleb C. Mumford (1841 to 1845), Henry Oman (1845 to 1849), Pardon W. Stevens (1849 to 1853) and John Case (1853 to 1863) followed.[^3]

In 1851 officers found Keeper Stevens absent on a routine inspection, LighthouseFriends says. The inspection report for the station still praised the light: "Everything managed better than in any light visited before."[^2]

John Heath kept the light from 1863 to 1868. He was followed by Mary Ann Heath, whom the archive lists under the principal keeper's heading from 1868 to 1873.[^3] Neither the archive nor LighthouseFriends says how she came to the post.[^3][^2]

Henry W. Crawford kept the light from 1873 to 1883.[^3] Lydia A. Crawford is listed as first assistant from 1878 to 1882.[^3] The archive does not say whether the two were related.[^3]

Charles Schoeneman kept the light from 1883 to 1921, the archive lists, and LighthouseFriends says he called the station home for roughly thirty-eight years.[^3][^2] In 1894 the Lighthouse Board said the station had a single keeper, paid $600 a year.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In June 1912 a launch from the destroyer *Mayrant* capsized in the harbor, spilling eight sailors into the water. LighthouseFriends dates the accident to June 9.[^2] The *Washington Times* of June 10, 1912, reported from Newport that Capt. Charles Schoeneman, keeper of the lighthouse, lowered his small boat, picked up a sailor who was swimming toward the lighthouse, and a short time later took two others from the sea. Boats from the submarine tender *Castine*, the flagship *Dixie* and the repair ship *Panther* picked up the rest and took them to the naval torpedo station.[^11]

Two years later, LighthouseFriends says, a powerboat from a contractor's wharf job on Rose Island drifted onto the rocks near the torpedo station. Schoeneman rescued the man aboard and secured the boat before it broke up.[^2]

The keeper's dwelling was struck on November 9, 1921. The nomination says it was "struck by a submarine from the U. S. Naval Torpedo Station on Goat Island."[^1] LighthouseFriends names the submarine as the N-4, which it says rammed the breakwater that day.[^2] Rowlett says a submarine ran aground on the island and dates the demolition of the dwelling to 1923. The nomination says it was torn down in 1922.[^1][^8]

The Bulletin's account of the September 1938 hurricane says "serious damage was experienced at a number of stations" in Rhode Island, including Newport Harbor.[^7]

## After the keepers

The keeper's house was not rebuilt.[^1] The nomination says the light was electrified and its operation handed to military personnel at the torpedo station.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1922 list of changes gives Newport Harbor's change from oil to electric incandescent light.[^6]

The light was automated in 1963, the nomination and LighthouseFriends agree.[^1][^2] The Coast Guard renovated the tower in 1989.[^2] The land between the tower and the shore was filled in when a hotel was built on Goat Island. The nomination names the Goat Island Sheraton, and LighthouseFriends dates the work to the 1970s.[^1][^2] Visit Newport says the landfill was done in the 1960s.[^10]

In 2000 the Coast Guard leased the tower to the American Lighthouse Foundation for preservation, and the foundation formed the Friends of Newport Harbor Light.[^2][^8] In 2005 the foundation announced a $120,000 restoration plan.[^8] LighthouseFriends says that in 2018 the foundation did not renew its lease.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light.[^8][^9] Rowlett names the Friends of Newport Harbor Light as site manager.[^8] The American Lighthouse Foundation's own page also gives the Coast Guard as owner.[^9] Visit Newport says the Friends lease the structure and run occasional open-house days.[^10] The sources do not agree on who holds the lease now.

The grounds are open to the public, although the lighthouse is not, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] Rowlett describes the site as open and the tower closed.[^8]

The nomination describes a green fixed light, 33 feet (10 m) above the water, visible for 11 miles.[^1] The American Lighthouse Foundation gives the 2005 Coast Guard Light List's figure of 11 nautical miles.[^9] LighthouseFriends dates the green light to 2005. The sources do not agree on when the light changed color.[^2]
