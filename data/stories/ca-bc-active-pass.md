Active Pass Light stands on Georgina Point, at the north end of Mayne Island in British Columbia's Gulf Islands, beside a channel where ferries and tugs pass between Mayne and Galiano islands.[^1][^2][^9] The Department of Marine and Fisheries first lit it on 10 June 1885. For its first thirty-five years one principal keeper, Henry "Scotty" Georgeson, tended it, and after the fog alarm was installed he paid his son George as an assistant.[^3] The tower has been rebuilt twice since, and the light is still on the Coast Guard's list of lights.[^1][^4]

## The place

Active Pass lies between Mayne and Galiano islands. By the early 1880s traffic through the Gulf Islands was growing, and two wrecks off Mayne and Saturna islands had made lights on the main channels a priority for the federal Department.[^3] The biography says the pass was then "called Plumper Pass on some charts" and was the shortest route between southern Vancouver Island and the mainland.[^3] The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1901 records a correction to the way the names "Active pass" and "Plumper passage" were applied.[^5] Lighthouse Friends says the pass takes its name from the United States revenue and survey vessel *Active*, which was the first steamship through it, in 1855.[^2]

The current was the hazard. The Department's report for the year ended 31 March 1908 gives the maximum strength of the current in Active Pass and Porlier Pass as five to nine knots, and says tugs handle most of the traffic through them.[^9] The Department began observing the slack water in Active Pass in 1905, and by the year ended 31 March 1907 it had added tables for slack water in the two principal passes to its report.[^7][^8] A report for the year ended 31 March 1924 says low-water slack is more complex to calculate than high-water slack on this coast, and that in Active Pass the low-water slacks must be sorted into three groups according to the moon's position.[^14]

## Building the light

The order in council that named Georgeson keeper came on 21 July 1884.[^3] The station was finished the next year, and he was told to light it on 10 June 1885.[^3] The deputy minister's report of June 1885, as the biography quotes it, described a square wooden tower, painted white, 12.8 m (42 ft) high to the vane of the lantern, with the keeper's dwelling attached.[^3]

The first light was a fixed white coal-oil lamp that floated in a tub of mercury. The biography says the mercury gave off fumes. The clockwork that turned the apparatus had to be wound by hand every two or three hours during the night.[^3] Within two months of opening, Georgeson asked for a fog horn, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^2] A fog bell went in about 1887, and a steam fog alarm replaced it in 1892.[^3] The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1901 records corrections to the pilot book's note on the light's arc of visibility, and to the list of lights' note on the fog alarm's timing.[^6]

The light changed in 1910 or 1911. The biography dates the change from a fixed white light to an occulting white light, shown with petroleum vapour, to 1910.[^3] The Department's report for the year ended 31 March 1911 says the fixed light "has been changed to an occulting white light, visible five seconds and eclipsed five seconds alternately."[^12]

The present tower is the third on the site. The wooden tower of 1885 gave way in 1940 to a square dwelling with a lantern room on top. In 1969 a cylindrical concrete tower with a lantern and gallery was built as the light, and the square house was kept for the keeper.[^1][^2] The heritage register describes the concrete shaft, which tapers to a metal lantern, as a version of the standard "apple core" plan, built with common and durable materials, in line with the Department of Transport's desire to minimize maintenance.[^1]

## The keepers

Henry Georgeson was born in 1835 in the Shetland Islands. The biography says he is supposed to have left home at fourteen to go to sea, though the story is not fully documented.[^3] He worked on the Cariboo gold-rush trail, ran a stopping house at Beaver Pass, and sold his share for $2,500 in 1863. He then settled on Galiano Island and fished for the Fraser River canneries. From 1868 to 1869 he held a post on the Sand Heads lightship at the mouth of the Fraser.[^3]

His salary began at $500 a year, rose to $550 in 1888, and jumped to $900 when the fog alarm went in. From that he paid his son George as assistant. By his retirement it was $1,680.[^3] The Department's keeper tables for 1906 list him at the "Active Pass Light and Fog Whistle."[^11] His logs were used as evidence on more than one occasion when Canadian Pacific captains complained that the fog signal had not sounded.[^3] He retired on 31 December 1920.[^3] His brother James kept the East Point light on Saturna Island from 1889 to 1921, and the biography calls the Georgeson name "synonymous with lighthouse keeping in the southern Gulf Islands."[^3]

Lighthouse Friends is the only source for the keepers after Georgeson. It lists Arthur Broughton Gurney from 1921 to 1945, Clarence Edgar Carver from 1944 to 1948, John Egerton Ruck from 1968 to 1983, Don DeRousie from 1983 to 1993, and Jean Beaudet from 1993 to 1997.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Department's list of vessels given assistance in the year ended 31 March 1908 includes the *Santa Barbara*, with the note "Struck in Active Pass." The entry gives no date, cause or detail, and it is not clear whether she was near the light.[^10]

The best-documented event is the grounding of the Canadian Pacific steamer *Princess Adelaide* on 13 October 1918. The biography says she ran onto the rocks in front of the lighthouse in dense fog, though the foghorn was working. No one died, and she was refloated.[^3] Lighthouse Friends prints a recollection by Georgeson's grandson Archie, who was working in the fog-alarm building with a neighbour that afternoon. He said the foghorn had been going for about ten hours before the ship came in. Lighthouse Friends adds that the captain was not on the bridge, and that the ship was a minute and a half farther along than the first mate had reckoned.[^2] The Department's report for the year ended 31 March 1919 records that on 13 October she was "stranded near Georgina point." Its inquiry at Victoria in November 1918 found the master and the second officer in default. The master was faulted for "not being in charge of vessel when approaching dangerous locality, in heavy weather," and the second officer for neglecting precautions. The master's certificate was suspended for two months and the second officer's for six months.[^13] The inquiry's words "heavy weather" do not match the biography's "dense fog," and the two accounts may describe the same afternoon.

## After the keepers

Jean Beaudet, the last keeper, left in 1997, and Lighthouse Friends ties his departure to what it calls "the de-staffing movement of the 1990s."[^2] In 2006 the property passed to the Gulf Islands National Park Reserve, while the Canadian Coast Guard kept responsibility for the navigation aid.[^2] The light is still lit, and the Coast Guard's list describes the tower as a white cylindrical tower with a red top.[^4]

## Today

The Coast Guard's list gives the light as a white flash every ten seconds, with a focal height of 17.4 m and a nominal range of 17 nautical miles. It operates year round.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says the light can be seen from the ferry, and that a closer view is possible by taking the ferry to Mayne Island and driving to the lighthouse along Georgina Point Road.[^2] The heritage register calls the light "a symbol of the Mayne Island community" and says it receives numerous visitors.[^1] The tower is a working aid to navigation. None of the sources says it is open to the public inside, and it should not be climbed or entered.
