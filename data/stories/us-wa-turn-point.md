Turn Point Light stands on the western tip of Stuart Island in Washington's San Juan Islands, where ships coming up Haro Strait make a hard right turn.[^2] The first light was a lens-lantern on a post near the point.[^2] The present small concrete tower dates from 1936, and the light has been automated since 1974.[^1][^2] The Coast Guard owns the station, and the Bureau of Land Management manages it.[^2][^4]

## The place

Vessels from the Strait of San Juan de Fuca followed Haro Strait due north toward places such as Vancouver, and turned at Turn Point, the northwest corner of Stuart Island.[^2] Canada had already built a light on Discovery Island to mark the southern entrance to the strait.[^2] In 1888 the Lighthouse Board said a light and fog signal at Turn Point "would complete the marking of this important channel," as the Turn Point society quotes the Board's report.[^3] The Board asked for $15,000 in 1888, and repeated the request each year until Congress paid it.[^2] The act of 3 March 1891 provided the money, and the Board's list of appropriations for that session includes $15,000 for the Turn Point light and fog-signal station.[^5][^3]

Stuart Island was surveyed in 1874 and most of it was opened to homesteading. The land at Turn Point was held back as a possible lighthouse site.[^2][^3]

## Building the light

A survey of the point was made in 1892, and a contract for the dwelling, fog-signal building, barn and water tanks was signed on 27 December 1892. Work began early in March 1893.[^2][^6] The Board's report for 1893 says the fog-signal building and barn were finished, the dwelling was nearly done, and part of the fog-signal machinery had arrived.[^6] The contractors finished the dwellings and buildings in July 1893, and the machinery was ready in October. The keepers were given a sailboat and a boathouse, and footwalks were laid.[^3][^8]

The station began operating on 30 November 1893.[^7][^2] The Board's report for the next year lists a "tubular-lantern light" and a Daboll trumpet fog signal at Turn Point, both from that day.[^7] LighthouseFriends, by contrast, describes a lens lantern on a post close to the point.[^2] The society quotes the 1892 report's plan for "a fixed white lens lantern near the end of the point upon a tripod platform about 10 or 12 feet above the ground."[^3] The sources do not agree on the form of the first light.

The fog signal was run by hot-air engines at first. In the year ended 30 June 1896 the Daboll trumpet ran 929 hours and burned about three tons of coal.[^9] In 1899 the Board found the hot-air engines too weak to run the compressors, and it ordered oil engines, which were shipped on 8 June 1899.[^10] The next year the old engines were removed, two oil engines were installed, and a 960-gallon redwood tank was built to cool them.[^2][^11] In 1900 the trumpet ran 136 hours and used 26 gallons of oil.[^11]

A 1930 *Lighthouse Service Bulletin* described the light then in use: two 300-millimeter lens lanterns, one above the other, with electric lamps. Their flashes made up a 20-second cycle of white occulting, then red.[^14] The archive and LighthouseFriends date the present tower and its 300-millimeter lens to 1936.[^1][^2] The Bulletin does not say how the 1930 arrangement fits with that date, and the sources do not explain it.

## The keepers

Albert F. Allen was the first head keeper and John Q. Latta his assistant.[^1][^2] Allen left in October 1895 for Point Wilson.[^3] Peter N. Christiansen, a Norwegian immigrant and merchant seaman living in Oakland, took the oath in June 1894 and arrived on the island with his wife Theodine and their daughter Anna. Their son Charlie, born on 15 August 1894, was the first white baby born on Stuart Island, according to the society.[^3][^2] Christiansen was promoted to head keeper on 15 January 1898, and left in 1906 to be the first keeper at Mukilteo.[^3][^1]

Edward Durgan, a New Yorker who had been assistant at Patos Island, became head keeper in 1895.[^3][^1] In 1897 Durgan and Christiansen were commended for saving the crew of a tug (see below).[^3] Edward A. Brooks was assistant from 1898 to 1902. The 1900 census, the society says, found him, his wife and daughter living at the station with his wife's sister and the schoolteacher.[^3][^1]

Louis A. Borchers arrived on 1 May 1902 with his wife and four children, after postings at Astoria and New Dungeness.[^3] He was promoted to head keeper in 1906 and kept the light until his death in September 1923.[^3][^1] During the First World War keepers were encouraged to produce food, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] Borchers canned 311 cans of fish in one season, using sockeye, pink and smoked salmon, San Juan Island sardines, grayfish and salmon caviar. The Bureau of Fisheries found the canned fish of high quality. Herbert Hoover, the Food Administrator, wrote to the Secretary of Commerce that the achievement was unusual "in view of the amount of work which I know is necessary for a light keeper to perform."[^13] LighthouseFriends adds that Borchers grew carnations around the light, and that a 1920 newspaper article described mariners turning their binoculars to the beds.[^2]

William Auld was head keeper from 1924 to 1930, and the Bulletin's index credits him with the station's efficiency flag.[^15][^16][^1] Edmund N. Cadwell, Walter Mabin, Thomas Wyman Albee and Arthur J. Woods followed between 1930 and 1939.[^1] The society says Woods was keeper until the Coast Guard took over in 1939.[^3]

George H. Wheeler had served at Tillamook Rock before he became assistant at Turn Point in 1932. LighthouseFriends says he was dismissed on 7 November 1932, after returning to the station drunk. His daughter had been born that year and died on 1 November, two days after her birth.[^2] He later became head keeper at Tillamook Rock.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the evening of 16 February 1897 the tug Enterprise ran aground on rocks near the station, and repeated whistle blasts brought Durgan and Christiansen out into the frigid night. LighthouseFriends says the keepers went into the water with pike poles, freed the tug and steered it to safe moorage. A barge the tug had cut loose was found, and its occupants were brought ashore by breeches buoy. A crewman who was drunk and threatened the others with a knife was put in a straitjacket and locked in the station's hen house.[^2] The Lighthouse Board wrote to the keepers that their services "merit the highest commendation."[^2]

In the fiscal year 1921 the Commissioner's report lists Borchers towing a disabled launch with three men aboard to a place of safety.[^12]

On 22 December 1955 the furnace in the officer-in-charge's quarters blew up in his face, burning him about the face and eyes. The station crew called the Port Angeles air station for a doctor and a Coast Guard helicopter. Force was taken to Prevost in the station's vehicle to meet the helicopter.[^2] A 1944 photograph in LighthouseFriends shows the station with a World War II lookout tower.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over the lighthouses in 1939.[^3] The light was automated in 1974, and the keepers' dwelling was later used by University of Washington researchers studying whale migration.[^2] A diaphragm foghorn replaced the Daboll trumpet in 1936, and LighthouseFriends says there is no longer an active fog signal.[^2]

Working with the Coast Guard, the Bureau of Land Management began a three-phase restoration. In 2005 work covered the roofs of the fog-signal building, the dwelling and the barn, and several windows were restored with wavy glass behind protective coverings. The second phase added a power station of solar panels and a propane generator on the hill above the station.[^2] The Turn Point Lighthouse Preservation Society was formed to educate the public and help preserve the station. In 2012 the Bureau of Land Management and the society signed a cooperative management agreement for the station, and the society has led work on a museum in the barn.[^2][^3]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the station, and the Bureau of Land Management manages it. The grounds are open, but the dwelling and tower are closed.[^2] The site is open year-round, and the Bureau says it is reached only by water craft.[^4] From the public dock at Prevost Harbor on Stuart Island it is a two-mile walk to the lighthouse, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The society's small interpretive museum is open daily from July to September.[^4]

The light flashes white every two and a half seconds.[^2]
