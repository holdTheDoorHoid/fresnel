Stage Harbor Light stands on Harding's Beach, on the western side of the harbor entrance near the southeastern tip of Cape Cod, in West Chatham, Massachusetts.[^2] It was a wood-frame keeper's house with an iron tower, first lit in the summer of 1880. The Lighthouse Service automated it in 1933, and the government then sold it. A private buyer had it by early 1936, and it has been privately owned since.[^1][^2]

## The place

The Lighthouse Board's 1878 report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, proposed a light that would help vessels into the old harbor: "The establishment of a light at this place giving a good range with one of the Chatham lights, would serve as a guide into Old Stage Harbor."[^2] The same report said such a light would be "of great value to vessels seeking refuge there at night and during bad weather."[^2] On 3 March 1879 Congress appropriated the money.[^2]

The harbor took its name, LighthouseFriends says, from the "fish drying racks, or stages" used locally. The beach takes its name from Joseph Harding, a farmer who had once owned the land.[^2]

## Building the light

The keeper's dwelling and iron tower were erected in 1880 "at a cost of $9,882.74," and the light was first exhibited on 15 July that year.[^2] The sources disagree on the tower. LighthouseFriends describes "a forty-eight-foot-tall iron tower" (14.6 m) with a fifth-order Fresnel lens. The Archive gives 36 feet (11 m) from the base to the lantern's centre, and a fourth-order lens.[^2][^1]

A covered walkway ran from the house to the tower.[^2][^1] A brick oil house was added in 1905, and in 1907 the tower went from brown paint to white.[^2]

## The keepers

Enoch Eldredge was appointed on 29 June 1880 at $560 a year. He died on 27 March 1884, while still keeper.[^2][^1] George W. Folger of Fayal, Massachusetts, took over on 14 April 1884 and kept the light until 1900.[^2][^1] Charles L. Ireland came in 1900, and Joseph F. Woods in 1904. Woods was transferred in October 1906.[^2]

Alfred A. Howard's appointment on 16 October 1906 began what LighthouseFriends calls "a series of outstanding keepers."[^2] He was first commended on 30 October 1912 and received an efficiency star, "one of seven in his area," in 1914. His salary rose to $600 in 1915. He transferred to Ipswich on 1 May 1916.[^2][^1]

Mills Gunderson, who had earned $804 a year as keeper at Boston Light, took over at Stage Harbor at $600 a year.[^2] LighthouseFriends records that he died by his own hand on 15 June 1918, for reasons the record does not give.[^2] His son Stanley, who had been helping him since leaving the army, "replaced his father."[^2] The Archive's roster dates the change to 1919.[^1]

Stanley M. Gunderson stayed until 30 June 1933, when his salary was $1,380 a year. LighthouseFriends says he won annual commendations from 1920 for the station's condition.[^2] It also says that during Prohibition he used the covered walkway "as a secret hiding place for booze," until a surprise inspection found the loose floorboards and he was "merely directed to nail them down."[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 30 March 1913 a "fresh southerly breeze" capsized the dory of Walter C. Harding, a surfman at the Monomoy Life Saving Station. Keeper Howard came to his aid. Harding wrote that he was "carried to the lighthouse where I was furnished with dry clothing."[^2]

On 12 September Howard towed the disabled catboat *Trilby* to the harbor. The Bulletin gives the day and month but no year.[^3] Several of her party were ladies.[^3] The Commissioner's report for the fiscal year 1915 lists him among those who saved life or property during that year.[^4]

In a letter dated 19 December 1914, Captain Ephraim Smith asked the Lighthouse Inspector in Boston to commend Howard "in saving my horse from drowning." Smith wrote that the horse "got mired and went down in mud and water" while being driven across the marsh, as LighthouseFriends quotes the letter.[^2]

On 12 October 1929 the auxiliary yawl *Golliway* "ran aground on the flats near the station." Stanley Gunderson went to her with his own powerboat and gave her crew food and dry clothing, and Commissioner Putnam commended his "timely assistance in this instance."[^2]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service announced plans to replace the old light with "an unwatched light set 308 feet from the old tower." Gunderson, the final keeper, wrote a critical letter to the Boston Post, arguing that "unattended lights regularly break down."[^2] The change "was implemented on June 16, 1933."[^2]

After automation, the lantern room came off and the top of the tower was closed over.[^2] The government sold the lighthouse and "the three acres it sat upon" for $1,501.[^2] Gunderson worked briefly at the Woods Hole depot, then became assistant keeper at Nantucket (Great Point), and "resigned from lighthouse service on March 31, 1935."[^2]

## Today

"The lighthouse is privately owned," LighthouseFriends says, and its "Grounds/dwelling/tower" are closed.[^2] The Archive lists the owner as private, the station as not open to the public, and the light as no longer an active aid to navigation.[^1]

From a distance, the lighthouse can be seen across Stage Harbor.[^2] It has stayed in one family since early 1936. LighthouseFriends says the owners occasionally open it to the public. The building "remains rather primitive," with no electricity and a single pump. A skeleton tower nearby now serves as the active aid to navigation.[^2]
