Sandy Point light is a small white wooden tower with a red lantern, standing on a concrete pier at the end of a sand spit on the east side of the entrance to Shelburne Harbour, Nova Scotia. At low tide the spit can be walked; at high tide the tower is surrounded by water.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The first light for Shelburne Harbour, Cape Roseway on McNutts Island, was lit in 1788, but ships that passed it still had a long way to go up the harbour. Its entrance, between Sandy Point and Fort Point, is a little over a kilometre wide, and a sand spit runs westward from Sandy Point for more than 200 metres. Lighthouse Friends gives that spit as the reason for a light at the narrow mouth.[^1]

## Building the light

The Department of Marine and Fisheries' agent for Nova Scotia reported in September 1873 that a fixed red light had been shown from a square wooden tower on the point since 1 September. According to Lighthouse Friends' summary of that report, George De Champ built it for $725 and Edward Goudock was appointed keeper at $200 a year.[^1]

On the night of 20 February 1878 that tower burned. Lighthouse Friends says Goudock had left about 10 p.m. and the tower was in flames some two hours later; an inquiry did not settle the cause.[^1] The agent first proposed moving the light to Surf Point across the harbour, then it was decided to build at the end of the spit instead. De Champ won a $1,725 contract for a timber pier and tower. In the form Lighthouse Friends quotes from the department's 1880 report, the new tower was 12.8 m (42 ft) high on a hemlock pier, with an iron plate on the pier's north-east angles as protection against ice, and the fixed red light went into operation on 15 December 1880.[^1] The Canadian Register of Historic Places also gives 1880 as the year of the present tower.[^2]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists Goudock as keeper from 1873 to 1878 and again from 1880 to 1899, then James Gordon Stevens (1903 to 1913), Norman D. Butler (1913 to 1919), Jacob Ralph Butler (1919 to 1953) and Robert Bush (1953 until automation in 1980).[^1] Goudock lived in his own house nearby rather than at the light.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

By 1888 the sea had undermined the pier and it had settled about 46 cm (18 in) on its south-west side, so the tower was wedged level and stone was dumped around the foundation.[^1] The department's report dated December 1904, for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1904, records the lighthouse as moved onto a new square concrete foundation 5.2 m (17 ft) high, just landward of the old wooden crib, built by day labour under the department's Halifax agency at $1,562.60.[^4] Other sources date the move to 1903.[^1][^2][^5]

## After the keepers

The Canadian Register says a fourth-order fixed red lens and a hand foghorn were installed in 1922, a hand fog signal answering vessels in 1930, and an electric foghorn in 1960; the register gives 1980 for automation and 1999 for decommissioning.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the Coast Guard discontinued the light in 1996, and the Lighthouse Directory also has it inactive from 1996; Lighthouse Friends gives 1914 for the fourth-order lens, which is not the register's date.[^1][^5] The Sandy Point Community Recreation Group opened a community hall in a park on the point in 1999, and in 2004 ownership left the Coast Guard: Lighthouse Friends says it went to the Recreation Group, the Lighthouse Directory that it went to a consortium of preservation groups.[^1][^5] After repairs in the summer of 2008 the light was shown again as a privately maintained aid.[^1][^5]

## Today

The tower is a federal heritage building, and the Canadian Register describes it as one of the square-tapered wooden towers typical of the years after Confederation.[^3] The Coast Guard's list of lights shows it as a private aid showing a fixed red light, 14.3 m above water, with a stated range of 6 nautical miles.[^6] Sources disagree on the owner: Lighthouse Friends says the community of Sandy Point and SaltWire the Recreation Group, while the Lighthouse Directory lists the National Lighthouse Trust with the Recreation Group managing the site. The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1][^5][^7] The light stands on its pier and is reachable on foot only across the sandbar at low tide, so check the tide.[^2][^1] A newspaper report says the group was raising money for foundation repairs.[^7]
