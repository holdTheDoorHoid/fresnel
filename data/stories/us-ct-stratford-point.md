Stratford Point Light marks the western side of the mouth of the Housatonic River, where the river meets Long Island Sound in Stratford, Connecticut. The white cast-iron tower with a brown band around its middle dates from 1881 and replaced a wooden tower first lit in 1822, and keepers, and later Coast Guard crews, lived beside it until 2018.[^1][^2]

## The place

The federal government set up the light in 1822 to mark the mouth of the Housatonic.[^1] Local tradition, as Kraig Anderson's LighthouseFriends site tells it, holds that a bonfire was lit on the point on foggy nights when a boat was expected, and that wood was later burned in an iron basket on a pole.[^2] The same source describes shifting sand bars and strong currents where the river meets the Sound.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress allocated $4,000 on March 3, 1821, and four acres on the point were bought from Betsy Walker.[^2] The National Register nomination says Ezekiel Gilbert of Derby and Robert Fairchild of Stratford contracted to build a wood-framed tower on a stone foundation to match the one at New Haven, with a one-and-a-half-story keeper's house nearby; LighthouseFriends names Judson Curtis as the man who directed the work.[^1][^2] The USLHS archive lists all three men as contractors.[^4] The tower was 28 feet (8.5 m) high and eight-sided.[^2][^3]

The nomination says the lighting apparatus began in 1821 with eight lamps used as a fixed light. Within a year Winslow Lewis, an inventor who had sold the government the rights to his improved lamps and silvered reflectors, visited and installed a revolving frame holding ten lamps, ten 16-inch reflectors and two spare lamps; a clock mechanism patented by the clockmaker Simon Willard was fitted in the lantern as well.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes the revolving light as two clusters of five lamps giving a flash every two minutes and fifteen seconds.[^2] The sources disagree on the later lens. The National Register nomination says a fifth-order Fresnel lens replaced the Lewis lamps in the decade before the Civil War, New England Lighthouses gives 1855 for a fifth-order lens, and LighthouseFriends gives a fourth-order lens in 1857.[^1][^3][^2] A fog bell was added in 1864 according to LighthouseFriends, although the nomination dates the station's fog signal to 1858.[^2][^1]

In 1867, LighthouseFriends reports, the Light-House Board's annual report said the wooden tower showed signs of decay, its lantern was constantly out of repair and leaky, and its two range lenses gave flashes too short and at intervals too long.[^2] The nomination likewise says the flashes of those lenses were too brief and too infrequent.[^1] Congress finally paid for a replacement in 1880.[^2] The Lighthouse Board approved the cast-iron design for Stratford Point in 1880, and the tower went up in 1881 under General Duane of the Engineering Corps.[^1] The nomination calls it the earliest Connecticut example of a cone-shaped, prefabricated cast-iron lighthouse.[^1] It is built of five courses of curved iron plates bolted through inside flanges, lined with brick, 21 feet (6.4 m) across at the base, and rises 35 feet (11 m) to the focal plane.[^1] A new two-story keeper's house was built the same year.[^2][^1] An 1881 inspection report, as the nomination summarises it, found a third-order flashing light: a constant lamp and two revolving range lenses made by L. Sautter and Co., fixed back to back.[^1] On May 15, 1899, the Light-House Board had a horizontal brown band painted around the middle of the white tower.[^5] An oil house was built at the station in the Board's report year ended June 30, 1895.[^6]

A compressed-air siren was ordered in 1910 to replace the fog bell, and a brick power house for the fog signal was built in 1911; LighthouseFriends gives 1911 for the siren's installation.[^1][^4][^2]

## The keepers

Samuel Buddington was the first keeper, and the Buddington family kept the light, with one break, until 1869.[^2][^4] An 1837 inspection report quoted by LighthouseFriends was savage: the light was "in the worst order imaginable."[^2] When Samuel died in 1848, his wife Amy was appointed keeper, and an 1850 inspection called the whole establishment "in good order."[^2][^4] Her son Rufus took over in 1861, and he and his wife Eliza lost four of their eight children to diphtheria in the winter of 1861 to 1862.[^2]

After Rufus came Benedict Lillingston (1869 to 1874), assisted by his son Frederick.[^4] LighthouseFriends records a story from a Bridgeport newspaper interview with Lillingston's granddaughter: one night in October 1871 both men went to the aid of a vessel in distress, leaving twelve-year-old Lottie Lillingston alone, and she noticed the light had gone out.[^2] With only what she had seen her grandfather do to go on, she lit a safety lamp and hung it in the lantern, and the steamer *Elm City* passed a dim light.[^2][^3] This is a recollection recorded long afterward, not a log entry.

The next three keepers, John L. Brush, Jerome B. Tuttle and Theodore Judson, were each assisted by their wives; the assistant's post was abolished in 1882.[^2][^4] Theodore Judson held the post from 1880 for decades.[^4] LighthouseFriends reports that in 1914 the 65-year-old Judson was told he would be transferred to a light off Newport, Rhode Island, and that steamboat officials petitioned against the move and won.[^2] The sources disagree about when he left: the USLHS archive and LighthouseFriends say 1919, New England Lighthouses says 1921.[^4][^2][^3] Judson also told a local newspaper that he had seen twelve to fifteen mermaids off the point, according to Jeremy D'Entremont.[^3]

William F. Petzolt, assistant from 1914, became keeper in 1919 and served until 1946 by the archive's dates; D'Entremont lists him as assistant from 1913 and keeper from 1921 to 1945.[^4][^3] Daniel F. McCoart, a Providence native who had run Bridgeport Harbor Light for over twenty years, followed him and retired in 1963 after 44 years in the service, still a civilian keeper long after the Coast Guard took over most lights.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends reports that his station won an "excellent" inspection a month before he retired, one of just nine in the country that year.[^2] From 1963 the archive lists a succession of head keepers and Coast Guard personnel, most serving a year or two, until 1970.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends writes that the new station survived one of the worst gales recorded there in the autumn of 1822, which moved houses off their foundations and tore up hundreds of trees.[^2] In a February storm, in a year the sources do not give, the fog bell had to be tolled continuously for 104 hours, then for another 103 after a lull, and Keeper Judson, spelled now and then by his family, wound the clockwork for almost nine days.[^2][^3]

In July 1897, LighthouseFriends reports, two Bridgeport fishermen upset their boat off the point; Judson's teenage daughter Agnes saw them from the tower, rang the fog bell and swam out, and her father and brother swam to the boat.[^2] New England Lighthouses tells the story differently, in which Agnes, then 17, and her brother Henry swam out and she threw a rope.[^3] Both versions are second-hand: LighthouseFriends lists the Bridgeport Herald of July 11, 1897 among its references.[^2]

On August 28, 1922, according to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin, Keeper Petzolt and Assistant Arthur G. Reuter took 30 passengers off the disabled launch *Ellen May*, which had grounded on a sandbar off the point.[^7] In 1934 the Bulletin credited Petzolt and Assistant James Kirkwood with rescuing a man who had drifted in a rowboat for more than 24 hours and was exhausted and suffering from cold.[^8]

## After the keepers

The lantern room came off in 1969 to make way for automated aerobeacons and went on display at Boothe Memorial Park; the headless tower stood until 1990, when a smaller optic was installed and the lantern refurbished and returned.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends dates automation to July 19, 1970, but Coast Guard personnel lived there until 1978, and the dwelling was reoccupied from 1982 to deter vandalism.[^2] The station was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1990.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says the last Coast Guard occupant left in 2018; the Connecticut Post says the Coast Guard largely vacated the site in 2019.[^2][^9]

## Today

The tower is still an active aid to navigation, with a 190 mm optic.[^4] The site is federally owned. The Town of Stratford and the Coast Guard agreed in 2019 to a lease giving public access, and the town reportedly paid a dollar for twenty years.[^2][^9] The first open house under the agreement was on October 17, 2020.[^2] On April 15, 2025 the property was offered to eligible public bodies and non-profits under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000.[^2] The town applied to take ownership, and a local arts group also applied.[^9][^10] No source used here says which applicant, if either, was chosen. LighthouseFriends says the grounds, dwelling and tower are open during scheduled tours; visitors should check with the Town of Stratford before going.[^2]
