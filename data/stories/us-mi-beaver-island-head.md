At the south end of Beaver Island, in northern Lake Michigan, a brick tower and keeper's house stand on a bluff above one of the main shipping tracks between the Straits of Mackinac and the ports of the western shore.[^14] First lit in 1851 (one source says 1852), the station outlasted the Mormon colony that had settled the island, gained a steam fog signal borrowed from another lighthouse, and was home for thirty years to a keeper, born on the island, who left it only four times.[^2] The Coast Guard put out the light in 1962.[^14]

## The place

The light was built to mark the channel about 9½ miles wide between Beaver Island and North Fox Island, which vessels sailing between the Straits and Green Bay or Milwaukee commonly used.[^14][^3] Shoal water runs out from both islands, and in the 1830s mariners were already asking for a lighthouse on the island's southern point.[^3] Lieutenant James T. Homans of the U.S. Navy, surveying for the Treasury in 1838, called the island's southern end the more necessary of two places in his district for a light. He wrote that "the loss of property from shipwrecks on the Beaver island has been considerable this season alone," and described the steamboat *De Witt Clinton* striking a shoal in the night that September and throwing nearly $20,000 of cargo overboard to get off.[^2]

Nothing came of it for more than a decade. In January 1849 Representative Kinsley Bingham presented a petition from "citizens of the western lakes," and Congress appropriated $5,000 for the light on 28 September 1850.[^2][^3] President Millard Fillmore reserved a 158-acre tract at the south end of the island for the purpose on 21 November 1850.[^3] The Beaver Island Historical Society adds a complication that the other accounts do not mention: that the buildings were put up about two miles east of the reserved land, a mix-up it says took a presidential order in 1954 to settle.[^13]

## Building the light

In March 1851 the Detroit contractor John McReynolds took the job for $4,480, to finish by 1 October 1851.[^2][^3] The plan was a brick tower and a dwelling. The accounts disagree on the details: one gives a one-story dwelling of 34 by 20 feet and ten lamps with 15-inch reflectors in the lantern, the other a dwelling of 26 by 30 feet joined to the tower by a covered passage and fourteen lamps.[^2][^3] The lamps revolved to give a flash once a minute.[^2] The National Register nomination, as summarized by the Beaver Island Archive, reports the tower and dwelling complete on 1 October 1851 and treats 1851 as the site's significant year, while the Historical Society dates the working light to 1852.[^14][^13] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists Henry Van Allen as first keeper, for 1851 to 1852.[^1]

The tower did not last. Both main accounts say the station was rebuilt in 1858, but they differ on why: one says the original tower "toppled over" in 1857, the other that the poor materials of the Pleasonton years had begun to deteriorate.[^2][^3] The tower that stands now is 46 feet (14 m) high; it was fitted with a fourth-order Fresnel lens at 103 feet (31 m) above the lake, showing a white flash every ninety seconds.[^2][^3] Congress appropriated $5,800 for a new two-story keeper's dwelling on 7 April 1866, and the brick house followed that year.[^2]

The biggest change came in 1890. The act of 2 March 1889 appropriated $5,500 for a steam fog signal here.[^4] The men and materials were landed on 27 August 1890, and the building, with a landing and water-supply crib, was done by 30 November.[^4] The machinery was second-hand: the signal at Skillagallee light was a siren, and since a steam whistle was thought better there, its sirens were transferred to Beaver Island.[^4] A tramway 142 feet (43 m) long and 5 feet wide, for landing coal and supplies, led from the landing to the signal, and the work crew renewed the five flights of stairs and platforms leading from the bluff to the lake, which the report called "rotten and in a dangerous condition."[^4] The siren began sounding on 6 December 1890.[^2] In the fiscal year ended June 1892 the tender *Warrington* delivered materials for a barn, 16 by 20 feet, and for repairs to the dwelling.[^5]

More keepers meant more building. A second assistant was added in 1898, and in 1902 the dwelling was extended to house three keepers; the same crew built a brick oil house and enlarged the boathouse.[^3][^2] A new fog signal building stood near the lake edge: the date is 1910 in one account, with the steam whistle replacing the sirens on 18 November that year, and 1915 in another.[^2][^3] The lamp was changed to incandescent oil vapor, which the Lighthouse Service's list of changes for 1916 includes; the two main accounts date it to 17 April 1916 and to 1917.[^12][^2][^3] An air Tyfon fog signal was installed in 1933 and the light was electrified in 1938.[^2] In fiscal 1921 the Lighthouse Service reported spending $7,296 on a new dock and general repairs here.[^11]

## The keepers

The first keepers served in a hostile place. James Strang had brought his Mormon followers to Beaver Island in 1848, and by the time the lighthouse opened there was open hostility between them and the island's other settlers. According to one account, Van Allen had hidden eighteen nets belonging to a Mormon at the light. When twenty-four of his own nets and buoys were dragged under by a current and thought lost, he offered to give back the eighteen in exchange. The current slackened, his buoys reappeared, and he was arrested, and he forfeited bail by leaving the island.[^2] The same account says the next two keepers were Strangites; the archive's list puts Dennis Chidester and Joseph Lobsdell next, though it does not say which of them the account means.[^2][^1]

After Strang was killed in 1856 most of his followers were driven off.[^2] In the memoir of Elizabeth Whitney Williams, who grew up there, the next keeper was an Irish settler, Mr. Loaney, whom the Mormons had run out; he came back once they were gone and was appointed keeper "of the Beaver Island lighthouse at the head of the Island," the second man to hold the post.[^10] The archive lists him as Patrick "Luney" Looney, 1857 to 1861, after Chidester and Lobsdell, so Williams's "second keeper" and the roster do not agree.[^1] Williams names the succession that followed: Captain Appleby of Buffalo, whose nephew Frank Blakeslee assisted him, and then Harrison Miller for eleven years or more, assisted by his nephew Edwin Bedford.[^10]

Harrison "Tip" Miller, keeper from 1863 to 1874, is the best documented of them.[^1] His father had followed Brigham Young to Utah, lost faith in him, and moved the family to Beaver Island to join Strang.[^2] Miller had ten children, and, by the accounts, a keeper's salary did not cover them: he fished, made barrels and carried the mail, which in winter meant hauling it 55 miles to Mackinac Island twice a week.[^2] He also held interests in two patents and made trips to Buffalo on that business.[^3] Forest fires on the south end of the island in 1868 nearly burned the station for want of water, and only then, in one account, were the keepers' complaints about the leaking cistern acted on.[^3] Pepper says he had not found why Miller left the lighthouse service in 1874. In 1876 he reappeared as the first keeper of the Beaver Harbor Life-Saving Station, and he held that post until a transfer to Point Betsie in 1887.[^3] The same account has him retiring from the Life-Saving Service in 1908 and dying at home on the island in 1931, aged ninety-three; Miller's Marsh carries his name.[^3] William H. Duclon followed him as head keeper, from 1874 to 1883, and Williams says he went from there to Eagle Bluff light in Wisconsin.[^1][^10]

The fog signal ended the era of the lone keeper. An assistant was assigned after the siren began in 1890, and the archive's roster shows William H. Shields as first assistant in 1891 and 1892.[^2][^1] By 1902 the station had three keepers, and the roster for the decade after shows Dominick D. Gallagher moving from second assistant (1902) to first assistant (1904) to head keeper (1912 to 1932).[^1]

Gallagher was an island native, a former lumberjack and sailor, and his twenty years as head keeper were longer than any other head keeper's at the station.[^2] During his thirty years at the light he left the island four times, three for relatives' funerals and once for an operation; when he retired in 1932 he planned to see more of the country by cruising down Lake Michigan and the Mississippi to the Gulf.[^2] The Beaver Island Archive says the Library of Congress catalog credits a "Gallagher, Dominic" as performer on eight items recorded at St. James on 24 to 27 August 1938, and identifies him as the island-born keeper, while admitting it has not settled his exact dates.[^16] Lighthousefriends also tells a family story from 1873, when Dominick was six: his father, also Dominick, was warned off a lake crossing in a gale by a Captain Roddy, and the next morning learned that the ship he had meant to sail on was lost. His wife and children, thinking him aboard, had already held a wake for him.[^2]

The Coast Guard took over the Lighthouse Service in 1939. Crews of seamen were then assigned to the station, and the three civilian keepers there all resigned within a year.[^3] The archive's list for the 1940s and 1950s runs through Alexander Durette (1940 to 1941), Joseph J. Stodola (1942 to 1944) and Bertrand J. McDonough, who is listed as keeper from at least 1950 until 1958.[^1] Pepper dates the telephone line to the station to 1953, but the archive cites a Charlevoix Courier report of February 1943 in which Coast Guardsmen followed the line to Beaver Head to repair it.[^3][^14] McDonough retired in September 1961 after thirty-three and a half years of service and received the Coast Guard's Albert Gallatin award, according to a notice the archive summarizes.[^15]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources give a few dated storm damages at the station itself. In the hard winter of 1885-86, ice on the lake shifted the boat landing, and a crew had to come and move it back; they lengthened it by 50 feet (15 m) with a stone-filled timber crib at the outer end. A storm the following November destroyed the boathouse and its ways, and a crew returned in 1887 to replace them and to put a second crib offshore.[^3]

The fog signal's work is recorded in hours. In the Light-House Board's reports the siren ran some 249 hours in the report for the fiscal year ended June 1892, some 367 hours in the 1899 report, and only some 112 in the 1901 report.[^6][^7][^8] On 12 September 1894 it was disabled, and was repaired on 21 September.[^9]

## After the keepers

In 1962 the Coast Guard decommissioned the light, removed the lens and illuminant, and replaced the light with an automated radiobeacon, which stood just north of the lighthouse by one account and to the east by another.[^14][^2][^3] McDonough's 1961 retirement notice, however, says the station "became automatic" in 1958 and that he stayed on as caretaker, and the Archive suggests a light can run unattended before the lens is formally removed.[^15]

The accounts differ on who used the buildings: one says the station was leased to four people and served as a hunting lodge, and the Historical Society says a private owner ran it as a hunting club between 1963 and 1970.[^2][^13] The Archive records a lease to three Charlevoix men as a hunting lodge in June 1960.[^15] The federal government declared the station surplus in December 1972, and in 1975 it was deeded to Charlevoix Public Schools for $1.[^2] When restoration work began, almost every window and door was broken and the interior walls were peppered with gunshot holes.[^2] Under Comprehensive Employment and Training Act funding, teenagers lived in tents at the site in the summers of 1978 to 1980 and helped restore it.[^2] The Beaver Island Lighthouse School, which took students aged sixteen to twenty-one who had left or were at risk of leaving school, ran at the station until funding was lost; it announced in July 2016 that it would close.[^2] A Clean Michigan Initiative grant of $257,813 and a Save America's Treasures grant of $150,000 paid for restoring the lighthouse and fog signal building in 2003 and 2004.[^2] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 29 December 1978, with five contributing buildings.[^14]

In 2019 Charlevoix County and Networks Northwest bought the property from Charlevoix Public Schools for $215,000.[^2] The Beaver Island Historical Society, working with the new owners, planned to bring Eastern Michigan University's historic-preservation field school to the island in summer 2022 to assess what the station needs.[^13]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by Charlevoix County and Networks Northwest, with the Beaver Island Historical Society involved in its care and restoration.[^13] The Historical Society says the tower is open to the public and that restoration is still going on; lighthousefriends.com reported the grounds and tower open and the dwelling closed.[^13][^2] A fourth-order Fresnel lens is on display inside the lighthouse. Lighthousefriends gives two different guesses about where it served: one account says it may have been used at South Fox Island lighthouse, while a sign near the lens says it was reportedly used at the William Livingstone light on Belle Isle near Detroit.[^2] Beaver Island is reached by ferry from Charlevoix or by air, and transport to the light can be rented on the island or arranged on an island tour.[^2][^3] Opening arrangements change, so check with the Historical Society before going, and keep to the parts of the site that are open.
