Leading Point Light was the rear light of a range that guided ships up the Brewerton Channel into Baltimore Harbor.[^1] Its keeper lived in a brick house with a lantern on the roof, and the light was first shown on the evening of 1 November 1868.[^1][^2][^3] In 1924 the light was electrified, the keeper's post ended, and a skeleton tower took over the light.[^4][^5]

## The place

The Brewerton Channel was marked by two lights. Congress appropriated $30,000 for them on 28 July 1866.[^5] The channel took its name from Henry Brewerton, an engineer who had worked on the defenses of Baltimore Harbor.[^1] The rear light stood on a high bluff on the south shore of the Patapsco River, about a mile from the front light at Hawkins Point, which was built on a shoal in six feet (2 m) of water.[^5] On the true course, the three lights "will be seen in line, one above the other."[^3]

Trees were a problem for the range.[^6][^13] In July 1891 the brush and trees were removed from a strip 65 feet (20 m) wide across Hawkins Point, "to open the range for Brewerton Channel formed by these lights."[^6] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1897 the Board recorded trees and underbrush cut down at Hawkins Point, where they had "obscured the range between this and the Leading Point light."[^13] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 records a $3,500 jury award for an easement between Hawkins Point and the Leading Point light, paid to the owner.[^7] The Chesapeake Chapter says a federal quarantine station stood not far off from the 1870s to the 1960s, where ships from foreign ports were inspected.[^5]

## Building the light

The rear light was a brick dwelling with a lantern on its roof.[^3][^5] Its light showed 40 feet (12 m) above the ground and 70 feet (21 m) above ordinary tides, according to the Board's 1868 report as LighthouseFriends prints it.[^3] The Chesapeake Chapter says it burned fourth-order Franklin lamps exhibiting a fixed white light, and that an unusual black ball protruded through the roof as its day signal.[^5]

The Board's reports record small works. In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1893 two new outbuildings were built, one 14 by 30 feet (4 by 9 m) in plan and the other 4 by 5 feet (1.2 by 1.5 m).[^12] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1897 an iron oil house was erected.[^13]

In the Board's reports for the fiscal years ended 30 June 1900 and 1901, the station appears under the heading "Day or unlighted beacons."[^8][^9] In August 1899 the anchor of one of the guys on its mast was reinforced.[^8] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 the Board reported that "A rotten guy post was replaced," and that the mast supporting the ball was "repaired and painted."[^9] In the same report a new plank walk, some 30 running feet (9 m) long, was laid in front of the dwelling.[^14]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists nine principal-keeper stints from 1868 to 1924, held by eight men.[^10] Thomas S. Hamlin was the first, from 1868 to 1869. Van Buren Ijams kept the light for ten years, from 1872 to 1882.[^10] William Raabe kept it from 1894 to 1909 and again from 1910 to 1919, with Frederick Raabe in charge between those stints.[^10]

On 16 April 1915 Raabe became keeper of both range lights, the front light at Hawkins Point and this one. The Chapter says his salary rose from $520 to $676 a year for tending both.[^5][^1] The last keeper was Edward Farrow, at least from 1919 to 1924.[^5][^10] The archive's record for Farrow also names him as principal keeper at Lower Cedar Point, on the Potomac.[^11]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In mid-January 1873 the English steamer Arcadian was struck by a huge ice field and sank near Fort Carroll, according to the German-language Baltimore paper Der Deutsche Correspondent.[^15] The paper says the revenue cutter James Guthrie found her in the channel between Hawkins' Point and Leading Point, about 300 feet (90 m) from shore, with her bow under water.[^15] The crew, the paper says, had packed their belongings, in case they had to take to the boats.[^15] (Rendered into English from the German.)

The paper says that when war broke out, the lights were taken out of service so the enemy could not learn the line of the channel.[^16] On 24 July 1898 the same paper reported that the lights were back in service, Leading Point among them, under the heading "Das Licht leuchtet wieder" ("The light shines again").[^16] The paper does not name the war. Its date fits the Spanish–American War of 1898; the link is an inference, not the paper's.[^16]

## After the keepers

The Bulletin's 1925 account of the change says the automatic electric apparatus ended the keeper's services and allowed the dwelling "to be sold or transferred to the Public Health Service."[^4] The rear light's candlepower rose from 2,500 to 40,000, and its focal plane was raised "to clear all trees."[^4] A skeleton pipe tower 55 feet (17 m) high went up "in front of the former tower and dwelling."[^4]

## Today

The Lighthouse Directory lists the rear range light as active. It is a skeletal tower with a continuous green light, located on Quarantine Road on Hawkins Point, and the Coast Guard is the owner.[^2] The site and tower are closed, but the light can be seen from the road.[^2][^3] The Directory adds that the portion of the Curtis Bay Yard that included the light station was declared surplus in 1956 and became the site of a gypsum wallboard plant.[^2]

The sources disagree on what became of the brick house. The 1925 Bulletin describes the new tower as standing in front of the former tower and dwelling.[^4] The Directory says the brick house was demolished in 1924.[^2] The Chesapeake Chapter says the dwelling is demolished and the light moved beside the old foundation.[^5][^1] The archive's record reads "Torn Down 1924."[^10] The two descriptions of the light also differ: the Chapter gives the 1868 light as fixed white, and the Directory gives the present light as continuous green.[^5][^2]
