Pipe Island Light is a brick tower on Pipe Island, in the St. Marys River in Michigan.[^1] The island sits at the north end of DeTour Passage, about four miles above the mouth of the river.[^1][^2] The light was first shown in 1888, and the island is privately owned today.[^1][^2]

## The place

The island is also known as Round Island, for its shape.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1891 records a $30,000 act of 3 March 1891 for lights and ranges on the St. Marys River, from Pipe Island to Sault Ste. Marie.[^3] LighthouseFriends notes that the island's light could be lined up with Frying Pan Light, two miles away, to make a range for ships entering and leaving DeTour Passage.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $10,000 for a light on the island on 3 March 1885. The deed to the island was finalized on 2 November 1885, following "much trouble and delay."[^1]

A contract for the tower, keeper's dwelling and boathouse, with a five-month deadline from 11 July 1887, was awarded.[^1] The octagonal tower was built of buff brick on a cut-limestone foundation and was capped by an octagonal cast-iron lantern. LighthouseFriends gives its height as 32.5 feet (9.9 m). The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 27 feet (8.2 m) from the base of the structure to the centre of the lantern, and this story does not reconcile the two figures.[^1][^2] Its fifth-order Henry-Lepaute Fresnel lens gave a fixed red light from a focal plane of 37.5 feet (11.4 m).[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends quotes the Board's 1888 report: the station was lighted on the night of 12 May 1888.[^1]

The tower was painted red at first.[^1] The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1894 records that the color was changed from red to white, to render the light more efficient as a day range with Frying Pan Island light.[^4] LighthouseFriends dates the repainting to 1895.[^1] The Board's report is the earlier of the two dates.

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1897 says the Detour range lights on Frying Pan and Pipe Island should be increased in power, and that Pipe Island should be made a flashing light. Both were then fixed red, of the fifth and sixth order.[^5]

## The keepers

Amos P. Foster kept the light from 1887 to 1891.[^2] Frank Bernard came in 1891, after nine years a few miles downriver at Frying Pan Light.[^1] He kept Pipe Island until he died at the station on 25 August 1910, aged 68.[^1][^2] For a while afterward, passing captains blew their horns to show respect.[^1]

John H. Malone came from Menagerie Island Light in Lake Superior, where he had served for 32 years, and kept Pipe Island until 1918.[^1][^2] William Pottinger is listed to 1919.[^2]

Norman P. Hawkins began in 1904 as an assistant keeper at Outer Island Light in Wisconsin. He also served at Sand Island Light, also in Wisconsin, and made two stints each at Crisp Point and DeTour Reef.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he was placed in charge of Pipe Island in 1919 or 1920, and the archive gives 1919.[^1][^2] He held the station until his death in April 1937.[^1][^2] Hawkins was also a photographer, and his pictures of passing boats and wildlife could be found on display around Detour for many years.[^1] The family spent their summers on the island, where they lived without electricity and running water.[^1] James Brander kept the station from 1937 to 1948.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

At midnight on the night of 13 May 1917, the downbound steamer Pentecost Mitchell, loaded with ore, collided with the upbound freighter Saxona at the mouth of the St. Marys River, according to two newspapers: the Richmond Palladium of Indiana and the Bismarck Tribune of North Dakota. Both reported that the steamers sank in about 40 feet of water and that the crews were saved.[^9][^10]

LighthouseFriends dates the collision to the early morning of 14 May. It places the collision in a dense fog near Pipe Island, which lies about four miles above the river's mouth, and says both steamers were raised, repaired and returned to service.[^1] The newspapers and LighthouseFriends differ on the place, and this story does not settle it. LighthouseFriends also says the Lighthouse Bureau placed two spar buoys with red lights to mark an alternate channel on the other side of the island.[^1]

## After the keepers

A 1919 issue of the Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists Pipe Island among the lights established: flashing white, with a lens lantern and acetylene.[^6] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1920 says that one appropriation covered changing the Cedar Point Range Light, Pipe Island and Frying Pan Island lights to nonattended acetylene. It also mentions two new beacons to mark a new upbound channel recently established at Pipe Island.[^7]

On 21 October 1920 the focal plane was raised to 52 feet. The lantern room was removed, and the light was shown from a fifteen-foot skeleton tower set on the brick tower. On 17 November the light was returned to its earlier fixed red.[^1] During the fiscal year ended 30 June 1921, the Commissioner's report says, the work at Pipe Island and Frying Pan Island was completed.[^8]

In 1937 Pipe Island Light was changed to an electric light that flashed red twice every five seconds. LighthouseFriends says it was then listed as unwatched, although James Brander kept the station until his retirement in 1948.[^1][^2]

## Today

The US Lighthouse Society's record says Pipe Island is not an active aid to navigation.[^2] The island is privately owned. Its grounds, dwelling and tower are closed, and LighthouseFriends says it is best seen from the water.[^1]
