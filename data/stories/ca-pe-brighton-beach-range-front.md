The Brighton Beach Front Range Light stands on a crib in the North (Yorke) River, where it meets Charlottetown Harbour on Prince Edward Island. It was built to guide ships through a narrow channel between Hillsborough Bay and the harbour, and it still works as one of a pair of range lights.[^1][^3]

## The place

The light sits at the point where the North (Yorke) River joins Charlottetown Harbour.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the range lights helped mariners hold a thirty-foot channel through Hillsborough Bay and into the harbour.[^2] The heritage register says the light helped ease harbour traffic and supported the city's maritime economy through the late nineteenth century and into the twentieth.[^1]

## Building the light

Work on the first range lights began in 1889. Lighthouse Friends says they went into service in 1890, when general navigation opened. They were fixed-red lanterns hoisted on masts, with diamond-shaped slatted daymarks on each mast.[^2] The register gives 1889 as the date of the front light, and describes its present form as a square, tapered wooden tower of a type built across Canada in the second half of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.[^1] Sources do not settle which date applies to the wooden tower that stands now.

In 1892 the masts were replaced by skeleton towers, built under contract by James Hanrahan for $383.90. Their upper parts were enclosed and topped with wooden lanterns.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives the front tower as 12.2 m (40 ft) high. The Coast Guard's list now gives 12.5 m.[^2][^3] In 1895 a red diamond twenty feet tall was painted on the channel faces of both towers, to make them more visible by day.[^2]

The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1906 says a foundation had been started for the front range lighthouse at Brighton Beach.[^5] Lighthouse Friends describes that base as a cribwork of seven tiers of hemlock, twenty feet square, built in 1907 at a cost of $139.86.[^2] The Department's report for the year ended 31 March 1909 says a 25 mm diamond vapour installation replaced the duplex lamp at the front light.[^6]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends is the main source for the keepers named here. James Galvin was the first keeper, at $100 a year.[^2] When he died his family was paid two months of his salary, $16.66, as a gratuity.[^2] James Lewis took over on 1 March 1899 at the same salary.[^2] The Department's keepers' table for the year ended 30 June 1903 gives the same appointment date.[^7] Francis W. Hughes served from 1909 to 1911, Joseph Purcell from 1911 to 1936, Bonar Dalziel from 1936 to 1942, and John Francis Duffy from 1942 to 1952.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 11 October 1900 a heavy gale blew down the rear range tower. The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1900 says that a temporary light was shown at once from a lantern hoisted between two poles.[^4] Lighthouse Friends gives the same date and the same temporary light.[^2]

In the winter of 2000 a storm surge severely damaged the front light. Lighthouse Friends says the top 6 m (20 ft) of the tower was cut off and set down in a nearby yard, while the bottom was taken out and rebuilt.[^2]

## After the keepers

The front light was moved in 1919 and again in 1926, when it was given a new concrete foundation.[^2] Its rear partner's wooden tower was destroyed by fire around 1930. The concrete tower that replaced it was completed on 24 December 1968.[^2]

The lights were electrified in 1952, when Duffy retired. A. A. LeClair was then appointed caretaker at $50 a year.[^2]

In a letter of 3 September 1924, the writer L. M. Montgomery described a summer bungalow on the south side, where the North River meets Hillsborough Bay. It stood between two range lights, she wrote, that "burned enchantingly through the twilights, pearl white against the ethereal skies."[^2]

In 2009 the Canadian Coast Guard proposed a concrete slab against ice, a shorter tower, vinyl siding and a new deck for the front light. Charlottetown's council rejected the plan, arguing it would damage the structure's historical integrity.[^2] In June 2012 the council accepted the only bid for the restoration, $146,700 from Ostridge Brothers, and the work was done that summer.[^2] On 12 September 2013 a ceremony transferred the front range from federal ownership to the City of Charlottetown.[^2]

## Today

The City of Charlottetown owns the front range light.[^2] It is listed in the Canadian Register of Historic Places.[^1] The Coast Guard's list still shows it as a working light: fixed yellow, with a focal height of 11.0 m and a range of 19 nautical miles, operating 24 hours a day. The list describes a white pyramid tower with a red upper part and a red vertical stripe, on a crib on the east side of the Yorke River. The rear light is listed as well.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says the grounds and the tower are closed.[^2] It stands on a crib in the river, so treat it as a working light and see it from the shore.
