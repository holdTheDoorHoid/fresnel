Lubec Channel Light stands in the water of the channel at Lubec, Maine. Its tower rises from a cast-iron base built in 1890, and it is one of three surviving "spark plug" lights in Maine.[^1] It was automated in 1939, has been privately owned since a 2007 auction, and is not open to the public.[^2][^10]

## The place

The narrow waterway, which one history calls the Lubec Narrows, lies between Lubec and Campobello Island, New Brunswick, and was dredged in the early 1880s.[^10] The National Register nomination calls the river ports of Calais, Eastport and Lubec "thriving commercial centers" whose livelihood depended on navigation on the Saint Croix River and the Lubec Channel.[^1] The nomination says the Light-House Board's 1883 annual report mentions the need for a light "in order to make the channel of value to commerce at night."[^1]

The nomination describes the site as "a submerged rock ledge in the Lubec Channel," and the light stands on it.[^1] Dredging went on after the light was built. In August 1896 a Maine notice in the *Portland Daily Press* reported that the Western Bar beacon, south of the light, had been removed "in consequence of dredging operations," and that the engineers were "widening Lubec Channel."[^3]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $40,000 in the summer of 1886 and $12,000 more in 1888, which makes the $52,000 that the nomination gives as the total by 1888.[^10][^1] A final appropriation of $15,500 finished the work by the end of 1890.[^10] Borings in 1887 went down into blue clay at the shoal.[^10]

New England Lighthouses quotes the Light-House Board's 1890 annual report on the design: a cast-iron cylinder 33 feet (10 m) across and 48 feet (15 m) high, made in eight sections, to be sunk 6 feet (1.8 m) into the site, filled with concrete, and topped by an iron tower 37 feet (11 m) from base to focal plane.[^10] The builders took advantage of the tide's range, 17 to 21 feet (5 to 6 m), to float the two lower sections to the site and sink them.[^10]

On 29 August 1889 water burst in through the subsoil inside the caisson and interrupted the work. The builders then added a sub-foundation of 185 spruce piles driven inside the cylinder. Work resumed the next April.[^10] The superstructure was completed by 5 November 1890, and the station went into operation on 31 December 1890.[^10][^1] The tower was painted brown until 1903 and white after that.[^10]

The sources disagree on the lens. New England Lighthouses says a fifth-order Fresnel lens went into the lantern. The US Lighthouse Society's record gives a fourth-order Fresnel, installed in 1890.[^10][^2] The record gives the tower as 40 feet (12 m) from base to lantern center, and the light as 53 feet (16 m) above mean high water.[^2]

A Board notice printed in January 1891 said that a fog bell would be established at the station from 31 January. In thick or foggy weather it would be "struck by machinery a single blow every ten seconds."[^5] New England Lighthouses dates the bell to the original installation, so the two accounts differ on the date.[^10]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 printed observations of how far the bell carried. The keeper's remarks say that in a south or southwest wind vessels "often hear the signal" but lose it after passing West Quoddy Head. The bell, the report says, "overhangs the water," and in a northeast wind it "can be heard distinctly in all directions."[^6]

## The keepers

The roster that the US Lighthouse Society prints lists 17 entries from 1890 to 1939: six principal keepers and eleven first assistants.[^2] Frederick W. Morong was the first principal keeper, and Loring W. Myers was his first assistant.[^2][^10] New England Lighthouses dates Myers's promotion to principal keeper to 1898. The roster dates it to 1895.[^10][^2] Myers stayed until 1923. Before he came to the light, he had been a steamboat captain.[^10][^2]

An Eastport dispatch reported Myers's retirement in October 1923. It says he was "retired a few days ago after 42 years in the service," that he entered the lighthouse service "in 1881," and that "For 32 years he was keeper in the Lubec channel lighthouse."[^4] The roster gives him 28 years as principal, and it lists no service before 1890. The two accounts do not agree, and the story does not choose between them. The dispatch also says the light "is on a sandbar," within half a mile (0.8 km) of the farmhouse at South Lubec where Myers was born, and that he "had many patents."[^4] The nomination describes a rock ledge.[^1]

New England Lighthouses says that in 1904 Myers was credited with inventing a new type of lifeboat, 16 feet (4.9 m) long. Loaded with 15 men, it sat only two or three inches (5 to 8 cm) lower in the water than when it was empty.[^10] The design was praised, but according to the same account it brought Myers no money, since steamship lines stuck with cheaper and less safe boats.[^10]

In the spring of 1899 the assistant, Herbert E. Robinson, spent an extended period on the mainland. During some of that time, according to New England Lighthouses, Myers's wife, Abbie, "served as the assistant." That history calls her the only woman keeper the station had.[^10] The roster does not list her, so the claim rests on that one source.

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 records repairs at the station. "The two inclined, movable, and rickety ladders were replaced by two vertical iron ladders bolted to the pier."[^7]

James W. Doran was first assistant from 1922 to 1923 and principal from 1923 to 1925. Parker M. Houghton was principal from 1925 to 1929. Nathaniel S. Alley had been first assistant from 1924, and he was principal from 1929 to 1934. Arthur R. Marston was the last principal keeper the roster names, from 1934 to 1939.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The *Lighthouse Service Bulletin* of 2 January 1925 reports that James W. Doran, keeper of the Lubec Channel Light Station, "assisted the occupants of a power boat which had become" disabled in the vicinity of the light station, on 25 November.[^8] The item gives no year. The issue that printed it is dated 2 January 1925, so the rescue most probably happened in November 1924.[^8]

New England Lighthouses reports a death connected with the station. Keeper Nathaniel Alley was alone on duty on 7 March 1934 when he was overcome by fumes from the coal stove. The captain of the Grand Manan ferry, which ran regularly through the area, was accustomed to hailing the man on duty; when no one appeared, he became suspicious. Alley was found and taken to Lubec for medical treatment, but he soon died.[^10] This account comes from that history alone.

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1939. New England Lighthouses says the change brought an acetylene gas system and a sun valve.[^2][^10] A solar panel has since been added to the gallery.[^1] The National Park Service's record gives 14 March 1988 as the date published for the station's National Register listing.[^9]

In 1989 the light was to be discontinued, and local residents mounted a "Save the Sparkplug" campaign.[^10] A $700,000 renovation in 1992 stabilized the foundation, which had developed a tilt over the years. The work also put new plates on the caisson and pumped in 200 cubic yards of concrete.[^10]

In 2006 the light was offered to a new steward under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000. No one applied, so in July 2007 it went to auction, where the high bid was $46,000.[^2][^10] The buyer was a private owner. The current optic is a solar-powered LED, installed in 2017.[^2]

## Today

The light is privately owned, and the US Lighthouse Society's record says it is not open to the public.[^2] Its base stands in the channel, so any approach would be by water.[^1] New England Lighthouses says the light is easy to see from many points on shore, and that the tower still has a six-degree list, though the foundation is considered stable.[^10] The tower is private property. Please do not land on it or try to climb it.
