Bonilla Island Lighthouse is a Canadian Coast Guard light station on a small island in Hecate Strait, off the northwest tip of Banks Island on British Columbia's North Coast.[^3] A light was first shown there in 1927, when an unattended beacon was set up. The staffed light station that stands today was first exhibited in 1960.[^2][^3]

## The place

Hecate Strait is a wide, shallow stretch of water between Haida Gwaii and the mainland.[^2] Most mariners followed the Inside Passage, a longer but protected route, while the strait offered a more direct one between Vancouver and Prince Rupert.[^2] Bonilla Island sits on the eastern side of the strait, and its 232 m (761 ft) summit, Dome Hill, serves mariners as a landmark.[^2]

The Department of Marine's annual report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1913 lists Bonilla Island among the new aids to navigation. It gives "preliminary expenses" for "the establishment of a lighthouse, dwelling, &c." and says work would start the next year.[^1] A 1928 report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, said the Hecate Strait route could be shortened by 100 miles on the Vancouver to Prince Rupert voyage. It named a "lighthouse with a first-class fog signal" on Bonilla Island as the aid most needed on that route.[^2]

## Building the light

The 1913 project does not appear to have produced a lighthouse.[^2] LighthouseFriends infers that it was dropped, because the first light was not placed on the island until 1927, when an unattended Aga beacon was established. That beacon had a concrete base, a white mast, a slatted wooden ball and a red lantern.[^2] The Lighthouse Directory also dates the station's establishment to 1927.[^3]

Between 1951 and 1954 Alcan built a reservoir, a hydroelectric station, a deep-water port and a townsite "to support the smelter" in the Kitimat Valley.[^2] LighthouseFriends ties the next lighthouse work to the marine traffic that the Kitimat smelter generated.[^2] In 1959 Stange Construction Company built three dwellings, a fog alarm building and a light tower on an islet just off the island, at a cost LighthouseFriends puts near $200,000.[^2]

The new light was first exhibited in 1960 from a steel tower, as a flashing white light with a red sector. A diaphone fog alarm sounded every thirty seconds in poor visibility.[^2] The Lighthouse Directory says the lighthouse "replaced a small beacon".[^3]

The round tower that stands now is 10 m (33 ft) high, with a red-painted lantern.[^3] LighthouseFriends says it went up at some point between 1982 and 1994, and no source here dates it more closely.[^2] The Canadian Coast Guard's list gives the present light as "Fl W R 5s": white, with red sectors and an obscured arc, flashing every five seconds from 36.6 m (120 ft) above the water, with a nominal range of 21 nautical miles.[^4]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends lists the head keepers from 1960 onward. The first was Henry Jabez MacArthur (1960 to 1971). The list runs to Robert D. Wilson, who held the post from 2022 and "at least 2024".[^2] Jean Beaudet served from 1979 to 1993, and Harvey Bergen from 1996 to 2013.[^2] The same LighthouseFriends page says Bergen retired in 2012, so his end date is not settled by the sources.[^2]

On 19 November 2010 Bergen gave evidence to a Senate committee in Prince Rupert as principal lightkeeper at Bonilla Island.[^5]

The committee's appendix table, drawn from Coast Guard data of April 2010, lists Bonilla Island as a remote station that is not automated, with two lightkeeping positions and permanent residents.[^5] LighthouseFriends also reports that keeper Eric Van Rooyen, besides issuing weather reports, broadcast Vancouver Canucks hockey scores for fishermen who called the station for them.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The water off the island has its own hazards. On 3 April 1923 the tug *Cape Scott* reported to Digby Island that she was "disabled with a broken tail shaft off Bonilla island and needed a tow." The *Newington* was sent to help and brought the tug and her raft in tow to a safe anchorage the next morning.[^8]

In April 1937 an Associated Press wire report, printed in the Nome Daily Nugget, said the steamer *Cordova* was still afire and had "anchored fifteen miles northwest of Bonilla Island", where she awaited the arrival of the *Swallow*. Three vessels were speeding to help her, having "braved the southeast gale" in rough seas.[^7] None of these sources says that the light or its keepers took part in either event.[^7][^8]

## Today

Bonilla Island Light is an active Coast Guard light station, which the Coast Guard's list says operates 24 hours a day.[^4] LighthouseFriends carries the keepers' list to 2024.[^2] The Coast Guard's July 2024 news release says the other twenty-five staffed light stations on the British Columbia coast will carry on as normal, and it does not name them.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is best seen from the water or the air, and its author chartered a floatplane out of Prince Rupert to photograph it.[^2] LighthouseFriends and the Lighthouse Directory both describe the grounds as open and the tower as closed.[^2][^3] The station is a working Coast Guard post where keepers live, so visitors should not land there without an invitation from the Coast Guard.[^5]
