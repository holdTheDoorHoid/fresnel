A tower with its fog-signal house stands at the outer end of the west breakwater, at the entrance to the Cuyahoga River on Lake Erie, where it has marked the main entrance to Cleveland Harbor since March 1911.[^1][^2]

## The place

Cleveland's first harbour lights were on the shore and at the end of the river pier. The breakwaters came later. According to LighthouseFriends, the west breakwater was built between 1876 and 1883, and a forty-foot-square pierhead was added at its east end in 1884.[^2] In 1885 an octagonal iron tower, moved from the west pier at Charlotte-Genesee on Lake Ontario, was set on that pierhead, and it first showed its flashing red and white light on 20 October 1885.[^2] That octagonal tower was the earlier breakwater light; the tower that stands today is a later building on the extended arm of the breakwater.[^2]

## Building the light

As the breakwaters were extended into the lake, spurs reached out beyond the old pierhead, and the Lighthouse Board asked for $45,000 in 1907 to mark them.[^2] Congress appropriated that sum on 27 May 1908, and the new West Pierhead light began operating at the eastern end of the extended west breakwater on 25 March 1911.[^2] It was a conical cast-iron tower with the keepers' quarters built into it; the old octagonal tower's fourth-order Fresnel lens was moved into it, and the octagonal tower was given a smaller light.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's archive records the tower's materials as cast iron, brick and wood, on a concrete foundation, with the quarters integral to the tower.[^1] Sources differ on the year of construction: the General Services Administration's 2026 release and a 2023 news report say 1910, while the US Lighthouse Society's archive gives 1911, the year the light was established.[^7][^8][^1] Materials are described differently too: LighthouseFriends calls it a cast-iron tower, the archive lists cast iron, brick and wood, and the GSA describes a brick tower sheathed in wood.[^2][^1][^7]

The fog signal came separately. In 1890 a ten-inch steam whistle replaced the fog bell on the older breakwater station, after Congress had appropriated $5,200 for it in March 1889.[^6][^2] When the main light moved out to the extended arm, the signal followed it. The Commissioner of Lighthouses explained that once the breakwater arms were finished and the main light stood on the west arm, it "became necessary to remove the fog signal to the same location" to get the best efficiency and to concentrate the keepers' duties.[^4] Congress had appropriated $17,600 for this on 22 October 1913.[^5] The new building was a rectangular steel structure 29 by 31 feet (8.8 by 9.4 m) and 24 feet (7.3 m) high, joined to the tower by a passageway (LighthouseFriends gives 29 by 30 feet), and its type "F" diaphone worked by compressed air gave two blasts every 30 seconds.[^4] It went into commission on 18 August 1916.[^4]

## The keepers

Frederick T. Hatch is the best-documented keeper of the breakwater. The Life-Saving Service's report for 1891 describes him as having served earlier in the Cleveland life-saving crew, and it credits him with "remarkable gallantry" in a rescue from the sinking schooner *Sophia Minch* in October 1883.[^3] By the time of the wreck described below he was keeper of the light on the breakwater.[^3] The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists him as principal keeper at the Main Entrance station from 1911 to 1914.[^1]

Charles E. Perry followed him as principal keeper from 1914 to 1941, and Samuel E. Crozier from 1941 to 1949.[^1] Perry had been on the new station from its first year: the roster has him as second assistant in 1911-1912 and first assistant in 1912-1914.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1912 to 1917 lists "Perry, Charles E., keeper Cleveland Harbor light station wins efficiency flag", on page 250 of the Bulletin.[^9] After Crozier the archive's list of principal keepers has a gap until 1963, then names George A. Dameron (1963), William C. Ekmark (1964) and Mervin L. Hamilton (1965); LighthouseFriends also lists Coast Guard crew for 1963 to 1965.[^1][^2] These lists give only years, not stories.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the night of Sunday, 26 October 1890, the schooner barge *Wahnapitae*, of Port Huron, Michigan, was totally wrecked against the west breakwater about half a mile from the Cleveland life-saving station.[^3] She had been in tow of the steamer *John M. Nicol*, bound from Ashland, Wisconsin, to Fairport, Ohio, with nearly two million feet of lumber, and eight people were aboard, including the captain's wife, who acted as cook.[^3] The Life-Saving Service's report says the night was overcast and dark and that a strong gale blew from the north directly onshore.[^3] The steamer cast off the tow, the schooner anchored off the breakwater, dragged, and struck the east end of the breakwater with a crash; her people jumped onto the breakwater and made for the lighthouse, about a hundred feet from where she lay.[^3]

Hatch, then keeper of the breakwater light, took in some of them and, when the waves kept him from reaching the rest, got into his boat, rowed along under the lee of the cribwork and picked off the captain's wife and one of the seamen.[^3] Before he had pulled a dozen strokes a huge wave swamped the boat and threw all three into the water. The report says that, on setting out, he had taken the precaution of tying one end of a small line to the cribwork near the tower and dropping the other into the boat, and that he pulled himself and the woman to the ladder by it.[^3] He then sheltered four people, including the captain and his wife, until morning, when the life-saving crew took them ashore.[^3] One of the crew, Orla W. Smith, of Oswego, New York, was drowned.[^3] The report says Hatch "deserves praise for his undaunted behavior on this occasion," and he was later awarded a gold bar for saving life.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the bar went with a gold lifesaving medal he already held.[^2]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends reports that the light was electrified in 1931, that a radiobeacon was established near it on 25 June 1936, and that the station was automated in 1965.[^2] The fog signal's deep note was nicknamed the "cow", according to the same source.[^2] The same account says the Fresnel lens was removed about 1995 and given to Cleveland's Great Lakes Science Center, though a 2023 news report quoting the National Register description says the light still shines through a Fresnel lens made in Paris in 1884.[^2][^8] The sources disagree, and which is right is not settled here.

## Today

The lighthouse is a federal property, in the hands of the General Services Administration, and is listed on the National Register of Historic Places and is a City of Cleveland landmark.[^7] It can only be reached by boat, and the archive records it as not open to the public.[^8][^1] In 2023 the GSA auctioned it; the high bid was $425,000 and the auction closed on 6 September.[^8] The buyers did not complete the purchase and the lighthouse stayed with the GSA.[^2] On 16 April 2026 the GSA announced that eligible public bodies and non-profits could take it at no cost under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act.[^7] LighthouseFriends suggests viewing it from a harbour cruise, with more distant views from the lakefront.[^2]
