The Manistee North Pierhead Light stands at the far end of the north pier, where the Manistee River meets Lake Michigan in Manistee County, Michigan.[^1] Its cast-iron tower dates from 1927, though one record says 1925, and the sources give its height as 38 feet (11.6 m) or 39 feet (about 12 m).[^1][^2][^8] A light has marked this river mouth since 1870, and the lights moved between the shore and the two piers more than once before they settled on the north pier in 1894.[^1][^3]

## The place

The river mouth needed work. The Historic Structures Report says the improvements were needed "to keep the river mouth free of sand and silt and to handle the ever increasing amount of ship traffic that traveled up the river to the various sawmills."[^3] Local citizens had already built a pair of short stub piers to stem the sand and silt.[^3][^7] In 1861 an Army Corps of Engineers survey, ordered by Congress, found a channel of seven to eight feet (2.1 to 2.4 m) between the piers, and a 250-foot (76 m) sandbar with less than five feet (1.5 m) of water over it, lying 600 feet (183 m) off the ends of the piers.[^7][^3] Pepper and the Historic Structures Report both say Congress approved money for the channel and a lighthouse in 1867.[^7][^3]

The first lighthouse site was on the north side of the river, near the shoreline and not at the end of a pier.[^3] The Historic Structures Report says a survey crew selected it in the summer of 1867.[^3] Pepper dates a survey of the site to 1876, which is after construction began in 1869.[^7] The first U.S. Life-Saving Service station at Manistee was set up in 1878 on that same north side of the river.[^3]

## Building the light

Construction of the first light began in 1869 on a two-story wood-frame house with a light tower attached.[^3] Its navigation light, a Fifth Order Fresnel lens, was shown at the opening of the navigation season in spring 1870.[^3] LighthouseFriends credits the keeper, Octavius W. Barney, with displaying it at that opening.[^2]

The sources do not agree on the year Congress first approved money for the work. LighthouseFriends gives a $10,000 allocation on July 28, 1866; Terry Pepper and the Historic Structures Report both say 1867.[^2][^7][^3]

A fire in the autumn of 1871 destroyed the new light about eighteen months after it was first shown.[^3] Congress gave $10,000 for a replacement in May 1872.[^2] The Historic Structures Report says the replacement was a duplicate of the original, and that a temporary light was exhibited in the fall of 1872. A permanent replacement lens was installed in 1873.[^3]

In 1875 the piers were extended 150 feet (46 m) into the lake, and a pierhead beacon went up at the outer end of the south pier.[^3][^7] The fifth-order lens was moved out to it, and the shore light was discontinued.[^7][^3] The old house stayed on as the keeper's dwelling, so the keeper had to row across the river to reach the light on the pier.[^3][^7] Pepper says the keeper, John McKee, rowed across to show the new light on the night of October 15, 1875.[^3][^7][^2] In 1879 the south pier was extended 156 feet (48 m), and the beacon was moved out to its new end.[^3][^7]

The south pier got a fog signal in 1889 and 1890.[^3][^2] The Historic Structures Report gives the appropriation as $5,000 in 1889, and Pepper says Congress approved the requested $5,000 on March 2, 1889.[^3][^7] LighthouseFriends gives the sum as $5,500.[^3][^7][^2] A ten-inch (25 cm) steam whistle was established on the pier on February 10, 1890.[^2]

The big change came in 1894. The Historic Structures Report's narrative says the shore light was reactivated in 1893, but its own chronology gives 1894, and Pepper and LighthouseFriends give June 18, 1894.[^3][^7][^2] The difficulty of tending that light across the river, as well as the lights and fog signal on the south pier, led to a plan to move every navigation aid to the north pier.[^3] In the spring of 1894 the fog-signal building was taken apart on the south pier and rebuilt on a new foundation on the north pier, and the pierhead light moved with it. LighthouseFriends dates the transfer to June 18, 1894.[^3][^2] The Lighthouse Board's report for that year says the "pierhead light and fog signal were transferred from the south" to the north pier.[^4][^3]

The new arrangement ran the lantern out along the pier on a conduit, an elevated tunnel carried on wooden trestles, with the light moved along rails by a pulley system.[^3] The Board described the lantern as one that "is run in and out" along it.[^4]

The conduit proved unsatisfactory, and in 1900 it was removed.[^3] The Board's report says the fog-signal building was moved "some 260 feet lakeward" and given a new lantern.[^5] The Historic Structures Report's narrative says instead that the north pier was extended 260 feet (79 m) and the fog-signal building moved to its new end.[^3] The Board's report for fiscal 1900 says the light "was changed on June 1, 1900, from a lens-lantern light to a sixth-order 180° lens." LighthouseFriends gives June 9 for the new sixth-order light.[^5][^2]

In 1914 construction was completed on the south breakwater, with a skeletal light near its outer edge, and the south pier was shortened to its present length.[^3] LighthouseFriends dates that skeletal tower to December 1915, after a temporary post lantern.[^2] The lights were electrified in 1925.[^2][^3]

The present tower was built in 1927 at the end of the north pier.[^3][^2] The fog-signal building and the old wooden walkway were taken down.[^2][^3] The lantern from the old shore house was installed on the new tower, and a fifth-order lens was installed in it.[^3][^2][^8] The Historic Structures Report says the upper sections of the new tower were relocated from Chicago's South Breakwater Tower, but adds that "conclusive evidence of this remains to be discovered."[^3] The USLHS record gives 1925 for the tower, not 1927.[^1]

## The keepers

Octavius W. Barney was the first keeper, from 30 December 1869 to 24 April 1871, according to the Historic Structures Report's table.[^3][^2]

John McKee followed and served until 26 October 1875, through the fire and the rebuilding.[^3] The Historic Structures Report's table calls him "Caretaker during rebuild." LighthouseFriends says the Lighthouse Board noted his "commendable energy" in setting up a temporary light within a few days of the fire.[^2][^3] McKee was removed on October 26, 1875. William King became acting keeper on 9 December 1875 and served until 1883.[^7][^3] King was followed by John H. Roberts, from 1883 to 1888.[^2][^3]

The sources do not agree on when Thomas Robinson began. The USLHS roster starts him in 1894 and ends him in 1906.[^1] LighthouseFriends and the Historic Structures Report both date his start to 1888. The report's table gives 20 March 1888 and an end on 31 May 1906.[^2][^3] All three end him in 1906.[^1][^2][^3]

Milton McClure took the head keeper's post in 1906 and kept it until 1923.[^1][^2] The Historic Structures Report's table also gives 1923 for his end, but marks that date as unsure.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he was given an efficiency pennant for 1915, and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin's cumulative index lists the station's efficiency flag and McClure's name on adjacent pages.[^2][^10]

An assistant was assigned to the station in 1890, and a second assistant was added in 1896.[^2] Frederick A. Hinman, a second assistant, is listed from 1896 to 1909.[^1][^3] The USLHS roster also lists a laborer, Johanna Robinson, for 1903.[^1]

Wallace S. Hall was head keeper from 1923 to 1941 on the USLHS roster and in LighthouseFriends.[^1][^2] The Historic Structures Report's table ends him in 1939 and marks that date as unsure.[^1][^2][^3] LighthouseFriends says he was a skilled carver, and that a model he crafted of the lighthouse tender *Sumac* was shown in the lighthouse exhibit at the Chicago World's Fair in 1933.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The fire that destroyed the first light came in the autumn of 1871.[^3] LighthouseFriends dates it to October 8, the same day as the Great Chicago Fire, and quotes one account of the fire: "Down from the circling hills on the lake shore pounced the devouring monster."[^2] Pepper gives the date as October 6.[^7] The Historic Structures Report says only that the fire came in the fall of that year.[^3]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1899 records that the fog-signal house at Manistee was damaged by the schooner *Smith and Post*, of Ashtabula, Ohio, and was then repaired.[^6]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1894 gives the fog signal's working time: the two ten-inch steam whistles ran "some 459 hours" that year.[^11]

## After the keepers

In 1939 the Bureau of Lighthouses and the Life-Saving Service were combined into the Coast Guard, which took over the Manistee navigation aids.[^3] The Historic Structures Report says that, with increased automation and declining budgets and personnel, the care of the tower and walkway declined.[^3]

In 1990 the elevated walkway was considered surplus property, and ownership of the catwalk went to the City of Manistee.[^3] The north pier was listed on the National Register of Historic Places that year.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the catwalk was refurbished in the early 1990s through a "Save the Catwalk" campaign, spearheaded by the local Chamber of Commerce.[^2]

The older shore light was put out in 1927, when its lantern was moved to the new tower.[^3][^8] LighthouseFriends says the old house was moved in 1993 to the western end of Second Avenue, where it is now a private residence.[^2][^8]

The Coast Guard declared the North Pierhead Light surplus in 2008.[^3] The Historic Structures Report gives 2010 as the year ownership went to the City of Manistee. LighthouseFriends and the Lighthouse Directory give a ceremony and transfer on June 30, 2011.[^3][^2][^8]

The state granted $20,000 in 2013 to fund a historic structures report on the restoration.[^8] The Historic Structures Report is dated 1 September 2016 and was prepared for the Manistee County Historical Museum, which had retained its consultant in 2014.[^3] The sources do not say whether that is the same study. Sources also differ on when the exterior was repainted in 2016: the Lighthouse Directory says spring, and the Historic Structures Report says summer.[^8][^3]

In January 2025 the Manistee City Council transferred management of the light to the Sable Points Lighthouse Keepers Association, which the Lighthouse Directory says is now called Lakeshore Keepers.[^8] Lakeshore Keepers says it acquired the light that month.[^9]

## Today

The tower is owned by the City of Manistee, and Lakeshore Keepers manages its operation and restoration.[^9] The light remains an active aid to navigation, and Lakeshore Keepers says it opened to the public for the first time in 2026.[^1][^8][^9]

Visitors walk the north pier from Fifth Avenue Beach, about a quarter mile (400 m), to reach the light.[^9] Tower climbs cost $8 for adults and $5 for anyone 17 or younger, and are free for active-duty service members and veterans. Climbers must be at least 40 inches (102 cm) tall, and children may not be carried up the stairs.[^9] Restrooms are at the Fifth Avenue Beach concession stand, not at the light.[^9] The light is closed for the 2026 season and reopens on May 21, 2027, according to the same listing. Check the Lakeshore Keepers page before you go, since hours and fees can change.[^9]

The original shore house is a private residence and is not open to the public.[^8][^2]
