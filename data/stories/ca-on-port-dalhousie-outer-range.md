The outer range light at Port Dalhousie stands at the end of the east pier, where the Welland Canal once met Lake Ontario, in St. Catharines, in the Regional Municipality of Niagara. Its square wooden tower was built in the late 1870s, and it is the front light of a pair that guided ships into the canal. It still shows a light.[^1]

## The place

Port Dalhousie was the Lake Ontario end of the Welland Canal from 1829 until 1932, when the canal's entrance moved about four kilometres east to Port Weller.[^2] A lighthouse was marking the harbour by 1833, when funds were provided for nails and wicking for it.[^1] In early 1837 a severe gale destroyed that lighthouse and injured most of the east pier, which needed 380 m (1,245 ft) of rebuilding. A frame lighthouse was hastily built in its place.[^1]

In 1850 the east pier was capped with a circular wooden lighthouse 13.4 m (44 ft) tall. Its lantern held ten lamps and reflectors, and LighthouseFriends says it was hailed as one of the best on Lake Ontario.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1878 a square wooden tower was built at the end of a new pier, about 91 m (300 ft) north of the main light, to form a range for entering the canal.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates it to 1878. The Canadian register of heritage places describes the front range as built in the 1870s and 1880s.[^3]

In 1893 the main light's cribwork foundation was found to be settling. William H. Noble, the Department's foreman of works, built a new foundation of a circular steel casing filled with cement. When the lighthouse was about to be moved onto that foundation, it was found to be in such poor condition that a new tower was deemed necessary. The old lantern and apparatus were reused, at a total cost of $2,157.51.[^1] In 1896 the Department of Railways and Canals replaced the decayed cribwork on top of the east pier with concrete, and the Department of Marine built a concrete foundation for the outer range light.[^1]

The Department's report for 1900, printed in 1901, says the light at Port Dalhousie still remained fixed red, and that arrangements for the proposed occulting light had not yet been completed.[^4] On 1 June 1901, the Department reported, the light in the back range tower changed from fixed red to occulting white.[^5] LighthouseFriends gives the change as 1 June 1902, when the range lights were electrified.[^2]

In 1908 a diaphone fog alarm was installed in the front range lighthouse at the outer end of the east pier. It sounded a four-second blast every thirty seconds.[^7][^1] In 1912 an oil engine replaced the fog alarm's electric plant.[^2]

## The keepers

Jonathan Woodall had kept the light for more than twenty years when he retired in 1872. William Woodall followed him until 1879.[^1]

Captain David Hunter kept the light from 1879 to 1907.[^1] The Department's keeper list, printed in its 1903 report, pairs his name with Port Dalhousie.[^6] Bernard J. McGrath followed him from 1907 to 1912, and George Houston from 1912 to 1923. L. Houston served in 1923 and 1924. P. F. Rooney was keeper from 1924 to at least 1937. LighthouseFriends lists Edward Rooney with "at least 1940" and no end date.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The gale of early 1837 is recorded above. In 1876 one of the tower's circular-burner lamps exploded and set a fire that badly damaged the lighting apparatus and lantern room. A new iron lantern and a new revolving apparatus were installed after that fire.[^1]

At 8:05 a.m. on 12 August 1898, lightning struck the inner lighthouse, which had been erected only five years before, and it burned to the ground. The keeper, Captain David Hunter, had gone home for breakfast after finishing his morning's work.[^2]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the ranges were automated in 1968, and the inner light was deactivated after the 1988 navigation season.[^1] After the fire, Noble built an octagonal wooden tower on the shore to serve as the new inner light. LighthouseFriends says it was planned about 457 m (1,500 ft) from the outer light, but the Canadian register gives the distance between the two towers as 91 m (300 ft). The sources disagree, and no source read settles it.[^2][^3] The tower has a beaver weathervane on its twelve-sided lantern, and LighthouseFriends counts it as one of six wooden octagonal lighthouses left on the Canadian side of the Great Lakes.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says the inner lighthouse was designated a federal heritage building in 1990 and transferred to the City of St. Catharines in 1997. During its restoration between 2000 and 2002, metal siding that the Coast Guard had fixed to the tower against vandal fires was removed.[^2] In 2010 the councillors agreed to pay $180,000 to restore and repaint its exterior.[^2]

## Today

The outer light is the front range light. LighthouseFriends says it remains active, showing a light that is on for two seconds and then off for two seconds, and that it is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard.[^1] The register notes that the front light still serves recreational boaters, while the rear light is no longer operational.[^3] The grounds are open. The tower is closed to visitors, and there is parking near the pier.[^1][^2]
