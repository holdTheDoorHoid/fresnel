The William Livingstone Memorial Light is a white marble lighthouse at the east end of Belle Isle, the park island in the Detroit River, in Detroit, Michigan.[^2] The Lighthouse Service lit it in 1930 in memory of William Livingstone, a Detroit banker, newspaper owner and shipping man.[^2] Ships coming down the river from Lake St. Clair steer toward it, and the Service called it a leading light.[^1]

## The place

Belle Isle had a light before this one.[^2] After Congress appropriated $10,000 in 1881, a lighthouse was built on the island, and it was placed in operation on the night of 15 May 1882.[^2] It was a square red-brick tower with a two-story brick house attached, and it showed a fixed red light from the island's southeast point.[^2]

Livingstone's name is also on a shipping channel in the lower Detroit River. He was president of the Lake Carriers' Association from 1902 until he died in 1925, and LighthouseFriends credits him with persuading the government to build a separate waterway for downbound ships there. That work began in 1908 and opened to commerce on 19 October 1912.[^2] Congress had appropriated $210,000 in 1911 for aids to navigation along the Livingstone Channel.[^7] In 1912 the Service's bulletin announced the aids that would mark "the newly completed Livingstone Channel."[^6]

Livingstone was born in Dundas, Ontario, in 1844, to Scottish parents, and moved to Detroit as an infant.[^2] He bought the Detroit Evening Journal in 1885. In April 1900 he became president of the Dime Savings Bank, a position he held until 17 October 1925, when he died in his office at the bank building.[^2]

## Building the light

After Livingstone's death, the Lake Carriers' Association and Detroit citizens paid for a memorial, and the city gave land at the east end of Belle Isle Park for it.[^2] The Detroit Historical Society names the architect Albert Kahn for the design, and says the ornament was designed in 1930 by the Hungarian sculptor Géza Maróti.[^4] LighthouseFriends says it was built of Georgia marble at a cost of $100,000.[^2]

The Service's bulletin of 1 June 1930 described a "fluted shaft of white marble," 47 feet (14 m) high, on an octagonal platform, with a bronze lantern at the top.[^1] The Detroit Historical Society gives the whole structure as 58 feet (18 m) tall.[^4] Inside the lantern, the bulletin said, a fourth-order lens with an electric lamp gave about 11,500 candlepower.[^1] The Detroit Historical Society gives the beacon 8,600 candlepower.[^4] Four auxiliary reflector lights were added to throw light into the shadows cast by the lantern's heavy bars.[^1] A low-voltage battery lamp in a sixth-order lens stood by, designed to work if the city current failed.[^1]

Accounts disagree about where that lens came from. The Detroit Historical Society says its bronze and glass lens came from the older Belle Isle light.[^4] LighthouseFriends, in a note on its page, reports an information sign at the Beaver Island lighthouse saying that a fourth-order lens on display there was "reportedly used for fifty years" at the memorial.[^2]

The bulletin reported that the light "is now in commission," although the approaches to the tower were not yet finished.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives 8 April 1930 as the day it commenced operation.[^2] The dedication took place on 17 October 1930, the fifth anniversary of Livingstone's death.[^2][^3] H. D. King, the Deputy Commissioner of Lighthouses, spoke, and the Service's bulletin reported his emphasis on the value of cooperation between government agencies and private interests.[^3]

## The keepers

The keepers named here served the older Belle Isle light, not the memorial.[^2][^5]

The Lighthouse Service's roster names William Anson Badger as its first keeper, from 1882 to 1886, then Louis Fetes from 1886 to 1907, George Sherman for 1907 and 1908, and Fetes again from 1908 to 1930.[^5] LighthouseFriends says Fetes kept the light for more than forty years. When he arrived in 1886 there was no bridge to the mainland, and to reach Detroit he would row to the Canadian side of the river, walk a few miles and then take the Walkerville ferry.[^2]

## After the keepers

The old Belle Isle light had no keeper after 1930.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the light was automated in 1930, and that Fetes died the following year, at seventy.[^2] The Detroit superintendent of the Lighthouse Service lived in the old lighthouse until 1940, when the dwelling was abandoned.[^2] In 1935 the old light was moved to a combined flagpole and light mast, because park trees were starting to hide it.[^2] The old tower was demolished in the fall of 1941 to make way for a Coast Guard station.[^2]

The memorial had its own troubles. Vandals broke into it in July 1980 and took two of its four lights from the lantern room.[^2][^4] The City of Detroit then put a fence around the lighthouse to stop further vandalism.[^4] In January 1981 it was broken into again, and graffiti was sprayed on a temporary plastic lens.[^2]

## Today

The City of Detroit owns the memorial. LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^2] The Detroit Historical Society says it is still in operation at the northern end of Belle Isle.[^4] LighthouseFriends describes its present signal as a white and red flash that alternates, with ten seconds between flashes.[^2] On the island, the Dossin Great Lakes Museum has a collection of Fresnel lenses.[^2]
