The Tchefuncte River lights stand on the north shore of Lake Pontchartrain, where the Tchefuncte River meets the lake below Madisonville, Louisiana. A brick tower has stood on the site since the late 1830s, and a black, pyramidal front light was added in 1903 so that mariners could line the two lights up on the river mouth.[^1][^2]

## The place

Madisonville lies just above the river's mouth.[^1] The nomination says the town "remains as a small shipyard and yacht harbor."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the town was named for President James Madison in 1811 and that for a century after it was a resort for wealthy New Orleanians, who reached it across the lake by steam ferry.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says storms were a constant threat to the exposed site.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $5,000 for a lighthouse on 30 June 1834. That money reverted to the Treasury in 1837, because of difficulties in getting title to the site, and had to be appropriated again.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the brick tower to 1837. It stood 36 feet (11 m) tall, and its lighting apparatus, supplied by Winslow Lewis, had nine lamps behind fourteen-inch reflectors.[^2] The nomination says the tower was "erected at the mouth of the river in 1838 with a Fifth Order lens, its 10 lamps visible 11 miles."[^1] The sources differ on the lamp count and on the year the light was first shown.[^1][^2]

A 212-foot (65 m) breakwater went in 1854 to protect the tower.[^2] The nomination says the lighthouse was "rebuilt in 1857."[^1] LighthouseFriends says a fifth-order Fresnel lens was installed in the lantern room in 1857.[^2]

The Civil War damaged the tower, and workmen came back in 1867 to restore it.[^2] The work stopped for four weeks in August and September 1867, when "the Yellow Fever epidemic that swept through the Gulf Coast" halted installation of the lantern.[^1] The new tower was built on the original foundation and rose ten feet higher than the first.[^2] Its lantern came from the Cat Island lighthouse, which had been damaged by a hurricane and burned at the outset of the war.[^1][^2] The nomination says the rebuilding was completed in early 1868.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the fifth-order lens was first shown on 1 December 1867.[^2]

## The keepers

Benjamin Thurston was the first keeper, hired at a salary of $500 a year.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he kept pet alligators at the station.[^2] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists 22 keeper lines at the station, from 1837 to 1939.[^10]

The first post-war keeper was William A. Stewart. He had served aboard the USS Richmond, which was part of Farragut's fleet at the Battle of Mobile Bay, before he took the post.[^2]

Bernard Segersteen kept the light in two stints, 1869 to 1871 and 1872 to 1883.[^10] Joseph P. Groux was keeper from 1889 to 1918, the longest stint on the archive's roster.[^10] LighthouseFriends says that in July 1915, when Groux was seventy-three, he was in his twenty-sixth year at the lighthouse.[^2]

Harry Brouwer was keeper from 1918 to 1920, and Frederick A. Schrieber from 1920 to 1935.[^10] LighthouseFriends says Schrieber lived at the lighthouse with his wife and their seven children, three of whom were born there, and that a telephone was installed in 1927.[^2] William D. Still, the last keeper, left in 1939.[^2][^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1874 a late gale badly damaged the breakwater, and Congress appropriated $3,500 on 3 March 1875 for a substantial new one.[^2] A storm in September 1879 swept that breakwater away, and it was rebuilt again in 1886.[^2]

On 19 and 20 August 1888 a storm "swept away all but the keeper's quarters and the tower."[^1] The outside kitchen, woodshed, washhouse, boathouse and picket fence were among the structures destroyed.[^1]

The Light-House Service listed the Chefuncte River light station among the stations damaged by a hurricane at New Orleans on 29 September 1915.[^3][^9] The Secretary of Commerce commended Keeper Joseph P. Groux, among others, for work "under hazardous and trying conditions during this hurricane."[^4] LighthouseFriends dates a hurricane at the station to July 1915.[^2] The Commissioner's report for 1918 lists a new cypress sheet-pile breakwater on the east and west sides of the station.[^8]

On 16 April 1920 Harry Brouwer, keeper, towed a steamship that had become disabled.[^5][^13] On 17 May 1923 Schrieber recovered a raft of cypress timber in the vicinity of the light station.[^6]

A hurricane visited a portion of the eighth lighthouse district directly west of New Orleans on 24 and 25 August 1926.[^7] The Light-House Service said the storm damaged several lighthouses, including Chefuncte River, where "wharves, boathouses, tie-rods, and rip rap" needed extensive repairs.[^7]

## After the keepers

Electricity reached the lighthouse in 1935.[^2] The keeper's dwelling was sold and moved into Madisonville three years after the light was solarized in 1952.[^2] In 2004 a donor gave the cottage to the town, and it was moved to the grounds of the Maritime Museum Louisiana.[^2] The town of Madisonville took over the lighthouse from the Coast Guard in 1999, and the first phase of a restoration was completed on 25 June 2008.[^2]

## Today

The town of Madisonville owns the lighthouse, and the Maritime Museum manages it for the town.[^11] The lake is now close to the tower.[^11] A news report from October 2024 says the front door is "perilously close to the water's edge," and a museum board member said the museum had been "out there with sandbags trying to protect this lighthouse."[^11] On 29 October 2024 St. Tammany Parish officials broke ground on the $1.6 million Tchefuncte Habitat Restoration, which includes a barrier to cut down on wave action and sediment pumped in from Lake Pontchartrain.[^11]

The museum's own page describes a further "comprehensive restoration effort, estimated at $3.5 million."[^12] LighthouseFriends says the best view is from a boat, and that a distant view is possible from Main Street in Madisonville.[^2] The museum says its keepers' cottage is "now open for historic tours."[^12] The Society's archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^10]
