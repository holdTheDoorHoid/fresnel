Coney Island Light stands on Norton's Point, at the western end of Coney Island in Brooklyn, where it marks the main channel into New York Harbor.[^4] It was first lit on 1 August 1890.[^5] The Coast Guard still operates it, but the light sits inside the gates of Sea Gate, a private community, and most people cannot walk up to it.[^1][^2]

## The place

Ferries, tugboats, barges and sailing vessels pass the point, and the light has guided them for more than 135 years.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the reason it was built: Coney Island's growing popularity meant steamers bound for its piers needed a guide, and garbage barges needed a mark for their dumping grounds nearby.[^3]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported in 1921 that the reservation sat on Sea Girt property at Nortons Point, and that it "was purchased in 1889, at which time there were no other buildings or roads in the vicinity."[^10] LighthouseFriends and Hoodline instead describe private owners whose asking price led to condemnation, and the sources do not explain the difference.[^3][^2] By 1921 the surrounding property had developed "in such a manner as to give the lighthouse reservation no street front and no other means of egress for the occupants."[^10] The Commissioner asked for $5,000 to buy a right of way.[^10]

## Building the light

The Coney Island History Project dates the Congressional statute to 2 March 1889.[^11] LighthouseFriends says the act approved $25,000 for two range lights at the west end of the island. When the owners asked twice the land's estimated value, the government condemned it and paid $3,500. Hoodline reports the same price, citing the Coast Guard Historian's Office.[^3][^2]

Hoodline names Major David Porter Heap of the Lighthouse Service as the designer, and says the Phoenix Iron Company of Trenton, New Jersey, prefabricated the tower in early 1890.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the metalwork contract to January 1890. Work on the tower, the front beacon, the fog signal and the keeper's dwelling began about two months later.[^3] The archive, however, gives 1896 as the year of the keeper's quarters.[^4] The rear tower was a square skeleton frame with a central steel column, and the front light was a small wooden tower on concrete footings.[^3]

The Board's report for 1891 and 1892 lists a fourth-order light established at Coney Island on 1 August 1890.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the first keeper, Thomas Higginbotham, lit it that day, and that the lens burned kerosene and showed a red flash.[^3]

In the fiscal year 1891 the Board moved the fog bell to a new position, and a close board fence went up along two sides of the reservation.[^6]

In 1896 the front beacon was no longer needed. The Board removed it to the general lighthouse depot and put the site up for auction.[^7] LighthouseFriends gives the sale date as 6 July 1896.[^3]

On 30 June 1899 the light's characteristic changed from a red flash every ten seconds to a red flash every five.[^8]

The sources disagree about when the present tower was first lit. The archive gives 1920 as the year the present tower was established.[^4] Gothamist says the lighthouse "was built in 1890," and Hoodline calls it "the 1890 beacon in Sea Gate, Brooklyn."[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says the original rear tower "remains standing."[^3] The sources do not settle the question.

## The keepers

The archive lists Thomas Higginbotham as principal keeper from 1890 to 1910.[^4] LighthouseFriends says he died of heat in July 1910, while he was on a picnic party aboard a Staten Island ferry. Ernest J. Larsson looked after the light for a few days, and Gilbert L. Rulon took charge in mid-July.[^3][^4]

Rulon kept the light until 1919, when Herbert L. Greenwood took over.[^4] Greenwood stayed for twenty-three years, according to LighthouseFriends, until Adrien J. Boisvert was transferred from Fire Island in 1941 to replace him.[^3][^4] LighthouseFriends says Boisvert climbed the tower's spiral stair several times each night to rewind the 150-pound weight that turned the lens.[^3]

Frank P. Schubert became keeper in 1960 and stayed until 2003, the archive records.[^4] Gothamist reports that he moved into the keeper's house in 1960 and stayed on after the light was automated in 1989. He is believed to have been the last civilian keeper of any lighthouse in the United States.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1914 a house about 250 yards (229 m) east of the light was washed away. LighthouseFriends says a 600-foot (183 m) stone wall went up the next year, and that a storm undermined much of it six months after it was finished.[^3]

A northeast storm of 10 to 12 April 1918 "caused considerable damage in the third and fourth lighthouse districts," the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported. At Coney Island the north corner of the reservation was cut away, and the fog-signal house fell into the water. The Bulletin adds that all of the apparatus was finally recovered.[^9] LighthouseFriends says a new skeletal fog-bell tower was built and surrounded with riprap.[^3]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1989.[^4][^1] LighthouseFriends says the original fourth-order lens came out that year and is now displayed at Coast Guard Headquarters in Washington, D.C.[^3] Hoodline gives the same account of the lens's removal.[^2]

On 20 October 2026 the city's Landmarks Preservation Commission voted to schedule a public hearing on the tower and the adjacent keeper's dwelling. It is the first of several steps toward landmark designation.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard still uses the light.[^1] It sits inside Sea Gate, a gated community that is closed to non-residents.[^2] Hoodline reports that the Coast Guard owns the lighthouse and leases it to the Sea Gate Association.[^2] The archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^4]

Landmark designation would not, by itself, open the site. The Sea Gate Association's community manager said the community supports the designation, but that public access would need to be worked out at a later date.[^1] Until Frank Schubert's death the family gave regular tours, Gothamist reports; since then they have been rarer.[^1] The two-story brick keeper's cottage beside the tower is part of the proposed landmark.[^2] The archive's record, by contrast, lists the keeper's quarters as wood-built.[^4] Visitors should not try to reach the light by water or through the community gates.
