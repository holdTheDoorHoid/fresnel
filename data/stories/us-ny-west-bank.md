West Bank Light is a cast-iron "spark plug" tower that stands out of open water in Lower New York Bay, about 22 feet deep at its foot, and marks one side of the big ship channel into New York Harbor. First lit in January 1901, it was built on a shoal with no land beside it, with the keepers' quarters inside the tower.[^1][^2]

## The place

West Bank is a shoal on the west side of the Ambrose Channel in Lower New York Bay. A Lighthouse Service report of 1917 places the station on the west prolongation of the axis of the outer section of the channel, in water about 22 feet deep.[^3] The Lighthouse Board's report for 1896 explained why a light was wanted: the work of improving the main ship channels at the harbor's principal entrance had reached a stage that called for additional aids, so that the channels would be usable by the larger vessels at night and in thick weather. It estimated that a light and fog signal could be built for $50,000.[^4] Congress made that appropriation by the act of 4 June 1897.[^5]

## Building the light

The Board's report for fiscal 1899 said the exact site had not yet been determined.[^5] By the report for fiscal 1900, the metal for the foundation cylinder, which was to weigh 380,000 pounds, had arrived, the site had been fixed, and a landing platform for materials was finished on 30 June 1900.[^6]

The Board's report for 1901 set out the work month by month. In July 1900 the bench mark was set and the site was excavated 8 to 10 feet below the bottom. In August the lowest sections of the foundation cylinder were sunk. By October the ninth course of plates was in progress, and in November the ninth and last course was done and the superstructure was begun and completed. "In December the erection of the station was completed."[^1] The same account says that on 1 January 1901 a fixed white light of the fourth order, with a red sector, was established, and that a blower siren fog signal followed on 1 June 1901.[^1] The report's list of new fog signals calls the same signal a compressed-air siren.[^1] The report's own list of new lights for the year gives 31 January 1901 for the fourth-order light, so the exact lighting date is not settled here.[^1] Riprap stone was also deposited around the station while the work went on; the September account puts 250 tons in place.[^1]

The tower did not stay at its first height. Acts of 30 June 1906 and 4 March 1909 each appropriated $50,000 for a new lighthouse on Staten Island and for raising West Bank Light, and the 1906 act added $10,000 for temporary structures to keep West Bank lit while it was raised.[^7] Kraig Anderson's LighthouseFriends history says the purpose was to bring West Bank into line with the new Staten Island tower as a range, that two stories were added between the watchroom and the deck below, and that the work was finished on 8 January 1908 at a cost of $9,198.[^8] The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported that the Staten Island light went into service on 15 April 1912;[^7] LighthouseFriends gives 5 April, so the exact day is not settled here.[^8] In 1909 a Daboll trumpet replaced the compressed-air siren, according to the same history.[^8]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists Edward B. Burdge as the first head keeper, from 1900 to 1906.[^9] LighthouseFriends says Burdge brought a fox terrier puppy to the new tower while his wife and six children stayed ashore.[^8] Burdge's recollections, as LighthouseFriends quotes them from magazine interviews, include: "A lighthouse is about the noisiest place in the world." He remembered that in his time "the tower rose right out of the water, with no footing at all around it, so the waves crashed against the whole tower."[^8]

The archive's roster runs from 1900 to 1981 and lists head keepers, first and second assistants, and in its later years Coast Guard crews.[^9] Turnover among the assistants was rapid: nine different men have stints in the two assistant posts that touch 1915 alone, some beginning in 1914 or running into 1916.[^9] In the summer of 1914, the three keepers wrote to thank the crew of the steamer Sandy Hook, which each morning around 7:45 threw a bundle of newspapers toward the station, where a keeper rowed out to fetch them.[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 28 December 1904, according to LighthouseFriends, the vessel Carrie Winslow, in tow of a tug, struck the lighthouse on a clear, cold evening with a northeast gale blowing, and the station sustained more than $1,200 in damage. Burdge, quoted there, said the vessel tore out one side of the tower; the tugboat company was held to blame.[^8] In January 1915 a barge, No. 19, in tow of the tug Minot L. Wilcox, struck the tower again and tore a section of cast iron from its base.[^8]

The sea also wore at the foundation. In July 1916 the Lighthouse Service spent $10,913.07 on a riprap bulkhead around the tower base to reinforce a foundation it described as "badly broken and deteriorated below low water."[^3] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1918 described the new work: heavy stones, from 200 pounds to 6 tons, laid in a ring with sloping sides, the top of the mass 6 feet above high water, with smaller stones packed inside against the pier.[^10] The earlier deposit of loose riprap, it said, had been leveled over time and no longer protected the pier at the water line.[^10]

Keepers also helped people in trouble on the bay. In April 1919, first assistant Daniel F. McCoart and second assistant Robert Wagoner went to the aid of the disabled motor boat Adelaide, which was drifting to sea in a heavy northeast wind, and gave lodging to its two men.[^11] On 10 November, which the roster suggests was in 1918, Arden A. Penn and Kiple A. Stryker took two men from a half-filled rowboat that had one oar into the station, gave them food and dry clothing and sheltered them for the night.[^12][^9] On 31 May 1918 first assistant Bruce Newton took the station's boat out at about midnight in thick fog, to Swinburne Island and then Hoffman Island, to get medical help for the injured second assistant.[^13] In the year to June 1922 keeper Fred Hainsworth towed a sinking power boat, with three men aboard, to a passing tug.[^14]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends records that the Coast Guard was running West Bank in the 1970s with crews of three, two on the station at a time and one ashore on a week's liberty, who were supplied from Sandy Hook by a 40-foot boat. In rough seas the boat could not dock at the slip and went back. On another occasion a tug ran into the riprap, and the coastguardsman telling the story began sleeping with a life jacket on his bed.[^8] The same history says the station was automated in the early 1980s and that its Fresnel lens was removed in 1998, when the light went over to solar power.[^8]

## Today

The Coast Guard declared West Bank Light excess in May 2007, and the General Services Administration auctioned it in 2008, with a winning bid of $245,000; a dispute over the auction's closing rules followed, and the light was offered again.[^8] The second auction closed in September 2010, and was won for $195,000 by a boater who, LighthouseFriends reports, meant to hand the tower to the group responsible for Romer Shoal Lighthouse.[^8] A 2013 article described the light as privately owned since 2010.[^15] The tower was added to the National Register of Historic Places on 9 January 2007.[^16]

The light is an active aid to navigation.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes it as privately owned and closed, best seen by boat, and visible distantly from New Dorp Beach and from the Coney Island boardwalk.[^8] Ownership has not been confirmed since the sources above, so anyone planning a visit should check current ownership first.
