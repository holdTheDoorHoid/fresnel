Port Greville Lighthouse is a white, A-shaped wooden tower built in 1907 to guide ships into the harbour at Port Greville, Nova Scotia, on the north side of the Minas Basin.[^1][^3] It has been moved twice, and since 1998 it has stood on the grounds of the Age of Sail Heritage Centre, where it can be visited in season.[^1][^3][^5]

## The place

Port Greville lies where the Ratchford River reaches the Minas Basin, roughly halfway between Cape d'Or and Parrsboro.[^1] A high gravel bar runs along the shore and forms the harbour, and the river runs between the bar and the shore for almost 1 km before it empties into the basin.[^1] Lighthouse Friends names the river the Ratchford River.[^1] The Heritage Guide's copy of the register and the Preservation Society say the lighthouse overlooks the Greville River.[^1][^4][^5] In 1874 a wall of round timber cribwork about 671 m (2,200 ft) long and 2.1 m (7 ft) high was built along the bar to protect the harbour.[^1]

Shipbuilding was the local business.[^1][^3] Lighthouse Friends counts 84 sailing vessels built at Port Greville, 76 of them schooners.[^1] The register notes that because land travel was so difficult here, the communities grew out of the ship-related economy.[^3] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society says the lighthouse presided for many years over a busy harbour of three-masted schooners, tugs and log rafts.[^5]

The shipyard was known as Wagstaff & Hatfield.[^13] The Centre's own pages say George Wagstaff moved to Port Greville around 1916, and that Otto Hatfield joined him as a partner in 1933.[^13] Lighthouse Friends says the firm was formed in 1933 by George Wagstaff and Otto Hatfield.[^1] Yet the Department's monthly list of sailing vessels building in Canada names "Wagstaff & Hatfield, Port Greville, N.S." in February 1917, and the firm appears again among the country's wooden shipbuilding plants in the annual report for the fiscal year 1917–18.[^11][^12] The sources do not agree on when the partnership began.

## Building the light

The lighthouse was built in 1907.[^1][^3] John D. Reid of Wallace Bay did the work by contract for $790.[^6] The Chief Engineer's report for the twelve months to 31 March 1908 says "A lighthouse was established at this port," and gives the tower as 8.3 m (27 ft) high, from its base to the ventilator on the lantern.[^6] Lighthouse Friends and Rowlett give 7.5 m (25 ft).[^1][^2] The register gives 8.3 m.[^3]

The light was first shown in 1908. The register says the tower was "lit in 1908".[^3] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society says it was first lit with kerosene that year.[^5]

Port Greville had two lights in a range. Lighthouse Friends describes the front light as the enclosed tower, showing a fixed red light 18 m (59 ft) above high water and visible six miles; the source does not say whether those are nautical miles.[^1] The back light stood 213 m (700 ft) away, north 17 degrees west, shown from an anchor lantern hoisted between two 4.6 m (15 ft) poles, with a fixed red light 40 m (130 ft) up.[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives the front light a sixth-order dioptric lens. The official list of new lights erected in 1907–08 gives the front light an anchor lantern and the back light a fifth-order French lens.[^7] The two sources do not agree on the apparatus.[^7][^1]

## The keepers

Ernest A. Hatfield was appointed the first keeper of the range lights on 29 June 1908, Lighthouse Friends says.[^1] The Department's keepers' statements in the 1909, 1910, 1911 and 1916 reports give the same appointment date against his name, under "Port Greville Range" or "Greville, Port, range".[^8][^9][^16][^10]

Lighthouse Friends lists him as keeper until at least 1923.[^1] The keeper of the range lights in the late 1930s is not named in the sources.[^1][^5] He wrote to his superiors that he had run out of kerosene.[^1] Villagers were covering the shortfall from their own supplies so the lights could stay lit each night, and he asked for a barrel to be sent on the next ship.[^1][^5] Burton Charles DeWolfe is listed as keeper in the 1950s and 1960s.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In late April 1923 a freshet, caused by melting snow and heavy rain, undermined the breakwater at Port Greville.[^15] A dispatch from Amherst, Nova Scotia, dated 30 April, printed in the St. Croix Avis of Christiansted on 4 May, says "the lighthouse slid into the channel" and that it was unoccupied at the time.[^15] The dispatch does not say which light it means. Lighthouse Friends does not mention the event.[^1]

On 2 February 1976 the Groundhog Day gale swept into Port Greville and destroyed the shipyard, Lighthouse Friends says.[^1] Lighthouse Friends also says George Wagstaff watched from the bluff above the yard as his main building collapsed and the wharves were washed out.[^1] The Age of Sail Heritage Centre says the storm reduced the large boat shed to rubble.[^13] The yard had stopped building in 1972, after losses on contracts priced before inflation; a hoped-for reopening ended with the 1976 gale.[^1][^14]

## After the keepers

In 1917 a pole with a shed was set near the outer end of the breakwater to show a fixed red front light.[^1] The former rear light was discontinued, and the enclosed tower that had been the front light took over the rear role. Its colour changed from red to white.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates this to 1917, and Rowlett puts the change in the 1920s, after the channel shifted.[^1][^2] The breakwater was rebuilt in 1926, and a pole light went on its outer end as the new front light. The front light was destroyed in 1940, and no cause is given.[^1]

The Coast Guard deactivated the light after the shipyard's closure, Lighthouse Friends says.[^1] Rowlett says it has been inactive since 1976.[^2] The register dates the decommissioning and the move to 1980, when the tower was cut into two pieces.[^3] Lighthouse Friends and the Preservation Society say the tower was sawn in half in 1981 and taken to the Canadian Coast Guard College at Sydney, Cape Breton, and Rowlett also gives 1981 for the move.[^1][^5][^2] The register and the other sources disagree by a year. The tower stood at the college until 1998, when local citizens lobbied to have it returned.[^3]

In June 1998 the tower was sawn in half again, trucked back to Port Greville and reassembled. It opened to the public on the Age of Sail grounds that August.[^1][^5] Local government recognised it as a municipally registered property on 5 May 2001.[^4] In 2010 it was re-clad in clapboard over the existing shingles, and a railing was added round the lantern the following spring.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse stands on the grounds of the Age of Sail Heritage Centre beside Route 209, west of Port Greville.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower is open in season.[^1] Rowlett says the tower is open daily except Monday, late May to late September.[^2] The register gives its current use as a museum.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says the village owns it and the museum manages it, while the Lighthouse Preservation Society says the museum owns it.[^1][^5] Opening times are seasonal and may have changed, so check with the museum before a visit.[^1][^2]
