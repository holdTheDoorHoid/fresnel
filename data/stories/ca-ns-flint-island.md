Flint Island Lighthouse stands on a small sandstone island off the eastern side of Cape Breton, roughly three kilometres east of Cape Perce, where a light has guided ships along that shore since November 1856.[^1][^3]

## The place

The island's top is flat and grassy, with steep sandstone cliffs around its edge.[^2] The name is misleading: the island is sandstone, not flint, and erosion has broken it into two pieces.[^1] It has one landing place, and the station's boat had to be hauled up the cliff there.[^1] Tide rips form around it, and the irregular tidal streams can reach two knots.[^3]

The light was a coast light, and also an aid for coal vessels trading between Cow Bay and Bridgeport.[^1] There was no well on the island, and not even a tree or shrub grew there. Keeper Cann had to catch rainwater in casks.[^1]

## Building the light

Nova Scotia built the light in 1856, the only light the province built that year.[^1] The contractor built an octagonal wooden tower with a detached keeper's dwelling, and George Cann lit it on 1 November 1856.[^1][^3] The work was not entirely completed that year. In July 1857 the tower was still unpainted and the dwelling unfinished.[^1]

The first winter was hard. Cann and his family suffered considerably from the cold, and almost used up a new cooking stove trying to keep warm.[^1] His salary was £60 a year with a £20 fuel allowance. Out of that money he had to hire a man to help him, because the station's boat had to be hauled up the cliff.[^1]

The first tower burned on the night of 31 August 1864, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] Cann had made his usual check of the lamps at 11 p.m., and at 2 a.m. he found the tower in flames.[^1] Refined petroleum and lamps had reached the station six days earlier, and he had been instructed in the use of the new oil.[^1] Cann could not account for the cause, but he kept his post.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says a second tower, built in 1865, was very similar to the first and showed the same white flash every fifteen seconds.[^1] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society's list dates the fire to 1 August 1865 and the replacement to 1866, with a question mark, so the two accounts disagree on the year.[^3]

The next tower, built in 1909 and 1910, was taken down in the early 1960s, while its replacement was built.[^1][^2] The Department's annual report for the twelve months ended 31 March 1909 describes a three-inch duplicate diaphone fog alarm, bought from the Canadian Fog Signal Company of Toronto for $8,100, being installed in a wooden fog alarm building.[^5] The report adds that "a reinforced steel concrete tower" would be erected the next season.[^5] The report for the twelve months ended 31 March 1910 says a new lighthouse has been built and fitted with a third-order dioptric quadruple flashing light, 22.6 m (74 ft) above high water.[^6] The report's table of new aids to navigation in Nova Scotia lists, under Flint Island, "a 62-foot reinforced steel concrete lighthouse tower", which is 18.9 m (62 ft) high.[^7] Lighthouse Friends describes the lens as flashing four times in each cycle of seven and a half seconds.[^1] The lamp burned petroleum vapour under a mantle, and the keeper had to crank up the weight that turned the lens every four hours.[^1]

## The keepers

George Cann kept the light from 1856 until he died on 12 July 1869.[^1] His son took charge until Benjamin Heney was appointed on 18 October 1869.[^1]

Heney's salary was raised from $400 to $500 in 1873, because of the difficult character of his post.[^1] In 1877 he complained about fishermen who spread their nets wherever they pleased on the island.[^1] They landed fifty-four large black fish just 35 m (114 ft) from the dwelling, and left the carcasses at the only spot where the keeper could land his boat.[^1] Each carcass weighed over two tons, so Heney hired two men to help remove them. The three men worked six hours a day for eight days, as the tide permitted, and Lighthouse Friends says the smell was becoming impossible to live on the island.[^1]

The Department's keepers' lists also name two of the keepers in Lighthouse Friends' list.[^8][^9] William Helm is printed against Flint Island in the list for the year ended 30 June 1901, and Daniel McKenzie against Flint Island in the list for the year ended 30 June 1903.[^8][^9] Lighthouse Friends gives Helm's time as 1883 to 1903 and McKenzie's as 1903 to 1904.[^1]

After 1903 the head keepers changed often.[^1] Michael Brean stayed from 1904 to 1910, and Avery Covey from 1913 to 1924, with short postings between.[^1] In 1927 John J. Martell took charge, with his wife and eight children, and four more children were born on the island.[^1] The family stayed until 1945.[^1] The register says that Robert Spears tended the present light and its predecessor from 1960, until the station was de-staffed and automated in the late 1980s.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In December 1869 men carrying supplies to the island were carried out to sea.[^1] They spent nine days exposed before they reached shore, and were severely frostbitten.[^1] The Department gave $600 to be divided among them, "proportionate to the necessities of each".[^1]

Vessels were ashore on the island too.[^10] The Department printed a list of vessels assisted by the Dominion Coal Company's wrecking plant in its report for the twelve months ended 31 March 1908.[^10] The list is headed as covering the company's fiscal year 1907-8, and one line reads: "Oscar, June 11. Ashore on Flint island. Successfully floated off." The line does not give the year.[^10]

On 27 April 1911 the tug *Douglas E. Thomas* was sent to search for a small sailboat with two men reported off Flint Island.[^11] The boat was disabled, and a storm was approaching.[^11] The list does not say how the search ended.[^11]

On 23 March 1933 Raymond Martell drowned near the island.[^1] He had set off with his older brother Billy to hunt seal on the ice near the island.[^1] Three big waves upset their boat.[^1] Raymond could swim, but Billy could not. Raymond, who had a cramp in his leg, did not reach the shore.[^1] Their father, the keeper, put out in a small canvas boat, and did not find Raymond's body until the next day.[^1] He stopped the revolving light as a signal to those ashore that something was wrong.[^1] It was a few days before the icebreaker *Montcalm* reached the island.[^1]

On 15 October 1933 a boat carrying Billy Martell and his new wife Edna, with his brother Kenny and their thirteen-year-old sister Ethel, had engine trouble.[^1] It was a stormy night with rough seas, and the boat was upset more than once.[^1] Kenny, the only one who could swim, was knocked unconscious by the boat.[^1] When he came round, he found Ethel missing and Billy and Edna clinging to the boat.[^1] Billy got the boat to shore with a rope in his teeth, but Edna was never seen again, even after days of searching the shore.[^1]

In January 1940 the Martells' two-and-a-half-year-old son Harold had pains in his belly.[^1] His father took him by boat, picking a way through the ice, to the hospital at Glace Bay.[^1] Harold's appendix had burst, and the resulting peritonitis soon took his life.[^1]

## After the keepers

By 1954 the old lighthouse was reported to be in poor condition.[^1] On 29 August 1961 a temporary lantern room was installed atop the dwelling, so that a light could show while the old lighthouse was torn down and a new reinforced concrete tower was built fifteen metres south of it.[^1] Three new dwellings went up at the same time.[^1] The register dates the present tower to 1963, as the replacement for the 1910 tower, which was in poor structural condition.[^2] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society gives 1962 as the year the present light was first lit.[^3] The present tower is 17.8 m (58 ft) tall.[^4]

In 1963 two diesel generators were placed on the island, and the lamp was changed to a 1,000-watt bulb.[^1] A small electric motor was installed to turn the lens, which had been done with hand-wound weights.[^1] In 1971 an electric foghorn replaced the diaphone, and the staff was cut to two keepers.[^1]

The sources do not agree on when the light was automated. Lighthouse Friends says 1985, when the remaining keepers left.[^1] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society's list says "by 1987",[^3] and the register says the late 1980s.[^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the station, and the tower is closed to visitors.[^1] The light still operates.[^4] The Coast Guard's list gives a white light that flashes for two seconds in every six, with a focal height of 22.6 m (74 ft) and a nominal range of eight nautical miles.[^4] The Society's list gives a different characteristic for 1992, a group flashing white light every 25 seconds.[^3] The register lists the light's continuing use as a coastal light among the features that give the station its heritage character.[^2]

The island is reached by boat only.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says the light is best viewed by boat, and that a distant view is possible from the hiking trails at Schooner Pond and from the waterfront in Port Morien.[^1]
