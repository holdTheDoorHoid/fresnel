Cape Scott Lighthouse stands on the northwest tip of Vancouver Island, British Columbia.[^1][^2] A light was first shown there in 1927 from a red lantern on a wooden mast. The steel tower that carries the light today dates from 1981, and a Coast Guard station with keepers' homes stands beside it.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Cape Scott is the extreme northwestern point of Vancouver Island.[^1] It is a promontory about 150 m high, joined to the island by a low sandy neck, and five islands known as the Scott Islands run westward from it.[^1]

James Strange's fur-trading expedition named the cape in 1786.[^1] The first white settlers were a group of Danes led by Rasmus Hansen, who arrived in 1897.[^1][^5] They planned to live by fishing until a road the government had promised was built, and the road was never built.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says most of the settlers had left by 1907. The 101 Squadron's history of the radar station gives the settlement's span as 1897 to 1917, and says the last resident, Alfred Spencer, left in 1956.[^5] The two sources disagree on how long the settlement lasted.

## Building the light

The first light on the cape was an unwatched acetylene gas light, shown from a red lantern on a wooden mast.[^1][^3] A Department of Marine notice printed in the Daily Colonist on 2 November 1927 said it had been established on 29 October.[^3]

During the Second World War the Royal Canadian Air Force built a radar unit at the cape.[^5] It was formed on 13 December 1942 and became operational on 5 February 1943.[^5] The 101 Squadron's history says it was staffed by roughly seventy men on two sites, while Lighthouse Friends says roughly fifty men.[^5][^1] The two sites were the radar operations centre near the tip and a camp in the woods across the sandy neck.[^5][^1] Lighthouse Friends says the radar station was abandoned in 1945.[^1]

Its concrete foundation was then used for a light tower.[^1][^5] The station was completed in 1959 on the radar foundation, with diesel generators in the old radar shack, three keepers' dwellings near the tower, and a fog alarm building with an annex on the very tip of the cape.[^1][^5] Rowlett gives 1960 for the same replacement of the mast light by a skeletal tower on the radar foundation.[^2]

In September 1981 a new tower replaced the first one.[^1][^5] Rowlett describes it as a 9 m (29 ft) square steel skeletal tower with a red lantern and gallery. Its white light flashes every ten seconds, from a focal plane 70 m (229 ft) above the sea.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

The first head keeper was William Gardiner, who moved up the coast from Kains Island.[^1] Lighthouse Friends spells his name Gardner in its text and Gardiner in its keeper list. Lighthouse Friends lists twelve keepers from 1960 to at least 2024.[^1] Don Weeden kept the light from 1973 to 1992, and the list runs on to Harvey Humchitt, from 2004 to at least 2024.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends says the keepers have the cape to themselves for most of the year, apart from summer hikers.[^1] It describes the cape as one of the final three manned lights built in British Columbia, and links that building programme to the rise in ocean traffic after the Alcan smelter at Kitimat was completed.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and Lighthouse Friends lists a keeper there until at least 2024.[^1]

BC Parks says the public may visit the lighthouse, which is outside the park and federally operated.[^4] Rowlett's directory, by contrast, says the lighthouse is surrounded by Cape Scott Provincial Park.[^2] BC Parks also says the federal government has removed the suspension bridges and closed all public access between the lighthouse and the foghorn at the cape.[^4] The trail is 23.6 km from the trailhead to the lighthouse, with an average one-way hike of 8.5 hours.[^4]

The sources disagree about the tower itself.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] Rowlett says the site is open and that visitors can climb to the gallery of the tower.[^2] This account does not suggest climbing the tower, and it does not suggest going past the lighthouse into the area BC Parks says is closed.[^4]
