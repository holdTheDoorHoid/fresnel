Lennard Island Lighthouse stands on the southwest point of Lennard Island, at the mouth of Templar Channel. The channel runs 6.4 km (four miles) north to Clayoquot Sound and the town of Tofino on Vancouver Island's west coast.[^2] The light has been shown since 1 November 1904, and the station is a staffed Canadian Coast Guard light today.[^1][^5]

## The place

Captain Richards of the survey vessel *Hecate* named the island in 1861, after Charles Edward Barrett-Lennard, who had sailed round Vancouver Island the year before in his yacht *Templar*.[^2] In 1902 the Department's chief engineer, Colonel William P. Anderson, inspected the lights of British Columbia and chose sites for several new ones, including Lennard Island, to serve mariners entering Clayoquot Sound and those coming up the island's coast after a great-circle voyage from the Far East.[^2] The department's report for 1903 gives the purpose in its own words: the lighthouse was proposed "to serve as a coast light and also to mark the entrance to the sound."[^6]

## Building the light

The site was cleared in 1903. About ten acres were slashed, and the fallen trees were burned "at a cost of $1,120."[^6] In April 1904 the Dominion steamer *Quadra* carried building material to the island. The station's buildings were put up by day labour under the foreman George Frost, at a cost LighthouseFriends gives as $10,955.69.[^2]

The department's report for 1904 says the light "was put in operation on the 1st November, 1904."[^1] The tower was "a wooden building, octagonal in plan, with sloping sides, painted white," with a red metal lantern. It stood 24 m (80 ft) from base to the vane, on rock about 11 m (35 ft) above high water. The apparatus was "dioptric, of the first order," with petroleum vapour burning under an incandescent mantle. The light flashed every eleven and a quarter seconds, and the report says it "should be visible 16 miles from all points of approach."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the lens came from Birmingham, England.[^2]

A fog alarm was added in 1905. The department's report describes it as "a rectangular, wooden structure," built by day labour under George Frost.[^7] LighthouseFriends says a three-inch steam diaphone was installed in duplicate, and the alarm was finished in December 1905.[^2]

The present tower is a different matter, and the sources disagree on its date. LighthouseFriends describes a 4,600-pound fibreglass tower, 17 m (55 ft) tall, flown in by helicopter in 1968 and set on a concrete pad, replacing the original wooden tower.[^2] The Lighthouse Directory gives 1959 for the present tower, 18 m (58 ft) high, and gives no source for the date.[^3] The Directory's 1959 date may refer to other work, since LighthouseFriends records a 1959 contract for two new dwellings at the station.[^2] Neither source explains the difference, so this story follows LighthouseFriends' account of the 1968 lift.[^2] The light now flashes every ten seconds, not every eleven and a quarter seconds as in 1904.[^4] The Coast Guard's list describes the tower as white with a red top.[^4]

## The keepers

Francis C. Garrard, born in 1863 in Broxbourne, England, had sailed round the world six times when he was appointed first keeper at a salary of $600 a year.[^2] He arrived with his wife Annie and six children before the lighthouse was finished, and the family lived in a cramped oil shed while he helped complete it. Garrard later complained that the lantern roof leaked so badly that he "found it advisable to wear oilskins while up in the lantern attending the light."[^2] The department's 1906 keeper list also gives Garrard against Lennard Island. The names and stations come from OCR columns and were matched by position.[^8]

Robert Pollock was hired as an assistant in December 1905, and he was head keeper from 1908 to 1922.[^2] Edward, the family's seventh child, died on 17 January 1908 at about a year old, after swallowing lye that LighthouseFriends says he had taken for sugar. Garrard resigned later that year.[^2] The family, LighthouseFriends says, could no longer expose its children to "the element of danger" of living on the island.[^2]

After Pollock came George Grandon, for a short time in 1922, and then Donald Bertram McPhee, head keeper until 1929.[^2]

The head-keeper list runs on through Sydney Walter Blackmore (1929 to 1930), Thomas A. McNabb (1930 to 1944) and a long run of keepers and assistants after the war. Anthony Holland kept the station from 1974 to 1996. LighthouseFriends says he spent 22 years there, longer than anyone else.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 24 June 1927 a fire broke out in the keeper's dwelling. That day the assistant keeper, Joe Nesbit, had lit the kitchen range. Soon afterwards the head keeper's daughter, Violet McPhee, opened the kitchen door and found smoke and flame. She alerted the keepers and telephoned the Tofino lifesaving station. A submarine telephone cable had been run from the island to Tofino in 1925. The lifeboat reached the island with fire extinguishers, but the dwelling burned to the ground. The fire reached the oil house, where hundreds of gallons of oil were stored, and the keepers beat it back with chemicals and buckets of water. The lighthouse and fog alarm building escaped serious damage. McPhee lived in a shack near the lighthouse until a new dwelling was built the following year.[^2]

At Christmas 1932 keeper Thomas McNab and his assistant R. Guppy, with their families, faced storms that threatened to keep their Christmas mail and dinner from reaching them. The Tofino lifeboat crew tried to land on Christmas Eve but was forced back by huge seas. On Christmas morning three young fishermen took a small salmon troller through the storm, loaded the mail and dinner supplies, and got around the rocks to a sheltered spot where Guppy could launch a boat and bring the goods ashore. The Victoria paper, the *Times Colonist*, reported the trip, as LighthouseFriends quotes it.[^9]

## Today

Lennard Island Light is an active Coast Guard station with several buildings.[^3] The Senate committee's appendix table, drawn from Coast Guard data of April 2010, lists it as a remote station, not automated, with two lightkeeping positions and permanent residents.[^5] LighthouseFriends lists keepers to Martin Mountford, "at least 2024", which suggests the station was still staffed that year.[^2] The Coast Guard's list gives a flash every ten seconds, from 35.1 m (115 ft) above the water, with a nominal range of 17 nautical miles.[^4]

LighthouseFriends describes a boat trip around the island and scenic flights offered from Tofino, and advises that the best views of the station are from the air, with decent views from the water.[^2] It describes the grounds as open and the tower closed.[^2] The Lighthouse Directory says the same.[^3] Keepers live on the island, so visitors should not land without the Coast Guard's invitation.[^5]
