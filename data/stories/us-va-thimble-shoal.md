Thimble Shoal Light is a red cast-iron "spark plug" lighthouse that rises from the water on Horseshoe Bar, about three and a half miles northeast of Old Point Comfort, at the entrance to Hampton Roads in Virginia.[^1][^2][^21] It is the third light on the shoal. The first two were wooden screw-pile houses, and both burned.[^3][^4]

## The place

A 1915 report of the Commissioner of Lighthouses says the station was first built in 1872 to take the place of the Willoughby Spit Light Vessel and to mark a shoal spot called "The Thimble," which it described as "a great source of danger" to vessels coming in from the sea.[^2] Lighthouse historian Kraig Anderson describes two parallel bars at the mouth of the roads, Willoughby's Spit and Horseshoe Bar, and says the Thimble had only eleven feet (3.4 m) of water over it at low tide.[^4]

## Building the light

By 1859, Anderson says, the Lighthouse Board had grown fond of screw-pile lighthouses, which are screwed directly into the seabed, and replaced lightships with them wherever it could because they cost much less to maintain and staff. The Thimble was chosen as a site, and because strong easterly winds buffeted it the Board gave the lighthouse an unusually strong foundation and a generous ring of riprap.[^4] Construction began in the spring of 1872, and Anderson reports that the fine, compact sand made the work hard, with a broken cast-iron follower column and a screw that snapped from a defective casting.[^4] The light was first shown on 15 October 1872, according to Anderson, as a fourth-order fixed light varied every thirty seconds by a red flash, and it replaced the last lightship in the district.[^4] The wooden hexagonal house stood on a centre pile ringed by six more, with fog bells on its north and south sides.[^4] When Arnold Burges Johnson described the Light-House Service in 1889, he chose this very lighthouse as his picture of a screw-pile light.[^5]

On 30 October 1880 the cottage burned. Anderson gives the cause as unknown.[^4][^6] The iron foundation survived, and the district reused it, setting on it a house that had been meant for Bell's Rock, Virginia. The tender *Tulip* brought the materials in icy weather, and the light was shown again on 24 December 1880, 55 days after the fire.[^4][^6] A 1927 Lighthouse Service Bulletin adds a detail about the lens. A fourth-order lens set up in the Light-House Board's room in the Treasury Building was moved to a window to flash down the avenue on the night of President Grant's second inauguration in March 1873, and it was afterwards installed in the screw-pile lighthouse on Thimble Shoal.[^7] The Bulletin says that lens was destroyed when the lighthouse burned, but its date there is damaged in the scan.[^7] The Board's 1899 report lists the light as a fourth-order fixed white for one minute, followed by six flashes, alternately red and white, at ten-second intervals.[^8]

The second house burned in its turn. Anderson says the schooner *Malcolm Baxter, Jr.*, coming in from a stormy Atlantic under tow, lost her steering in a gale with snow flurries on 27 December 1909 and drove her jib boom into the lighthouse. The stove overturned and the burning coals set the wooden structure alight, and the keepers escaped by boat.[^4] The Chesapeake Chapter's timeline agrees on the date and the three keepers.[^6] The Commissioner's 1915 report says only that the earlier structure was "destroyed by fire in 1909."[^2] Congress appropriated $68,000 on 25 June 1910 and $39,000 on 26 August 1912, and the Commissioner's report gives the cost as $99,952.76.[^9][^2]

The new station was built to resist fire and collision. Its metal came from Champion Iron Company of Kenton, Ohio, according to Anderson.[^4] The cast-iron pier was assembled ashore at Berkley, Virginia, launched on a marine railway, towed out and sunk by the pneumatic process 12 feet 9 inches (3.9 m) into the bottom, then filled with concrete. It is 42 feet (12.8 m) across at the base. A three-story conical cast-iron dwelling stands on its deck, with a lantern whose focal plane is 55 feet 3 inches (16.8 m) above mean high water.[^2] The report says the light was visible 13 miles in clear weather and gave quarters for three keepers.[^2] Work started in December 1912 and was practically finished on 1 December 1914, when the new light and fog signal went into commission.[^2][^10] The Bulletin that month records that a temporary light on the old structure was discontinued and that the fog signal was changed from a bell to a reed horn.[^10] The reed horn did not last. A Bulletin summary of changes in 1919 records the station's fog signal altered from a reed horn to an air diaphone.[^11]

## The keepers

The Archive's roster for the screw-pile station names John Brown as the first head keeper, from 1872 to 1883.[^12] Anderson says Brown was later transferred to Craney Island Lighthouse, and he quotes a character reference Brown wrote about a former assistant, listed as "A. J. Lee," whom he said he had caught asleep and intoxicated.[^4] (The Archive's roster spells the assistant's name Alexander G. Lee.[^12]) Wesley F. Ripley had the longest stay of the screw-pile years, 1888 to 1904.[^12]

The roster for the cast-iron tower begins with Horace C. Groom, head keeper from 1915 to 1920.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists him against Thimble Shoal in its roll of stations that won efficiency flags for 1918.[^13] In August 1918, the Bulletin says, Groom and Second Assistant Benjamin D. Preston towed a disabled motor boat with six people aboard into Hampton Creek.[^14] John T. Twiford held the post from 1920, and Loren E. Tillett followed in 1931.[^1] (Lighthousefriends dates the change a year earlier, with Twiford ending in 1930 and Tillett starting then.)[^4] The roster lists a head keeper and two assistants at a time through the 1930s, and the 1915 report says the quarters held three keepers.[^1][^2] Coast Guard crew appear in the roster from 1947, and the last head keeper, Preston F. Meekins, served from 1961 until 1964.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Collisions are a recurring theme. Anderson dates the first to 1891, when a steamer struck the lighthouse. In 1898 a coal barge hit it, taking away the lower gallery on the southeast side and lifting the house about half an inch off its radial beams.[^4] The Chesapeake Chapter's timeline records the same two years.[^6] After the new tower opened, the Commissioner's report lists the schooner *Addie M. Laurence* colliding with the station on 6 March 1915, with $600 of repairs paid by her owners.[^15] (The Chesapeake Chapter's timeline spells it "Lawrence.")[^6]

The keepers' own work is recorded in the Bulletin. On 25 October, the 1921 Bulletin reports, Twiford and Second Assistant Charles P. Morgan saved from drowning two men whose fish steamer had sunk near the station. The men had got away in a small boat that sank as they reached the station, and the keepers recovered the boat.[^16] On 4 February, the 1920 Bulletin says, heavy seas carried away the station's motor boat and boat davits, broke two port glasses in the basement and took a portion of the deck. The motor boat was recovered later at Ocean View, where it had washed ashore.[^17] The 1923 Bulletin prints a report from the captain of the tender *Orchid* that on the evening of 31 January, in dense fog and a light westerly wind, the diaphone was plainly heard from a mile south of Thimble Shoal Channel Gas and Bell Buoy No. 2.[^18]

In 1926 the Bulletin printed letters from the keepers of three fifth-district lighthouses, Hooper Island, Ragged Point and Thimble Shoal, about radio sets donated to them.[^19] One keeper wrote: "We have been able to hear very plainly a good sermon every Sunday since we received it and also lots of good music."[^19] The printed letters carry no station names beside them, so the Bulletin does not say which keeper wrote that one. Anderson assigns it to Thimble Shoal.[^19][^4]

## After the keepers

The Archive records the light as automated in 1964, the year Meekins's service ended.[^1] Anderson says the station was then run on lead-acid batteries until four 35-watt solar panels were installed in 1986, and that a 1988 crew of forty refurbished the exposed tower.[^4] (The Chesapeake Chapter's timeline gives a power cable and battery backup for 1964 and solar cells for 1987.)[^6] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 2 December 2002 and on the Virginia Landmarks Register in 2003.[^20]

Lighthouse Digest says the lighthouse was offered to a qualified new owner under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act but drew no applicants, and that it was sold at auction in October 2005 to Peter Jurewicz of Smithfield, Virginia, for $65,000.[^21] Anderson adds that the auction was held online and that bidders could tour the lighthouse in September.[^4]

## Today

Thimble Shoal Light is privately owned, is not open to the public and can be reached only by boat. Lighthouse Digest says the Coast Guard still maintains the optic as an active aid to navigation, now a solar-powered RB 355 unit.[^21] The station can be seen from the boardwalk at Fort Monroe in Hampton and from beaches in Norfolk.[^21] A fourth-order Fresnel lens used in the lighthouse is on display at the Coast Guard Training Center at Yorktown, according to Anderson.[^4] A supplier's case study says the owner replaced 33 corroded canopy and gutter supports on the tower with fiberglass beams.[^22]
