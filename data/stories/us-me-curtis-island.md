Curtis Island Light stands on a small rocky island at the southern entrance to Camden Harbor, on the Maine coast.[^1][^2] The first light there was a rubblestone tower built in 1836.[^1][^2] It was replaced in 1896 by the brick tower that stands today, and the Coast Guard automated the light in 1972.[^2][^3] The nomination says the light was known as Negro Island Light for one hundred years. In 1934 the town of Camden honored the publisher Cyrus H. K. Curtis, a summer resident, and the island was renamed for him.[^1]

## The place

Camden incorporated in 1791, when its population reached 331. As the town grew it gained a shipyard and a wharf for the steamers running between Boston and Bangor.[^2] The National Register nomination calls the island a natural breakwater, one that takes the force of the open sea so that the harbor stays calm.[^1]

For many years the island was a signal station for the Boston–Bangor boats, many of which stopped at Camden.[^1] The keeper raised a ball on a pole near the tower to show that a ship was coming from one direction or the other. When the ball went up, LighthouseFriends says, the people with an interest in the ship would set out for the wharf.[^2] Rowers tested their skill by lining their boats up in the wake of the big steamers.[^2] The island was also a picnic spot for Camden people and summer visitors.[^1]

James Richards, the first European settler to arrive, entered Camden Harbor with his family in 1769.[^2] LighthouseFriends traces the island's first name to an 1858 history of Camden. In that account, the African cook of the Richards family pointed to the island and called it his. The island was known as Negro Island until it was renamed in 1934.[^2]

## Building the light

In 1834 Congress appropriated $4,500 for a lighthouse on Negro Island.[^2] The National Register nomination says the light "was built by George Galilt of Boston." LighthouseFriends and the Digital Archive name him George Galt. LighthouseFriends says Galt completed the lighthouse in 1836, on land bought from John Dorr of Boston for $400.[^1][^2] The archive's roster lists George Galt as a contractor, not a keeper.[^3] The Digital Archive's place record gives 1835 as the year the station was established.[^3] The sources therefore differ by a year on when the station began, and this story uses 1836 for the tower.

The first tower was rubblestone, about twenty feet (6.1 m) to its lantern deck. Galt also built a two-story stone dwelling with three rooms on the first floor.[^2] Eight oil lamps with fourteen-inch reflectors gave a fixed white light.[^2] In 1856 the lighthouse received a new reflecting apparatus, and a fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed later that year.[^2]

The Light-House Board's annual reports record some of the station's later work. In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 the Board recorded materials for an oil house landed at the station, and in the fiscal year ended 30 June 1895 it recorded an oil house built.[^4][^5] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 the lower 30 feet of the boat slip was rebuilt and the fuel house was moved and repaired. In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 a brick cistern was built in the dwelling.[^8][^9] LighthouseFriends dates the replacement of the old dwelling to 1889, when a framed house was built on the same foundation.[^2]

The tower was rebuilt in the 1890s. The nomination says President Grover Cleveland ordered the light rebuilt in 1896.[^1] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1896 lists the tower, its workroom and the covered way as rebuilt.[^6] On 11 May 1896 the lantern was moved to a temporary tower forty feet to the east.[^2] The Board's report for the following fiscal year says the lantern moved into the new tower on 31 July 1896.[^7] LighthouseFriends gives 30 July as the day the new cylindrical brick tower, twenty-five feet (7.6 m) tall, was completed and lit.[^2] The sources do not agree on the sequence. The Board's report for the year to 30 June 1896 already lists the tower as rebuilt, before either of the July dates.

The Board recorded a change from a hand bell to a 1,000-pound bell struck by hand on 20 October 1896.[^7] LighthouseFriends says the bell and its materials reached the island in September 1896.[^2] Keepers had been striking a small bell by hand in fog since 1882, and the new bell was struck the same way.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists 32 principal keeper stints between 1835 and 1972, held by 29 men.[^3] The table on this page carries every stint. The stories below are the ones the sources tell in detail.

Henry K. M. Bowers was the first keeper and stayed until 1841.[^1][^3] Ephraim S. Fly kept the light from 1841 to 1845. LighthouseFriends says the station's rainwater cisterns were useless to him, which forced him to row ashore for water. He built a landing wharf on the island with twenty-five dollars of his own money.[^2]

Obadiah Brown kept the light twice, from 1845 to 1849 and again from 1855 to 1857.[^3] LighthouseFriends quotes a newspaper, which it does not name, printed after Brown's death in 1857. The paper said that building a wall on the island had injured his stomach and resulted in his death. LighthouseFriends adds that Brown was said to be "universally respected."[^2]

Henry Wiley was keeper for fourteen years, from 1882 to 1896.[^3] He died in September 1896, two days after a fog bell and materials for a bell tower arrived on the island.[^2]

Howard M. Gilley kept the light from 1896 to 1909.[^3] LighthouseFriends names him as the father of Louise, the fourteen-year-old who rowed out to a capsized boat in 1897. That rescue is described below.[^2]

Aldiverd A. Norton kept the light from 1909 to 1919, and Elmer Reed from 1919 to 1938.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the island's closeness to Camden made it a choice station, and that keepers such as Wiley, Gilley, Norton and Reed stayed on for long stints.[^2]

Myrick R. Morrison kept the light from 1938 to 1950, during the Second World War. LighthouseFriends says six Coast Guardsmen were stationed on the island as lookouts during the war, and that Morrison was said to have treated them like sons.[^2][^3]

After 1950 the station changed keepers quickly. The roster lists fifteen stints that began between 1950 and 1971.[^3] Francis X. McCarthy was keeper from 1960 to 1962.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A gale in February 1842 cracked the first tower from top to bottom.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that an engineer named I. W. P. Lewis found the crack on a visit that year, and that the tower still leaked after the lantern was repaired.[^2] In 1855 the tower was sheathed in wood and shingled as a stopgap measure.[^2]

On 10 June 1897 the Republican Journal of Belfast printed a story under the heading "Maine's Grace Darling." The paper reported that two boys had capsized in a rowboat "on Saturday last." Their screams brought the lighthouse keeper's fourteen-year-old daughter, Louise Gilley, to the shore, and she got into a boat at once and rowed out to them.[^10] Unable to get the boys into her boat without capsizing it, she had them hold on to its sides and paddled ashore.[^10] The paper says that without her help the boys would have drowned.[^10] LighthouseFriends names the keeper as Howard M. Gilley.[^2]

In 1962 an Air Force T-33 jet ran out of fuel and crashed in Penobscot Bay.[^2] LighthouseFriends says keeper McCarthy pulled the pilot from the water near the island, and that a search began for a second crewman who had bailed out farther out at sea.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard automated the light in 1972, and the nomination says the station was closed as a manned lighthouse after that.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends says Camden residents persuaded the Coast Guard in 1970 to turn the station over to the town rather than auction it to a private buyer. The town took the island and its buildings in 1972, except the tower.[^2]

The town agreed to accept ownership of the tower in November 1997 under Maine's Lights Program.[^2] A fog bell on long-term loan from the Coast Guard was dedicated on Memorial Day weekend in 2000 to the men and women who served at the station.[^2]

A caretaker couple began looking after the island in 1980. When the family's stewardship ended, LighthouseFriends says, the station fell back under the town's attention.[^2] In December 2023 the Town of Camden and the Camden Island Lighthouse Foundation agreed to pay jointly for a third-party assessment of the property to guide its restoration.[^2]

## Today

The Town of Camden owns the island and the light.[^2][^3] The island is a town park.[^2] LighthouseFriends says its grounds are open and the dwelling and tower are closed.[^2] The Digital Archive's record lists the station as not open to the public.[^3]

There is no dock, so the island is reached by boat. The town's harbor office warns that visitors who come by paddle or dinghy need proper footwear to scramble ashore and up onto the trail.[^11] The island is best seen from the water, and several harbor cruises pass the light.[^2] From land, the harbor office suggests a four-minute drive or a twenty-minute stroll down Bay View Street, parking at the bottom of Beacon Avenue, and then taking the Curtis Island Overlook path.[^11]

The fourth-order Fresnel lens that was used in the light from 1857 to 1994 is on display at the Camden Public Library.[^2] A fog bell dedicated to the island's keepers is on display along the Camden waterfront.[^2]
