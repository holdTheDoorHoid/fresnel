Forty Mile Point Light is a red-brick lighthouse and keepers' duplex on the Lake Huron shore of Presque Isle County, Michigan, about six miles north of Rogers City by Lighthousefriends' reckoning, seven by the society's driving directions. It was built in 1896 to fill a gap in the chain of lights between Cheboygan and Presque Isle, and it still shows a light from a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^1][^2]

## The place

By the early 1890s the Light-House Board saw a hole in the coast of northern Lake Huron. Its report for 1891-92, renewing a recommendation made in its two previous reports, said the Cheboygan and Presque Isle lights were about 50 miles apart, with "no aid to navigation between them," and that a station with a fog signal and a coast light was needed about midway.[^3] The Board put the proper site on "the prominence of the coast eastward of Hammond Bay," about equidistant between Cheboygan and Presque Isle.[^3]

The name caused confusion afterwards. A later Commissioner of Lighthouses report noted that the station had been placed on the site marked Forty Mile Point on the county-survey charts, while "Sailing masters expected the station to be placed at Nine Mile Point, near the entrance to the Straits of Mackinac."[^4]

## Building the light

Congress authorised a light and fog signal here in February 1893 but made no appropriation; the Board's 1894 report repeated the request, adding a note that $25,000 had been appropriated by the act approved 18 August 1894.[^5] The Board's 1895 report says negotiations for the site began in September and an offer to sell it for $200 was accepted; contracts were let for two fog-signal boilers.[^6] The size of the parcel is given differently: Lighthousefriends says nearly twenty-seven acres, and the lighthouse society says over 22 acres.[^2][^7]

The tender *Amaranth* landed a work party and materials on 5 July 1896, and a 120-foot (37 m) landing dock with a tramway was built to carry them ashore.[^2] The tramway needed work within a few years: the Board's 1900 report says its rebuilding over a gap to the boat landing was finished in November 1899.[^8]

Lighthousefriends calls the station very similar to Big Bay Point Light, also built in 1896, and the society says the building was modelled on that light, then going up near Marquette.[^2][^7] A two-story duplex of red brick holds a 12-foot-square (3.7 m) tower, 52 feet (16 m) high, centred on the lake side, so the keeper and his assistant each had an apartment with its own door into the tower.[^2][^7] The society notes that the walls are two layers of brick with an air space between them, "the only insulation," and that skylights over the stairways let each keeper watch the light from inside his own apartment.[^7] A red-brick fog-signal building near the shore housed ten-inch steam whistles.[^2]

Sources differ on when the work finished: the society says construction was complete by 12 November 1896, while Lighthousefriends says the lighthouse was finished in October and the work wrapped up on 23 November, after which a custodian was left in charge for the winter.[^7][^2] The first lens was a fourth-order Sautter lens that turned once a minute to give white flashes ten seconds apart.[^2] The Light-House Board's list of new lights records a fourth-order light established on 30 April 1897.[^9]

## The keepers

Xavier Rains moved from Round Island Light, on the St. Marys River, to be the first head keeper, and the society says he arrived around 4 January 1897 with his wife, Evelyn Smith Rains, his sister and his mother. Edward J. Lane came from Devils Island Light on Lake Superior as first assistant, about the same time, with his wife, Tine Dissett Lane, and their small child.[^7] Rains's first journal entry, as the society prints it, reads: "We got everything ready and lit up at sunset. Raining all day and night. Blowing almost a gale from the North."[^7]

The Light-House Board's report for the year records that the 10-inch steam whistle, also established on 30 April 1897, ran some 107 hours in that first fiscal year and used about 3 tons of coal.[^10] Lighthousefriends gives a later yearly norm of about 225 hours and seven tons.[^2]

The USLHS archive lists Rains as head keeper from 1897 to 1911 and John E. Smith, his assistant from 1906, as head keeper from 1911 to 1929.[^1] Lighthousefriends adds that Rains was reportedly transferred elsewhere in 1911 but was back as Smith's assistant in 1912, serving until he retired in 1921 at the age of seventy.[^2][^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index lists "Forty-Mile Point light station keeper John E. Smith, efficiency pennant received."[^11] Lighthousefriends dates the district's model-station awards to 1912, 1913 and 1925.[^2]

Water was a problem. By 1910, Lighthousefriends says, the windmill supplying the dwelling had blown over, and water had to be carried up from the lake.[^2]

Clarence P. Tupper was assistant from 1927 to 1932 and head keeper from 1936 to 1943.[^1] His last journal entry, on 31 December 1943, reads: "Keeper working on inventory a.m. Went to town to get car from garage."[^7] The Coast Guard took charge the next day.[^2] The archive roster continues with Coast Guard-era head keepers to 1968.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 19 October 1905 the wooden steamer *Joseph S. Fay*, towing the barge *D. P. Rhodes* and loaded with iron ore, ran into heavy seas on Lake Huron. The strain on the towline became too great, and the barge went adrift, carrying away part of the steamer's stern; the *Fay* turned for shore and struck a sandbar, and her forward part, with the captain and ten crew aboard, washed up by the station.[^2][^12] NOAA's account says winds topped 60 miles an hour and that the towline was cut, where the lighthouse society and Lighthousefriends say it broke.[^13][^12][^2] NOAA also has the surviving crew escaping in a lifeboat after a wave swept the mate overboard, while the society and Lighthousefriends have most of the crew coming ashore on the wreck's pilot house.[^13][^12][^2]

The keeper's log for 20 October recorded: "At 8:30 p.m. last night the steamer, J. S. Fay, came ashore here in a sinking condition. She soon broke up." The mate, David Syze of Port Huron, was lost; on 4 December the log notes that the assistant found a dead man on the beach about a mile up, thought to be the mate.[^12] The *Rhodes* and her crew survived.[^12]

In 1931 the steam whistles gave way to a compressed-air plant with diesel engines and diaphones. On 15 July 1931 the tender *Amaranth* steamed away toward Spectacle Reef to test the new signal; the Bulletin reported the test, and Lighthousefriends reproduces its results: the high note was heard to 7 miles and the low note to 11.[^14][^2]

## After the keepers

Lighthousefriends says the light was electrified in 1942 and the station automated and de-staffed on 1 January 1969, and that after vandalism the Coast Guard moved quickly to dispose of the property.[^2] The county received all but 2.4 acres in the "Legacy of Parks" program on 16 August 1971, and the society says the station, particularly the lighthouse, then began to deteriorate.[^2][^7] The lighthouse was declared surplus in 1996, and the county received the deed in November 1998; the county and the 40 Mile Point Lighthouse Society have restored the site since.[^7][^2] The site was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 19 July 1984.[^7]

## Today

Lighthouse Park is owned by Presque Isle County and managed by the 40 Mile Point Lighthouse Society.[^2] The park is open year-round; the lighthouse museum, gift shop and the pilot house of the steamer *Calcite*, built in 1912 and moved here, are open on the society's seasonal schedule, from Memorial Day weekend to mid-October, admission free.[^15][^16][^7] One apartment is the museum and the other is occupied by a full-time caretaker.[^7] The Fresnel lens is a fourth-order lens engraved Henri Lepaute, Paris, 1872, owned by the Coast Guard and still in service; the society says old logbooks make it probably the third or fourth light installed in this tower.[^17] Lighthousefriends, which names the maker as Henry-Lepaute, says a new Fresnel lens went in on 15 May 1919 when the light's characteristic was changed.[^2] The remains of the *Joseph S. Fay* lie just off and on the beach nearby, and the wreck is on the National Register.[^13][^12]
