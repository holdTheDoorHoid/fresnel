Gloucester Breakwater Light, also called Dog Bar Breakwater Light, is a light on the granite end of the Dog Bar Breakwater, on the east side of the entrance to Gloucester Harbor, Massachusetts.[^1][^2] Its electric fog bell, put in service in July 1911, was the last of three electric fog bells the Lighthouse Service had established by 1912.[^9][^1]

## Building the light

Dog Bar Reef runs shoreward from Eastern Point, and ships kept running onto it after Eastern Point Light was lit in 1832.[^1] The Army Corps of Engineers built the breakwater from Eastern Point Light between 1894 and 1905, as Congress provided the money.[^1]

A light went up on the unfinished breakwater before it was finished. On 1 July 1901 a fixed red lens lantern was shown from an iron spindle on a pile of riprap, and a storm on 5 November 1901 carried the lantern away.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 lists a "Gloucester Breakwater gas buoy" at the harbor entrance as "Extinguished; relighted August 21, 1900."[^3] LighthouseFriends says a gas buoy was placed off the end of the breakwater after the post light proved hard to maintain, and it gives no date for the buoy.[^1] The two sources differ on the order of events: the Board's entry shows a buoy in service in August 1900, before the lantern was lost.[^3][^1]

A wooden tripod with a fixed white light, known as Gloucester Breakwater Light or Dog Bar Light, was set on a granite mound at the end of the breakwater on 4 December 1905.[^1][^2]

In 1910 $10,000 was requested from Congress to improve the light and add a fog signal. LighthouseFriends quotes the request as saying the lens light on the breakwater "has not been adequate to prevent collisions with the breakwater."[^1] A white house and a tower on a square skeleton base were built at the breakwater's end soon after.[^1] On 1 July 1911 an electric fog bell went into operation. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1912 names the Gloucester bell as the last of the electric fog bells established, the others being at Cape Charles and Marquette. It describes the bell and its striker as housed in a small house on the outer end of the jetty or breakwater.[^9]

## The keepers

Keepers at Eastern Point Light were given the added duty of caring for the new breakwater light after it was established in 1905.[^1] No keeper is named for the breakwater light itself.

When the schooner Flo F. Mader struck the breakwater in December 1902, LighthouseFriends says the crew was rescued by Dolliver's Neck lifesavers and housed by Keeper George F. Bailey at Eastern Point Lighthouse.[^1][^6] The Worcester Spy, writing the next day, says the captain and crew were landed at Eastern Point.[^6]

In November 1902 the schooner Beta struck about 500 yards from Eastern Point Light on a Saturday night. A Boston Globe item of 23 November 1902, printed in the Life-Saving Service's report for that season, thanks Captain George E. Bailey and Assistant Keeper Wheeler of the Eastern Point light for their prompt response.[^5] LighthouseFriends names the Eastern Point keeper as George F. Bailey. The two sources differ on the middle initial, and this story does not decide whether they name the same man.[^1][^5]

In the year covered by the Life-Saving Service's 1906 report, the breakwater light went out at 2 in the morning. The station keeper informed the Eastern Point light keeper, who went out and relighted it.[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 12 August 1902 the gasoline launch Marion stranded on Dog Bar breakwater, a mile southeast of the Gloucester station, at 8:40 in the evening. The Life-Saving Service report attributes the casualty to a mistake in locating lights.[^4]

On 5 December 1902 the Nova Scotian schooner Flo F. Mader struck the unfinished breakwater in a northeast gale.[^6] The Worcester Spy reports that Capt. David Mader and his crew of six were rescued by the Dolliver's Neck lifesavers, and that the blow damaged the vessel beyond hope of repair. The Spy adds that the schooner was floated that afternoon and was leaking badly.[^6] LighthouseFriends calls it a total loss.[^1]

On 13 November 1904 the schooner Nautilus came to anchor off the breakwater in a heavy northeast gale. Her anchor failed to hold, and she dragged onto the breakwater and was lost. The surfmen reached her by a mooring to windward and rescued her crew of four.[^7]

In early March 1931 a severe northeasterly gale swept the Massachusetts shore. At Gloucester Breakwater, granite blocks were washed off the breakwater, breaking the cable that fed the light and fog bell.[^1]

On 17 February 1943 a fire destroyed nearly all of the fog bell house. LighthouseFriends says it was soon rebuilt.[^1]

## Today

The US Lighthouse Society's record lists the Coast Guard as owner and manager of the light.[^2] In 1985 the Eastern Point and breakwater lights were automated, and a sensor was placed at the end of the breakwater to start the electronic fog signal.[^1] Electronic horns later replaced the bell. LighthouseFriends says they formed a chorus with "Mother Ann's Cow," the name of the whistling buoy to the south.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the breakwater is part of a 53-acre nature preserve owned by Massachusetts Audubon. The grounds are open, and the tower is closed. The breakwater is open from sunup to sundown in good weather, and it is closed to the public when the weather turns foul and the fog signal sounds.[^1]
