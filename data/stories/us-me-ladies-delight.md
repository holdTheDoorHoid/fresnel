Ladies Delight Light is a small stone tower on a reef island in Cobbosseecontee Lake, near Winthrop, Maine. The Cobbosseecontee Yacht Club built it in 1908.[^7] A Boston paper reported that the power boats on the lake at night needed "some light fixed to mark the reefs."[^4] The USLHS record lists the club as the light's owner and manager, and the club describes itself as preserving it.[^8][^9]

## The place

By 1909 the lake had a summer crowd. The club's report counts "no less than one hundred and twenty-five cottages" and "about a dozen hotels and large boarding houses."[^3] Near the north end of the lake sat Ladies Delight Island, which the report says lay "in the direct line of traffic." Around it lay "jagged reefs, which extend over a space of several acres just below the surface of the water."[^3][^4]

The island's name also needed sorting out. The 1983 National Register nomination says it is "properly named Ladies Delight," not "Packards Ledge," as a survey map had it.[^1]

The island had its own record of danger before the tower. An 1881 description of the lake, printed in the Kennebec Journal and reprinted by the lake association, says that "several years ago" two men, Dr. Babcock and Mr. Sanborn, took shelter under a cedar tree on the island during a shower. The writer describes "a blinding flash" and "the clash of a thunder-bolt," and says the two men met "a tragic death" there, with their bodies found shortly afterward. The article gives no date more exact than "several years ago."[^5]

On 21 July 1908 the Daily Kennebec Journal reported that the lake people hoped the club would act favorably on a light for the island "at the next meeting of the Cobbosseecontee Yacht Club, Saturday."[^2]

## Building the light

The club's report, printed in July 1909, says that in the autumn of 1907 the club began work on the island. It "proceeded to construct a protecting wall" around it. Over the winter, while the ice was firm, loads of rock were hauled out to it.[^3]

The tower contract went to "William W. Jackson of Augusta." A separate contract for supplying and carrying materials went to "Albert E. Farr, of Manchester." The design came from the Lighthouse Engineer's office in Boston.[^3] The 1908 Boston Globe report, as quoted in Forest and Stream, names "Frank Morse" of that office as the designer.[^4] LighthouseFriends calls Morse "a Boston architect" whom Daniel Robinson hired.[^7] The 1983 nomination gives the builder or architect as unknown.[^1]

The sources do not agree on the tower's height. The 1908 Boston Globe report, as quoted by Forest and Stream, describes stonework "about 16 ft. high," with a wooden lantern frame "adding about 9 ft. more." The nomination says the tower is "approximately 20 feet tall." The club's report gives the tower a diameter of nine feet (2.7 m).[^3] The stonework was whitewashed, and the lantern and dome were painted black.[^3] The 1908 Globe report gives the cost of the tower as $400, with another $100 for the seawall.[^4]

The light took some working out. The club's report says a "stationary white light" was tried first, but it could not be told apart from the many lights on shore. A red light was tried next, but cottagers and hotelkeepers hung out red, white and green lights on their verandas too, so "some other distinguishing mark was needed."[^3] The answer was a light that appeared and disappeared, then flashed. Two oil lamps with "reflectors like the headlight of a locomotive" sat on an iron table that turned, driven by "heavy weights falling from the top to the base of the tower." In the report's words, "Thus the lamps revolve providing an unmistakable warning" to navigators.[^3] In 1908 the Boston Globe report found the light in the tower "steady," and said the club meant to install a flashing light the following year.[^4]

The sources differ on how the club held the island. The 1909 report describes "a ninety-nine year lease" from the owner, Mrs. Clifford B. Farr of Philadelphia. The 1908 Globe report says Mrs. Farr "deeded it to the club for a period of 99 years," and calls her Catherine L. Farr, wife of Dr. Clifford B. Farr.[^3][^4] Under the lease the club had to keep a light on the island each summer, or "the island reverts to the lessor."[^3] The 1983 nomination says the tower was funded by Daniel Robinson, "the first commodore of the Cobbosseecontee Yacht Club," and gives the club as the owner.[^1]

## The keepers

When the tower was new, its light was "two kerosene lamps which had to be tended every day."[^1] A volunteer from the yacht club visited the island each evening to service the light.[^7]

The lake association's history records the 2005 rededication. The tower was "formally dedicated on July 16, 2005" by "retiring long-time CYC Commodore, Captain Hugh Stephens, also Keeper of the Light."[^6]

## After the keepers

In the 1930s a wind-powered generator ran an electric light in the lantern room.[^7] By the time of the nomination, "storage batteries lasting 13 days" powered an electric lamp, and "an underwater cable" served "a revolving light."[^1] The light is put out in winter, when the lake freezes over.[^1]

The tower was listed on the National Register in 1984.[^6] In 2001 a severe list in the tower was corrected. In July 2005 a new marine-grade aluminum top went on, replacing "a wooden one that had been in service for about fifteen years."[^6][^7] A bronze plaque listing donors was installed on the tower in 2008, and the tower was power-washed and refinished in 2011.[^7]

## Today

The Cobbosseecontee Yacht Club describes itself as a nonprofit that exists "to preserve, maintain, and protect this historic lighthouse."[^9] The USLHS Digital Archive lists the club as owner and manager, and says the site is not open to the public.[^8] LighthouseFriends says the Cobbosseecontee Lake Association maintains the lighthouse, so the upkeep is described differently by different sources.[^7] The lake association says the light "flashes 365 nights a year."[^6]

The grounds and tower are closed.[^7] The lighthouse "can be seen distantly from various points along the shoreline, but a boat is really needed to get a good view of the lighthouse."[^7]
