Two towers, one square and one octagonal, joined by a brownstone-and-brick building that looks more like a fort than a lighthouse, stand on the Navesink Highlands at Highlands, Monmouth County, New Jersey. They mark the way into New York Harbor, and over a hundred years of service the station was where American lighthouses first used Fresnel lenses and an electric arc light, and where Marconi set up a wireless aerial.[^1][^3][^4][^9]

## The place

The Highlands of Navesink is a peninsula of high ground, about 200 feet (61 m) at its height, pushing toward the Atlantic. It is shielded on the east by the barrier beach of Sandy Hook and the Shrewsbury River, with Sandy Hook Bay to the north and the Navesink River to the south.[^5] When Henry Hudson's ship *Half Moon* came by in 1609, Robert Juet wrote in the ship's journal that this was "a very good Land to fall with, and a pleasant Land to see."[^5] The National Historic Landmark nomination says there is no higher land on the coast between here and Florida, which is what made the hills useful to mariners hunting for the narrow entrances to the harbor.[^5]

Long before there was a lighthouse, the hills were a lookout. In 1746 the colonists kept a beacon here to warn New York of French warships. In a letter dated 27 September 1746, John Hamilton of Perth Amboy told New York's council that the beacon had been accidentally set on fire one night and that nobody on the New York side had noticed, which made him think the watch there had been negligent.[^4]

A lighthouse had stood on Sandy Hook since 1764.[^5] The twin lights of Navesink were arranged so that mariners would not mistake them for the single light on Sandy Hook, or for the lightship stationed nearby; the nomination puts Sandy Hook Light about five miles to the north.[^1]

## Building the light

The first station went up in 1827 and 1828. On 25 June 1827 Jonathan Thompson, the Collector of the Port of New York and Superintendent of Lighthouses, signed a contract with Charles H. Smith of Stonington, Connecticut, for two octagonal towers of "good blue split stone" and a keeper's house, for $8,440. The towers stood 320 feet (98 m) apart, with their lights 246 feet (75 m) above high water.[^2] The government had bought about two and three-quarter acres from Nimrod Woodward for $600, and David Melville of Newport, Rhode Island, supplied the lamps and reflectors, fitting out the towers for $1,840.[^5] (LighthouseFriends gives $1,850 for the lamps and reflectors.[^8]) The buildings were finished by 9 May 1828.[^2]

The station's first claim to fame came in 1841. Commodore Matthew Perry had been sent to France in 1838 to obtain two Fresnel lenses, and the pair, one of the first order and one of the second, went into the Navesink towers, which made them the first lights in the country to use the new French optic.[^3] The Lighthouse Service's own bulletin of 1915 later called the 1841 first-order lens "the first lighthouse lens of the Fresnel system used in the United States", and said it stayed in service until 1898.[^11] The lenses had been meant for Sandy Hook and for the Isles of Shoals, but a Mr. Bernard, the French artisan who came to install them, found the Sandy Hook lantern too small and the tower too low.[^3] The installation cost $24,000, and Stephen Pleasonton, the Fifth Auditor of the Treasury, told the House Committee on Commerce that pilots reported seeing the light forty miles away in clear weather. He also warned that such a lamp could go out if the keeper was not "an intelligent mechanic".[^3] Sources disagree about which tower got which lens. The nomination puts the first-order fixed lens in the south tower and the second-order revolving lens in the north.[^3] LighthouseFriends reverses it.[^8]

By 1852 the buildings themselves were failing. The Light-House Board's report that year called the towers "very badly constructed of rubble stone", leaking and cracked, and one of the lighthouse inspectors wrote that a common lead pencil could be pushed about six inches into the wall.[^2] In 1857 the Board warned that the towers might not stand much longer against winter storms and estimated $72,941 for replacements.[^5] Congress appropriated that sum on 20 June 1860.[^1]

The new station was designed by Joseph Lederle, who sent in his sketches in August 1860 and then oversaw the work as the Board's superintendent of construction.[^3] Unlike the old pair, it was a single building: the two towers, 223 feet (68 m) apart centre to centre, were tied together by keepers' quarters and workrooms. (LighthouseFriends gives 228 feet.[^8]) The outside was reddish-brown sandstone from Belleville, near Newark, floated by barge to Highlands, with brick behind.[^1] The nomination calls the design a fortress and says no other American lighthouse repeats it.[^3] The new lights were first shown on 1 May 1862.[^6] The old towers were pulled down, and the nomination says their foundations are all that is known to remain, found in test pits dug in 1999.[^2] The keepers' quarters followed in 1863, eighteen rooms for four keepers and their families, and the Board's report for that year said the new station presented "a highly ornate and substantial appearance."[^1][^6]

Sources do not agree on how the lights were arranged in 1862. The nomination says the south tower kept the old first-order lens and the north tower got a new fixed first-order light in place of a second-order revolving one.[^1][^6] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1931 says both towers showed fixed first-order lights, replacing one fixed and one flashing light in the old towers.[^9] The Twin Lights Historical Society's FAQ says the south tower held a first-order lens and the north tower a second-order one.[^14]

The station went on being a testing ground. In 1883 a mineral-oil lamp was tried in a first-order light here, in place of lard oil. The Board's report, as the nomination quotes it, called this "the first instance in which mineral oil, has been used in a first-order lamp in this country."[^6] The nomination puts that lamp in the north tower, while LighthouseFriends says south; the 1931 Bulletin says only that mineral-oil lamps were soon in both.[^6][^8][^9] Two oil houses followed, in 1890 and 1892, to keep the fuel away from the buildings.[^2]

The biggest change came in 1898. The Board bought a "bivalve" lens made by Henri Lepaute that had been shown at the Chicago exposition, built of 386 separate lenses, 9 feet (2.7 m) across and 5 feet (1.5 m) high, and shaped like a clam shell. It was made to work with an electric arc lamp.[^6] With no current available, a powerhouse was built behind the station between 22 April and 30 June 1898, and an electrician was authorized to serve as a keeper to look after the machinery.[^1][^6] The light was given as 25,000,000 candlepower, a flash every five seconds, visible 22 miles at sea.[^6] The Bulletin of 1931 called it the most powerful light ever installed in a lighthouse in the United States.[^9]

Neighbors did not welcome it. The Board's report for the year recorded "many complaints" from residents of "great discomfort and annoyance caused by the brilliancy of the flash", and three panels of the lantern were darkened.[^10] The north tower's fixed white light was discontinued on 15 September 1898 but held in reserve in case the electric light failed.[^10] The Bulletin of 1931 gives 2 June 1898 for the south tower's old light going out and the north tower's being discontinued at the same time, while LighthouseFriends says the arc light began on 30 June 1898.[^9][^8]

The arc light ran until 1917, when the worn machinery was due for costly repairs and the Service switched to incandescent oil vapor at 710,000 candlepower.[^9] Commercial electricity came later, and the light was raised to 9,000,000 candlepower with three 500-watt bulbs in the second-order lens, as the 1931 Bulletin announced.[^9] The nomination dates the return to electricity at 1939; the Bulletin says the oil-vapor lamp was used from 1917 to 1924, and LighthouseFriends says commercial power arrived in 1924.[^6][^9][^8]

The hill was also a place to send messages. A semaphore tower of the Merchants' Exchange went up in 1829, and telegraph companies followed.[^4] In 1899 Guglielmo Marconi set up an antenna and receiving station here, hired by the *New York Herald* to report the America's Cup yacht races off Sandy Hook, and the station became the first commercial wireless station in the United States.[^4] The Navy later built its own wireless house on the grounds, then abandoned the site by 1909.[^4] In 1935 the Army Signal Corps used the towers to test heat-seeking equipment for tracking ships, and the *Long Branch Record* ran the headline "Ray Which Detects Ships Off Shore To Be Tested Secretly at Highlands."[^4] In 1939 the Signal Corps returned to test long-range radar, with the Lighthouse Service letting it take over half the reservation.[^4]

## The keepers

The first keeper was Joseph Doty of Somerville, New Jersey, appointed in 1828 at $600 a year. The nomination also calls him Joshua in one place.[^5][^2] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists 17 principal keepers between 1828 and 1952, from Doty to Murphy L. Rockette.[^7]

After the Fresnel lenses came, the principal keeper had four assistants, each paid $30 a month.[^5] The Light-House Board's 1852 report described how they shared the night: two assistants for each tower, in two watches, the first from lighting until midnight and the second from midnight until daylight. The principal keeper, it said, "generally retires to bed at nine o'clock, and is only called when something goes wrong." The Board thought one principal and two assistants would be enough.[^5] In later years the keepers also looked after buoys in Sandy Hook Bay and the Shrewsbury and Navesink rivers, using a dock at the foot of the hill to keep the station boat and supplies.[^2] In 1927 the Service replaced dolphin lights in the Navesink and Shrewsbury rivers, which the ice carried away each winter, with fourteen gas buoys that the keepers of Navesink Light Station looked after.[^12]

Not every keeper met the standard. According to LighthouseFriends, in 1872 head keeper Taber Chadwick and his assistants were dismissed after complaints that the assistants had neglected their duty, and that one night they had abandoned the light, which would likely have gone out had the neglect not been found by accident.[^8] The archive lists Chadwick as principal keeper from 1869 to 1872.[^7]

When the electric plant was installed in 1898, the archive lists two "mechanicians" that year, Burtis (Bertie) S. Newton and Alfred C. Pickell, besides the keepers.[^7] The new head keeper that year was Robert A. Bishop, who stayed until 1917.[^7] LighthouseFriends says he had been a locomotive engineer on the New York, New Haven and Hartford Railroad, hired because the generator needed particular skills, and it prints his remark to a visiting writer about birds that fell stunned against the lens glass in the migration season, and the neighborhood cats that came to find them.[^8]

Murphy Rockette's career ran through the quiet end of the station. The archive lists him as second assistant from 1921, first assistant from 1928 and principal keeper from 1931 to 1952.[^7] LighthouseFriends says he came to the Lighthouse Service after Army and Navy service and a job at the National Biscuit Company, and that his wife Elise's condition for marrying him was that he work on land. It says the family began with an outhouse, a pump and kerosene lamps and later gained indoor plumbing, central heat and electricity.[^8] The nomination dates the modern bathrooms to the 1930s.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he retired from the Coast Guard in 1951 but was allowed to stay on until the station closed for good the next year.[^8]

## After the keepers

The nomination says the station's importance shrank in the 1930s because of better lightships off New York, and that radar and improved floating aids finished the job.[^6] The big lens went out of service in 1949 and was crated and sent to the Staten Island depot at St. George; later it was given to the Boston Museum of Science and in 1979 came back to Navesink under the Twin Lights Historical Society.[^6] The Coast Guard declared the station surplus in 1953. In 1954 the General Services Administration passed it to the Borough of Highlands as a museum and park, and the Historical Society was formed to run the museum with volunteers.[^6] That year the Coast Guard also donated an old Life-Saving Service boathouse from Spermaceti Cove on Sandy Hook, which was trucked to the site.[^2]

The Borough could not keep up with the maintenance and asked the State of New Jersey to take over. The State Park Service, the Society and the Rumson Garden Club raised close to a million dollars, and restoration began in 1978.[^6] The nomination reports the museum exhibits were redesigned in 1987.[^6] LighthouseFriends adds a $460,000 restoration of the towers and exterior in 2002 and a full museum renovation in 2015.[^8] The Secretary of the Interior designated Navesink Light Station a National Historic Landmark on 17 February 2006.[^1]

The north tower was lit again in 1962. The nomination says a fifth-order Fresnel lens dating to 1881 was installed then, and the light is a private aid to navigation.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes the north tower's lens as sixth-order and in use until 2007.[^8] None of these sources says whether the north tower is lit today.

## Today

Twin Lights State Historic Site is owned and operated by the New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection, Division of Parks, Forestry and Historic Sites, with the Twin Lights Historical Society as its friends group.[^14] The museum and grounds are free, and the grounds are open daily from 9:00 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. all year. From Memorial Day to 31 October there is a fee to climb the towers, $5 for adults and $2 for children under 12; hours change with the season (the society lists Wednesday to Sunday only after Labor Day), so check ahead.[^13] The society's FAQ says the north tower is free to climb in the off season, though its visitors page and FAQ differ on when that season begins.[^14][^13] The 1898 bivalve lens is on display in the powerhouse and, according to the 2006 nomination, the principal keeper's apartment is staff housing and the north wing holds the museum.[^1]
