The western tower of the Two Lights at Cape Elizabeth, Maine, is the one that went dark. It is a cast-iron tower of 1874 standing about 300 yards (270 m) from its twin, which still shows a light; the western tower was put out for good in 1924 and is now private property.[^1][^2]

## The place

Cape Elizabeth is the point at the southwestern limit of Casco Bay, in Cumberland County, Maine, about five miles southeast of Portland Harbor.[^3] A 50-foot (15 m) stone day beacon went up on the headland in 1811; the National Register nomination attributes it to Gen. Henry Dearborn, while Jeremy D'Entremont's history says it was completed by the contractors Edward Robinson and John F. Bartlett.[^1][^3]

When the government put lights here in 1828 it chose two, one fixed and one revolving, so that a captain could tell the station from Wood Island Light (revolving) to the south and Portland Head Light (fixed) to the north.[^3] The western tower was the revolving one.[^2][^3]

## Building the light

D'Entremont gives $4,500 appropriated for the station in February 1828; Lighthousefriends adds an earlier $3,000 in March 1827.[^2][^3] The mason Jeremiah Berry built both towers, which were 895 feet (273 m) apart.[^2][^3] Each was an octagonal rubblestone tower of about 65 feet (20 m), topped with a wrought-iron lantern; the western one held 14 lamps with 14-inch reflectors, turned to give a flashing light 132 feet (40 m) above the high-water mark.[^3] Both were lit by the end of October 1828.[^3] The work had been left late: the local superintendent, John Chandler, wrote the next year that the towers "were built so late in the season that the mortar froze," as Lighthousefriends quotes his letter.[^2]

The stone did not last. In 1843 the civil engineer I. W. P. Lewis reported on the station, and among his findings was that the western tower's rotating light mechanism was defective.[^2] Four years later, the Portland poet Henry Wadsworth Longfellow is recorded as having visited the station and climbed the western tower.[^3] In 1854 new Fresnel lenses were put in both towers.[^1] On 1 August 1855 the western light was put out and the eastern one changed to occulting; the single revolving light that resulted was often hard to tell from Wood Island's, and on 1 April 1856 the two lights were restored, with Wood Island's changed from white to red.[^1][^3] In 1865 the towers were repainted to make them easier to recognise by day: the western one got a single large vertical red stripe.[^3]

In 1872 the Lighthouse Board announced that the two towers had deteriorated to the point that they had to be rebuilt.[^3] A congressional appropriation of $30,000 paid for a matched pair of cast-iron towers in 1874, made in segments at the Portland Machine Works.[^3][^1] The US Lighthouse Society's archive names one contractor for the western tower, Ira Winn, with no dates or other detail.[^4] The new towers stood about 67 feet (20 m) tall (the National Register nomination says 65) and were fitted with Italianate detail; Lighthousefriends gives the distance between them as 923 feet (281 m), the nomination as 300 yards.[^1][^2][^3] The western light was a second-order Fresnel lens, a revolving one according to Lighthousefriends.[^2][^4]

In October 1882 the Light-House Board resolved to put the western light out again and move its revolving machinery to the east tower, in an item that goes on to mention a saving of $3,000 a year.[^5] The Portland Daily Press of 28 October said the action, "it is said," had been "rendered necessary by the reduction in the appropriation" for keepers' pay.[^6] Portland's Board of Trade made a formal protest, and remonstrances were to be circulated in the coast towns.[^6] A letter in the Press of 9 November, headed "A Reply from Capt. Deering," answers one from an Admiral Wyman, which it quotes as holding that, in an age of distinctive characteristics, two lights to mark a headland were unnecessary.[^7] The protest worked: the nomination credits Maine's congressman Thomas B. Reed, and D'Entremont says the light was relit after complaints that the remaining one was too easily confused with Wood Island.[^1][^3] Lighthousefriends dates the discontinuance to 1883, and the accounts do not give the date the western light shone again.[^2]

## The keepers

A few glimpses of the western tower's keepers survive. During the Civil War the assistant in charge of the western light, Asbury Staples, enlisted in the Second Maine Battery Light Artillery; D'Entremont writes that his father Michael Staples, also an assistant keeper, asked that his other children be appointed, and that Michael's teenaged daughter Amelia and her younger brother Charles became responsible for the light and its equipment.[^3] Amelia and Charles also draped the towers in black at the news of Lincoln's assassination.[^3]

Lighthousefriends says that in 1878 the first assistant lived in a frame dwelling near the western tower.[^2] By the Board's report for 1893 the station had four families but only three dwellings, two near the northeast tower and one near the southwest one. The Board asked again for a fourth house there, for the second of the two assistants who attended the light in the southwest tower, so that he could be properly housed "in the severe winter weather of that climate."[^8] It was renewing a request made in earlier reports.[^8] D'Entremont says nothing was done until 1901.[^3] That year the western keeper's dwelling was enlarged and rearranged into two sets of quarters, for the first and third assistants, and the covered way to the western tower was rebuilt.[^9]

## After the keepers

In 1924 the western light was extinguished for good.[^1] The Lighthouse Service's own account, in its Bulletin of 1932, lists Cape Elizabeth among the Atlantic coast's twin lights whose duplicate light was discontinued, in 1924, and explains that the twins had been built to tell one lighthouse from another, which modern revolving lenses did better.[^10]

The tower lost its lantern. By the time of the National Register nomination (1974), a box-like observation tower with two narrow tiers of windows had replaced the glass lantern room, work D'Entremont and the nomination both tie to the Second World War.[^1][^3] The government sold the tower, with several buildings and 10.5 acres (4.2 ha), to the highest bidder in 1959.[^3] Who bought it, and when, is told two ways: the nomination reads as though the screen actor Gary Merrill acquired it in 1959, while D'Entremont says Merrill bought it in 1971 for $28,000.[^1][^3] Lighthousefriends says Merrill sold it in 1983, after which a new house was built beside it, and D'Entremont adds that it changed hands twice in the 1980s.[^2][^3]

## Today

The western tower still stands and is privately owned; the tower and grounds are closed to the public.[^2][^4] Lighthousefriends suggests the Lobster Shack restaurant, at the end of Two Lights Road, as perhaps the best public spot for seeing both towers.[^2] The eastern tower is still an aid to navigation.[^2]
