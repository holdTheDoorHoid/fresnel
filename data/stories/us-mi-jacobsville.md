Portage River Light, also called Jacobsville Lighthouse, stands at the mouth of the Portage River on Keweenaw Bay in Houghton County, Michigan. It was first lit in 1856, rebuilt in 1869 and 1870, and put out of service in 1920. It is a private property now, and its grounds are closed to visitors.[^1][^2]

## The place

Ransom Shelden built a store on the north side of the river's mouth in 1846, aimed at miners bound for the interior.[^1] Terry Pepper says Shelden foresaw "the incredible impact" that the 1854 opening of the first lock at the Soo would have on the Keweenaw mines, and that he pressed federal representatives for a lighthouse to guide mariners to the river's entrance.[^2] In February 1853 Senator Alpheus Felch presented a petition from Shelden and others to Congress, "praying the erection of a light-house at the mouth of Portage River."[^1][^2] Congress appropriated $5,000 on 3 March 1853.[^1][^2]

The station stood on about 57 acres just north of the river's mouth.[^1] A survey of the reservation in 1879 found that the station was on private land.[^1] Funds were appropriated in August 1882, and the land around the light was bought the following spring for $700.[^1]

## Building the light

The contract went to Alanson Sweet, Luzerne Ransom and Morgan Shinn on 17 July 1854, and most of the work was done in the 1855 season.[^1] The first tower was a cylindrical rubblestone tower 39 feet (12 m) tall, with an octagonal lantern and a one-and-a-half-story dwelling beside it.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes a cast-iron lantern; Terry Pepper describes a wooden one.[^1][^2] The lens was a fifth-order Fresnel lens by Henry-Lepaute.[^1][^2][^3]

The sources disagree on how the light showed. The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1892, describes the light before 15 August 1891 as "a fixed white, varied by a red flash, every two minutes," and records that on that date it was changed "to a fixed white light, varied by a red flash, every minute."[^4] Pepper describes a single white flash every minute from the original lens.[^2] The sources do not settle which account is right.

LighthouseFriends says the superintending engineer rejected the station for not being "built in conformity to the terms of the contract," but the light was shown anyway in 1856.[^1][^2] Michael W. Lyons became the first keeper that September.[^1][^2][^3]

By 1868 an inspector found the buildings "in a dilapidated condition."[^1] As LighthouseFriends prints the report, "During wet weather the water stands on the floor of the cellar, and it is damp at all times," and the lantern "leaks, and is too small for the apparatus."[^1] Congress provided $12,000 on 3 March 1869 to rebuild the tower and the dwelling.[^1] A temporary structure carried the light while the old buildings came down.[^1][^2]

The new tower had a decagonal lantern and was joined by a covered passage to an eight-room brick dwelling.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives its height as 51 feet (15.5 m). Pepper gives 51 feet (15.5 m) for the vent ball above the foundation.[^1][^2] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record gives 32 feet (9.8 m) from the base of the structure to the lantern's center, which matches neither account.[^3]

Pepper and the restorers' timeline say the tower was local sandstone; the Society's place record gives brick.[^2][^3][^9] The light was first shown from the new tower in the 1870 navigation season.[^2][^3]

In 1907 a seawall about nine feet (2.7 m) high and a hundred feet (30 m) long was built south of the light to slow erosion.[^1]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster for the station runs from 1856 to 1920.[^3] Michael W. Lyons was followed by John Crane (1857), Samuel Stephenson (1857 to 1861) and Guy Earl Edgerton (1861 to 1865).[^3]

In 1865 a protective pier was built at the river mouth with a post light at its outer end. The keeper took on tending it, and his pay rose by $200 a year.[^2] Pepper names "Samuel Stevenson" as that keeper.[^2] The roster has John B. Crebassa starting that year and lists no Stephenson after 1861, so the two accounts do not match.[^2][^3]

George Craig kept the light from 1878 to 1888.[^1][^3] He had discovered the sandstone near Portage Entry in 1861, and a small settlement named for him was formed to support a quarry there.[^1] He came to the light after John B. Crebassa was moved to the new light at Sand Point, and he retired in 1888 at the age of 69.[^1]

Carl John kept it from 1888 to 1895, and Carl (Charles) T. Rosell from 1895 to 1902.[^3]

Franklin W. Witz was keeper from 1902 to 1920, the last one.[^1][^3] He had started at Stannard Rock in 1893 as third assistant, become first assistant in 1897, looked after several range lights at the river, and been placed in charge here in 1902.[^1] The roster also names Frank W. Dolsen as first assistant from 1912 to 1914.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Lights at the river's mouth were hit in these years.[^5][^10] In June 1899 the front range light there was blown down and a temporary light shown in its place.[^10] On 9 May 1900 ice carried away the structures of several of these lights, and their lights were extinguished.[^5]

## After the keepers

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' estimates, printed in the volume covering 1915 to 1922, recommended a light and fog signal on the outer end of the breakwater.[^6] The estimates said of the old light: "The Portage River (Main) Light may be discontinued on the completion of this project, and the dwelling retained as shore station for one keeper."[^6]

The new light at the breakwater was first shown on 1 August 1920, and Witz was transferred to it.[^1][^2] The Commissioner's report for that year said the new light "will make unnecessary the further maintenance of the present Portage River Main Light."[^7] The old station was decommissioned.[^2]

The property went to the State of Michigan on 10 August 1932, with a plan for a park that was never carried out.[^1][^2] It reverted to the Coast Guard on 29 October 1948, and the General Services Administration sold it at auction on 25 November 1958 for $18,251.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends names the buyers as Ray and Sylvia Joynt; Pepper names Joynt Automotive of Alma.[^1][^2]

After the 1958 sale the keeper's cottage was enlarged and converted into a single-family house, the restorers' timeline says.[^9] Mike and Cheri Ditty bought the station in 2004 and ran it as the Jacobsville Lighthouse Inn until the fall of 2019.[^1] It was sold again in 2022, and it is now under restoration.[^9]

## Today

The lighthouse is privately owned.[^1] LighthouseFriends lists the grounds, tower and dwelling as closed.[^1] Pepper's page notes that the light is now a private dwelling, largely surrounded by other private property, and declines to give directions to it.[^2] Please do not visit the property without the owners' permission.

The current owners run a restoration site.[^8] They describe restoring the tower, repairing brick hidden under wooden panels, and putting back double-hung windows in place of modern slide windows.[^8] They also hope to host creative workshops and retreats there.[^8]
