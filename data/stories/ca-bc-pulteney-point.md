Pulteney Point is a sandspit at the southwest corner of Malcolm Island, British Columbia, where Broughton Strait meets Queen Charlotte Strait, and a lighthouse has marked that meeting since 1905; today's is a red-and-white concrete tower.[^1][^2][^6]

The light was set up to guide vessels from Queen Charlotte Sound into Queen Charlotte Strait and Broughton Strait. Parks Canada's heritage statement says the light was established in 1905 to guide shipping and fishing boats, and that by 1940 most boats passing were fishing boats.[^2] The point lies on the island where a Finnish community settled in 1901, according to the heritage statement; Lighthouse Friends says the mariners' petition for a light came at the same time as the Finns' arrival, and names the community's main town as Sointula.[^1][^2]

The first lighthouse was a square wooden building with a lantern on its roof. Its fixed white seventh-order light was first shown on 12 September 1905, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] In 1906 the Department of Marine supplied the keeper with a hand fog horn, to be sounded in thick weather in answer to the fog whistles of steamers.[^3] The following year's report says hand horns went to Pulteney Point and Scarlett Point stations on the same terms.[^4]

The first two keepers were both Finnish immigrants and social democrats, in the words of the heritage statement, and they took on an isolated posting with, as it puts it, a wide range of challenges and relatively low wages.[^2] The first, August Bernhard Makela, who anglicised his name to Austin McKela, had been a leader of the Kalevan Kansa community; Lighthouse Friends says he and his wife Elli kept the light until 1913 and saved it from a wildfire in 1911 that burned the boathouse.[^1] The Department's lightkeeper list for 1912-13 shows "A. McKela" at Pulteney Point.[^5] Toivo Aro, another Finnish settler, took charge and was hired full-time in 1915, the year a mechanical fog bell replaced the hand horn.[^1] Aro answered an inspector's poor mark for tidiness with a letter saying he was sometimes tired of keeping the house in rigid order, and he married Kyllikki Jaakola, who came to Sointula in 1922. Their son Vic was born the next year.[^1]

The Aro family left in 1930 to be nearer town for Vic's schooling. Sula Wartie then served until June 1931, when Oliver "Shorty" Maisonville became permanent keeper; a diesel-driven diaphone horn was added that year.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the wooden tower was replaced around 1943 by the present square concrete tower, attached to a one-storey fog signal building.[^1]

In June 1942, after the shelling of Estevan Point lighthouse, West Coast lights were ordered extinguished. The Alaska Steamship Company's Columbia then ran aground on the unlit point, a third of her length ashore beside the lighthouse, and stayed about three weeks until tugs floated her off at high tide, with Maisonville at the station.[^1] Maisonville served until 1945, when Jonathan Fleming replaced him; Walter Tansky is listed as keeper from 1963 to 1985.[^1]

The Canadian Register of Historic Places describes the square concrete tower rising from the corner of a flat-roofed, single-storey fog-alarm building.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, with the grounds open and the tower closed, and that the approach ends at a gate to the lighthouse property; the tower is not open to visitors, and the gate and the buildings behind it are not to be passed without permission.[^1]
