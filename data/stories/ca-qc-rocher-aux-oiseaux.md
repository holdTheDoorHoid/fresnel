Bird Rock lighthouse stands on a steep island of red sandstone in the Gulf of St. Lawrence, off the Magdalen Islands in Quebec. The keeper Wilfrid Bourque put the nearest of those islands about eleven miles away in a letter of 1909; Lighthouse Friends says the rock lies twenty-five miles northeast of the inhabited islands.[^1][^5] The light was first lit in 1870 for ships on the route up the St. Lawrence, and until 1961 its keepers lived on the rock with their families, among a colony of seabirds.[^3][^5]

## The place

The keeper's 1909 letter gives the summit's area as about five acres and the rock's height as 130 ft (40 m) above the sea. Lighthouse Friends gives the summit as seven acres.[^1][^5] Bourque wrote that the rock could be landed on at one spot only, and only in very calm weather.[^1]

The hazard was traffic. The rock lay on the route of ships coming up the river, and Bourque's letter, as Hubert printed it in 1926, says it had often witnessed maritime disasters.[^1] Lighthouse Friends records at least seventeen shipwrecks there between 1845 and 1857, many of them carrying immigrants.[^5]

Reports recommended a light there from as early as 1830. In May 1861 an engineer, John Page, put the cost of one at $70,000, which was judged too much, and the plan waited until 1869.[^3]

Hubert calls the birds the rock's only population since time immemorial. He describes the ground as so covered with nests and birds that one did not know where to put a foot without crushing some ("si couvert de nids et d'oiseaux qu'on ne savait où placer le pied pour n'en point écraser"; translation).[^1] He adds that before the light was built, Americans climbed the rock in the nesting season and took eggs by the barrel, loading them onto their schooners.[^1]

## Building the light

The money was voted on 22 May 1870, after the minister, Peter Mitchell, had pressed his colleagues in January for lights around the islands.[^3] The Îles archive dates the first tower to 1870, and Lighthouse Friends gives 20 September 1870 as the date it first showed.[^3][^5] Bourque's letter says the government gave the work to a Mr. Fraser, who had to cut a path up the rock before he could hoist the materials, and that he erected the tower in 1871.[^1] The archive and Lighthouse Friends both date it to 1870, so the sources disagree by a year.

Lighthouse Friends names the builders as Daley, Carter & Doolan of Miramichi, New Brunswick, at just under $10,000 before the lens, which the department already had in store. The light used a second-order Fresnel lens.[^5] Bourque's letter says a cannon was placed at the same time to guide ships in fog.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates the fog cannon to 1873.[^5]

In 1887 the first tower was replaced by a hexagonal wooden tower, which Lighthouse Friends gives as about 12 m (39 ft) high.[^3][^5] In 1908 that tower was raised onto a concrete base.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says it was reinforced in concrete and made 3.7 m (12 ft) taller, so that it could be seen above the fog-signal building. Bourque's 1909 letter says the increase was eighteen feet (5.5 m).[^5][^1]

The light itself changed more than once. On 1 October 1903 the Department advertised that the fixed white light would become occulting, showing for 15 seconds and eclipsed for 5, burning petroleum vapour under an incandescent mantle. The keeper appears to have had trouble with the burners and the mechanism, and the light was reported as showing fixed during the late autumn.[^7] The following year's report says the occulting mechanism broke down, and the light was fixed white until a new clockwork machine arrived.[^8] The 1905 report describes the occulting light as showing for 5 seconds and eclipsed for 15, so the Department's own reports disagree on the timing.[^9] Bourque's letter gives a further change: in 1904, the light, fixed until then, became revolving.[^1]

The fog signal began as a cannon. Lighthouse Friends says cartridge guns replaced it in 1895.[^5] In 1906 the Department said it would replace "the existing explosive fog signal" with a compressed-air plant.[^10] Bourque's letter dates the compressed-air whistle that replaced the cannon to 1907.[^1] In 1907 a new fog-alarm building was going up to hold a 5-inch diaphone, and a 3-inch diaphone had already been supplied by the Canadian Fog Signal Company of Toronto, at a price of $7,100.[^11] In 1908 the building was finished and the 3-inch diaphone installed.[^13] Lighthouse Friends dates the diaphone to 1906; the Marine reports date its installation to 1908.[^5]

## The keepers

The first keepers are hard to pin down, because the sources do not agree. Bourque's letter, as Hubert printed it, names a Mr. Fennelton, who refused the post, and a Mr. Guitté, who took it and resigned in 1873 because he could not get used to the hermit's life the post required (translation).[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives another run of names: J. Chapman in 1870, Jacques Couette in 1871, George Preston, who was relieved after a few months, and Peter Whalen from 1873 to 1880.[^5] The Îles archive notes that the keeper's pay in 1881 was lower than the pay once offered to a man named Couette, about ten years earlier.[^3]

Peter Whalen (Hubert calls him Patrick) was keeper when, in early April 1880, he went out on the ice with his son and an assistant to hunt seals. A storm carried the three men away on the ice, and his wife was left alone on the rock. The next day the assistant, Thomas Thivierge, came back half frozen to tell Madame Whalen that her husband and son had died in the night.[^1][^2] Hubert says the loss led the government to lay a submarine telegraph cable from the rock to Grosse-Île the next summer.[^1][^2]

Charles Chiasson, of Havre-aux-Maisons, was named keeper on 25 July 1880, according to Hubert. The archive gives 7 July 1880 as the date he took up the post.[^2][^4]

On a summer day in 1881, visitors came out to the rock: Paul Chenell, his ten-year-old daughter, and Jean Turbide. Chiasson agreed to fire the alarm gun for them. The gun blew up. Chiasson and his seven-year-old son were killed, and Chenell died about two hours later. The blast broke the lighthouse's windows; the archive says every pane went.[^2][^4] The sources give different days for it. Hubert and Lighthouse Friends say 23 August 1881, and the Îles archive says 13 August.[^2][^4][^5]

Télesphore Turbide, the assistant, had no telegraph training. Hubert says he "ignorait complètement le maniement du télégraphe" (he knew nothing about handling the telegraph; translation), yet he got a message through to the operator at Cap-aux-Meules before the cable broke an hour later.[^2] The archive says he sent the call in Morse code, which he knew only a little.[^4] Hubert says Turbide was named keeper a few weeks later and held the post until 1896.[^1]

In 1891 another gun explosion tore off one of Turbide's hands, according to Hubert. The cable was gone by then. After two days of agony, a passing vessel under Captain Frédéric Poirier of Arichat took him to Chéticamp.[^1] Hubert says the cable was taken up in 1890, leaving the keeper with no direct line to the mainland.[^1] In 1896 Turbide fell badly, and in September he resigned.[^1]

His cousin Arsène Turbide kept the light as acting keeper. The new keeper, Pierre Bourque, was named in November 1896 but could not be landed before the winter.[^1][^5] Lighthouse Friends says Bourque arrived in May 1897.[^5]

On 7 March 1897, in fine weather, Arsène Turbide went out on the ice with Charles Turbide, the 17-year-old son of the former keeper, and the assistant Damien Cormier, who was 60. A storm came up and carried them away on the ice. Charles and Cormier died of cold on the first night. Arsène walked over the ice for three days, with no food and only seal blood to drink, and came ashore at Baie Saint-Laurent, Cape Breton. He died fifteen days later.[^1][^5] Lighthouse Friends says a government vessel was sent to cut through the ice and rescue Cormier's wife, who had been left alone on the island.[^5] Hubert gives a different rescue: five seal hunters reached the rock on 9 March and found her half out of her mind with terror.[^1]

Pierre Bourque's son Wilfrid became an assistant, and Lighthouse Friends says he was promoted to head keeper in 1905.[^5] Bourque's letter confirms that he took the post when his father resigned.[^1] The Department's keeper lists for 1907 and 1908 put him at Bird Rocks.[^12][^14]

In a letter of 6 July 1909 to Théophile Béland, the Department's agent at Quebec, Wilfrid Bourque described the station's history and its isolation. The roll of the light's keepers, he wrote, was a hard one, "plusieurs y ayant laissé leur vie" (several having left their lives there; translation).[^1] He wrote that no station under the Department could be seriously compared with this rock (translation).[^1] Passing ships, he wrote, often asked him for news of the river and the ice, but when the keepers needed help, nine in ten ships did not answer or said no.[^1] He told of a steamer that passed half a mile off on a calm day; when he asked whether it would take a letter, the answer was no.[^1]

Bourque's death is dated differently. Hubert places it in early March 1911. Wilfrid went hunting on the ice below the cliff, and when he did not come back, his wife sent their nephew to look for him; the assistant Daniel Turbide went after them. They got him onto the ice, but he was dead, and Hubert says the cause was never established.[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives 11 March 1912 as the date.[^5] The sealing steamer Seal of St. John's, Newfoundland, carried his body and his widow to Havre-aux-Maisons.[^1]

His nephew Elphège Bourque took the post in the summer of 1912.[^1][^5] In November 1922 everyone on the rock was poisoned by water they had collected from the rain in a large tank.[^1] Albin Bourque, Elphège's brother, who had taken charge while Elphège was away, died.[^1][^5] Philias Richard, an assistant, died about eighteen months later.[^1] Octave Langford survived, though in a wretched state; Hubert spells the name Lancford.[^1][^5] Lighthouse Friends says the vessel that came for them took the four to Charlottetown, and Albin died on the way.[^5]

Lighthouse Friends quotes keeper J. Montague Arsenault, who complained in 1928 about the water: "In the winter, we are forced to gather up snow on the Island which is very dirty".[^5]

Lighthouse Friends also reports a visit in 1939. The keeper J. Marc Richard recalled in a 1971 newspaper interview that a man he took to be President Roosevelt came ashore by speedboat from a ship on 20 August 1939, and that they posed for a photograph with a bird that let itself be picked up.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 13 July 1903 the steamer Dominion, on passage from Montreal to Sydney, touched bottom at Bird Rocks "so heavily as to cause material damage." The court of inquiry found that the sparse soundings near the rock had misled the master, and that the fog signal, sounded every fifteen minutes, was "quite useless as an efficient aid to navigation." It recommended a fog signal that could be sounded at more frequent intervals. The ship was held thirty-eight days in dock.[^6]

On 24 July 1916 the steamer English Monarch struck Bird Rocks. She went on to Sydney, Cape Breton, leaking, and then returned to Montreal for repairs.[^15]

On 11 July 1955 a fire badly damaged the keeper's house and some outbuildings while a Coast Guard crew from the Saurel was repairing the landing wharf.[^3] Lighthouse Friends, which dates the fire only to 1955, gives the cause as lightning, which set many of the station's buildings alight that year.[^5]

Lighthouse Friends also describes the winters. The ice on the rock was so slick that keepers crawled about on their hands and knees for fear of being blown off, and a light was lit on Sundays in winter so that people on the other Magdalen Islands would know all was well on Bird Rock.[^5]

## After the keepers

The Îles archive gives a federal decree of 29 March 1919 making the rock a protected sanctuary for birds, and Lighthouse Friends also gives 1919.[^3][^5] Hubert says instead that the provincial government reserved the rock and banned hunting there in 1920.[^1] Lighthouse Friends adds that shooting birds and taking their eggs continued after the designation.[^5]

From 1961, families no longer lived on the rock. The Îles archive says teams of two keepers were flown in by helicopter every month. Lighthouse Friends says Coast Guard crews rotated on and off the island every twenty-eight days until the light was automated in 1988.[^3][^5]

In 1967 the wooden upper part of the tower was replaced. The archive says that in the 1980s and 1990s it was replaced again, with the skeletal tower that stands now.[^3] Lighthouse Friends describes the 1967 tower as a 9.4 m (31 ft) hexagon with a concrete lower half and a wooden top, and says the top was later removed to leave a skeletal tower on the concrete base.[^5] The light was automated in 1988.[^3][^5]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the station, and its grounds and tower are closed.[^5] Lighthouse Friends says that today only Coast Guard crews may land on the rock, usually by helicopter, to inspect and repair the lighting equipment.[^5] Lighthouse Friends lists five-hour excursions from Excursions en mer as offered, but Les amis des phares du Saint-Laurent says those excursions are no longer offered.[^5][^17]

The Coast Guard's list of lights, updated on 28 July 2026, still gives a year-round light on a square skeleton tower: a long flash every eight seconds (2 s on, 6 s off), with a focal height of 49 m above the water and a nominal range of 9 nautical miles.[^16] A fog cannon from the station is on display outside the Musée de la Mer at Havre-Aubert, on the Magdalen Islands.[^5]
