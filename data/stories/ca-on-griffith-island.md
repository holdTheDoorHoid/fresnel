Griffith Island Light stands on the northeast point of Griffith Island, off Cape Commodore, at the base of the Bruce Peninsula on Georgian Bay.[^1][^2] It is one of the six "Imperial Towers" that John Brown built on Lake Huron and Georgian Bay in the 1850s, and it still shows a white flash every four seconds.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The Lake Huron coast was poorly lit when the Province of Canada looked at it.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes the Commissioners of Public Works for 1852 to 1853: "the mariner is wholly unaided by either Lights or Buoys, with the exception of the Solitary Light at Goderich."[^1] On the advice of Captain MacGregor, who had commanded a steamboat on Lake Huron, and of the Honorable W.B. Robinson, the Commissioners "recommended the construction of nine lighthouses."[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the light "helped many a mariner locate the entrance to Owen Sound and Colpoy's Bay, where safe shelter could be found behind White Cloud Island."[^1]

## Building the light

In 1855 John Brown, a builder from Thorold, was contracted to construct eleven lighthouses.[^1] Six of them, "now known as the Imperial Towers," were built.[^1] The 1855 season was well advanced before Brown could assemble the steam and sailing craft, machinery and workers needed at sites "so remote and difficult of approach."[^1]

In 1856 the foundations for the tower and dwelling on Griffith Island were prepared, and the limestone was quarried and dressed.[^1] The work on the six towers suffered a setback that season, when LighthouseFriends says "two steamers and a schooner were" wrecked and "much timber was lost." The page does not say where that happened.[^1] Carrying the cut stone up to about 240 km (150 miles) delayed the job, and so did the late arrival of "the lantern rooms and Fresnel lenses from France."[^1]

In 1857 the tower and one-and-a-half-storey dwelling were finished.[^1] A temporary light was set up on the tower because the lantern room "had arrived too late in the season to be put in place."[^1]

The light was placed in operation on December 27, 1858.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls it the third of the six Imperial Towers to be formally placed in operation, "exhibiting a fixed white light from a third-order Fresnel lens."[^1] The Lighthouse Directory gives the date as "1858 (John Brown)."[^2]

The sources do not agree on the dimensions. LighthouseFriends says the tower "stood eighty five feet tall" (about 26 m) and gives a focal plane about 40 m (130 ft) above the bay.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory gives a focal plane of 31 m (102 ft) and a tower of about 20 m (65 ft).[^2] The Coast Guard list gives a focal height of 31.3 m and a tower of 19.5 m above ground.[^3] This story does not choose between them.

## The keepers

John Frame was the first keeper.[^1] He "served for a few months before Vesey C. Hill took charge," and Hill kept the station for twenty-two years.[^1] LighthouseFriends notes, without giving a source, that "Vesey had been born in Ireland, while Sarah was born in Africa."[^1]

In 1876 Darius Smith, the Superintendent of Lights above Montreal, wrote that the tower was "a circular stone building, whitewashed, and is 50 feet high to platform" (about 15 m). He added: "The keeper is old and not very strong," and that Hill "would like to be superannuated, or removed to some more accessible station."[^1] The 1878 inspection listed the light as "very well kept," and noted that Keeper Hill had built a boat for the station.[^1] Hill was "superannuated at the age of sixty-six on April 27, 1881."[^1]

Garrett Washington Patterson kept the light from 1881 to 1889.[^1] William S. Boyd, from 1889 to 1922, "had the longest tenure of any keeper on Griffith Island."[^1] The Department of Marine's report for the fiscal year ended March 31, 1909, printed in 1910, lists a change: "A 55 mm. Diamond vapour installation and adjustable brass stand replaces the duplex lamp heretofore used."[^5]

George H. Bennett followed Boyd in 1922.[^1] He "had lost a leg during World War I." LighthouseFriends says he "left the dock on the south side of the island on December 13, 1922 for the lighthouse and was swept out into Georgian Bay."[^1]

Frederick Thornley, "whose family had been the original European settlers on Griffith Island," took charge after Bennett.[^1] He served "until an acetylene apparatus was used to automate the lighthouse before the opening of the 1924 season."[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Evening Star of Washington printed an Owen Sound dispatch on 19 December 1922.[^4] It named "George Bennett, lighthouse keeper" of Griffiths Island (the paper's spelling) as the man who had disappeared in a small motorboat on Wednesday, and said a tug "skirted the west shore of Owen Sound" without finding any trace of him.[^4] LighthouseFriends gives December 13, 1922, as the day Bennett left the dock, and says he was "swept out into Georgian Bay and perished in the wind and snow."[^1]

## After the keepers

The light was automated with acetylene before the 1924 season.[^1] In 1928 the Lake Carriers' Association, as LighthouseFriends quotes its annual report, noted that the light had been improved by "replacing the seventh-order lens with a fourth-order lens."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the light "remains active today with a modern optic producing a white flash every four seconds atop the tower."[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, according to LighthouseFriends and the Lighthouse Directory.[^1][^2] Since 1973 the island, except the small parcel the light stands on, has been privately owned by the Griffith Island Club, which runs a hunting and social retreat.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory says the Club "has applied to assume ownership of the lighthouse."[^2] LighthouseFriends says the Club "has reportedly applied for ownership of the lighthouse."[^1]

The island is private and the tower is closed.[^1][^2] The Lighthouse Directory says the site is "Accessible only by boat," and "Site and tower closed."[^2] LighthouseFriends, on the other hand, says "Grounds open, tower closed."[^1] The two sources disagree about the grounds, and this story does not settle it. LighthouseFriends advises that "As Griffith Island is privately owned, the lighthouse is best viewed by boat."[^1] The keeper's house, "abandoned since 1955," is "collapsing into ruins."[^2] The Coast Guard list gives a white cylindrical tower of 19.5 m, showing a white flash every four seconds.[^3]
