Baileys Harbor Light stood on a small island at the mouth of Baileys Harbor, on the Lake Michigan shore of Door County, Wisconsin. It was lit in 1853 and kept until December 1869, when its work passed to range lights at the head of the bay and to a new light on Cana Island. The tower still stands, its lantern in an advanced state of decay, but the island is private and closed.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The harbor takes its name from Captain Justice Bailey.[^2] In October 1848 a storm drove him into an inlet on the Lake Michigan shore, where he found stone and timber, and he told his employer, Alanson Sweet, what he had seen.[^2][^3]

LighthouseFriends says shoals and reefs lay on both sides of the harbor's opening, and that several vessels had been wrecked trying to get in.[^2] Congress agreed to build a light at Baileys Harbor and gave the contract to Sweet, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The National Register nomination for the later range lights says Sweet received federal approval for the light in 1851.[^4] The small island now called Lighthouse Island was bought from Alexander Mitchell in November 1851 for $100.[^2]

## Building the light

Construction began in 1852.[^2] LighthouseFriends says lake levels were so low that year that horse-drawn wagons could carry the materials straight out to the island, and that limestone from Sweet's own quarry built the tower and the attached one-and-a-half-story keeper's dwelling.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's record instead gives the tower's material as rubblestone and its shape as conical.[^1] The two accounts disagree on the material, and this story does not settle which is right.

The tower stood 52 feet (15.8 m) from the ground to its focal plane. Its walls were thicker at the base, three feet (0.9 m), than at the lantern, two feet (0.6 m), and the tower and the keeper's dwelling were finished in stucco and whitewash.[^2] Of the lights built in Door County, it was the third, after Plum Island and Rock Island.[^2]

The tower's lantern is a birdcage.[^2] LighthouseFriends counts it as one of four lighthouses in the United States still fitted with that older style of lantern.[^2]

The light was first equipped with a sixth-order Fresnel lens and was given a fifth-order lens in 1858.[^2][^3] The US Lighthouse Society's record instead gives the original optic as a reflector, installed in 1853.[^1] The two accounts disagree, and this story does not settle which is right.

## The keepers

David Ward was appointed the first keeper on 10 February 1853, and the light went into operation when navigation opened that year.[^2] The roster lists Ward twice, for 1853 to 1854 and again for 1854, with Albert Barry in between.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Ward was replaced in September 1854 by Michael Green, who served only six months before he died in March 1855.[^2]

Newton Bacon followed Green. LighthouseFriends says he "kept the light for nearly seven years before being replaced by William Mitchell," and the roster dates his stint from 1855 to 1862.[^2][^1] In August 1863 the Lighthouse Board appointed William Darling keeper and moved Mitchell to another light.[^2]

Darling was born in 1820, according to LighthouseFriends. He had served in the Mexican-American War and enlisted in the Sixth Wisconsin Infantry, part of the Iron Brigade. He had a foot amputated after an injury at the Battle of Antietam, was discharged in January 1863, and briefly worked as a postmaster before taking the keeper's post, which paid more.[^2] He kept the light until it was put out in 1869.[^1]

## After the keepers

By 1866 the light was in poor shape. LighthouseFriends says an inspector for the Eleventh Lighthouse District found it in "very defective condition," and on 2 March 1867 Congress granted $15,000 to rebuild it, three times what the light had originally cost.[^2]

The plan then changed. LighthouseFriends says the Board decided that the light's place inside the bay meant it could not serve as either a coast light or a guide into the harbor. The Board proposed a new station on exposed Cana Island, with a pair of range lights at the head of the bay.[^2] The National Register nomination says that on 20 July 1868 Congress authorized moving Sweet's light to Cana Island and appropriated $6,000 for the range lights. It also says the light "apparently did neither adequately."[^4]

On 1 December 1869, the last day of navigation that season, Keeper Darling put out the light for the last time.[^2][^3] The range lights and the Cana Island light took over the work in early 1870.[^2][^3] A Manitowoc paper had reported on 6 January 1870 that the former light would be discontinued once the new lights were lit.[^5] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1870 as the year the station was discontinued.[^1]

## Today

The tower and the attached dwelling still stand, though the lantern is in an advanced state of decay.[^2] The island is privately owned, and the dwelling serves as a summer cottage.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's record lists the owner as private and the station as not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the grounds, dwelling and tower are closed. The island and light can be seen from the road that runs around the harbor, but they cannot be visited.[^2]
