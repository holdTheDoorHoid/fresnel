The Frankfort North Breakwater Light stands at the outer end of the north breakwater at Frankfort, Michigan, where a man-made channel joins Lake Betsie to Lake Michigan.[^1][^2][^3] A light has marked that harbor since 1873.[^1][^2] The tower on the breakwater is a steel pyramid that was moved there from the north pier in 1932, and it carries a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^1][^2]

## The place

Terry Pepper's history of the light begins with a Buffalo captain, George Tifft, who in 1854 was driven toward the beach in a damaged schooner until a wave carried him over the shallow bar at the river mouth and into Lake Betsie. Pepper says Tifft's crew then cut an opening through the bar, and that Tifft saw in the lake a perfect location for a town and a harbor.[^2]

Private interests dredged a channel between two short piers in 1859.[^2] In 1867 the Army Corps of Engineers began a new cut about 750 feet (229 m) south of the natural outlet, to be dredged 200 feet (61 m) wide between parallel piers.[^2] The Eleventh District's engineer, General Orlando M. Poe, reported in 1871 that a pierhead beacon would be needed when the piers were done.[^2]

In January 1893 a railroad began running car ferries between Frankfort and Kewaunee, Wisconsin, and the Corps extended the piers again in 1896 to serve them.[^2]

## Building the light

Pepper says John H. King was appointed acting keeper of the Frankfort Pierhead Light on 9 May 1873. The appropriation for the station had no money for a keeper's dwelling, so King rented rooms in town.[^2] LighthouseFriends says King was the station's first keeper.[^3]

The light was a timber-framed pyramid beacon with a square gallery at the outer end of the South Pier. A fixed red sixth-order Fresnel lens sat in an iron lantern at a focal plane of 25 feet (7.6 m).[^2][^3] King first exhibited it on the night of 15 October 1873.[^2][^3]

The beacon moved along the pier as the pier grew.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends gives a move of 261 feet (80 m) in 1880.[^3] In 1884 the South Pier was lengthened by 200 feet (61 m), and the beacon was moved 195 feet (59 m) to the new pierhead.[^2][^3]

Congress appropriated $1,000 for a fog bell on 5 August 1892.[^2][^4] The Lighthouse Board's report says the work began on 14 April 1893 and that the signal was in operation by 15 June. The Board describes a small wooden structure, 10 feet (3.0 m) by 16 feet (4.9 m) in plan, used as a watchroom on the south pier. Pepper says its lower section housed the striking machinery.[^4][^2]

In 1896 the light and fog-bell building were moved 600 feet (183 m) to the outer end of the extended south pier. The Board's report gives two dates: 12 November 1896 in its list of works, and 16 November in its detailed entry.[^5][^13] Pepper gives 16 November, and LighthouseFriends gives 15 November.[^2][^3] On 22 January 1897 a rear light went up, a lens lantern on a 60-foot (18 m) steel post 600 feet (183 m) behind the pierhead.[^5][^2]

The Board's report for 1899 says 280 feet (85 m) of the elevated walk was renewed, and that the old fog-bell striking apparatus, which had come from Chicago, was repaired and shipped to Frankfort.[^6] In 1900 the striking apparatus was removed and sent to St. Joseph, and the repaired Chicago apparatus was put in its place.[^7] Pepper dates the removal of the old bell equipment to 1905.[^2] According to the Board's report for 1901, a siren was established on 11 May 1901. The Board calls it a compressed-air siren in one list and a blower steam siren in another. Pepper gives 1 May.[^8][^2]

The lights changed again in 1912. A new steel pyramid tower stood on the north pier, painted white, with a fixed red fourth-order Fresnel lens at a focal plane of 46 feet (14 m). Pepper says the air siren from the south pier was moved into it. The rear range light went onto a 66-foot (20 m) wooden post behind it.[^2][^3] The Bulletin for 1912 records that two lights and the fog signal at Frankfort were moved that year.[^9] Pepper and LighthouseFriends differ on what replaced the south pierhead light. Pepper says it became an acetylene lantern on an open 35-foot (11 m) pyramid. LighthouseFriends says it was a white flashing light, changed to red in 1917.[^2][^3]

The lights were electrified in 1919.[^2] Pepper says the north pier light's fog signal was changed to an electric, compressor-driven diaphone that sounds one two-second blast every 20 seconds.[^2] The Bulletin for 1919 says the front range light's fog signal changed from siren to diaphone that year.[^10] The Commissioner's report for 1919 lists a Frankfort Pierhead Light Station item in its table of station works.[^11]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Service's roster for the station lists thirteen principal keepers from 1873 to 1967, with assistants, a laborer and a Coast Guard member among the others.[^1] Some dates in the roster differ from LighthouseFriends, and this story follows the roster. The roster has John C. McKinnon as principal keeper from 1911 to 1924, and Albert F. Straubel from 1928 to 1941. LighthouseFriends gives McKinnon's post until 1928.[^1][^3]

Pepper says that in the winter after the 1884 extension, the schooner *Amsden* was entering the harbor under tow when she parted her line and ran her bowsprit through 32 feet (9.8 m) of the elevated walk. Keeper Albert Vorce hired a local crew to repair it.[^2] The roster lists Vorce as principal keeper from 1884 to 1899.[^1]

Joseph H. Wilmot kept the light from 1899 to 1911.[^1] LighthouseFriends, quoting a local newspaper, says that one night in August 1911 Wilmot, who was ill, rowed out to start the fog signal. He got it running, then told his wife he was deathly sick. She ran for help, and a captain carried him out onto the pier, where he died a few minutes later.[^3] The roster gives his service as 1899 to 1911.[^1] LighthouseFriends is the only source cited for the account of his death.

Sheridan J. King was one of the station's first assistants, in 1902.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he had been transferred to Frankfort from the North Manitou Island light in March 1902, to help with the fog signal. It says he was killed a few months later, when an Ann Arbor car ferry crushed his small boat as he tried to move it out of the ferry's path. The account it quotes is from *Scott's New Coast Pilot*.[^3]

LighthouseFriends says no dwelling was provided for the keepers until one was built in 1909.[^3] Pepper says that early in the twentieth century the Ninth District inspector, Commander Lucien Young, asked for $6,500 for a duplex dwelling. Condemnation proceedings for a site began in 1907. Pepper writes that "we have been unable to identify definitively whether the much needed dwelling was ever built," and notes a 1910 report of bids for a brick oil building at the station.[^2] Pepper could not confirm that a dwelling was built, so the two accounts differ.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1884 a storm swept across the pier while the South Pier was being lengthened.[^2] Pepper says the waves destroyed 90 feet (27 m) of the elevated walkway and severely damaged another 170 feet (52 m).[^2]

On 25 November 1893 a boat dredging the harbor damaged the elevated walk.[^4] The Board's report for 1894 calls the damage slight and says the walk was repaired.[^4]

On 19 January 1901 a gale shook a prism out of a lens lantern at the Frankfort Pierhead Range light. A 270-degree lens lantern was put in until the original was repaired.[^8]

The accounts of King's death in 1902 and Wilmot's in 1911 both come from LighthouseFriends alone.[^3]

## After the keepers

In March 1925 a harbor improvement was adopted for Frankfort that called for arrowhead breakwaters. Work on the south breakwater began in 1928 and was completed in 1931.[^3] Pepper says that when the breakwaters were finished the twin piers no longer served any purpose, and that they were to be shortened into stubs. The north pierhead light was lifted from the pier onto a barge and carried to the end of the north breakwater, where a square steel base 25 feet (7.6 m) high had been built to receive it.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the scow transfer to 8 August 1932.[^3]

Pepper says the tower then stood 67 feet (20 m) from the upper level of the pier to the top of its ventilator, and that the light's focal plane was 72 feet (22 m).[^2] LighthouseFriends says a radiobeacon was established on the breakwater on 31 December 1932, and that a fourteen-room duplex dwelling from Chicago Pierhead was moved to Frankfort that year and placed near the north pier. No source cited here says whether that house became the keepers' dwelling.[^3]

The car-ferry trade declined in the 1960s and 1970s.[^2] Pepper says the last ferry working out of Frankfort was sold and towed out of the harbor in 1967.[^2] The archive's roster has Wesley E. Davis as principal keeper from 1967, with no end date.[^1]

The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 6 September 2005.[^12] In 2010 the Coast Guard declared the lighthouse excess and offered it to eligible bodies. In 2011 ownership was announced for transfer to the City of Frankfort, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3]

## Today

The City of Frankfort owns the lighthouse.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower closed, and that the light can be reached by walking the breakwater.[^3] The archive lists the station as an active aid to navigation and not open to the public.[^1] A gateway arch built in 1925 to welcome travelers to Frankfort carries the lighthouse's likeness.[^3]
