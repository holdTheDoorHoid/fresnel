The Staten Island Light is an octagonal brick tower on Lighthouse Hill, above historic Richmondtown. It is the rear light of a range that guides ships into the Ambrose Channel, and its current tower was put into service in 1912.[^1][^2][^5]

## The place

The tower stands "on Lighthouse Hill and overlooking historic Richmondtown," at a point "two hundred and thirty-one feet above sea level" (about 70 m).[^1] The landmark report says it "lines up with the West Bank Light" about five miles away, and is visible "on range only."[^1] It "works in conjunction with the Ambrose Light Tower, which replaced the famous Ambrose Light Ship," and is "one of the range lights which helps to guide ships into the channels of New York Harbor."[^1]

The city's landmark report describes the tower as "the tall, octagonal shaped brick tower with a rusticated limestone base," and says that "One would sooner expect to come upon this picturesque lighthouse on the rocky coast of New England than within the boundaries of New York City."[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated money for the work twice.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1915 says the acts of 1906 and 1909 each "appropriated $50,000 for a lighthouse on Staten Island, N. Y., and the raising of West Bank Light, N. J." The 1906 act also appropriated $10,000 for "a temporary structure to maintain West Bank Light while being raised."[^2]

The U.S. Lighthouse Society record gives 1909 as the year the station was established and the year its original lens was installed. It gives 1912 for the tower.[^5] The Commissioner's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1915 says that "the new light was put into operation on April 15, 1912," and the report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1916 repeats that "Staten Island Light Station went into commission on April 15, 1912."[^2][^3] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1912 lists the "Staten Island (rear) light station" as "Completed."[^4] In its list of important changes in aids to navigation the same year, the Bulletin records the "Staten Island (Ambrose Channel outer range rear) light station established; fixed white light of about 300,000 candlepower."[^4]

The landmark report gives the light's power as "350,000 candlelight power." The Bulletin's figure, for the light when it was established, was about 300,000 candlepower.[^1][^4] The record gives the original optic as a second-order range lens and the current one as second order.[^5]

## The keepers

The archive's roster begins in 1910, two years before the tower was in service.[^5] Frederick Nielsen is the first principal keeper it names, "Frederick Nielsen (1910-at least 1912)."[^5] William H. Moon was first assistant from 1912 and principal from at least 1913 to at least 1918.[^5] Vernon M. Ball was first assistant from at least 1913 to 1918 and principal from 1918 to 1920.[^5] Nicolai Jensen was principal from 1920 to 1923.[^5] Other first assistants on the roster were Lewis W. Herring (1919), Paul A. Southard (1920 to 1921) and Paul G. Peterson (1921 to 1923).[^5]

The roster gives only names and years.[^5] Its entries say nothing about the work, the families or the weather at the station.[^5] The roster's last entries fall in 1923.[^5] The archive does not say when the keepers left.[^5] By 1968 the city's landmark report called the station "a light attendant station," operated "under the jurisdiction of the United States Coast Guard."[^1]

## Today

The city made the Staten Island Lighthouse a landmark on 17 January 1968.[^1] The report records that a federal representative questioned the designation in a 1966 letter, saying that "Federally-owned property is not subject to regulation by local law."[^1] The 1968 report described the light as operated under the Coast Guard.[^1] The Society's record lists it as an active aid to navigation that is not open to the public.[^5] The sources give no public access to the tower, so visitors should not expect to climb it.
