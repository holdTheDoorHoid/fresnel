Old Field Point Light stands on a point of land in the village of Old Field, on Long Island's North Shore, with views across Long Island Sound toward Connecticut.[^1][^2][^8] A light has been shown there since 1824.[^3] The granite dwelling and the tower that stand now were finished in 1869.[^2][^3] The light moved to a steel tower in 1933 and returned to the 1869 lantern in 1991.[^2][^3]

## The place

Before the first light was built in 1823, there were no lighthouses on the north shore of Long Island between Eaton's Neck and Little Gull Island.[^3] Sound traffic was growing, and the rocky shoals and sandy reefs off the point needed warning, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] Captains were told to keep well clear of the point at night.[^3] One early instruction, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, said not to come "nearer Old Field Point than 8 fathoms" in the dark, to avoid large rocks off the point.[^3]

Before a lightship was stationed off Stratford Shoal in 1837, mariners were told to steer for Old Field Point to clear the shoal, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] The Old Field and Stratford Point lights, on opposite shores of the Sound, helped ships avoid the shoal on the frequent occasions when its lightship dragged its anchor.[^3]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $2,500 for the station and then another $1,500 on 3 March 1823, the Keeper's Log says.[^2] The land was bought from Samuel Ludlow Thompson and Ruth Thompson.[^2][^3] The Keeper's Log gives the price as $400 on 27 January 1823 for 7.545 acres. LighthouseFriends says a three-acre parcel cost $600.[^2][^3] The sources do not agree on the price or the acreage.

The contract for the tower and dwelling went to Timothy, Ezra and Elisha Daboll.[^2][^3] The Keeper's Log gives the final cost as $3,999.25.[^2] The tower was an octagonal stone tower, 30 feet (9 m) from base to lantern. Its lantern held nine Winslow Lewis lamps, burning whale oil.[^2] The rough-stone dwelling had five rooms.[^2] In 1838, Navy Lieutenant George Bache described the station as "badly constructed," the Keeper's Log says.[^2]

The lamps were changed in 1839 and again in 1849.[^2] A fourth-order Fresnel lens went in during 1855.[^2][^3] A supply ship that called on 17 June 1850 found the lighthouse and dwelling "in good order," and delivered 231 gallons of spring oil and 124 gallons of winter oil that day, the Keeper's Log says.[^2]

Congress provided $12,000 for a new tower on 3 March 1867, LighthouseFriends says, and the plan was approved in April of that year.[^2][^3] The new tower was finished in 1869 at a cost of $11,995.42, the Keeper's Log says.[^2] The village's own page gives the year as 1868.[^8] The Board's 1894 report describes the result: "a square tower rising from the gable end of a gray two-story granite dwelling, which gives it a church-like appearance."[^4] The sources disagree about the tower's material. LighthouseFriends and the Keeper's Log describe cast iron above the granite house.[^2][^3] The USLHS place record says wood.[^1][^2][^3]

## The keepers

The first keeper, Edward Shoemaker, began in 1824 and died on 21 December 1826, the Keeper's Log says.[^2] The archive's roster starts his service in 1823.[^1] His widow then kept the light until Walter Smith took over on 5 June 1827, the Keeper's Log says.[^2] LighthouseFriends calls her the first female keeper in the Third District, and says her name "has so far remained a mystery."[^3] The USLHS roster lists her only as "Mrs. Shoemaker."[^1]

Walter Smith died in April 1830.[^2] His wife, Elizabeth Smith, took over and kept the light for more than 25 years, with help from her son Henry, the Keeper's Log says.[^2] In 1833 she asked for a small barn to house horses. LighthouseFriends says it was built.[^3] A 1901 reprint in the New-York Tribune, from The Woman's Home Companion, said "the first woman to act as a lighthouse keeper was probably Elizabeth Smith, who in 1830 kept the light at Old Field Point, Long Island, and had full charge of it for twenty-five years."[^7] The Keeper's Log says that in 1855 her pay was much lower than that of male keepers at similar stations, although she had earned a great reputation.[^2]

Mary A. Foster, said to be Elizabeth Smith's daughter, took over in 1856 and kept the light until 21 June 1869.[^2] The Keeper's Log says her time was "largely uneventful," apart from a storm in December 1859 that damaged the station and a request for a covered breezeway to the old tower.[^2] Benjamin Floyd was appointed on 21 June 1869 and removed on 9 August, when George D. Lee was appointed.[^2] Lee had served three years with the 102nd New York Volunteers, the Keeper's Log says.[^2]

Lee's father-in-law, Azariah Davis, kept a daily record at the station.[^2] Its entries covered the weather, the ships in sight, wrecks, visitors and local deaths and illnesses. The Keeper's Log quotes from it.[^2] Davis counted 850 visitors in 1870, 888 in 1871, 1,214 in 1872 and 1,363 in 1873.[^2] In April 1874 the Lee family left the station for Port Jefferson, the Keeper's Log says. Charles F. Jayne, whom the Keeper's Log describes as a farmer reputed to be an accomplished violinist, became keeper on 25 April.[^2]

Jayne's time was disputed. A letter from the Treasury on 28 January 1886 reported the alleged neglect of the keeper in helping the schooner Stony Brook, which had run ashore near the light.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends says the complaint came from the schooner's captain, Francis Weller.[^2][^3] The Keeper's Log says Jayne was exonerated, and that it was suspected the captain may have wanted the keeper's post for himself.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the Board's investigation found the charges "frivolous."[^3]

Edgar S. Maclay was appointed on 26 July 1895.[^2] He had written a multi-volume history of the United States Navy before he came to the station, and he wrote a history of American privateers while he was there, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] He left on 1 September 1900.[^2] In 1901 he was dismissed from a post at the New York Navy Yard for criticizing Rear Admiral Winfield Scott Schley in his naval history, the Keeper's Log says.[^2] Richard E. Ray, who had been keeper of the Stratford Shoal light, replaced him in 1900.[^2] Ray kept the light until 1933, and the Keeper's Log says he was the last keeper at the station.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 8 October 1869, Azariah Davis's diary, as the Keeper's Log quotes it, recorded that a small schooner, the WM. PITTS of N.Y., "laden with sand," struck a rock a few feet east of the light, and at 8 p.m. was filled with water.[^2] The Keeper's Log says the vessel's remains were auctioned on 16 October for about $175.[^2]

The Board's 1894 report says the station's one keeper received $520 a year, and that the station burned 151 gallons of mineral oil each year.[^4] In March 1896 a Tribune article on the Port Jefferson yachting season said the light was about two miles (3 km) from the harbor breakwater, and that "while in fogs it is impossible, as there is no foghorn."[^6] The Keeper's Log says breakwater lights were built at the entrance to Port Jefferson Harbor in 1896 as adjuncts to the Old Field light.[^2]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 lists 600 running feet (180 m) of wire line fence and 150 running feet (46 m) of garden picket fence built at Old Field Point that year.[^5] In 1923, LighthouseFriends says, Keeper Ray rescued two men whose powerboat had run aground on the rocks in front of the station, and gave them clothing, food and shelter for two days.[^3]

## After the keepers

The Keeper's Log says the property was assessed in 1929 in preparation for automation.[^2] The light was removed from the lantern in 1933 and shown from a new 74-foot (23 m) skeleton tower.[^2] The old fourth-order Fresnel lens was replaced by a DCB-10 aerobeacon.[^2] An Act of Congress of 28 May 1935 authorized the station's transfer to the Village of Old Field "for public-park purposes." The conveyance was made on 9 August 1935.[^2][^3]

In January 1942, the Keeper's Log says, the village mayor was told that the Coast Guard would occupy the lighthouse as part of a wartime defense area.[^2] After the war, control returned to the village.[^2] A modern beacon went back into the lantern room in 1991, and the old steel tower was removed.[^2][^3]

## Today

The Village of Old Field owns the lighthouse.[^3] Its website says the village remains responsible for the building, and that the lighthouse, the keeper's home and the oil shed are on the New York State Register of Historic Places and the National Register of Historic Places.[^8] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the dwelling and tower are closed.[^3] The village calls its village hall one of the most unique in the United States, situated in the lighthouse.[^8] LighthouseFriends says instead that the 1824 keeper's house has served as the village hall since 1963, and that the chief constable lives in the lighthouse.[^3]

The village is raising money for a major restoration.[^8] It cites a survey by architects, a plan that combines village tax revenue, grants and gifts, and a not-for-profit Lighthouse Foundation formed in 2018.[^8] The village invites visitors to get a National Parks passport stamp from its clerk-administrator, on Mondays, Tuesdays and Thursdays from 9 a.m. to 3 p.m.[^8]
