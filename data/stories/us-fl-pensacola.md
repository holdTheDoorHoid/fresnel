Pensacola Light is a brick tower, white below and black above, that was first lit on New Year's Day 1859 to mark the entrance to Pensacola Bay. It stands on the grounds of Naval Air Station Pensacola, near old Fort Barrancas, and its first-order Fresnel lens is still in the lantern.[^1][^2][^3][^4] It is the second lighthouse on this shore: a tower built in 1824 stood about half a mile to the east.[^1]

## The place

Pensacola Bay is entered between Santa Rosa Island on the east and Perdido Key on the west. Spain had put Fort Barrancas on a bluff on the mainland to guard the entrance, and after the United States took Florida from Spain in 1821 the federal government moved to set up both a naval yard and a lighthouse.[^1] In March 1823, by the association's account, Congress authorized $6,000 for a light, and until it was ready the floating light vessel *Aurora Borealis*, moved from the mouth of the Mississippi, was stationed behind the west end of Santa Rosa Island. A site just west of Fort Barrancas was chosen for the tower so that ships could steer straight for it into the bay, which a lightship could not offer.[^1]

## Building the light

The first tower was a contract job. The Pensacola Lighthouse Association says Winslow Lewis, answering an advertisement in the *Boston Patriot*, offered on 24 March 1824 to build the tower and keeper's dwelling for $4,927, plus $750 to fit it with ten lamps and ten 14-inch reflectors, and that Stephen Pleasonton, Fifth Auditor of the Treasury, accepted on 2 April 1824.[^1] The National Register nomination instead dates the contract to 13 April 1824.[^2] Both sources agree that it was first lit on 20 December 1824.[^1][^2] The lamps were fixed in two groups of five on a framework that clockwork turned, to give the light a flash.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive dates the station from 1825, which is also the year its keeper roster starts.[^4]

Complaints about the first tower began early. Inspectors recommended in 1837 and 1838 that it be moved to higher ground, and nothing was done.[^2] In 1851 an inspection found the light "deficient in power," and in 1853 the naval station's commandant recommended raising it 20 to 25 feet (6 to 8 m) so that it showed over the nearby pines.[^2] The association adds that trees on Santa Rosa Island were also said to block it.[^1] Congress appropriated $25,000 in 1854 and a further $30,000 in 1856.[^2] The new tower went up half a mile west of the old one under John Newton of the Army Corps of Engineers and was finished in 1858.[^1] The nomination gives its height as 160 feet (49 m) above the ground, and the association gives 159 feet (48 m); the two sources differ by a foot on the same tower.[^1][^2] Its first-order lens was first lit on 1 January 1859 by keeper Joseph Palmes.[^1]

## The keepers

Jeremiah Ingraham, a bachelor, lit the first tower. After two years at the post he married Michaela Penalber of Pensacola, and the couple raised three children at the station.[^1] When he died in 1840 Michaela took over the light and kept it until her own death in 1855, according to the association. The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster lists her as principal keeper from 1840 to 1854, with her son-in-law Joseph Palmes from 1855.[^1][^4] In the late 1840s the clockwork failed, and two men were hired to turn the lamps by hand until it was repaired.[^1] In 1998 archaeologists dug at the site of the first tower and dwelling ahead of a building addition; LighthouseFriends reports they recovered glass, keys, latches, toys and hairpins, and could tell something of the Ingraham family's diet.[^3]

The roster for the new tower shows the larger crew a first-order light needed: a principal keeper and, from 1859, a first and second assistant.[^4] Women appear among the assistants in the archive's list, among them Ellery (Ellen) McCormick as second assistant from 1864 to 1867, Martha V. Watts as first assistant from 1867 to 1869, and Martha C. Lawrence as second assistant from 1880 to 1885.[^4]

After the war the head keepers changed quickly. LighthouseFriends counts eleven in the twenty-three years after the relighting and says nine were removed, for reasons running from intoxication to dereliction of duty.[^3] The appointment of George T. Clifford in 1886 ended the run of short tenures, according to LighthouseFriends. The roster has him as principal keeper from 1886 to 1917, and Adrian Whiting served as assistant from 1886 until 1917.[^4] In the autumn of 1895 the Lighthouse Board sent Clifford away from Pensacola for four weeks, from 17 September to 14 October, as one of the regular keepers detailed to run the lighthouse exhibit at an exposition "as a mark of appreciation of faithful service."[^7]

William B. Thompson succeeded Clifford in 1917 and stayed until 1929.[^4] LighthouseFriends tells of a 1919 quarrel between Thompson and his first assistant, Robert Purcell, over Purcell's going into the barn where Thompson kept his chickens. In Thompson's report, quoted there, Purcell attacked him on 25 June and nearly bit off the index finger of his left hand. Purcell resigned and was dismissed instead for insubordination and assault.[^3] The account rests on that one report, as LighthouseFriends prints it.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The first war damage came in 1861. Florida seceded on 10 January, the federal artillery company at Barrancas Barracks crossed to Santa Rosa Island, and Confederate troops took ground near the lighthouse.[^2] In the bombardment of November 1861 the nomination says direct hits by solid shot put the light out of order.[^2] The association tells it differently: roughly half a dozen rounds struck the tower, none got through the outer wall, and when Union forces took over in May 1862 the tower was found in good condition.[^1] A fourth-order lens was lit in the lantern on 20 December 1862, and the first-order lens was back in 1869.[^1] The nomination dates the restoration to 1869 as well, after Congress voted $20,000 in 1867.[^2]

Weather did its own damage. LighthouseFriends reports that lightning struck the tower in 1874 and again in 1875, melting fixtures and breaking holes in the masonry of the covered way that joined tower and dwelling, and that the rod was found defective.[^3] On 31 August 1886 an earthquake shook the tower; the association prints a keeper's report that the shock lasted three to four minutes, made the lens vibrate from side to side and stopped the pendulum clock on the lower floor at 9:07 p.m.[^1] Cracks had appeared inside the tower beneath the lantern in 1878, and money for repairs was made available in 1879; the nomination presumes the cracks were late effects of the Civil War shelling.[^2] The Lighthouse Board's later reports record routine work: a first-order oil house built, reported in 1892, and a frame kitchen added behind the dwelling, reported in the 1896-97 volume.[^5][^6]

Three Gulf hurricanes in 1916 and 1917 are on record in the Lighthouse Service Bulletin. The storm of 5 July 1916 damaged property at 75 light stations and other lights and beacons, and at two depots, in the Eighth District, and Pensacola Light Station is on the list of stations that took the more severe damage.[^8] The hurricane of 18 October 1916 damaged property at 12 light stations and other lights and beacons, Pensacola Light Station among them, with no lives in the Service lost.[^9] The Bulletin reported that the hurricane of 27 to 29 September 1917 damaged about 25 stations, and later listed Pensacola among nine with damage of a minor nature.[^10][^11] Keeper Thompson, first assistant Robert Purcell and second assistant Harvey Holdeman of Pensacola were among twenty employees of the district whom the Secretary of Commerce commended for work under hazardous conditions. The Bulletin describes that work in general terms, as keeping lights at full intensity, recovering washed-away property, sheltering people in the buildings and making temporary repairs, and does not say which of these each person did.[^11]

LighthouseFriends also reports that in February 1936 an Eastern Airlines pilot, his radio dead and lost in a storm, saw the flash of Pensacola Light and followed it to the airfield.[^3] That story is told only by that site.

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the station was electrified and passed to the Coast Guard in 1939, and was staffed until it was automated in 1965; the roster's last keeper, Darrell L. Daugherty, is listed from 1963 to 1965.[^3][^4] With the dwelling unused and the tower seen as a possible risk to jets using a newly completed airfield, plans to demolish the station were discussed, according to LighthouseFriends, and that fed a preservation movement.[^3] The station was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1974.[^2]

## Today

The tower stands within Naval Air Station Pensacola and is still an active aid to navigation with its first-order revolving lens.[^4] The keepers' duplex is leased to the Pensacola Lighthouse Association, formed in 2006. One half has been restored as an 1880s to 1890s keepers' dwelling, and the other holds exhibits; the old carriage house became a visitor centre in 2012.[^3] A four-year, four-phase restoration costing about $2.5 million was completed in 2018, and the lens was restored later that year.[^3]

Because it is on a military installation, visitors enter through the NAS Pensacola public gate and must show valid identification. The association's website says the lighthouse can be climbed, 177 steps, with age and height limits, and gives opening times of 9 a.m. to 4 p.m. daily, while its history page gives base visitation through the West Gate as 9 a.m. to 3 p.m.; check its page before going, as access depends on the base.[^12][^1]
