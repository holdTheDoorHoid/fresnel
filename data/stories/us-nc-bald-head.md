Old Baldy, the Bald Head Lighthouse, stands on Bald Head Island where the Cape Fear River meets the sea.[^1] It is North Carolina's oldest standing lighthouse: an octagonal brick tower finished in 1817 to replace a first light lit in 1794.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The island takes its name from its dunes.[^4] On the south beach they became worn down until they resembled a bald head.[^4]

The danger lay offshore. The Town says a light was needed both to guide ships through the channels and sandbars of the Cape Fear River, and to help vessels avoid the Frying Pan Shoals, which it says extend "over 20 miles" into the Atlantic.[^4] LighthouseFriends gives the shoals' length as twenty-eight miles.[^1]

In 1789 Benjamin Smith, who had recently acquired the island, promised the state ten acres for a lighthouse.[^2] The legislature set terms: no one could keep cattle, hogs or other stock on the island, except poultry and a cow and calf kept by the keeper.[^2] Congress appropriated money for the light on 2 April 1792.[^2] The state also levied a tax of six pence per ton on freight moved up the Cape Fear River.[^2]

## Building the light

The first tower was completed in 1794, the state's marker says.[^2] LighthouseFriends says it was first activated on 23 December of that year.[^1] The sources do not agree on the first lighting. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1939 history dates the light to 1796, and the Town gives 1795 for the first structure.[^8][^4]

On 18 April 1793 President Washington approved a contract for superintending the building, and endorsed it in his own hand.[^7] The endorsement says the allowance "seems high, but as none have offered to execute the business so far," and that a superintendent of the building was necessary.[^7]

Abisha Woodward, who would later build two lighthouses in Connecticut, oversaw the task of finishing the lighthouse, LighthouseFriends says.[^1]

Soil erosion along the river damaged the first tower beyond repair, probably around 1812, and Congress appropriated $15,000 for a replacement.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the order to demolish it to 1813.[^1]

Daniel Way accepted the contract in July 1816 and finished the new tower the following year.[^2] The Old Baldy Foundation says that when Way arrived on Bald Head Island in December 1816, the superintendent, Robert Cochran, had moved the site about three-quarters of a mile northeast. Way moved the 60,000 bricks and the lantern room to the new site anyway.[^3]

A stone plaque above the entrance names Daniel S. Way as builder, and R. Cochran as the foundry for the lantern room.[^1] The Foundation dates completion to 1 April 1817.[^3] The cost was just under $16,000, LighthouseFriends says.[^1]

The tower's height is disputed.[^4] The Town gives 109 feet and 10¾ inches (33.5 m), the Foundation gives 110 feet (about 34 m), and LighthouseFriends says ninety feet (27 m).[^4][^3][^1]

The lantern sits off-center. The Foundation says the Board, trying to economize, used a standard lantern room design that did not fit the old tower precisely.[^3]

In 1849 fifteen brass lamps with twenty-one-inch reflectors replaced the older apparatus.[^1] A third-order Fresnel lens followed in 1855, LighthouseFriends says.[^1] The Society's record gives 1851 for the original lens, so the dates differ.[^5]

## The keepers

Henry Long was the first keeper.[^1] He served until October 1806, when LighthouseFriends says he was killed by his son-in-law in a hunting accident.[^1] Twelve residents then recommended Sedgwick Springs to President Jefferson, describing him as "a fit and proper Person to take charge and keep up the said Light."[^1] The Foundation, by contrast, calls Springs the first official keeper of the 1817 lighthouse.[^3] Springs was born in 1756 and died in 1837, the Society says, and kept the light from 1807 to 1837.[^6][^5]

In 1836 Captain Henry D. Hunter visited the light and found the keeper "an old revolutionary soldier" who was "unable from sickness to give the lighthouse his constant personal attention." Hunter added that the light "shows well from a distance."[^1]

LighthouseFriends says Springs's thirty years in charge were the longest tenure of any keeper of the light.[^1]

Bryan Morse kept the light from 1837 until his death in 1848.[^5][^1] His son Francis followed. LighthouseFriends says Francis went on working as a river pilot and often left his family to tend the light for long stretches.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the light was discontinued in 1866, when the Federal Point light was built eight miles upstream, and was returned to service in 1879.[^1] The state marker gives 1835 for the end of its use.[^2]

In 1879 the light was relit, and Joseph Bell became the first keeper of its "second life," the Foundation says.[^3] The Society lists Bell for 1879 to 1881.[^5]

James Henry Dosher kept the light from 1882 to 1913.[^5][^1] LighthouseFriends says he reported an earthquake that shook the tower for ten seconds at 9:50 p.m. on 31 August 1886, and that the quake broke the glass chimney in the lamp.[^1] When the light went over to acetylene in 1913, LighthouseFriends says the seventy-year-old Dosher was out of a job after more than thirty years in charge.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A Board finding of 1851, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, judged the light "in its present position and with its present apparatus, &c., is comparatively useless."[^1] The Foundation dates the finding to 1852.[^3]

The Board's report for fiscal 1891 and 1892 says the tower does not cover the shoal, and so gives too little warning to vessels when a light-ship may have drifted from her moorings.[^10]

LighthouseFriends says a 150-foot (46 m) stone jetty built in 1883 to protect the tower from erosion probably saved it from a hurricane that struck that September. That is LighthouseFriends's own inference, not a contemporary report.[^1]

No source ties a wreck or rescue to the tower itself. The Commissioner's report for 1921 says the lighthouse tender Cypress found the steamer National Bridge aground on Bald Head Shoal, at the river's bar, and that the steamer was later towed into deep water. The report places the event on the shoal, not at the light.[^11]

## After the keepers

In March 1883 the light changed from fixed white to a red flash every thirty seconds, through the installation of a new fourth-order lens, LighthouseFriends says.[^1] In March 1893 the flash turned white again.[^1][^9]

The Board's report for fiscal 1893 says the old tower had almost been eliminated as a harbor light, and that its only use was as "a rear beacon to a stake light."[^9]

In 1903 the Cape Fear Lighthouse, a separate tower on the island's southeastern end, was built.[^3][^1] Old Baldy then became Bald Head Lighthouse, with a fourth-order fixed light.[^1]

The light changed from oil to acetylene in November 1913.[^1] The Commissioner's annual report says Bald Head Light "has also been changed from oil burning to acetylene equipment."[^1][^11]

The lens came out after the light was decommissioned in 1935, LighthouseFriends says.[^1] The Society's place record gives 1930 for discontinuance instead.[^5]

From 1941 to 1958 the tower housed a radio beacon.[^1] It was sold to a private owner in 1963 and later given to the Old Baldy Foundation.[^1]

In 1931 the larger keepers' dwelling, then used as an office for the "Palmetto Island" development, was lost to fire.[^1] The tower was placed on the National Register in 1975.[^2] In 1985 it was relit as an unofficial aid to navigation.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says a replica of the 1850s keepers' cottage was finished beside the tower in 2000 and houses the Smith Island Museum.[^1] The Town describes a different building: a two-story cottage built in 1883 on the same spot, lost to fire in 1931, which the museum replicates.[^4] The sources disagree on which cottage the replica copies.

## Today

The tower is no longer in service and is a tourist attraction, the Town says.[^4] Bald Head Island can be visited by a 20-minute ferry from Southport.[^1] The Town says the ferry is for people only and costs $15 per person for a round trip.[^4]

The Town says it costs $3 to climb the tower.[^4] LighthouseFriends says visitors may climb for a fee, up 112 restored wooden stairs.[^1] The Town asks visitors to call ahead for hours.[^4] The Old Baldy Foundation owns the lighthouse.[^1]
