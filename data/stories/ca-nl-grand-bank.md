Grand Bank Lighthouse stands on a pier in the harbour of Grand Bank, a town on the western side of the Burin Peninsula in Newfoundland and Labrador.[^9] A light has shown from the harbour since 1890. The octagonal concrete tower standing now dates from 1922.[^1]

## The place

Grand Bank lies on Fortune Bay. LHF says French fishermen settled there in the seventeenth century. After the Treaty of Utrecht banned permanent French settlements in 1713, English settlers eventually moved in. Cook recorded the largest fishery in Fortune Bay at Grand Bank when he surveyed the south coast of Newfoundland in 1765.[^1]

In 1889 the House of Assembly passed resolutions for raising money for public works. One item was eight thousand dollars for harbour improvements at Grand Bank and Fortune, to be carried out by completing the piers.[^2]

## Building the light

The Inspector's report for 1881 says that the lantern, glass, lens and lamp for a light planned for the end of the Grand Bank breakwater had been supplied.[^3] LHF dates the start of the light to 1 May 1890, when it began to show a white light from an eighth-order dioptric lens, and names Josiah Hiscock as its first keeper.[^1]

The Board of Works' accounts for the year ended 31 December 1890 carry an item for a "Grand Bank Beacon," with $184.97 spent on it.[^4][^5] The Inspector's report of 1 January 1891 says that the voyage of inspection, which left St. John's on 17 July, visited the Grand Bank light station along with thirteen other light stations.[^6] LHF describes the light in 1891 as a wooden beacon, painted in red and white horizontal bands, standing 91 m (299 ft) from the end of the breakwater.[^1]

## The keepers

LHF names the keepers as Josiah Hiscock from 1890 to 1896, R. E. Forsey in 1897, Hiscock again in 1898, David Banfield from 1898 to 1900, Robert C. Forsey from 1901 to 1911, John Tibbo from 1912, and Bert Riggs in the 1930s and 1940s.[^1] LHF also says the light was kept by a veteran of the Second World War when it was converted to battery power in the 1950s, and that it now runs on solar power.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In October 1896 a fire at the Grand Bank light destroyed the lantern glass and so damaged the lens that it was useless. The Board of Works report printed in 1897 says that a new and more powerful lens had been provided and would be placed in position early in April.[^7]

LHF, citing an account in The Daily News of 12 January 1923, reports that the three-masted schooner Frank R. Forsey, sailing home from Setubal in Portugal with a cargo of salt under Captain Rose, struck the pier at Grand Bank and sank. The salt dissolved and she floated again. Her bowsprit gouged the tower, and the letter B of the name later painted on the seaward face now sits where the gouge was.[^1]

## After the keepers

LHF says the original tower was reconstructed in 1920, and that in 1922 the present concrete octagonal tower was erected on the eastern pier.[^1] The Coast Guard list gives the tower's height as 7.1 m (23 ft). It describes the light as quick-flashing green, once a second, with a focal height of 8.2 m (27 ft) and a range of five nautical miles.[^8] Rowlett's entry gives a different characteristic for the same light: a white flash every four seconds, with a focal plane of 8 m (27 ft).[^9] LHF notes that the tower first showed a flashing white light, and later a fixed white one.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard list also gives the names of other lights at the harbour: a square skeleton tower called the Sector Light, a West Pier light and a Breakwater light.[^8]

The sources differ on ownership and on access. LHF says the Canadian Coast Guard owns the light, that the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] Rowlett says the site and tower are closed, with good views from the waterfront. It reports that in 2014 ownership was being transferred, presumably to the town, and it names the Town of Grand Bank as site manager.[^9]
