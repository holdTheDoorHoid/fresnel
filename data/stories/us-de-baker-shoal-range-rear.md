Baker Range is a pair of range lights on the western bank of the Delaware River, just north of Port Penn, Delaware. Its historic rear tower is a 110-foot (34 m) iron skeleton structure, first built at Reedy Island in 1896 and moved here in 1904.[^1] Since 2016 the rear light has shown from a new structure about 3,700 feet (1,130 m) south of that tower.[^1] The front light that first stood beside it is gone, and the active front light now stands farther south, at Reedy Island.[^1]

## The place

LighthouseFriends quotes the Lighthouse Board's annual report for 1900 as recording the Army Corps of Engineers' plan to improve the Delaware River shipping channel, from a point nearly opposite Appoquinimink Creek to deep water above Reedy Island, a distance of about 20,500 feet (6.2 km).[^1] To serve the new channel, three sets of range lights were needed: Baker, Liston and Reedy Island New.[^1] The site was soft. The mud there was 79 feet (24 m) deep, so the foundation piles had to be driven about 81 feet (25 m).[^1]

## Building the light

Baker Range was first established on 11 November 1902, with temporary headlight lanterns on posts.[^1] Permanent construction did not get under way until 1904.[^1] The necessary materials were boated to the site.[^1] Concrete iron columns were placed on the foundation piles, and a barn, an oil house and the lighthouse were built atop the piles.[^1] The lighthouse was a two-story, square dwelling with a hipped roof.[^1] The front light was focused downriver from the top floor, and it replaced the temporary light on 16 September 1904.[^1]

The rear tower came from the Reedy Island Old Rear Range, which was discontinued on 16 May 1904 after the new channel opened. LighthouseFriends quotes the 1906 report describing it as "a triangular pyramidal iron skeleton structure 110 feet high."[^1] The archive gives the station's year of establishment as 1902.[^2] The Reedy Island range had been established in 1896, and its keeper, Willard H. Hall, was made the first keeper here.[^1]

## The keepers

Hall was the first keeper here. He stayed less than two years before Harry F. Hann replaced him on 10 June 1906.[^1] One keeper looked after both lights. LighthouseFriends gives the distance between them as 760 yards in one place and as roughly 2,000 feet (610 m) north of the front light in another.[^1] Four men held the post in its first four years, and the shortest term of service was just a few weeks.[^1] The archive also lists Benjamin Burton as acting keeper for 1906, and then Harry E. Spencer (1908 to 1913) and William Harrington (1913 to 1917).[^2]

The keeper William Harrington died in March 1917. The *Evening Journal* of Wilmington reported that he had been in poor health for some time and had undergone an operation, and that on the Saturday night before its paper of 26 March his wife found him dying in a wheat field between the light station and the Port Penn road. He had died before he could be brought to the house.[^3] The archive lists a Mary Harrington for 1917. The paper gives the widow only as "Mrs. Harrington," and it does not say whether she is the Mary Harrington the archive lists.[^2][^3]

Freddie C. Hill followed him. LighthouseFriends calls him Fred C. Hill and says he kept the range for roughly eight years before it was automated.[^1] The archive's roster runs his service from 1917 to 1926.[^2] The Commissioner's report for 1919 lists him as keeper of the Baker Range Light Station.[^6] Hill's wife, Hannah, helped him. In her 1950 testimony to a congressional subcommittee, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, she said that when he fell ill she walked a mile night and morning, "climbed 81 feet in the tower, straight up," and lit the light at night and put it out in the morning.[^1] She is not on the archive's roster.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the night of 28 December 1917 Hill gave aid to two men who had been in an open boat for more than 12 hours and had been working in ice, wet, with their clothing frozen on them. They were duck hunters from Delaware City.[^1] The Lighthouse Service's bulletin for 1918 reports the same rescue.[^4] On 26 September 1923 Hill ran about three-quarters of a mile across the marsh to a man who had sunk up to his armpits in mud, and after two hours of work got him out.[^1][^5] LighthouseFriends also reports an undated rescue, when Hill saved a man who had fallen through the ice near the station and then gave him medical attention, dry clothes and food.[^1]

## After the keepers

The keepers' turnover ended about 1925, when the lights were automated. The mechanism for raising and lowering the rear light was removed, and Coast Guard crews must climb a narrow ladder to service the beacon.[^1] A steel skeleton tower with a white slatted daymark was placed near the southern tip of Reedy Island, 1.9 miles (3.1 km) south of the rear tower, and it remains active.[^1]

LighthouseFriends reports that the Wilmington *Evening Journal* of 12 October 1929 carried a notice offering the front station and its 7.85 acres of marshland to the highest bidder. The bids were to be opened at the Philadelphia office of the Fourth Lighthouse District's superintendent on 20 November.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it is not known whether anyone bid. All that remains at the front site are concrete-filled iron pilings.[^1] In 2016 the Coast Guard moved the rear light to a new structure about 3,700 feet (1,130 m) south of the historic tower.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the station, and the tower is closed.[^1] From Port Penn, Highway 9 runs north for 1.7 miles (2.7 km), and the tower can be seen on the right. Walking trails in the area give access to the river, and one of them may give a better view of the tower.[^1]
