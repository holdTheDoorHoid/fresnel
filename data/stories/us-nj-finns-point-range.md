Finns Point Range Light stands on the Delaware River at Fort Mott, in Pennsville Township, Salem County, New Jersey.[^1][^2] Its wrought-iron rear tower went up in 1876 and 1877 to help ships keep to the river channel.[^1] The sources disagree on when the light was put out of use, with dates around 1930 and 1950.[^1][^2]

## The place

The hazard was the channel itself. The National Register nomination says that in the early 1870s the Lighthouse Establishment proposed "range lights to mark the channel of the Delaware River from Reedy Point south to Ship John Shoal."[^1] Congress approved two pairs of range lights on 3 March 1875, and "In 1876 Congress appropriated $55,000 for construction costs."[^1]

The first plan put all four lights on one property. The nomination says that "the cost of the property was thought to be too high," so two of the lights went to New Jersey, where they "mark the channel north of Stony Point, near Finn's Point."[^1]

## Building the light

The Kellogg Bridge Company of Buffalo, New York, held a contract worth $16,400 "to erect both the Finn's Point and Liston rear range lights."[^1] The company first put the towers up on its own grounds so they could be inspected, and then "the towers were dismantled, crated and shipped." Final erection and placement was completed early in 1877.[^1]

The nomination calls the rear tower a wrought-iron, riveted, skeletal tower, and says "Wrought iron was chosen because of its corrosion resistance." It gives the height from base to focal plane as about 100 feet (30 m).[^1] The Society's record gives the tower 95 feet (29 m) high, with its light 105 feet (32 m) above the water, and lists its material as wrought iron.[^2]

The front light was a different kind of structure. The nomination describes it as "a low wooden structure painted white (believed demolished in 1930's)."[^1]

The Board's reports record repairs in the 1890s.[^4][^5] In 1894 a "driven well and pump were furnished for additional water supply" at the front light.[^4] In the Board's 1896 and 1897 volume (scan page 338), the front light's "day mark, now useless" was removed from the roof, and the rear light's "high tower was put in order" and got pumps and hose "against fire."[^5]

Congress, "by the act approved June 6, 1900," authorized the "reestablishment of this and the Port Penn and Reedy Island ranges."[^6] By the act approved March 3, 1901, $60,000 was appropriated for that work.[^6] The Board's 1901 report records a boring 70 feet (21 m) below the bottom of the river at the crossing of the new ranges.[^6]

## The keepers

The rear light's roster has four lines. Edward Dickinson is printed as principal keeper from 1876 to 1907.[^2] Laura Dickinson followed him as acting principal keeper from 1907 to 1908, and she is the only woman on the rear roster.[^2] Charles W. Norton kept the rear light from 1908 to 1919, and Milton A. Duffield from 1919 to 1933.[^2]

The front light's roster has eleven lines. William S. White is the first keeper printed, for 1876 to 1880. Jonathan Woodruff kept the front light from 1880 to 1894, and Charles W. Norton from 1894 to 1908.[^3] Harry F. Hann served from 1908 to 1916. Minnie Hann is printed for 1916 alone, between Hann and Milton A. Duffield, who was at the front from 1916 to 1919. The roster does not say whether she was related to Harry Hann.[^3] John E. Collins served at the front from 1923 to 1931.[^3]

Norton and Duffield each moved from the front light to the rear. Norton had the front from 1894 to 1908 and the rear from 1908 to 1919, and Duffield had the front from 1916 to 1919 and the rear from 1919 to 1933.[^2][^3] The front roster also prints Norton there for 1919, a year the rear roster counts toward his rear service, so the two rosters overlap for him.[^3]

## After the keepers

In 1918 the Commissioner of Lighthouses' report lists the rear light among lights changed under the heading "INCANDESCENT OIL-VAPOR LIGHTS CHANGED FROM OIL-WICK LIGHTS," with the entry "Finns Point Range Rear, Delaware River, N. J."[^7]

The nomination says that "The Finn's Point Light was abandoned circa 1930, and the lenses removed."[^1] The Society's record gives 1950 as the year the light was discontinued, and says the current optic was removed.[^2] The nomination and the record disagree on the date, and the sources do not say which light the nomination's 1930 refers to.

## Today

The light is opened to visitors by the Supawna Meadows National Wildlife Refuge on set days.[^8] The refuge's 2026 schedule lists openings on 19 April, 17 May, 21 June, 19 July, 9 August and 20 September, from 1 to 4 p.m., and on 17 and 18 October, from 8 a.m. to 6 p.m. during the New Jersey Lighthouse Challenge.[^8] The refuge warns that "Openings are weather dependent and will be canceled in the event of thunderstorms or extreme heat."[^8] Those dates have passed, so check the refuge's page for the next season. The refuge office is currently closed, but the Grassland Trail can be walked from the large parking area on Lighthouse Road.[^8]
