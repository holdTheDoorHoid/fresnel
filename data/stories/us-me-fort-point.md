Fort Point Light stands at the mouth of the Penobscot River, on Cape Jellison in Stockton Springs, Maine.[^2][^3] The National Register nomination dates the first light on the point, a granite tower, to 1836.[^1][^2] The brick tower that stands there now was built in 1857, and it still carries a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^1][^2] The Coast Guard automated the light in 1988, and under the Maine Lights Program the deed passed to the state in 1998.[^2][^3] The light sits inside Fort Point State Park.[^2]

## The place

The point had a fort before it had a light. In May 1759 a force of about four hundred men under Massachusetts Royal Governor Thomas Pownall reached the mouth of the Penobscot, and they built Fort Pownall on Cape Jellison. The town's history spells the governor's name Pownal.[^8][^2] The fort stayed occupied until the British removed its cannons and powder in 1775.[^2]

Congress appropriated $5,000 on 30 June 1834 for a lighthouse on Fort Point.[^2] The point marks the entrance to the Penobscot River, and ships hauling potatoes and timber from the area relied on the light.[^1][^2]

A hotel on the point, the Wassaumkeag, is dated to 1872 by LighthouseFriends, which says it was described in 1875 as having 125 rooms. Its guests were invited to walk over the light station's grounds, and a lighthouse inspector called for fencing to keep the keeper's privacy.[^2] The Stockton Springs town history dates an elegant summer hotel of that name to 1895, so the sources disagree about when it was built.[^8]

## Building the light

The first tower was a conical tower of undressed split granite, with an octagonal iron lantern holding eight lamps with thirteen-inch reflectors. LighthouseFriends says its light stood ninety-nine feet (30.2 m) above high water, and that the tower and dwelling cost just over $4,377.[^2] The Digital Archive gives 1837 as the year the station was established, one year later than the nomination's 1836.[^3][^1]

Within about six years, Inspector I. W. P. Lewis reported that the keeper's dwelling walls were cracked on all four sides and that the tower was cracked from roof to base, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The keeper, William Clewley, described the house in his own words: "My dwelling-house is very much out of repair."[^2] Levi Bowden took over in April 1850, and LighthouseFriends says the problems still had not been corrected. LighthouseFriends spells his name Bowdin in this passage.[^2][^3] It was not until 1855 that the Board judged the station "entirely worn out" and asked Congress for $5,000 to rebuild it.[^2]

The new tower went up in 1857. The nomination gives its height as twenty-six feet (7.9 m) from the base to the center of the lantern. LighthouseFriends and Maine Lights Today give thirty-one feet (9.5 m).[^1][^2][^9] It is a square brick tower attached to a keeper's house that the nomination also dates to 1857.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes the house as two-story and wooden. The nomination describes it as a one-and-a-half-story frame house with an L-shaped plan.[^1][^2]

Later changes followed in order. A new lantern was fitted in 1868, and a bell tower and barn were added in 1890.[^2] The nomination dates the pyramidal wooden bell tower to 1890.[^1] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 the Light-House Board recorded a 1,200-pound bell provided for the station, with minor repairs to the dwelling and tower.[^4] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1893 the Board recorded that the fuel house was rebuilt and the barn repaired.[^5] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1897 records an oil house built, and the nomination dates it to 1897.[^6][^1] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 the Board recorded the dwelling's interior rearranged to provide one more room, and three dormer windows built.[^7][^1]

In 1935 an incandescent oil vapor lamp was installed. The light was electrified in 1950, to 4,500 candlepower.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists 24 principal keeper stints for the station from 1836 to 1988.[^3] The table on this page carries every stint. The stories below are the ones the sources tell in detail.

William Clewley was the first keeper, from 1836 to 1850, and he sold the land for the light to the government.[^2][^3] Levi Bowden followed him, and John Odom was keeper when the Board asked Congress for money to rebuild the station in 1855.[^2][^3]

Hiram J. Grant kept the light from 1866 to 1882. LighthouseFriends calls him a Civil War veteran.[^2][^3]

Arthur B. Mitchell kept the light from 1929 to 1950. He was a former captain of a two-masted schooner, and he said the new oil vapor lamp gave more light but also took more work.[^2][^3]

Ernest H. Mathie, from 1950 to 1957, was the first Coast Guard-employed keeper at the station.[^2][^3] Larry Baum, from 1984 to 1988, was the last keeper when the Coast Guard automated the light in October 1988.[^2][^3]

Lawrence "Terry" Cole kept the light from 1973 to 1976. LighthouseFriends says he later served as the park's manager.[^2][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Fort Point Hotel burned on the night of 7 June 1898.[^2][^10] The Ellsworth American reported the next day that the hotel had burned and that it would not be rebuilt.[^10] LighthouseFriends quotes the keeper's report of the alarm: "Alarm was given at 10 pm, that the Fort Point Hotel was on fire."[^2] The ruins of its foundation are still on view beside the ruins of the old fort and the lighthouse, according to the town's history.[^8]

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 records that the fog bell was disabled on 15 March and put back into working order on 17 March 1899.[^7]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard leased the station to the Maine Bureau of Parks and Land in 1989.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the keeper's house has since served as housing for a park supervisor.[^2] Under the Maine Lights Program, the deed passed to the State of Maine in 1998.[^2]

The pyramidal wooden bell tower is listed on the National Register of Historic Places, and LighthouseFriends calls it one of the few left in New England.[^2][^1]

## Today

The light is in Fort Point State Park, and LighthouseFriends says the Maine Bureau of Parks and Lands owns the lighthouse. The grounds are open daily from 9 a.m. to sunset. The tower is closed to visitors.[^2]

From Highway 1 near Stockton Springs, LighthouseFriends directs visitors into town on Main Street and then south on Cape Jellison Road to the park entrance. The road just past the entrance leads directly to the lighthouse.[^2] The fort's ruins and the hotel's foundation are on view nearby.[^8] The light remains an active guide to navigation, and it is one of nine lighthouses in Maine that still use a historic Fresnel lens.[^2]
