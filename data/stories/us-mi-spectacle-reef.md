Spectacle Reef Light is a stone tower in northern Lake Huron, built on a limestone shoal about eleven miles from the nearest land, off the eastern end of the Straits of Mackinac.[^1] The Light-House Board raised it from rock lying eleven feet under the lake, behind a timber pier and a pumped-out coffer-dam, and lit it for the first time on the night of June 1, 1874.[^1] It has been a place of ice, drownings and two gold medals, and today it belongs to a volunteer society that is restoring it.

## The place

The reef is two shoals of limestone covered with boulders, placed so that together they were said to resemble a pair of spectacles. The light stands on the southerly end of the more northerly shoal.[^1] In 1868 the Light-House Board recommended an appropriation to build a light there, and it pressed the point by noting that the wreck of two vessels the previous fall had cost more than marking the danger would.[^1] Lighthousefriends names the two as the barques *Annie Vought* and *Alice Richards*, lost in the fall of 1867, and the Spectacle Reef Preservation Society spells the first *Annie Voight*.[^2][^3]

The water was the problem. Heap, writing in 1889, described currents of two to three miles an hour that carried ice-fields "two feet thick" and thousands of acres wide to and fro across the reef in winter, and waves with a fetch of about 170 miles from the southeast.[^1] The light was shut down for the winter, and in the spring of 1875, when the keepers came back, they found ice piled seven feet above the sill of the tower's doorway, which stood 23 feet (7 m) above the lake. They had to cut their way in.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress gave $100,000 to begin and the same sum the next year, according to Heap.[^1] Lighthousefriends dates the first grant to March 3, 1869.[^2] A survey found the least depth on the shoals to be about seven feet (2 m) and bedrock at the chosen spot at eleven feet (3.4 m). The nearest land, Bois Blanc Island, was about eleven miles away, so the work was run from a depot at Scammon's Harbor in Les Cheneaux, which Heap puts sixteen miles from the site and Lighthousefriends seventeen.[^1][^2]

Two obstacles came early. In the survey of 1870 the engineers found the hull of the schooner *Nightingale*, wrecked the previous fall with a cargo of iron ore, lying over much of the bare rock where the tower was to stand, and the part covering the coffer-dam's area had to be removed.[^1] And the granite the tower was meant to use never arrived: the contractor, in Heap's words, "utterly failed" to supply it, so limestone from Marblehead, Ohio, was bought instead.[^1]

The plan had three stages. First a timber "protection pier" with a large central opening was built at the harbor in 1870 and 1871. It was 92 feet (28 m) square and between eleven and twelve feet high, with an opening 48 feet (14.6 m) square. Then a coffer-dam was set in the opening and pumped dry, so the rock could be levelled. Only then could the stone tower go up.[^1] On the evening of July 18, 1871, two tugs started towing the whole crib to the reef, fifteen miles off. It was placed the next morning "with considerable difficulty," and by four that afternoon 1,200 tons of stone had been thrown into it.[^1] A schooner moored on the reef housed the workmen until quarters were built on the pier.[^1]

The coffer-dam, a hollow cylinder 41 feet (12.5 m) across made of wooden staves, was pumped dry on October 14, 1871, and the first course of masonry was finished on October 27.[^1] Water kept forcing its way up through seams in the rock and disturbing the mortar, so each evening water was let into the dam and each morning it was pumped out again, to give the mortar the night to harden.[^1] By the end of that October every man had left the rock but two, who stayed to tend the fog signal and a fourth-order light on the men's quarters until navigation closed.[^1]

The next year's work began on the reef on May 20, 1872, with ice still lying on top of the pier.[^1] That September a gale struck. An account that Heap prints without naming its author says the sea "burst in the doors and windows of the workmen's quarters, tore up the floors and all the bunks on the side nearest the edge of the pier," and that at three in the morning the men were obliged "to run for their lives"; many lost their clothing.[^1] Lighthousefriends dates the storm to the night of September 28 and says the account came from the superintendent of construction.[^2] That winter ice carried away the quarters and destroyed the lantern, though the lens had been taken out and stored beforehand.[^1] Heap names Anthony L. Ederle as superintendent of construction from the start of the work.[^1]

The tower is a cone with a diameter of 32 feet (9.8 m) at the base, solid to a height of 34 feet (10.4 m) and hollow above, in five stories. Heap gives the masonry as 93 feet (28 m) above the base.[^1] The crib and coffer-dam were designed by Col. W. F. Raynolds and the tower by Lt. Col. O. M. Poe, both of the Army's Corps of Engineers; Maj. Godfrey Weitzel finished the tower.[^1] The lens was a second-order Henry-Lepaute Fresnel, with alternating white and red flashes, and Patrick J. McCann was the first head keeper.[^2] Heap gives a total cost of about $375,000, including the steamer and other plant, while Lighthousefriends and the Society give $406,000.[^1][^2][^3]

The 1934 article calls the crib temporary in nature but says it was retained, once the tower was completed, to provide deck space for a fog-signal building, and Heap notes that it served as a landing place and a site for the steam fog whistles.[^4][^1] Lighthousefriends reports $15,000 granted in October 1888 for renewing it, with the upper part rebuilt between May and September 1889, and a concrete pier with an iron fog-signal building built over 1904 to 1906.[^2] A Lighthouse Service article of 1934 says that in 1904 a wall of mass concrete was poured inside the crib, 20 feet (6 m) thick at the bottom.[^4] By 1915 the Commissioner of Lighthouses was reporting that the wall was "disintegrating at the water line" and asking for $20,000.[^5] In 1921 a concrete protection wall was being placed around three sides of the pier, and in 1922 the foundation repair was reported nearly complete.[^6][^7] Steel sheet piling was driven against the pier in the 1930s; Lighthousefriends counts 110 piles driven in 1934.[^4][^2]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive, drawn from the keepers' registers, lists Patrick J. McCann as head keeper from 1874 to 1880, with Lorenzo O. Holden as first assistant from 1874 to 1881 and John Mulcrone as second assistant from 1874 to 1877. A third assistant, Charles Martin, appears from 1876.[^8]

The best-recorded family on the roster is the Marshalls. William Marshall II is listed as head keeper from 1881 to 1896, after serving as third assistant from 1877. Others of the name are listed as Thomas (second and first assistant, 1879 to 1882), James C. (1882 to 1883), Walter G. (assistant from 1883, head keeper 1898 to 1910) and Arthur S. (1902 to 1910).[^8] The sources do not say how most of them were related to William, apart from his son James. Mackinac State Historic Parks adds that William was one of five siblings who served as keepers.[^9]

Careers moved up the ladder. Lawrence W. Clark appears first as a third assistant in 1911, was first assistant from 1913 to 1921, and was head keeper in 1921 and 1922.[^8]

Fog work was a large part of the job. The Light-House Board's table for one year in the volume covering 1891 and 1892 records the 10-inch steam whistle running 202 hours, and notes that driftwood picked up by the keepers and burned as fuel made the coal burned per hour of fog "much less than at any other station." By the later 1890s a report gives about 400 hours in a year.[^10][^11] The Lighthouse Service changed the station's steam whistle to an air diaphone in 1925, and Lighthousefriends says the lamp was changed to incandescent oil vapor on October 25, 1912.[^12][^2]

After the Coast Guard took over, the roster lists Coast Guard crew members, and Richard LeLievre, listed for 1971 to 1972; Lighthousefriends calls him the last officer in charge.[^8][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

**April 1883.** The keepers' boat capsized on the way out to the light, and the keeper's son James drowned. The accounts differ on some details.

The Life-Saving Service's list of medal awards dates the rescue to April 15, 1883.[^13] The Northern Tribune of April 21 says that "last Sunday" (April 15) the keeper, "Thomas Marshall," left Mackinac in a sail boat with his assistants Edward Lasley, Edward Chambers and James Marshall, the keeper's son.[^14] When they were about two miles from the Bois Blanc light the boat upset and James Marshall was drowned; the rest, the paper says, spent about three hours in the water before getting ashore.[^14] When the same paper reported the medals in August, it named the keeper as Wm. Marshall and dated the rescue April 18.[^15] The roster lists William Marshall II as head keeper in 1883 and Thomas Marshall as an assistant only to 1882.[^8] The Mackinac parks' account likewise names Thomas as the keeper at the start and William later.[^9]

The Mackinac parks' account quotes special correspondent Robert Graves, writing in 1891: "The weather was bitter cold, a gale was blowing and the lake was full of ice, both fixed and floating." The men clung to the hull until two brothers, Joseph and Alfred Cardran (the park account spells the name Cadreau), put out from the Bois Blanc shore in a small skiff. Joseph reached the keeper and his son, but the skiff capsized in the breakers; Alfred then emptied it and brought off Lasley and Chambers one at a time.[^9] The Holden family, according to the same account, rubbed William Marshall for five hours to bring him back; James Marshall's body was later recovered, at age 21.[^9] Lighthousefriends tells the story the same way and adds that the steamer meant to take the keepers out had been disabled.[^2]

Both brothers received gold medals of the Life-Saving Service, awarded June 7, 1883, for rescuing "the keeper of the Spectacle Reef lighthouse, Lake Huron, and two of his assistants."[^13] The park account says the medals were presented on Mackinac Island on July 15, 1884, and that both brothers later worked for the Lighthouse Service.[^9] The roster bears that out: Joseph Cardran is listed as a third assistant at Spectacle Reef in 1884 and 1885, and Alfred Cardran in 1889 and 1890.[^8]

**November 1896.** Assistant keeper David D. Spaulding drowned at the reef. The Crawford Avalanche of November 26 reported that he left Cheboygan in a small boat during "the awful storm," reached the reef, but anchored so far from the light that a rope thrown to him could not reach, and in pulling up the anchor he "was thrown into the waves and drowned." He left a wife and family in Alpena.[^16] The Society dates the accident to November 10.[^3]

**December 1927.** The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that the whole of December was very cold with unusually strong gales, and that at Spectacle Reef the keepers had to leave the tower by the windows, because ice had formed in great masses on the pier and blocked the doors.[^17] Lighthousefriends says they were taken off on December 14 by the crew of the lightship *Poe* and lowered by rope.[^2]

**1959.** In February a plane flown by Sergeant William J. Wyman went down in Lake Huron about a mile from the light. When the keepers opened the station on April 8 they found a note from him, saying he had reached the tower over the ice and had signalled with the winter light before trying to walk to shore. He was never found.[^2] That September head keeper Cyril J. Jones drowned after wind swept him and another man out from the base of the tower while they were swimming.[^2][^8]

**1970.** Opening the station that spring, Richard LeLievre's crew went out on the cutter *Sundew*, which had to ram the ice several times, and then chop through about five feet of it to reach the tower door.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard removed its last keepers in 1972 and the light was automated.[^2][^18] The Society and Lighthousefriends give LeLievre's tour as 1970 to 1972, where the archive roster lists him from 1971.[^3][^2][^8] The second-order lens stayed until 1982, when a modern optic replaced it; the lens is now on display at the National Museum of the Great Lakes in Toledo, Ohio.[^3]

The Coast Guard deemed the station excess in May 2014 and offered it under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act. No qualified owner came forward, and an online auction in 2015 ended with a high bid of $43,575 from a private buyer.[^2] In December 2020 ownership passed to the non-profit Spectacle Reef Preservation Society.[^2]

## Today

The tower is listed on the National Register of Historic Places (reference 05000744).[^3] It is owned by the Spectacle Reef Preservation Society, a non-profit formed in 2020 to preserve and interpret it, and the Society's site says the restoration is a multi-year project aimed at opening to the public in 2026.[^18] The light is now a modern LED showing a red flash every five seconds.[^3]

It stands miles from shore on private property, and the tower is closed to casual visitors.[^2][^18] Lighthousefriends says it is best seen from a boat, and names an extended lighthouse cruise offered by Shepler's as a way to see it.[^2]
