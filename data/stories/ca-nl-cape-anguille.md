Cape Anguille is the westernmost point of Newfoundland, the headland that forms the southwest edge of St. George's Bay.[^1] A fog alarm and a flying-buttress concrete tower were built here from 1906 on, and members of one family, the Patrys, kept the light from 1908 into the 1980s; a Patry was keeper again from 1992.[^2][^3]

## The place

Cape Anguille lies eighteen miles northeast of Cape Ray, on the route that sailing vessels used when they entered or left the Gulf of St. Lawrence south-west of Newfoundland.[^1] In 1860 John Page, chief engineer of the Department of Public Works of the Province of Canada, inspected four points on this coast, Cape Anguille among them. He noted that mariners had long regarded a light on the south-west of the island as one of the most important that could be established, and recommended Cape Ray if only one light were built; Cape Ray was eventually chosen and lit in 1871, as Lighthouse Friends summarises his report.[^1] Parks Canada's statement of significance says the Canadian government built aids to navigation on Newfoundland's west coast even before Newfoundland joined Confederation in 1949, because the coast lay on an important route for shipping to and from the St. Lawrence.[^14]

## Building the light

The Department of Marine and Fisheries took up the site in the 1900s. Its report for 1906 recorded "a new fog alarm station" under construction at Cape Anguille, with materials bought and on the spot, and a cost to date of $2,541.81, all by day labour under the Quebec agency.[^4] The same report listed the place as "Cape Anguille, P.Q." and said a third-order double flashing light like the one at Matane would be installed, in operation, it hoped, early in the summer of 1907.[^5] The Heritage Foundation of Newfoundland and Labrador, by contrast, says the station was authorised in 1905, that the land came from a Mr. James Samms and that building began in 1907; it dates the tower to 1908-1909.[^6]

The report for 1907 described a fog-alarm building holding a 5-inch diaphone and two 50 horse-power boilers as nearly finished, with the machinery shipped ready to install, and a reinforced steel concrete tower in course of construction, with work on the tower to be started shortly; the cost to date was $15,231.41.[^7] The 1908 report said the tower was octagonal, 10 feet in diameter, braced by eight flying buttresses, and 105 feet (32 m) from base to the top of its lantern. The wooden fog alarm building measured 54 by 32 feet, and a dwelling for the fog alarm engineer, a flag house and an oil store were built too, at a cost that year of $13,329.15.[^8] The following year's report said the tower and fog alarm building were completed and the diaphone installed, with a coal shed and boathouse added, for a further $6,911.91.[^9] It also named M. de Miffonis among the engineers sent out to supervise reinforced-concrete towers, among them Cap Anguille.[^10]

The light was a third-order dioptric lens showing two white flashes every ten seconds, burned petroleum vapour under an incandescent mantle, and was rated at 100,000 candlepower.[^11] Lighthouse Friends adds that the lens came from Barbier, Benard & Turenne of Paris, and that the tower and fog alarm were placed in operation in 1908.[^2] The sources thus differ a little on timing: the 1908 report describes the tower as still to receive its lantern, the Heritage Foundation dates the tower to 1908-1909, and the 1909 report calls the works completed.[^8][^6][^9]

## The keepers

The dwelling was a double house, meant for the keeper and for the fog alarm engineer.[^6] Alfred Patry, a bilingual resident of Quebec City, was appointed the first keeper in October 1908.[^2] According to his son Laurier, as the heritage record reports, relations between the two families soured quickly, the engineer moved to nearby Codroy, and Patry opened doors between the two halves so that his family had the whole house.[^6] By 1923 Alfred and his wife Catherine had fifteen children.[^2]

Catherine Patry died in April 1943 and Alfred fell ill the same year, leaving the light to Laurier, who was made head keeper in 1945; Alfred died two years later.[^2] Laurier retired on 31 December 1983, and Henry Reid, an assistant since 1959, replaced him.[^2] Reid retired in 1991, and in early 1992 Laurier's son Leonard Patry, then keeper at Channel Head, Port aux Basques, was transferred to Cape Anguille.[^2] A 2011 newspaper article described Leonard Patry as keeper at the light, checking the equipment and signing the log book each morning.[^12] The dates differ slightly: Lighthouse Friends says Laurier retired at the end of 1983, while the federal heritage statement says Patry family members served as lightkeepers until 1982.[^2][^3]

## After the keepers

Between 1958 and 1960 a new octagonal concrete tower and fog alarm building replaced the 1908 works, which had begun to deteriorate; the old lighthouse was demolished.[^2] The new tower is 59 feet (18 m) tall and flashes white every five seconds.[^2] New bungalows for the keepers followed in 1965 and 1968, and Laurier Patry's family left the old double dwelling in 1968.[^2][^6] The Coast Guard decided in 1988 to demolish it, but after a heritage evaluation it was left standing empty.[^6]

The station was de-staffed in June 1992 and later staffed again.[^2] In 2002 the station, apart from the tower and a small office, was handed to the Southwest Coast Development Association; two years later Linkum Tours opened the Cape Anguille Lighthouse Inn in the old duplex, which had become a provincial Registered Heritage Structure in 2003.[^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard List of Lights gives the present light as a white flash of 0.1 second every 5 seconds, 24.7 m above the water, with a range of 15 nautical miles. The tower is white and octagonal with a red top and stands 17.8 m; a fog horn gives a 3-second blast every 30 seconds.[^13] The 1960 tower was designated a Recognized Federal Heritage Building in 2007.[^2] The tower is owned by the Coast Guard, with the grounds open and the tower closed; the former keeper's duplex, with a newer dwelling, has been used to host guests.[^2]
