Throgs Neck Light stood on the northerly side of the entrance from Long Island Sound into the East River, at the southeastern end of the Throgs Neck peninsula in the Bronx, New York.[^1][^4] Its site is now on the campus of the SUNY Maritime College.[^5] Its history runs from a tower of the 1820s, through the building of a fort that brought the first light down, to a brick tower on the fort's walls in 1906 and a light discontinued in 1934.[^4][^5]

## The place

Throgs Neck projects into Long Island Sound at the point where the Sound meets the East River.[^5] The State of New York obtained the land for a lighthouse in 1823.[^5] LighthouseFriends says a light at this entrance was recognised as needed early on.[^5] Building began in 1826 under the direction of Timothy, Ezra and Elisha Daboll.[^5] The rest of the peninsula was bought while the work went on, and the plan was a defensive fort on the strategic spot.[^5]

## Building the light

The archive gives 1827 as the year the station was established, while LighthouseFriends says the first lighthouse was completed in 1826.[^4][^5] Less than a decade later the first light and the original keeper's dwelling were torn down during the building of Fort Schuyler in 1835, and a temporary wooden octagonal tower forty feet (12 m) tall was set up near the fort.[^5] In 1838 the wooden tower was cited as leaky.[^5] LighthouseFriends reports that in 1868 it was described as old and leaky, in need of major repairs, with an inferior lantern and no room for supplies. The Lighthouse Board proposed a new lantern and storage for oil and supplies in the lower tower, and the work was carried out in 1869.[^5]

The first fog bell was established at the station in 1854, and in 1869 a hot-air-engine fog bell was set up. In 1855 a Fresnel lens took the place of the older reflectors and lamps.[^5] In 1890 an iron skeleton tower took the place of the wooden one, with a fifth-order Fresnel lens in its lantern.[^5]

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1896 records that an oil house was built at the station.[^2] The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 records that the War Department was authorised to extend a battery embankment roughly six feet into the light-house reservation.[^3]

In its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901, the Board called the position "objectionable from a military point of view," because the tower and keeper's dwelling stood in the field of fire of recently built batteries.[^1] It recommended a small light on the bastion of Fort Schuyler, an iron stairway up the wall, repairs to a building that could serve as the keeper's residence if it could be secured from the War Department, a fence around the reservation, and taking the present light down for storage. It estimated the new tower at $6,000.[^1]

LighthouseFriends dates what followed. A contract for a thirty-five-foot (11 m) tower was let on 2 October 1905. The brick tower on the fort's walls began operating on 25 July 1906 with a fourth-order lens.[^5] The archive gives 1906 as the year the tower was established, with a conical brick tower 35 feet (11 m) high.[^4]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends describes the keeper's house as "in a good state of repair" in 1838.[^5] By 1874, it reports, its decayed sills had left the doors and windows out of position.[^5] The Lighthouse Board proposed a new dwelling for $8,000, but no money was provided, so repairs were made in 1875, the same year kerosene replaced lard oil as the lamp's fuel.[^5] In 1881 the house was judged beyond repair, and a new one was begun in 1883 and finished the following year.[^5]

The archive's roster has 22 entries, from 1826 to at least 1959. Every entry is a principal keeper, and Riley Sherwood appears twice.[^4] Samuel Youngs served from 1826 to 1831, and his successor James Bayliss to 1841.[^4] LighthouseFriends, which spells the first keeper's name Young, says that in the early 1830s duck hunters knew they could drop in at the station. It adds that the first two keepers ran a bar there to supplement their pay.[^5]

Ellen Lyons, whom the archive lists as Ellen Lyons (Kilmartin), was principal keeper from 1876 to 1881.[^4][^5]

The archive has no entry for 1857 to 1859, between Riley Sherwood and Manus Kelly. LighthouseFriends names Lawrence Lewis for those years.[^5]

Alexander Ferreira kept the light from 1884 to 1910, and his son Charles A. Ferreira from 1910 to 1944.[^4][^5] LighthouseFriends says Charles was born at the Navesink light, came to Throgs Neck with his father in 1884 at the age of ten, and lived there for sixty years. It reports his claim that he never missed a day's work and that the light never went dark.[^5] It describes his 1944 retirement ceremony, which drew former keepers, coastguardsmen, a Navy vice admiral and 450 midshipmen, and cites a New York Sun article of 9 August 1944 among its references.[^5]

Adam L. Kohlman is listed from 1944, and Albert G. Possell from at least 1950 to 1959.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 8 December 1929 Charles Ferreira rescued a soldier whose canoe had capsized off Fort Schuyler, according to LighthouseFriends.[^5]

## After the keepers

The archive gives 1934 as the year the station was discontinued. It says the keeper's house remains and the tower does not.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the brick tower was discontinued in 1934 and replaced by a skeletal tower sixty feet (18 m) tall, with a fixed red light and a range of eleven miles (about 9.6 nautical miles).[^5] The same year, SUNY Maritime College established a campus at Fort Schuyler.[^5] A plain modern tower with a red light replaced that tower in 1986.[^5]

The keeper's dwelling of 1884 served as Coast Guard housing into the late 1980s. LighthouseFriends says it is now the home of a SUNY Maritime College faculty member.[^4][^5]

## Today

The grounds sit on the SUNY Maritime campus, beneath the northern end of the Throgs Neck Bridge. The college owns the dwelling.[^5] LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds are open and the dwelling closed, and that the Maritime Industry Museum at Fort Schuyler has a fog bell and a lens that were used at the station.[^5]

The archive lists the station as not open to the public, and as no longer an active aid to navigation.[^4] The sources disagree about whether a light still shows here. The archive says the tower does not survive, while LighthouseFriends describes the 1986 modern tower with its red light.[^4][^5] Check with the college before going.
