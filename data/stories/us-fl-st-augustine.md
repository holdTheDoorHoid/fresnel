A 165-foot (50 m) brick tower banded in black and white spirals rises at the north end of Anastasia Island, across the inlet from the city of St. Augustine, Florida.[^1] It is the second American lighthouse on a shore where the Spanish had built a watchtower, and where the sea eventually took the first American tower.[^1][^3]

## The place

St. Augustine was the leading port of the new Territory of Florida, and in 1823 Congress appropriated $5,000 to turn the old Spanish watchtower on Anastasia Island into a lighthouse.[^7] The National Register nomination traces the site back to a wooden Spanish lookout of the sixteenth century, replaced later by a stone tower that was converted to a light in 1823.[^1] The St. Augustine Lighthouse & Maritime Museum dates the first documented wooden watchtower to a 1589 map and says that by 1737 the Spanish had a 30-foot (9 m) tower of coquina (a shell rock) and wood on the spot.[^3] Sources do not agree on when this tower first counted as a lighthouse: the nomination says 1823, Lighthouse Friends says it went into service in 1824, and the museum points to British-era charts of the 1760s and 1780s that already mark a "lighthouse" or a signal there.[^1][^7][^3]

The American light was kept first by Juan Andreu, a Minorcan, who was paid $350 a year to tend ten lamps in silver reflectors.[^7] The archive of the U.S. Lighthouse Society lists him as head keeper from 1824 to 1849.[^2] The tower was raised by about ten feet in 1852 and fitted with a fourth-order Fresnel lens soon after; the museum gives 1853 for the lens and Lighthouse Friends 1854.[^3][^7]

The trouble was the shore. According to Lighthouse Friends, the distance from the keeper's dwelling to the high-water mark was seventy feet on 1 July 1870 and only forty by November, and the Lighthouse Board concluded that a new tower was needed.[^7] A five-acre site farther back and on higher ground was bought from Dr. Charles W. Ballard of Albert Lea, Minnesota, and on 16 May 1872 Governor Harrison Reed signed the document ceding the state's jurisdiction over it.[^1]

## Building the light

The new tower was designed, Lighthouse Friends says, by Paul J. Pelz, chief draftsman of the Lighthouse Board.[^7] The National Register nomination names Hezekiah H. Pittee (1826-1918), a native of Maine and superintendent of lighthouse construction along the Atlantic coast, as the man in charge of the building.[^1] The Board signed a contract on 10 October 1871 with Paulding, Kemble and Company of Cold Spring, New York, for the metal work of a first-order light, to be delivered by 1 April 1872.[^1]

Money came in stages. Congress gave $60,000 on 3 March 1871 and $20,000 more in 1872; the walls had risen only a few feet when work stopped for lack of funds, and a further $25,000 on 3 March 1873 let it go on.[^7] The nomination gives the total cost as $105,000, which matches the three sums.[^1][^7] The museum adds that the temporary buildings put up for workers and horses were pulled down afterwards, as regulations required, so as not to change the look of the station from the sea, and that African American residents of St. Augustine were among the workers.[^3] Brick came from Alabama and granite from Georgia, according to Lighthouse Friends, with the ironwork forged in Philadelphia, though the contract the nomination cites for the metal work was with a firm in Cold Spring, New York.[^7][^1]

The first-order lens was made in Paris by Sauter & Lemonnier and stands nine feet tall.[^3] The museum quotes the *Saint Augustine Weekly News* of 28 February 1889 on how it worked: a ten-gallon brass lamp, a five-wick burner, and a revolving glass case around the flame; of the whole assembly the paper wrote that "The cage weighs two tons."[^3] The tower was lit on 15 October 1874.[^3][^7] In its first month, Lighthouse Friends reports, a flock of ducks flew into the lantern, broke three panes and slightly damaged the lens, and a wire screen was fitted around the lantern room afterwards.[^7]

The sources differ on the tower's figures. The nomination gives a height of 165 feet and a focal plane of 101 feet above the sea, with the light visible for nineteen miles; Lighthouse Friends gives a focal plane of 165 feet; the Light-House Board's own 1894 report describes a brick tower 150 feet high.[^1][^7][^8] The Board explained the black and white spirals in practical terms: the shaft was painted so that it could not be mistaken by day for the other tall brick towers on the coast.[^8]

A keepers' house was needed too, since the old dwelling was too far from the new tower and too small for the three keepers a first-order light required.[^7] Lighthouse Friends dates the $20,000 appropriation to 23 June 1874 and the duplex to 1875; the nomination puts the appropriation in 1872 and the completed house in 1876.[^1][^7] The house had a coquina basement, which the museum's marker says stored supplies delivered by steamship until 1927.[^13]

## The keepers

Three keepers worked the light: a head keeper and two assistants.[^4] The Lighthouse Society's list names the station's keepers and assistants from 1824 to 1955.[^2]

The best-documented early story is that of the Andreus. Joseph J. Andreu, a cousin of Juan, was head keeper from 1854, and in 1859 he fell about sixty feet to his death from the scaffolding while whitewashing the old tower.[^7][^2] His widow, Maria Mestre de los Dolores Andreu, became keeper and served until about 1862; the museum says she waited several weeks for the government to appoint her officially and that the light apparently kept burning meanwhile.[^6][^2] The Coast Guard, the museum notes, has recognized her as the first Hispanic American woman to serve in the Coast Guard or its predecessors.[^6]

The Civil War took the lens. The museum says that Paul Arnau, a Minorcan harbor master and Confederate sympathizer, had a man named Nelligan remove the fourth-order lens and hid it with the clockwork; Union forces arrested Arnau and held him aboard a gunboat until he gave the equipment up.[^6] Lighthouse Friends tells it differently, saying that Joseph Pacetti, a Confederate soldier, was wounded while removing the lens.[^7] The light was relit on 1 June 1867.[^7]

At the new tower, the museum names William R. Russell as the keeper who lit the lamp on 15 October 1874, while the Lighthouse Society's list gives the head keeper of 1873-1875 as William W. Russell; this may be one man recorded two ways, but no source says so.[^3][^2] The first long-serving head keeper was William A. Harn, listed from 1875 to 1889.[^2][^14] Harn had been a Union soldier at Fort Sumter in 1861; by Gettysburg, the museum says, he commanded his own artillery unit, the 3rd New York Independent Artillery, and he earned the rank of brevet major.[^4] He and his wife Kate (Skillen) arrived with five daughters; a sixth, Grace, was born in 1882.[^4] The family lived for a year in the old station's keepers' house and then moved into the new one, the first keeper's family to do so.[^4] Harn died of tuberculosis at one in the morning on 31 May 1889; the local post of the Grand Army of the Republic called him "a faithful servant" of the government.[^4]

Within days, according to the museum, townspeople circulated a petition "urging the government to appoint Mrs. Harn as lighthouse keeper," which was "extensively signed." Kate Harn served as second assistant for about six months, until she secured her husband's military pension, and later moved with her daughters back to her home state of Maine.[^4][^2] First assistant Joseph Rantia, a descendant of the Minorcan settlers of New Smyrna, became head keeper; he held the post from 1889 to 1901.[^4][^5] His wife Mary died at nine o'clock on the evening of 23 September 1894, and the keepers' log, the museum says, shows him carrying on with the work.[^5]

In 1901 Rantia and Peter Rasmusson swapped stations: Rasmusson came from Hunting Island, South Carolina, at $720 a year, and stayed 23 years, the longest head-keeper tenure at the station.[^5][^2] The Lighthouse Service later awarded the station an efficiency flag in Rasmusson's time.[^9] He kept a count of visitors, and reported 1,500 visitors in 1901, 8,500 in 1912 and 5,500 in the first three months of 1913, adding that about a quarter of visitors slipped away without signing the register.[^5][^7]

John Lindquist, who had been first assistant from 1893 to 1901, became head keeper in 1924.[^2] The Bulletin of the Lighthouse Service for 1924 credits him with helping to take ashore the sick chief engineer of the motor vessel *Solitaire*, and its index records an efficiency flag for the station under his name.[^10][^12] Later heads included Cardell D. Daniels (1935-1943 in the Lighthouse Society's list; the nomination puts his arrival in 1936), who had served as a second assistant in 1911-1914, and James L. Pippin, who was the last resident keeper.[^2][^1] Lighthouse Friends retells a family story about Daniels's son, who used the tower as a launch pad for model aeroplanes and parachutes, and a family cat as the reluctant passenger.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The keepers' log, which the museum quotes, records the sea's last advance on the old station. On 10 September 1878 a storm "washed away the foundation from under the dwelling of the old lighthouse and the building tumbled over"; the old tower itself fell "at 5 p.m." on a "very high tide" on 20 June 1880.[^4] Lighthouse Friends dates the fall to 22 August 1880, and the nomination says only 1880.[^7][^1] The Bulletin of 1914 shows the water was still a worry: St. Augustine was among the stations for which shore protection was "under consideration" that year.[^11]

The log also notes earthquakes. On 11 January 1879 it recorded "the building to shake and windows to rattle," and on 31 August 1886, the day of the Charleston earthquake, the "tower swayed in a violent manner"; no damage was reported.[^4]

Keepers sometimes served as lifesavers. Lighthouse Friends quotes two log entries: on 23 September 1887 the schooner *Dream* went on the sand bar near the old lighthouse at 3 a.m., and nine passengers were rescued by the keepers; on 13 November 1890 the steamer *Star Spangled Banner* foundered on the bar and her crew was rescued by the keeper.[^7]

Lightning was a recurring hazard. Between 1898 and 1908 the log recorded four strikes, three on the tower and one on the Navy wireless station built in 1905. On 11 August 1908 a strike ran along the call-bell wires between tower and house and tore out part of the front door frame in the assistant keepers' quarters.[^5]

Wartime brought darkness of another kind. In the Second World War the light was cut from 20,000 candlepower to 5,000 fixed, which the nomination says was meant to reduce the number of ships sunk by enemy submarines.[^1] The museum adds that a coastal lookout building went up in late 1941 and early 1942, and that armed Coast Guard guards watched the sea around the clock.[^3] The commander of U-123, it says, mentioned the slender lighthouse in his log when he torpedoed the tanker *Gulf America* off the Florida coast in April 1942.[^3]

## After the keepers

Electricity arrived in stages. The keepers' house was wired in 1925, and the tower in 1936, when the light's kerosene lamp gave way to an electric bulb and a motor replaced the hand-wound clockwork; candlepower rose from 13,000 to 20,000.[^1][^7] The second assistant's post was dropped that year.[^7] Until then the light had burned lard oil, then kerosene; the museum's log summary dates the changeover to 17 December 1885, and a separate brick oil house was built in 1890 to keep the kerosene at a distance from the tower.[^4][^7]

The nomination says the Coast Guard assumed responsibility for the lighthouse in July 1939.[^1] The station was de-staffed when Pippin left in 1955; David Swain, a former assistant keeper, was lamplighter from 1956 to 1968, and the light was fully automated about 1971 with a sun relay on top of the tower.[^1][^7] The empty keepers' house was rented for a time, then boarded up, declared surplus and offered at auction; on 28 July 1970 an arson fire gutted it.[^7] The nomination says St. Johns County acquired the house in 1970, while the museum and Lighthouse Friends describe the county as about to buy, or negotiating to buy, the house when it burned.[^1][^3][^7]

The Junior Service League of St. Augustine offered in 1980 to restore the ruin.[^7][^3] The museum's history adds that the site was added to the National Register of Historic Places.[^3] The museum puts the finish of the house's restoration in 1990; Lighthouse Friends says the house opened as a maritime museum in 1988 and the tower to climbers in 1991.[^3][^7] In 1986 a rifle bullet shattered nineteen prisms of the first-order lens; the lens was taken out of service in September 1991 and an airport beacon was placed in the tower, and after new prisms were made the lens was relit on 22 May 1993.[^7] The museum was incorporated separately from the League in January 1998, and the Coast Guard transferred the tower and lens to it in 2002 under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act.[^7][^3] In 2014 the museum bought the keepers' house and the remaining station property from the county for $150,000.[^7]

## Today

The St. Augustine Lighthouse & Maritime Museum owns the whole light station, tower and lens included, and the light remains a private aid to navigation.[^3][^13] The nomination gives the light a flash every thirty seconds, visible nineteen miles.[^1] Visitors can climb the tower, a 219-step ascent with exhibits on eight landings, and tour the restored keepers' house; the museum's history page lists daily opening from 9:00 a.m. to 6:00 p.m., closed on Thanksgiving and Christmas, so check the museum's own site before going.[^14][^3] The address is 81 Lighthouse Avenue, St. Augustine.[^7]
