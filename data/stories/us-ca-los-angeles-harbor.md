Los Angeles Harbor Light, which locals call Angels Gate, is a five-story concrete tower at the seaward end of the two-mile San Pedro Breakwater in Los Angeles Harbor, California.[^1][^3] It has marked the way into the harbor since 1913, and its slight lean has produced more legend than record.[^2][^3]

## The place

The harbor at San Pedro was made by hand. The National Register nomination traces the idea to Phineas Banning, who in 1872 secured the first federal appropriation for a breakwater to turn an open roadstead into a harbor, and says the breakwater itself was started in 1909.[^1] The nomination dates the monolithic concrete block on which the lighthouse stands to 1910.[^1]

A lighthouse was not part of the first plan. According to Marifrances Trivelli, director of the Los Angeles Maritime Museum, the end of the breakwater was meant to be a plain concrete platform, and a temporary fixed red light marked it. Vessels had trouble working around the new structure, and after several collisions mariners circulated petitions for a proper light station.[^3] Trivelli adds that the station's first name, "San Pedro Breakwater Light Station", was changed to "Los Angeles Harbor Light" in 1914, and that the earliest published mention of "Angels Gate" that she knows is in the Los Angeles Harbor Department's report for 1913.[^3]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $36,000 for the station on 4 March 1911.[^4][^3] The National Register nomination gives the finished cost as $35,971.49.[^1] The design was by Edward L. Woodruff, an assistant superintendent in the Lighthouse Service's eighteenth district, according to LighthouseFriends.[^4][^3] Llewellyn Iron Works of Los Angeles made the steel frame, and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported the contract for the steel work awarded in 1912.[^4][^5] The lantern room and its cast-iron parapet came from Champion Iron Works of Canton, Ohio.[^4]

The result was unlike any other tower on the West Coast. A steel frame runs through it. The lowest storey and its deck are octagonal and clad in steel plates, the second storey has twelve sides, and the upper storeys are round, topped by a lantern with a helical-bar frame.[^1] In the original arrangement the fog-signal engine and the water and fuel tanks were on the bottom floor, the kitchen and living room on the third, two bedrooms on the fourth, and a third bedroom and the watchroom on the fifth. The keepers' families lived ashore.[^4] Trivelli calls it a "bachelor station", and says the keepers' correspondence often compared the posting to remote, dangerous West Coast assignments such as St. George Reef and Mile Rocks.[^3]

The light was a fourth-order Fresnel lens bought from Barbier, Bénard and Turenne of Paris. It went into service on 1 March 1913, flashing white every fifteen seconds with an incandescent oil-vapor lamp, and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin records the station in commission that month.[^4][^3][^6] The fog signal was a siren driven by a gas-engine compressor.[^4] There was no water pipe from the mainland, so the lighthouse tenders brought drinking water and supplies.[^3]

## The keepers

John Olsen was the first head keeper, with two assistants.[^4][^7] LighthouseFriends says he had earlier been head keeper at St. George Reef. His tenure was short. In November 1913 the second assistant, Philip Hughes, was suspended for striking Olsen, and the station log then ordered that "no distilled or malt liquors are to be brought to this station".[^4] LighthouseFriends dates the log entry to 23 November and Trivelli to 24 November.[^4][^3] Olsen, LighthouseFriends adds, was reprimanded for being drunk at the station and moved to Oakland Harbor, swapping places with George D. Jeffrey.[^4]

Assistants came and went quickly. Trivelli quotes a 1936 letter from the district superintendent, H. W. Rhodes, calling the duties "more arduous than any other three-man station in the district", and records that William Stokes resigned in July 1933 after two weeks because, in Rhodes's words, his wife did not like California.[^3] Several head keepers stayed longer.

Willard D. Miller, head keeper from 1915 to 1922, was, in Trivelli's account, a native of Nova Scotia who served in the U.S. Navy and won the Medal of Honor at Cienfuegos in the Spanish-American War. He joined the Lighthouse Service in 1906.[^3][^7] Trivelli says that when the Pacific Fleet's battleships arrived at San Pedro and Long Beach, he began turning wood and other material their crews discarded overboard into ornate phonographs, and that his collection included a replica of the lighthouse.[^3] The Bulletin lists "W. Miller" as keeper of the Los Angeles Harbor station when it was awarded the eighteenth district's efficiency flag for 1920.[^8]

Frank Weller was first assigned as an assistant in 1916 and retired as keeper in 1930;[^3] the USLHS roster lists him as first assistant from 1916 and head keeper from 1922.[^7] On 9 May 1922 a launch that had broken adrift from the battleship California was wrecked on the breakwater near the light, and Weller rescued two men from drowning.[^9] LighthouseFriends adds that the launch broke away in a gale, that Weller found one man clinging to a pipe and the other in the kelp, and that he took them into the tower for food and dry clothes.[^4] The same source says that in October 1923 Weller was working at a harbor beacon with his launch tied to it when the steamer Coalinga struck it and he had to swim for shore, and that the steamer's owner repaid him $46 for a lost coat and cap.[^4]

Irving D. Conklin, head keeper from 1931 to 1942, had served at Point Reyes, Alcatraz and Angel Island before coming to Angels Gate, and was an accomplished photographer.[^3][^7] He was also a favorite with reporters. In one story he told, voices heard in the watchroom at four in the morning turned out to be a party of sailors' sweethearts at the foot of the tower, come to see the fleet in at daybreak. LighthouseFriends, which records the tale, cautions that some of his stories may be only stories.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The station was shaken from the start. Keeper Jeffrey's report of 19 January 1914 described a "terrific wind and sea" that swept over the lantern and flooded the watchroom, living room and engine room, washing away the launch and skiff. The new oil house was submerged nearly all night. The blows broke two mantles and spilled about half the mercury from the lens base, and he wrote that he had expected the lens to topple.[^10]

On 12 September 1918, with the sea smooth and no recent storms, three heavy ground swells rolled in from the southwest without warning at 8.15 a.m., poured over the breakwater and carried away everything movable. Green seas six to seven feet high kept coming over, and the swell did not moderate until the evening of the next day. Damage to the property was slight.[^11] On 29 January 1922 the keeper reported the worst storm he had yet seen at the station. At high water the sea came all around the tower, it was not safe anywhere outside the building, the spray sometimes reached the third-floor windows, and a pile of sand brought by the tender for repairs was washed away.[^12]

In November 1922 the keeper reported a "most unusual submarine disturbance". The water of the bay swirled in a whirlpool for about ten minutes at half-hour intervals, then ran backwards, with a current of 7 or 8 knots under the lighthouse wharf. The steam schooner Daisy Matthews was thrown off her course, and the battleship Arizona, anchored 300 yards north, dragged anchor and came within 75 feet of the rocks at the base of the tower before she was moved. The Bulletin adds that an earthquake had been suggested as the cause.[^13]

When the Long Beach earthquake struck on 10 March 1933, the first assistant keeper, left in charge, logged the shocks from 5.54 to 11 p.m. His first entry reads "Earth shock of great intensity shook tower violently for a period of 20 seconds, approximately". Mercury was shaken from the lens base, but the tower was not damaged.[^14]

The tower's lean has several explanations, and they do not agree. The National Register nomination says a furious gale bathed the tower in salt water for five days, and that a plumb line dropped from the gallery afterward showed a tilt toward the shore.[^1] LighthouseFriends places the gale in February 1931 and attributes the plumb-line story to Conklin, but thinks uneven settling of the ground under the foundation more likely.[^4] A San Pedro tourism site puts the storm in 1939.[^15] Trivelli finds neither the storm nor the battleship stories documented. She points instead to a breakwater slab that was out of plumb while the tower was being designed, and to ground subsidence from oil drilling after the 1920s, and says the nomination "includes numerous errors".[^3] The museum's exhibit page calls the battleship story untrue and says the lean is most likely a combination of subsidence, earthquakes and wind storms.[^2] The battleship story may have a source. On 23 October 1925 the Oklahoma struck the breakwater coming into the harbor, with, the Los Angeles Times reported, only superficial damage to her bow and none to the tower.[^3]

In the war years anti-submarine nets were hung at the breakwater entrance and the light kept burning.[^3] A concrete quarters building was put up in 1942, and its porthole windows were used for the net winches.[^1] A tidal wave in May 1960 destroyed the dock, footbridge and workboat.[^3] In late December 1965 the light went dark for about four hours after the commercial power failed, the emergency generator would not start and no kerosene could be found; LighthouseFriends calls it the first time anyone could remember.[^4]

## After the keepers

A power cable was laid along the breakwater in 1970, and the generator on the first floor became a backup.[^1] The nomination, written in 1977, says no personnel had been stationed there since 1971. LighthouseFriends dates automation to 1 January 1973, with the last keepers leaving thirty days later, and the USLHS roster lists two Coast Guard crew members as late as 1972-1973.[^1][^4][^7]

In 1987 the original lens was taken out and a lightweight plastic one was installed on solar power, a first for a California lighthouse. After complaints from mariners that the new light was too dim, a larger lens with a generator followed a little over two years later. The Fresnel lens went first into a Coast Guard warehouse.[^3] LighthouseFriends dates its removal to September 1987 and says it was donated to the Los Angeles Maritime Museum in February 1990; the museum describes it as on long-term loan.[^4][^2] The color of the light had changed earlier, in 1932, when a green cover over the lens turned the flash from white to green.[^4]

Without full-time keepers the tower suffered from salt and vandals.[^3] In 2009 the Cabrillo Beach Boosters, a San Pedro nonprofit, were awarded a $1.8 million mitigation grant from the Port of Los Angeles to restore it.[^3] The engineering firm Wiss, Janney, Elstner Associates, retained in fall 2010, found widespread corrosion and deterioration, with more distress at the lower levels and on the south and southeast sides, which face the ocean and the harbor channel, and prepared the rehabilitation drawings. The firm gives February 2012 as the completion date of the work.[^16] LighthouseFriends puts the start of construction in October 2011 and describes a centennial celebration on 27 October 2013.[^4]

## Today

The tower is a Coast Guard aid to navigation, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1980, and it shows a green flash every fifteen seconds.[^15][^4] The tower is closed to the public and the breakwater is officially closed. LighthouseFriends reports fishermen on the rocks despite the signs, but the local tourism site says walking out is dangerous and not allowed, so look from a distance.[^4][^15] The usual places to see it are the end of the Cabrillo Beach fishing pier, the bluffs at Point Fermin Park, and harbor cruises and whale-watching trips leaving San Pedro.[^15]

The Fresnel lens that served from 1913 to 1987 is displayed at the Los Angeles Maritime Museum, at Berth 84 in San Pedro.[^4][^2]
