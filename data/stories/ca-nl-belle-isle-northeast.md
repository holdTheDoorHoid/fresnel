Belle Isle Northeast Light stands on the northeast point of Belle Isle, the island that sits in the eastern mouth of the Strait of Belle Isle, between the northern tip of Newfoundland and the coast of Labrador.[^7] The Department of Marine and Fisheries put the light into operation when navigation opened in 1905.[^1] The concrete tower built for it is no longer the station's working light.[^9] The Coast Guard now shows the light from a skeletal tower.[^8][^9]

## The place

Once steamships came into use, more vessels took the shorter northern route through the Strait of Belle Isle instead of sailing south of Newfoundland.[^7] Most of those ships were bound to or from the St. Lawrence.[^7] LighthouseFriends says the Province of Canada built a light on the island's southwest end in 1858, and that the northeast point was the eighth and final major light marking the strait.[^7]

The Department's agents at Belle Isle sent in monthly notes on the ice in the strait.[^5] The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 covers July 1900 to June 1901.[^5] Its first note, dated 12 December, reads: "Very little ice appeared and vessels could have navigated the Straits very easily during this month."[^5]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says Chief Engineer William P. Anderson surveyed the northeast point in 1901.[^7] The tower that went up in 1905 was a cylindrical iron tower with a polygonal iron lantern, painted red.[^1] The Department's report for that year gives its site as 27 m (90 ft) above high water and 64 m (210 ft) back from the water's edge.[^1] The tower was 17 m (55 ft) from its base to the ventilator on the lantern.[^1]

The light showed a white flash every 11 seconds from a second-order dioptric apparatus.[^1][^2] The report gives its height as 42 m (137 ft) above high water, and says it could be seen for 17 miles from all points of approach by water.[^1] The optical apparatus was supplied by Chance Brothers of Birmingham, and the illuminant was to be petroleum vapour burned under a mantle.[^1][^2]

The fog alarm was being built by day labour under a foreman named Audibert.[^1] The Department's report for 1906 says the new fog alarm was still being installed, and that the keeper's dwelling and other buildings were under construction.[^3] The fog alarm building stood at the edge of the cliff at the northeast end of the island, about 60 m (200 ft) from the tower.[^4] Its diaphone was operated with air compressed by an oil engine, and gave one blast of about three and a half seconds each minute in thick weather.[^4]

In 1908 the cast-iron tower was encased in reinforced concrete and braced with six flying buttresses.[^6][^7][^9] Parks Canada's register treats the tower as an example of the transition to reinforced concrete in Canada. It is one of nine flying-buttressed towers known to have been built by the Department under Anderson's direction.[^6]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends names Paul Thomas as the first keeper of the light and fog alarm, at a salary of $1,100 a year. It says three keepers were stationed here in the early years.[^7] Thomas served until 1913.[^7] When electricity reached the station, one of the keeper posts was dropped.[^7]

The house built for the keepers in the early years was replaced in the 1950s with two dwellings, one double and one single.[^7] Cecil Wellman, one of the last head keepers, served from 1957 to 1964. LighthouseFriends reports that he was killed in an accident on Belle Isle in 1964.[^7]

## After the keepers

Electricity had reached the light by 1966, and an electronic fog horn was installed in 1972.[^7] Staff had left the station before 2005.[^7] The Recognized Federal Heritage Building designation followed in 1989.[^7]

The Lighthouse Directory says the old concrete tower has been inactive since 2017, and that the light has moved to a short skeletal tower.[^9] The Coast Guard list, which does not mention the concrete tower, shows the light on a square skeleton tower. Neither source dates that tower.[^8][^9]

## Today

The Coast Guard lists the light as a white flash every ten seconds, 39.1 m above the water, with a nominal range of 17 nautical miles. It is shown year round from a square skeletal tower with a red and white daymark.[^8] LighthouseFriends describes a fog signal of one three-second blast every thirty seconds, sounded when needed.[^7]

The station is Coast Guard property.[^7] The Lighthouse Directory says it is accessible only by boat (in heavy seas) or by helicopter.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the best views come by boat or from the air in good weather.[^7] The grounds are open, and the concrete tower is closed to visitors.[^9][^7] The light remains an active aid to navigation.[^8]
