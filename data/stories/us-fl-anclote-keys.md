Anclote Key Light stands at the southern tip of Anclote Key, three miles off Tarpon Springs on Florida's Gulf coast.[^1][^2] It is a 110-foot (33.5 m) cast-iron skeleton tower, lit for the first time on 15 September 1887. The Coast Guard deactivated it in the mid-1980s, and it stood dark until it was relit in 2003.[^1][^2]

## The place

The island sits at the mouth of the Anclote River, which the nomination calls "one of the best natural harbors on the Florida Gulf Coast."[^1][^2] The name is Spanish for anchor.[^2] The light was one of a chain of lights the federal government built on the Gulf Coast in the 1880s; the nomination says this one went up in 1887 by order of President Grover Cleveland.[^1]

The island also drew visitors. "Important visitors, including Greek premier Venizelos, former President Calvin Coolidge and former Vice President Spiro Agnew, were taken out to the island on sponge boats," according to the nomination.[^1] The nomination ties the town's growth to the light and the sponge trade. It says Tarpon Springs went from 200 people in 1887, the year it was incorporated, to more than 2,000 by 1920.[^1] It also credits a businessman, John K. Cheyney, and a Greek sponge captain, John Corcoris, with founding the Tarpon Springs Sponge Exchange.[^1] LighthouseFriends names a John M. Cocoris, a Greek whom Cheyney hired for sponge work. The sources do not say whether he is the same man as the captain.[^2]

## Building the light

The Lighthouse Board requested $20,000 in 1884 for a new light at the south end of the island.[^1] Congress appropriated $17,500 on 3 March 1885.[^1][^2] The land had been acquired in 1866, nineteen years before the money came.[^2] The Board wanted a tower like the iron skeleton at Cape San Blas, which had cost $35,000,[^1][^2] so it asked for another $17,500, approved by Congress on 4 August 1886.[^1]

Proposals for the metal work reached the Board on 4 October 1886.[^1] The metal was not delivered until late June 1887.[^1] The keepers' two dwellings were built with hired labor, because the bids for them were too expensive.[^1] Keeper James Gardner lit the light for the first time on 15 September 1887.[^1][^2]

The first lens was a third-order Fresnel. LighthouseFriends describes four grouped white flashes every thirty seconds, shown from a focal plane of 101 feet (31 m).[^2] The nomination says it burned kerosene from 1887 until the early 1940s, and that the flash was every thirty seconds.[^1] The two sources give different later histories. The nomination says a fourth-order lens replaced the original in 1898, but LighthouseFriends says the signature was changed to one red flash in 1899.[^1][^2] The Service Bulletin for 1917 lists a change of characteristic at the station, from a single flash to a triple flash, although the scanned text of that passage is poor.[^6]

The lamp changed more than once. The Service Bulletin for 1912 lists Anclote Keys among the stations whose illuminant changed to incandescent oil vapor.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the light was converted from oil to automatic acetylene in 1923.[^2] The nomination's kerosene dates conflict with both. The nomination also says an oil house went up in 1894 or soon after, and a wharf 209 feet (64 m) long in 1898.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive's roster gives James Gardner (1887–1888), Samuel E. Hope (1888–1889), James M. Baggett (1889–1891) and Robert S. Meyer (1891–1914) as the first head keepers.[^8] LighthouseFriends prints two entries from the station log. On 7 October 1889 the log records that a baby boy died that morning, and that the keeper and his wife went over to bury him that day.[^2] On 30 August 1890 it records a baby born at the station, in bad weather.[^2]

The nomination says that several times a week a keeper crossed the short stretch of water to Tarpon Springs to attend church, get provisions, pick up mail or visit family.[^1] Keeper Meyer's description of his work in 1928 includes the line "Go to town twice a day to get mail and supplies."[^2]

The nomination names "keeper Robert E. Meyer," who bought a small cannon for self-defense during the Spanish-American War, although the island was never attacked.[^1] The roster names Robert S. Meyer as keeper throughout those years, and the 1913 Service Bulletin prints the same name. The sources do not say whether the two names belong to one man.[^1][^8] The nomination also records that one keeper kept pigs on the island, which wandered until a Cuban boat crew came ashore and stole them. It does not date that story.[^1]

Thomas A. Moody was head keeper from 1914 to 1923.[^2][^8] Meyer came back in 1923 and stayed until he retired in 1933.[^2] Afterwards the roster lists James L. Pippin (1933–1949) and Peter DeBoer (1949–1951) as head keepers.[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A storm in late September 1896 caused damage on the Florida coast.[^3] The Light-House Board's report says the tender Arbutus went to see the damage, and that her crew repaired the Anclote Keys light station.[^3] The same report dates the storm to "September 28 and 30, 1896" in one passage, while another passage on the Cedar Keys repairs gives the dates as "September 28 and 29."[^3][^10]

On 23 June the Service's bulletin for 1913 reports that keeper Robert S. Meyer and assistant Clifton H. Lopez "rendered assistance to a party in a motor boat which was aground," and to two men whose motor boat had drifted near the station after its engine failed.[^5]

A storm on 27 and 28 September 1918 hit the west coast of Florida.[^7] The Service reported that it cut narrow paths through the pines on Anclote Keys, and that the tide was said to be higher than it had ever been before.[^7] Damage to aids to navigation from Charlotte Harbor to Anclote Keys was put at $6,450.[^7]

On 28 September, while searching for the station boat the storm had blown away, keeper T. A. Moody rescued two men from a capsized sloop. He then towed a disabled sponge divers' boat with two men aboard to safety, and towed a schooner that was six miles offshore in a disabled condition.[^7][^2] The Service recognized Moody for these rescues in 1919.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service merged into the Coast Guard in 1939, as the nomination records.[^1] The two sources date the automation differently. LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard fully automated the light in 1952.[^2] The nomination describes how, in the 1960s, the Coast Guard installed a 250-watt electric light, powered by 56 batteries with an electronic sun sensor.[^2][^1]

The end of the light is also dated two ways. LighthouseFriends says the light was discontinued in 1984, while the nomination says the Coast Guard relinquished it in 1985, decommissioned it and abandoned the island.[^2][^1]

After the abandonment, the nomination records, arsonists burned both keepers' quarters to the ground, gunfire shattered the lantern panels, and vandals destroyed the Fresnel lens.[^1] The nomination was prepared in February 1999, and it states that there was no optic in the lighthouse at the time.[^1]

Restoration came in stages. A Re-Light the Light committee was formed and more than a million dollars was secured to restore the lighthouse property.[^2] In January 2003 International Chimney Corporation began an eight-month restoration of the oil house and tower, which ended in a relighting ceremony on 13 September 2003.[^2] A replica of a fourth-order Fresnel lens went into the lantern on 15 November 2004. The Friends of Anclote Key State Park & Lighthouse formed in 2005, and the lighthouse was first opened to visitors on 15 October that year.[^2] Tours were suspended in 2014 because of contaminated soil near the tower, and climbing resumed in June 2018. A restoration of the tower and its paint, costing about $800,000, was completed in 2023.[^2]

## Today

The State of Florida owns the island and the lighthouse, which are part of Anclote Key Preserve State Park.[^1][^2] The island can only be reached by boat.[^1] The Friends do not run boats: getting to the island is left to visitors. The dock is for park staff, and boaters must anchor or beach their boats.[^9]

The tower can be climbed on open-house days run by the Friends. The Friends' open-house page, as read on 22 October 2026, gives open-house hours of 10:00 am to 4:00 pm on the second weekend of each month, from October to May, and lists the lighthouse as closed from June through September.[^9] A $7 donation per person is requested.[^9] Only eight people may be inside at a time, and the climb is 123 steps.[^9] The boardwalk to the tower is rough and splintered, so wear shoes.[^9] Dogs are not allowed on the island, and there are no public restrooms.[^9] Check the Friends' site before you go, because open houses are cancelled for weather.[^9]
