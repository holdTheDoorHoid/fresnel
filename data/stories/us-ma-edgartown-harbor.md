Edgartown Harbor Light stands on the western side of the inner harbor at Edgartown, opposite Chappaquiddick Island.[^1] A light has marked the harbor since 1828. The tower there now is a cast-iron tower brought by barge from Ipswich in 1939, after the 1938 hurricane damaged the original light; the sources differ on how badly.[^1][^2][^3] The federal government transferred the light to the Town of Edgartown in 2014, and the Martha's Vineyard Museum looks after it.[^3][^4]

## The place

The light was built for the harbor. Congress's 1828 appropriation was for a pier and light-house at the entrance to Edgartown Harbor.[^2] The nomination says the harbor "has long been an active yachting center," and LighthouseFriends says Edgartown was home to over a hundred whaling captains.[^1][^2]

Cape Poge already guided ships toward the harbor. In 1838 Lt. Edward W. Carpender inspected the station. As LighthouseFriends reports his findings, the light on the breakwater needed only to be small, because Cape Poge, four miles outside, was the guide to the harbor.[^2]

The harbor had a different problem: sand. In 1842 the inspector I. W. P. Lewis warned, as LighthouseFriends reports, that without a firm breakwater to replace the rotten pilings the harbor would in a few years be closed, or its entrance moved so that ships could enter only with great danger.[^2]

## Building the light

The sources give two amounts for the first building. The nomination says "Congress allocated $4400" for the job. LighthouseFriends says Congress approved $5,500 for a pier and light-house on the Point of Flats on 23 May 1828.[^1][^2]

The first building was a combination dwelling and light: a wood-frame house with the lantern on its roof, set out in the water on pilings or a platform. LighthouseFriends calls it a two-story house, and the nomination calls it two and a half stories. LighthouseFriends says a Mr. Bowker erected it.[^1][^2] The nomination records the land as acquired from Seth Vincent on 30 July 1828.[^1]

According to a diary entry that LighthouseFriends prints, keeper Jeremiah Pease recorded on 15 October 1828 that Captain John Akin lit the lamps for the first time.[^2]

The structure was later rebuilt in pieces. LighthouseFriends says a wooden walkway of about 1,500 feet (457 m) was built to the shore in 1830, at a cost of $2,500. The pilings were replaced by a stone pier in 1840, and a stone breakwater topped by a wooden walkway replaced the walkway in 1847.[^1][^2] A fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps and reflectors in 1856.[^2] The Board's report for 1895 says a well was driven at the station.[^5] In 1900 the dwelling, which stood on a pier of dry stone masonry, was given a new lower floor and 900 running feet (274 m) of pipe to the well.[^7]

The tower now standing is the cast-iron one. The 2014 Vineyard Gazette says it was built in 1875 and shipped by barge to Edgartown after it was dismantled.[^3] The nomination also gives 1875 for the cast-iron tower.[^1] LighthouseFriends disagrees, dating it to 1881 and calling it a disused rear range light on Crane's Beach in Ipswich.[^2] The Gazette article, as read here, calls the Ipswich light the Essex Light. A reader comment on its page gave the location as Crane Beach, and the Gazette replied that the story had been updated.[^3]

## The keepers

Jeremiah Pease kept the light from 1828 to 1841 and again from 1843 to 1849.[^8] LighthouseFriends says Pease was replaced twice when the Whig Party was in power, because he was a Democrat.[^2] A local paper later charged that the lighthouse had become "a rendezvous for all kinds of dissipating." LighthouseFriends says a young man boarding with the keeper was the cause of the allegations, and that Pease kept his post.[^2]

Sylvanus Crocker was appointed keeper in March 1841. In his description of the station, as LighthouseFriends reports it, he judged that the whole establishment had been very badly built in the first place, and he found the lantern shaken by storms.[^2] Keepers did not all live in the house. LighthouseFriends says some preferred to find lodging on the nearby shore.[^2]

Benjamin B. Huxford kept the light from 1870 to 1883. Jethro Worth followed him from 1883 to 1892, after serving at Cape Poge, and Isaac Hamblen kept the light from 1895 to 1918.[^8][^9]

Joseph E. Barrus and then Henry L. Thomas also came here from Cape Poge, in 1919 and 1931.[^8][^9] In 1934 a reporter visited the Thomas family. As LighthouseFriends reports it, the house had practically every modern convenience except electricity.[^2]

Fred S. Vidler was keeper in 1938. The roster lists Victor A. Danberg as watchman and caretaker from 1939.[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Crocker described a gale of October 1841 in his report. As LighthouseFriends reports it, the lantern shook so hard in that gale that he had great difficulty keeping the light burning.[^2]

The causeway to the shore was another problem. LighthouseFriends says it had been knocked to pieces five or six times.[^2] The nomination says the walkway was replaced by a stone breakwater because of frequent damage by storms.[^1]

The Light-House Board's annual report for 1897 records that "Damage to the bridge by a storm was repaired."[^6]

LighthouseFriends says the Great Hurricane of 1938 inflicted significant damage on the lighthouse, and the Vineyard Gazette says it badly damaged the light.[^2][^3] A reader comment on the Gazette's page disputes this. Its writer, who said the paper's own 1938 reports showed the building had already been condemned that summer, says the storm did little or no damage.[^3]

## After the keepers

In 1939, when the Coast Guard took over the lights, LighthouseFriends says it promptly tore down the old dwelling and lantern, and the cast-iron tower was barged in from Ipswich.[^1][^2][^3]

The Coast Guard refurbished the light in 1985. LighthouseFriends says the Martha's Vineyard Historical Society was granted a thirty-year lease in 1994. In 2014 the Gazette named the Martha's Vineyard Museum as the light's steward.[^2][^3] The Children's Memorial came next. LighthouseFriends says it was dedicated on 14 July 2001, and that some of its cobblestones are large enough to be inscribed with a child's name.[^2] The Museum says more than a thousand engraved stones now surround the light.[^4]

In 2007 the Historical Society received $250,000 from the Community Preservation Act. An $80,000 staircase replaced the old ladder, and LighthouseFriends says it made the light open to visitors who could climb it for the first time.[^2]

The Gazette reported that the light had been announced as surplus property in May 2012.[^3] Edgartown signed off on an application in January 2013, and in January 2014 the General Services Administration announced the transfer.[^3] The town became the new owner of the old lighthouse, the Gazette reported, and the light cost the town about one dollar.[^3]

## Today

The Museum's page says the light is closed for the season and will reopen in spring 2027.[^4] When it is open, the doors stay open during visiting hours with a lighthouse keeper available to answer questions. Adults pay $5, and children 12 and under go free.[^4]

The Children's Memorial is a place where families remember children they have lost. Each September the Museum holds a ceremony of remembrance there.[^4]

Parking is available, but limited, on North Water Street.[^4] The Vineyard Transit Authority buses drop off on Church Street, and the Museum says the walk from there takes about 15 minutes.[^4]

The Town of Edgartown owns the light, and the Museum looks after it. The Museum calls itself the steward of both the Edgartown and East Chop lights.[^4][^3] LighthouseFriends' page still gives the Coast Guard as owner and the Martha's Vineyard Historical Society as manager.[^2]
