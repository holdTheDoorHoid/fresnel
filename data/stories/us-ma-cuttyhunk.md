Cuttyhunk Light was a lighthouse on Cuttyhunk Island, the outermost of the Elizabeth Islands, where it marked the entrance to Buzzards Bay.[^2] Its first light was shown on 2 May 1823. The light on its last tower, a steel frame put up in 1947, was switched off in 2005, and the tower was taken down.[^2][^1]

## The place

The Elizabeth Islands run from Woods Hole and separate Vineyard Sound, to the south, from Buzzards Bay, to the north.[^2] The hazard at Cuttyhunk was a reef off the island's southwest end. In 1842 I. W. P. Lewis, who visited the light, complained that it was "hidden away under a side hill," and that the reef, "called the Sow and Pigs," ran about two miles from the southwest end, so that vessels coming from the east could not see the light until they were close to it.[^2] A lightship was established on the Sow and Pigs in 1847.[^2]

On 7 May 1822 Congress allocated $3,000 "for a lighthouse to be built" on the island, or nearby.[^2]

## Building the light

The first tower was a conical stone tower with a detached stone dwelling. The light went into operation on 2 May 1823 with Benjamin Sayer as its first keeper.[^2] In 1838 Lieutenant Edward W. Carpender of the U.S. Navy inspected the light, when Nickerson Chase was keeper. He found that the reflectors had been wiped rather than burnished, and wrote that they were "cleaned in the too ordinary acceptation of the term."[^2] The stone tower had been built so badly that it had to be encased in brick twice.[^2]

In 1857 a fifth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps and reflectors.[^2][^1] In 1860 a second story was added to the keeper's dwelling, with a square tower and lantern room on top. That raised the focal plane by ten feet (3 m), and the old tower could be taken down.[^2][^1]

In 1891 the dwelling built in 1823 was demolished, and a frame dwelling went up on its foundation.[^3][^2] A temporary conical wooden tower was built near it and linked to it by a covered way. LighthouseFriends notes that the "temporary" tower lasted more than fifty years.[^2] A stone oil house was added in 1903 to store the kerosene.[^2]

In 1947 the Coast Guard erected a metal skeletal tower and demolished the wooden lighthouse and its companion dwelling.[^2][^1]

## The keepers

Benjamin Sayer kept the light from 1823 to 1832, and Nickerson Chase followed him until 1844.[^1] The roster and LighthouseFriends both list Fanny Chase as head keeper from 1844 to 1847. The roster also lists Ella F. Terpeny as principal keeper in 1909. Neither record says anything about the work of either of them beyond those dates.[^1][^2]

Samuel Austin Smith kept the light from 1864 to 1881.[^1] His log for 31 January 1872 reads: "Struck on the NW point at 7 o'clock PM the schooner Connecticut." LighthouseFriends, quoting a newspaper of the time, says Smith and a man named John Black went out in a small boat "to save the entire crew of the Connecticut."[^2]

Alfred G. Eisener was keeper from 1890 to 1894.[^1] He appears in the wreck reports below.

Levi B. Clark came to Cuttyhunk in 1911 after service at Boston Light and Minot's Ledge, and stayed six years.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends says he obtained a license as an Episcopalian lay reader, and that in August 1913 he preached to a crowd of nearly 200 summer visitors.[^2] Octave J. Ponsart, the last keeper on the roster, served from 1943 to 1946.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1892 the British schooner Rob and Harry, of St. John, New Brunswick, was dismasted in a gale about three miles west of the Hen and Chickens lightship. Her hulk drifted onto the west end of Cuttyhunk.[^4][^5] Her steward, Ephraim McKeil, was lost.[^4] The Life-Saving Service report says that she lay in the breakers about one hundred yards from the shore, and that it was "fully two hours before the wreck was lighted by A. G. Eisener," the light keeper.[^4] Eisener sent messengers to rouse the villagers and to call the life-savers.[^4]

On 24 February 1893 the British brig Aquatic, bound from Sagua, Cuba, to Boston with sugar, stranded on a reef near the western end of the island.[^6][^7] About half an hour after she struck, the keeper's young son saw the distress signals and ran to tell his father.[^6] Eisener sent a messenger to the village and to the United States life-saving station "located at the extreme eastern end." He was also keeper of the Massachusetts Humane Society's Lifeboat Station No. 43, whose boathouse was "located near the light-house," and he got its boat ready.[^6]

Eisener thought the sea too rough for the trip and "warned them" against it.[^6] A crew of six set out between nine and ten at night, and the boat capsized near the brig. Josiah H. Tilton, who could not swim, was hauled aboard the brig. The other five men were lost. Fred Akin's body was found on the beach at dawn; a man "told the watchers gathered there" in the light-house dwelling what he had seen. Timothy Akin, Jr., who commanded the boat, was found near his home days later, and Isaiah Tilton at Gay Head. Eugene Brightman and Hiram S. Jackson "were never found."[^6] The Life-Saving report's heading is "Loss of five members of a crew of the Massachusetts Humane Society."[^6] The brig's crew were rescued the next day by the Cuttyhunk Life-Saving Station's crew.[^6]

LighthouseFriends notes that the light was damaged in the Great Atlantic Hurricane of 1944.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard's steel skeletal tower of 1947 was the last light on the site. The light on it was discontinued in 2005, and the tower was removed. Only the stone oil house is left of the station.[^2]

## Today

A ferry runs to the island from New Bedford. Once ashore, the site where the lighthouse stood is about two miles on foot.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open. The USLHS record says the site is not open to the public.[^2][^1] Check with the Town of Gosnold, to which the islands belong, before going onto the land, and do not climb anything you find there.[^2]

The stone tower built on the island in 1902 "to commemorate the tercentenary of Gosnold's landing" is a memorial, not a light.[^2]
