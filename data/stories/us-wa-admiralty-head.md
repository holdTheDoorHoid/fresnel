Admiralty Head Light stands on a bluff in Fort Casey State Park, on Whidbey Island near Coupeville, at the eastern point of the entrance to Admiralty Inlet.[^2][^3] The lighthouse there is a brick-and-stucco tower of 1903.[^2] The first light on the bluff was a wooden house with a tower, first shown on January 21, 1861.[^3] It was discontinued in 1922, and it is now a museum that opens in season.[^1][^2]

## The place

Admiralty Head is a 90-foot (27 m) promontory that projects into Admiralty Inlet on the west coast of Whidbey Island, four miles south of Coupeville.[^3] Captain George Vancouver named the inlet in 1792, and in 1841 Lieutenant Charles Wilkes named the headland Red Bluff, for the reddish color of its cliffs.[^3]

In 1856 Congress paid for only one light for the inlet's two shores.[^3] The Lighthouse Board chose Red Bluff, to help sailing vessels clear the shallow water around Point Wilson.[^3] The act authorizing the station was dated August 18, 1856,[^2] and in 1858 the Board bought ten acres from Dr. John Kellogg for $400.[^3]

## Building the light

The first light was a square wooden tower on the roof of a two-story keeper's house, painted white.[^2] It had a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^2][^3] It was completed in January 1861 and first exhibited on January 21 of that year.[^2][^3] The archive's place record gives 1860 as the year the station was established.[^1] Across the inlet the companion light at Point Wilson was not established until December 15, 1879, a little under nineteen years later.[^2][^3]

The Army changed the bluff's use. In 1896 it picked Admiralty Head as one of three sites for the Puget Sound defenses, and in 1897 it bought 123 more acres from Dr. Kellogg for $7,200 for Fort Casey.[^3] The Army wanted the lighthouse's spot for a 10-inch gun battery, so the old house was moved several hundred feet north.[^3] The Board's reports record the same thing. The 1899 report says the station had to be moved because of the fortification works.[^4] The 1901 report records that the old reservation had been exchanged for two pieces of ground nearby.[^7]

The Board's 1900 estimates asked for $12,000 for "removal and reconstruction of buildings" at the station.[^6] The new buildings were to be erected by the War Department.[^5] The bids came in above the appropriation, and the War Department did the work itself under Major John Millis.[^2] The brick-and-stucco lighthouse, designed by the Board's architect, Carl Leick, was built with walls 18 inches (46 cm) thick against the shock of the fort's big guns.[^3] It was lit on June 25, 1903.[^2][^3] The fourth-order lens went from the old tower into the new lantern.[^3]

## The keepers

William Robertson, a retired sea captain, was the first keeper, from 1861 to 1864.[^1][^3] His family's log cabin had been burned in an attack in the mid-1850s, and afterwards he mounted a small ship's cannon in the yard.[^3] He also served Island County as postmaster and coroner.[^2]

Daniel N. Pearson was appointed the second keeper on November 28, 1864.[^3] He had come west with two of his daughters, young women whom Asa Mercer had recruited in Lowell, Massachusetts, to teach in the territory.[^2][^3] Josephine died suddenly on August 21, 1864, soon after arriving in Coupeville.[^3] Georgianna, the younger, was his assistant.[^3] She married in October 1867, and Flora, 17, took over the assistant's duties.[^2][^3] Flora kept the log, and she recorded the birth of her son at the lighthouse in 1877.[^2][^3] In her memoirs, as LighthouseFriends prints them, she describes an inspector's visit in 1875 and the order keepers were given: they were "forbidden to depart from the Territory of Single Blessedness."[^2] Pearson resigned on October 1, 1878, and died in 1897.[^3] The Seattle *Post-Intelligencer*'s obituary, as HistoryLink quotes it, said that for 13 years "he was never absent from his post for one night."[^3]

Laurence Nessel, French-born, a bachelor of 50, was assigned temporarily on October 21, 1878, and given the post permanently on June 3, 1880.[^3] On the night of December 15, 1879, he saw the Point Wilson light across the inlet and wrote in his log: "Light at Point Wilson in operation for first time."[^3] LighthouseFriends prints the same entry under 1897, but its own account puts the Point Wilson light nineteen years after 1861, so this story follows HistoryLink's 1879.[^2][^3] The archive spells the name Lawrence.[^1]

Joseph E. Evans replaced Nessel on November 2, 1887.[^3] He and his wife left in 1899, after their 13-year-old son disappeared in a boating accident that October.[^3] Charles Henry Davis, a former riverboat captain, was appointed on January 23, 1900.[^3] He and his wife Mary were the last keepers of the old house; Mary Davis died on January 21, 1901.[^3] Davis and his second wife, Delia, were the first occupants of the new house.[^3] Delia died in September 1913, and Davis on January 8, 1914, at 81.[^3]

Edward Scannell, who had spent 26 years at Point No Point, took the post in 1914 and retired on May 9, 1919, at 77.[^3] On May 1, 1919, the Board changed the light from white to red, for reasons the sources do not give.[^3] The last keeper, Hans P. Score, kept the light with his wife Carrie until June 30, 1922.[^1][^3] His log for that night reads: "After tonight Admiralty Head is discontinued by order of Superintendent of Lighthouses Mr. Warrick."[^2]

## After the keepers

The light was decommissioned on July 1, 1922.[^3] HistoryLink says it was no longer needed once steamships had replaced sailing ships on the Sound, and that it "did not mark any navigational hazard."[^3] In 1927 the Lighthouse Service took the iron-and-glass lantern down and placed it on the reshaped tower at New Dungeness.[^2][^3] The fourth-order lens was removed and went missing.[^3] In 1928 the Army dismantled the old wooden house, and its lumber went into a house on south Whidbey Island.[^2][^3]

The Coast Guard took over the lighthouses in 1939.[^3] In March 1941 the Army took the lighthouse, painted it olive drab, and used it as barracks for the K-9 Corps that patrolled the fort and beaches at night.[^2][^3]

In 1955 the state parks commission acquired 100 acres of the fort's battery area as a park.[^3] In 1957 the Island County Historical Society and the state parks began a major restoration of the lighthouse, including a rebuilt lantern room.[^3] A fourth-order lens on display later came from Alki Point Lighthouse, which was modernized in 1962.[^3] In 1973 the Central Whidbey Island historic district went on the National Register of Historic Places, and in 1980 the state park, with the lighthouse, was added to the listing.[^3] In 1990 the Postal Service's set of five lighthouse stamps included Admiralty Head.[^3]

When park rangers were cut in the early 1990s, the Washington State University extension office at Island County offered to staff the lighthouse with volunteers in exchange for office space.[^2] In 2003 the Coast Guard lent the museum a fourth-order lens that HistoryLink says may be identical to the one that went missing in 1927.[^3] A replacement lantern room, built by local high-school metal shops, was placed on the tower on August 23, 2012.[^2]

## Today

The state parks department took control of the lighthouse in 2015, and in early 2020 the legislature approved $1.7 million to restore it.[^2] The work repaired the cracked stucco, replaced corroded railings and repainted the building.[^2] The lighthouse sits in the Ebey's Landing National Historic Reserve.[^2]

It has a museum, a gift shop and volunteer docents.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends gives its hours as noon to 4 p.m. daily in June, July and August, and tells visitors to check the park for other months.[^2] HistoryLink, writing in 2021, says it is open seasonally.[^3] A support group, the Keepers of Admiralty Head Lighthouse, backs its restoration and education.[^3]
