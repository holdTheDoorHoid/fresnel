Kīlauea Point is the northernmost point of the main Hawaiian Islands, a lava headland on Kauaʻi's north shore that the Lighthouse Board approved in 1909 for a light to greet ships coming from the Orient.[^1][^2] A 52-foot (16 m) tower of reinforced concrete was first lit there on 1 May 1913. Its second-order Fresnel lens still stands in the lantern room, but the lens no longer turns, and in 1976 a smaller rotating beacon on a concrete column took over the light's work.[^3][^13][^2] (This is Kīlauea on Kauaʻi, near the plantation town of that name, not the volcano on Hawaiʻi Island.)

## The place

George R. Putnam, the Commissioner of Lighthouses, described the light in 1917 as a landfall for ships bound for Honolulu from the Orient.[^4] The Service's own 1912 bulletin, while the tower was going up, called it "the principal landfall light on the north shore of the island of Kauai."[^5]

The money came through Congress. The National Register nomination credits Jonah Kūhiō, Hawaiʻi's delegate, with sponsoring the legislation behind the appropriation of 27 May 1908.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for 1915 gives the same date and $75,000 for "a light and fog-signal station at some point on the northerly or westerly coast of Kauai Island."[^6] Surveyors visited the point in April 1909, and in December 1909 the government bought 31.4 acres from the Kīlauea Sugar Plantation Company for one dollar. The deed was executed on 9 December 1909.[^2] The crest of the point averages about 180 feet (55 m) above the sea.[^2]

## Building the light

A contract was issued in 1911 and work began in July 1912 under Frank C. Palmer, an assistant superintendent of the nineteenth lighthouse district.[^2] The Garden Island, writing in May 1913, said the lighthouse "was begun in August of last year."[^19] Delivery of materials was the first problem. The nomination says it came by the tender *Kukui* lying offshore, with lighters bringing the cargo to a derrick and landing platform built below the point on the southwest side. A crew of twenty-six day labourers and mechanics landed on the point and put up the derrick and landing platform within ten days.[^2] The derrick stood 90 feet (27 m) above the water and the platform 110 feet (34 m).[^2]

The ground was a problem. The nomination records that the builders poured footings and dug a basement 11 feet (3.4 m) deep for the tower.[^2] The finished reinforced-concrete tower measures 53 feet 1 inch (16.2 m) to the top of the ventilator ball, with four stories linked by a cast-iron stair.[^2] Its lantern-room metalwork came from the Champion Iron Company of Kenton, Ohio, which an inspector approved in August 1912.[^2] The nomination notes that reinforced concrete was still a new material for lighthouses, and counts only five surviving concrete towers older than this one.[^2]

The lens was made in Paris by Barbier, Bernard & Turenne and cost $12,000.[^2] According to the nomination, it arrived with assembly instructions in French, and Palmer sent an urgent message to the district's chief, Frederick Albert Edgecomb, who travelled by inter-island ship and then rode on horseback to the point to help.[^2] The nomination names Anahola Bay as his landing and fifteen miles on horseback; LighthouseFriends, a modern enthusiast site, says he landed at Nawiliwili and rode twenty.[^2][^7] Putnam described the finished apparatus as two groups of two panels, the moving part weighing nearly four tons and floating on mercury, turning once every twenty seconds to give a double flash every ten.[^4] He put the light 216 feet (66 m) above the water; the nomination gives the focal plane as 217 feet.[^4][^2]

The Commissioner's report says the light "went into operation" on 1 May 1913.[^6] The Garden Island, Kauaʻi's newspaper, put the news on its front page on 6 May 1913 under the headline "Biggest Lamp in the World Flings Signal of Protection to the Sailor Boy," as the nomination quotes it.[^2] The page itself says Palmer, with plantation manager J. R. Myers and other citizens, "touched the button which set in motion the 250,000 candle power lamp," which "responded as if by magic."[^19] The nomination says the lighthouse was dedicated on 1 May with a luau and a shark shoot, attended by officials and residents of Kīlauea town.[^2] The light was a 250,000-candlepower oil-vapour lamp.[^2]

The station was not finished. Funds ran out on 30 June 1913. Congress appropriated another $3,000 in August 1914, work resumed on 27 November 1914 and the station was completed on 2 February 1915, at a total cost to 30 June 1915 of $77,982.07.[^6] In February 1914 officials were already repairing the mercury vat, whose casting had left honeycombs and pitting.[^2] Three keepers' dwellings of rubble basalt stood about 300 feet (90 m) apart from one another, with an oil house and concrete cisterns.[^2] The tower's bare grey cement was left unpainted until 1924, when it was painted white.[^2]

The station kept changing. The *Kukui* stopped serving it in 1927 and the derrick and landing platform were dismantled.[^2] A radiobeacon began working at Kīlauea Point on 15 July 1930, a 200-watt Class B station that the Bulletin said could be used for bearings from 100 or 200 miles.[^8] The generators that powered it also electrified the lighthouse. The nomination says the light's intensity rose from 240,000 to 540,000 candlepower, and a 500-watt bulb in 1934 brought it to 1,100,000.[^2] The Bulletin of 1936 gave the same figure and described a four-and-a-half-ton revolving lens.[^9] An electric motor replaced the clockwork drive in 1939, with the clockwork kept as a standby.[^2]

## The keepers

The station was staffed by a head keeper and two assistants. Harry W. Flint was the first head keeper. The nomination names his first two assistants as beginning work on 6 May 1913; the archive of the U.S. Lighthouse Society lists them as Daniel P. Haleamau and Luther K. Kalama, though the nomination's scanned text reads differently.[^2][^1] In October 1914 and January 1915, with staff short, the supervisor of the still-working construction crew was pressed into service as temporary head keeper.[^2]

Samuel A. Amalu took charge in 1915 and stayed until 1924.[^1] He had joined the Lighthouse Service in 1906.[^2] His logs, as the nomination reports them, recorded thirty people from Kīlauea and another north-shore community visiting on a single day in his first year, along with trips to ball games and movies in Kīlauea, a polo game at a town the scan spells "Kopao," and a church service held in the keepers' quarters and attended by twenty.[^2] LighthouseFriends adds that he was known for swimming out through rough water to Mokuʻaeʻae Rock, which lies offshore, and that he flew the efficiency flag in 1920, 1921 and 1923.[^7] The Service's index for 1924 records an efficiency flag awarded to him at Kīlauea Point.[^10] Harry Flint, the first head keeper, came back for a second term from 1925 to 1932, according to the archive.[^1]

Visitors were a constant. When Claude Platt, a Coast Guard quartermaster (the nomination's scan reads "Plott"), took up his duties in April 1939, he noted that his children could go to school in Kīlauea. During his time at least one keeper had to stay on site to host visitors, and the nomination says a Hawaiian Tourist Bureau sign in Kīlauea marked the station as a tourist attraction.[^2] Stanley Huntington, a chief boatswain's mate, made visitors remove their shoes before entering the lighthouse. The nomination dates his command to 1949 to 1954; the archive's roster has him from 1949 to 1957.[^2][^1] When David Kahaunaele arrived in 1957, the staff was cut from three to two on alternating 48-hour shifts, and the public could visit only on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays, many of them arriving by tour bus.[^2]

The archive lists seventy keeper lines at the station between 1913 and 1974, among them Coast Guard personnel in its later decades.[^1] The roster table on this page gives the names.

As general context, not a record of any one keeper: the nomination describes the routine on such stations as lighting the lamp at sunset, keeping a night watch, winding the clockwork, and spending the days polishing brass and the lens and straining the mercury, which collected dust and slowed the rotation.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The light's best-known service was to an aircraft. When the Army aviators Lieutenants Maitland and Hegenberger made the first flight from the mainland to Hawaiʻi in June 1927, the Bulletin reported that the first landfall they made was Kīlauea Point Light, which they picked up "at a distance of about 90 miles." They recognised it as a lighthouse and then identified it by its flashes. The Service said that without it they might have passed the islands altogether.[^11] The nomination also records the flight.[^2]

On 7 December 1941 Japanese aircraft flew past the station and along Kauaʻi's coast toward Pearl Harbor. The light and the radiobeacon were shut down for the rest of the war.[^2] The petty officer in charge and a crew of six logged passing aircraft and vessels and broke the routine with general quarters, air raid and fire drills, and by March 1942 the window glass had been painted black.[^2]

The nomination mentions hurricane damage twice. The 1930 radiobeacon building was razed in 1956 after a hurricane damaged it.[^2] In 1992 Hurricane Iniki did extensive damage to the station, including to the lens.[^2]

## After the keepers

The station was fully staffed until 1974, when it was automated; it was one of the last lights in the Hawaiian Islands the Coast Guard automated.[^2] The nomination says the lighthouse windows and ventilation devices were filled in with concrete and an electronic optic was strapped to the railing outside the lantern room.[^2] In 1976, the nomination says, concern for workers exposed to mercury residue in the tower led the Coast Guard to decommission the lighthouse; a 24-inch rotating beacon on a ten-foot concrete column took over, with a single white flash.[^2] The nomination gives 1974 in one place and February 1976 in others for when the lens stopped serving as the light.[^2] The lighthouse was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 18 October 1979.[^2]

In 1985 the Coast Guard transferred the station to the General Services Administration, which passed it to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and it became part of Kīlauea Point National Wildlife Refuge.[^2] In 1989 the Coast Guard removed the mercury from the lens's float to decontaminate the station, which stopped the lens turning; later that year volunteers rigged a temporary arrangement to let it move briefly for the Lighthouse Service's bicentennial.[^2]

Salt air and weather had corroded the tower. A preservation committee formed in 2003 with the refuge's friends group, and a capital campaign called "Beacon for the Generations to Come" opened in 2008, raising nearly $1 million in private gifts alongside $1.5 million in public funds.[^12] In 2008 crews replaced anchor bolts holding the lantern room to the tower, some of which had completely disintegrated. The roof and lantern were repaired in spring 2012, the concrete restored in November 2012, and the work was finished by 30 April 2013.[^12] The tower was dedicated to Senator Daniel K. Inouye on 1 May 2013, the hundredth anniversary of its lighting.[^13] The Park Service register amended the property's common name at the Fish and Wildlife Service's request.[^2]

The restoration did not make the light work again. The lens cannot rotate because the mercury float that carried it cannot be restored, and the lamp is lit only on ceremonial occasions, the friends group says.[^13]

## Today

The tower stands within Kīlauea Point National Wildlife Refuge, managed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and is listed on the National Register as part of the Kīlauea Point Light Station.[^14] Storms in 2020 and 2021 let rain and moisture into the lantern room, raising humidity and speeding deterioration inside.[^15] Repairs to clean the lens, weatherproof the lantern glass and restore the vent ball began in 2025, and the friends group reports that the work concluded in May 2026.[^15][^16]

The refuge is open Wednesday to Saturday, 10 a.m. to 4 p.m., and visitors must arrive before 3:30 p.m. Reservations are required for everyone aged 16 and over.[^17] The Friends group's visitor page says tours of the lighthouse interior are suspended until further notice, and that reservations give access to the exterior of the lighthouse grounds, the refuge and its visitor center.[^18] The refuge and Friends pages read for this story do not say whether interior tours resumed after the 2026 repairs.
