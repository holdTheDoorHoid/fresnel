Frying Pan Shoals Light is a steel "Texas tower" standing on four legs 32 miles off Cape Fear, North Carolina.[^3] It was first lit on 24 November 1964, replacing a lightship that had marked the same shoal since 1854.[^3][^1]

## The place

The shoal runs out from Cape Fear into the Atlantic, extending some eighteen miles southeast of Bald Head Island.[^1] Two lighthouses on the island, built in 1794 and 1817, did not mark the far end of the shoal well enough, so the Lighthouse Board ordered a lightship placed on the shoal in 1854.[^1] Seven lightships served there between 1854 and 1930. The last of them, LV 115, stayed until the tower took over in 1964.[^1][^3]

The Board's annual report for the early 1890s lists light-vessel No. 29 on the station and describes her as in fair order and well kept.[^5] The Board's report for 1893 says a new steel vessel with a 12-inch steam whistle replaced No. 29.[^6] The Board's report for 1894 says light-vessel No. 53 was off station for repairs in January and February. It adds that there was no fog in the other ten months of the year, so her fog signal was not in use all year.[^7]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends describes the tower as an oil-drilling platform with a light on top.[^1] Its upper part was built in Louisiana by J. Ray McDermott Co., barged to the shoal, and lifted onto four steel legs that reach nearly 300 feet (91 m) below the water.[^1] The sources give two costs for it. LighthouseFriends calls the tower a $1.5 million structure in one place and says the upper portion cost $2 million in another.[^1]

The height is also disputed. The owners' site gives 135 feet (41 m) to the light above the Atlantic.[^3] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record gives 118 feet (36 m) for the height of the light above mean high water.[^2]

The tower was activated on 24 November 1964.[^3] Rear Admiral Oscar J. Rohnke then dedicated it at commissioning ceremonies, and Coast Guard helicopters shuttled the guests out to it.[^1] The lightship WAL 537 circled the new tower, gave three blasts of her whistle, and sailed for Cape May, New Jersey, to take up a new station.[^1]

## The keepers

Oscar B. O'Neal headed the station's first six-man crew, LighthouseFriends says.[^1] The Society's roster lists eight principal keepers from 1964 to the late 1970s, beginning with O'Neal and ending with Larry Smith, listed as serving at least from 1978.[^2] Its roster also has 54 Coast Guard crew members, each with the years the archive gives.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In September 1960 the lightship LV 115, known in Coast Guard records as WAL 537, rode out Hurricane Donna on station.[^1] LighthouseFriends prints the recollection of a crewman who was aboard.[^1] The anemometer read its maximum, 100 mph, and was then blown from the ship.[^1] When the captain got a Loran fix afterwards, the lightship was about fourteen miles south of her assigned position.[^1]

On 12 April 1882 the schooner Minnie, bound from New York for Charleston with a cargo of guano and empty barrels, stranded on the Frying Pan Shoals at three in the morning during a severe northeast rain storm.[^8] The Life-Saving Service's annual report says eight people were aboard, including the captain's wife.[^8] The captain had mistaken his position, supposing he was south of the lightship, and the schooner grounded in the breakers about five miles south of Smith's Island.[^8]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard automated the light in 1979, and the resident watch gave way to automated operation.[^3][^1] LighthouseFriends gives the deactivation date as 2003.[^1] The owners' site gives 2004 for the station's retirement from Coast Guard service.[^3] The Society's record still marks it as an active aid to navigation.[^2]

The General Services Administration auctioned the station in 2009, LighthouseFriends says.[^1] The winning bid of $515,000 came from Shipwrecks, Inc., but that company did not close the deal.[^1] In a sealed round that followed, Richard Neal's high bid of $11,262 came in below the fair market value the agency had to obtain.[^1] After further negotiation, Neal bought the tower for $85,000 in August 2010.[^1][^4] The owners' site dates the sale of the station to a private buyer to 2010 as well.[^3]

According to FPTower Inc., Neal gave up his own stake and split the tower into 32 fractional ownership interests.[^4] The nonprofit was set up to coordinate the restoration.[^4] LighthouseFriends notes that in May 2018 Neal had announced an auction, which he cancelled in favour of fractional ownership.[^1]

Ownership changed again in July 2026.[^1] LighthouseFriends says FPTower Inc. sold the tower to new owners.[^1] FPTower's own history dates its majority ownership to July 2025, and says that the nonprofit later transferred its interest, without giving a date.[^4] The owners' site says ownership consolidated within the station's existing co-owners, with no new outside buyers.[^3] The sources do not agree on who owns the tower now.

## Today

The tower is privately owned.[^3] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record lists it as not open to the public.[^2] The owners' site says its visitor program has returned, with trips open for booking. Each trip is two nights aboard, and the station is reached by boat across 32 miles of open Atlantic.[^3] LighthouseFriends says it is best viewed by boat or plane, and that the new owner plans to offer stays.[^1] The owners' site describes a programme of stabilization, and says its aim is to prepare the station for responsible scientific, educational and preservation work.[^3] FPTower Inc. describes thousands of volunteer hours spent on the restoration.[^4] Visits are arranged directly with the owners.[^3]
