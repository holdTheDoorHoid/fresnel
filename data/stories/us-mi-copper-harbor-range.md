The Copper Harbor Range Lights are a pair of small lights on the Keweenaw Peninsula, set to guide ships through the reef-lined entrance to Copper Harbor on Lake Superior.[^2] The rear light was shown from a lantern on the keeper's dwelling, built in 1868.[^2] A skeletal tower replaced that lantern in 1964, and a steel tower replaced the wooden front tower in 1927.[^2] The rear light belongs to the Michigan Historical Museum System, and its dwelling now houses the assistant manager of Fort Wilkins State Park.[^2]

## The place

Copper Harbor lies near the tip of the Keweenaw Peninsula, and its entrance is narrow and bounded by dangerous reefs.[^2] The Lighthouse Friends site, which describes a Lighthouse Board committee that toured the Great Lakes in 1863, says that without range lights the harbor could not be entered even on the clearest night, and that the range lights were then kept up at private expense.[^2] The Life-Saving Service's 1884 report lists "Copper Harbor (reef at entrance)" among the places on the coast where vessels stranded.[^7] Congress appropriated $3,500 for range lights on 20 June 1860.[^2]

## Building the light

The War Department transferred part of the Fort Wilkins reservation to the Lighthouse Board, and in 1865 a pair of range lights went up on it.[^2][^1] The Lighthouse Friends site says the front light was a wooden tower 22 feet tall with an octagonal lantern, set 450 feet north of the keeper's dwelling. The rear light was shown at a focal plane of 39 feet.[^2][^1]

The keeper first lived in one of the Fort Wilkins buildings.[^2] The War Department asked for them back in 1867, and Congress appropriated $5,000 on July 20, 1868 for a new keeper's dwelling.[^2] The Lighthouse Friends site describes a six-room, one-and-a-half-story house built that summer, with the rear light in a lantern on its lakeward gable.[^2]

In 1883 lightning struck the front tower.[^2] The damage was repaired the following year, and a lightning conductor was fitted to prevent a repeat.[^2]

## The keepers

The USLHS archive lists seven principal keepers at the station between 1865 and 1937.[^1] George W. Perry was the first, in 1865 and 1866, followed by Henry Mayor, William C. Turrell and Edward Bennett, who each served a year or a few years.[^1] William Tresise kept the lights from 1870 to 1885.[^1] The Lighthouse Friends site says he had been shot in the neck and shoulder at Petersburg, Virginia, in 1864, before he came to the lights.[^2] He died in February 1885 at 45, and "twenty-four-year-old Charles T. Davis was appointed in his stead."[^2]

Davis kept the range lights for 45 years, until he retired in 1930.[^1][^2] The Lighthouse Friends site reports that a newspaper of 1896 called Copper Harbor "a splendid place to take short vacations," and that Davis, who had fitted out his double house, took boarders at $1.50 a day.[^2] Davis kept a log. Its entries, as the Lighthouse Friends site prints them, include a note in 1900 that he had used the old journal for nearly fifteen years, and his entry of 30 September 1930: "Am retiring from the Lighthouse Service today, leaving Mr. Haven in charge of the station."[^2] For the fiscal year ended 30 June 1917, the Commissioner's report still names him as keeper of the Copper Harbor Range Lights.[^6]

Charles H. Haven succeeded Davis and served from 1930 to 1937.[^1] In 1919 the main Copper Harbor Lighthouse was converted to acetylene and placed under the care of the range keeper, according to the Lighthouse Friends site.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In October 1923 the steamer Samuel Mather ran aground on Gull Rock, off the tip of the peninsula, and two of her crew reached Copper Harbor the next day in an exhausted condition.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's notice for that autumn names the station's keeper, Charles T. Davis, and its assistant keeper, George R. Holman. It says that two men from the disabled steamer were helped, and that the keeper traveled through the night, over snow-covered hills, to send a message to the telegraph office at Calumet.[^3] The scan of the notice is garbled, so which man was credited with which act is uncertain. The Lighthouse Friends site credits Davis with the food, shelter and night walk.[^2]

In a letter printed in the Bulletin in 1925, Davis described the close of the 1924 season.[^4] He sent the team on with his wife and their luggage, and started to walk back.[^4] The lock on the front tower was frozen.[^4] It was blowing too hard to row over to the main light, so he snowshoed around the bay to light it as well.[^4] The Bulletin says the letter "shows the hardship encountered."[^4] He wrote that the snow was quite deep and the walking hard, and that the snowshoes "would sink about a foot and load up with snow at every step."[^4]

A letter dated 31 December 1926 from the Coast Guard district office at Green Bay, passed on to the lighthouse superintendent at Detroit, acknowledged the services of the keepers at Eagle Harbor, Copper Harbor and Mendota.[^5] The Eagle Harbor Coast Guard crew was helping two steamers at the time, and "the keepers at Copper Harbor and Mendota lights provided food" as well as shelter for the shipwrecked sailors and Coast Guard men.[^5]

## After the keepers

The range lights were electrified in 1937, "eliminating the need for a resident keeper."[^2] The Lighthouse Friends site says a two-year lease on the dwelling was advertised in local papers that August.[^2] In 1927 a white steel tower on a tank house replaced the wooden front tower.[^2] In 1964 a skeletal tower replaced the lantern on the dwelling.[^2]

## Today

The rear light is owned by the Michigan Historical Museum System.[^2] The Lighthouse Friends site reports that the grounds are open and the dwelling and tower are closed.[^2] The dwelling is home to the assistant manager of Fort Wilkins State Park, and visitors are asked to respect their privacy.[^2] From the range lights you can walk to the harbor and see the main Copper Harbor Lighthouse across the water.[^2]
