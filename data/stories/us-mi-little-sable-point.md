Little Sable Point Light stands in Silver Lake State Park, west of Mears in Oceana County, Michigan, on the Lake Michigan shore. Its brick tower rises over 100 feet (30 m). Work on it began in 1873, and it first showed its light in the 1874 navigation season. It is now the only structure left of the station. The Coast Guard decommissioned the navigation light in 2013, and in 2014 it gave permission to light the tower for historical display.[^1][^9][^8]

## The place

The report says the name Petite Pointe au Sable came from French voyageurs who paddled their canoes around the "little sandy point."[^3] By the early 1850s the lumbermen Charles Mears and William Ferry were running sawmills in the woods around it. The report says the mills were far from any river transportation, and that their life was short.[^3]

The schooner Pride ran aground near the point in 1871, and the report says the wreck increased the urgency for navigation aids in the area.[^8] The Lighthouse Board's 1870 report recommended a light for the point, and Congress appropriated $35,000 for it on 10 June 1872.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends gives the Board's note as 1871, quoting its remark that "a simple inspection of the chart of Lake Michigan" would show the need.[^2]

Orlando Metcalfe Poe, who became chief engineer of the Eleventh Lighthouse District in 1870, had recommended four identical 100-foot (30 m) coast lights for the district, and this was one of them.[^3] The state of Michigan held the land and handed over 231.26 acres. President Grant signed the reservation on 2 July 1872. The federal government then gave back 191.86 acres, leaving 39.4 acres of dune and scrub forest.[^3]

## Building the light

Work began in April 1873 with a small crew, a dock and a shack. In June the lighthouse tender Warrington loaded timber, brick, cement and iron castings at Detroit and brought them to the point.[^4] The builders drove 109 one-foot pilings into the sand, laid about twelve feet of concrete and cut stone on them, and built a brick tower with a double wall and an air space between the two. The walls were five feet (1.5 m) thick at the base and two feet (0.6 m) at the gallery.[^4][^9]

A one-and-a-half-story brick dwelling stood beside the tower, joined to it by an enclosed walkway. The first floor was fitted out for the keeper and the second floor for his assistant.[^4] LighthouseFriends describes a larger dwelling, with twelve rooms over two and a half stories.[^2] The report says a 1911 remodel added large dormers and a third floor to accommodate the two keepers and their families.[^6] The crew came back in spring 1874 to finish the interior, and the new light was exhibited for the first time on the opening of the 1874 season of navigation.[^4]

The lens was a third-order Fresnel lens built under contract by L. Sautter & Cie. of Paris. The Historic Structures Report says it was the only lens of its kind ever used in a lighthouse on the Great Lakes. The lower sections stayed fixed and only the upper belt turned, so the light showed a "fixed white varied by white flash" characteristic. The report says the reason no other lake light used this arrangement is "as yet unknown."[^5] A 90-pound weight, lowered 85 feet (26 m) between the tower walls, turned the clockwork, and LighthouseFriends says the keepers had to wind it every eleven hours.[^5][^2] The lamp burned lard oil with three concentric wicks. The report says it was visible 18¼ miles (about 16 nautical miles) at sea in clear weather.[^5]

The bare brick was hard to see against the dunes.[^5] In September 1875 District Engineer Godfrey Weitzel ordered the district's brick towers whitewashed, and the report says the keepers then faced "the drudgery and danger of this annual whitewashing ritual."[^5]

## The keepers

The Archive's roster for the station lists fifteen principal keepers, from James Davenport (1873-1879) to Henry Vavrina (1948-1954), together with a long run of first assistants and a second assistant.[^12] LighthouseFriends says Davenport was moved from the assistant's post at Waugoshance Lighthouse to become the first head keeper, and that he lit the light when navigation opened in 1874. John Carley was the first assistant.[^2]

Joseph Arthur Hunter was head keeper from 1899 to 1922, "longer than any other keeper," according to LighthouseFriends.[^2][^12] It says he met his first automobile in 1904 while driving his team of horses, that he patrolled the beach gathering anything that washed ashore, and that by 1922 he had salvaged enough shingles and lumber to build his retirement home.[^2]

The roster also names Gertrude Helen Hunter as first assistant keeper in 1910. She is the only woman on the station's roster. The sources do not say how she was related to Joseph Arthur Hunter, if at all.[^12]

Henry Vavrina served as first assistant under William Kruwell in 1940, and was head keeper from 1948 until 1954.[^12][^2] LighthouseFriends describes a 1947 dinner he gave at the lighthouse, which it calls quite a feat "considering the station had no electricity."[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In August 1875 the schooner Black Hawk ran aground on the point, and Keeper Davenport entered it in the station logbook. LighthouseFriends quotes his note, "crew all saved," and calls it the first of several shipwrecks recorded there.[^2]

The Armistice Day storm of 11 November 1940 hit this shore hard.[^13] LighthouseFriends gives gusts of ninety-two miles an hour and waves of thirty feet. A Pentwater marker, quoted by the Michigan Shipwreck Research Association, gives seventy-five miles an hour and waves of twenty feet, and says the storm killed fifty-nine seamen in all.[^2][^13]

LighthouseFriends says the freighters William B. Davock and Anna C. Minch "foundered off Little Sable Point," taking fifty-six crew with them. The Association's pages differ. They place the Davock 1.9 miles (about 1.7 nautical miles) off the light, but say she sank off Pentwater with all 32 men aboard. They put the Minch about 1.5 miles (about 1.3 nautical miles) south of Pentwater.[^2][^13][^14] The coordinates on the Association's page put the Minch about 6.7 nautical miles north of the light's coordinates in the report, so LighthouseFriends' phrase does not match the Association's position for her.[^1][^14]

The Canadian freighter Novadoc ran aground just north of the point. LighthouseFriends says all but two of her nineteen crew survived. The Association says two cooks were washed overboard and the remaining 17 were rescued by the little fishing tug of Clyde Cross, who steered alongside the stranded ship.[^2][^15] LighthouseFriends says Captain Steip of the Novadoc handed Cross a roll of bills, and Cross replied, "Glad to be of service."[^2] A Coast Guard board of inquiry asked whether the Ludington station was at fault for not reaching the ship sooner. Keeper Kruwell testified that conditions kept the Ludington crew from responding sooner.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1893 records a circular iron oil house of 360 gallons, built 100 feet (30 m) northeast of the tower. The report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1894 notes a plank walk built to the oil house.[^10][^11] In October 1915 a district lampist upgraded the lamp to an incandescent oil vapor light, which gave about 24,000 candlepower, though the extra range was only about a mile.[^6] In 1926 Congress let the Secretary of Commerce transfer most of the reservation to the state for park use, and it became part of Silver Lake State Park.[^6][^2]

Electricity reached the station in 1954. The Coast Guard proposed a line from the Oceana Rural Electric Cooperative in May, and the work was not finished until December. The light was converted to a 1,000-watt bulb with a spare, and the flash was made by a solenoid, not by turning the lens. The keepers were reassigned, and the buildings were emptied and boarded up.[^7]

The report dates the demolition of the dwelling, oil houses and privy to 1955. LighthouseFriends says the brick dwelling was torn down in 1958.[^7][^2] The tower stayed white until 1977, when the Coast Guard sandblasted it, according to the report. LighthouseFriends dates the sandblasting to 1974 and says the tower was painted white in 1900.[^7][^2]

## Today

The State of Michigan owns the site, which is part of Silver Lake State Park.[^1] A paved road called Lighthouse Drive leads through the park and ends at the station.[^1] The tower is the only structure left.[^1] In 1999 a local group called the Little Sable Point Lighthouse Seekers formed to open the tower.[^7] In 2005 the Michigan Department of Natural Resources leased the tower for 25 years to the Big Sable Point Lighthouse Keepers Association, now the Sable Points Lighthouse Keepers Association. LighthouseFriends says it was the first time the tower had been open to the public since the late 1940s.[^7][^2] A paved path through the dunes was completed in 2010 with a federal coastal grant.[^2]

The report dates the Coast Guard's decommissioning of the navigation light to 2013, and the Coast Guard's permission to light it for historical display to 2014. A State of Michigan historical marker was placed at the site in 2014.[^8][^1] LighthouseFriends gives 2014 as the date of deactivation.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says the tower is open Tuesday through Sunday from 10 a.m. to 5 p.m., late May through early September. It says the grounds are open, and the tower is open in season, and it advises visitors to call or check the association's website first.[^2]
