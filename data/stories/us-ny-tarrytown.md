Tarrytown Light stands at Kingsland Point, in Sleepy Hollow, New York, on a caisson in the Hudson.[^2][^3] It was established in 1883 to help river traffic past dangerous shoals, after pleas from local steamship companies.[^1][^2][^3] When it was built it stood about half a mile (0.8 km) from shore; later landfill moved the shoreline to within a few feet of the light.[^3]

## The place

The light was built for the river's shoals. The county's page says local steamship companies asked for help with dangerous shoals, and the light was built in answer.[^2]

In 1891–1892 the Light-House Board renewed its recommendation for a light and fog signal on the Oyster Beds shoal, off Rockland Lake Landing. The Board's directions for that light, which had not yet been built, said that vessels coming downstream from Stony Point Light would "lay their course direct for the new light" until the Kingsland Point light became visible, which would mark a turning point.[^4] The archive lists Kingsland Point as an alternate name for this station.[^1]

## Building the light

The archive's record for the station gives 1883 as the year the tower was established and the year its original lens was installed. The lens was a fourth-order Fresnel.[^1] The archive's figures come from the Light List of 1908. That list puts the tower at 44 feet (13.4 m) from base to lantern, and the light at 54.5 feet (16.6 m) above high water.[^1]

The sources disagree on the construction material. The archive records cast iron on a stone pier with a cast-iron caisson.[^1] The county describes a cast-iron tower on a concrete-and-metal caisson, but also calls it "the only conical steel structure in Westchester."[^2] Visit Sleepy Hollow repeats the steel description, calling it "the only conical steel structure in Westchester County."[^3]

The pier needed repeated work. The Board's 1891–1892 report says that "Some 500 tons of riprap stone were placed for the protection of the pier."[^4] The report for 1893 records a contract for 600 tons of riprap for the same purpose,[^6] and a 1896 report records a contract for 200 tons more.[^7]

The light's color was changed in the early years. The Board's report says the light "was changed from white to red" on 30 September 1893.[^6]

## The keepers

The archive's roster for the station names thirteen keepers, from Jacob Ackerman (1883 to 1904) to Fred C. Fleck (1958 to 1965). It also has one Coast Guard name, Edward Brown (1954 to 1955), with the position not stated.[^1] Ackerman kept the light for twenty-one years. Ten keepers followed between 1904 and 1954, and four of them have spans of one year on the roster: 1916 to 1917, 1940 to 1941, 1941 to 1942 and 1942 to 1943.[^1]

Sources differ on how many people lived here. The county says the light housed fourteen keepers, some with their families. A tourism site for Sleepy Hollow says eight keepers and their families occupied the five-story tower over its years of operation.[^2][^3] The archive's roster has fourteen names, counting the Coast Guard entry.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources for this light mostly record pier work and the change of color. The one rescue in the sources read is in the Lighthouse Service Bulletin's issue of 1 July 1925, which commends "keeper of Tarrytown Lighthouse" John A. Brown for rescuing two men from drowning on 8 June. The archive's roster has Brown as keeper from 1917 to 1930.[^8][^1] The Bulletin's indexes list Brown's tow of a boat, his assist to a vessel and the rescue.[^8][^9][^10]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1957, and the archive gives 1961 as the year it was discontinued.[^1] The county's page says the Tappan Zee Bridge made the lighthouse obsolete.[^2] Westchester County acquired the decommissioned structure from the federal government in the 1970s.[^3] The National Register's asset record gives 29 May 1979 as its publication date.[^5]

## Today

The lighthouse is at Kingsland Point Park, a county park. Parking is charged at the park, and the light is a ten-minute walk south along the Riverwalk path.[^2] In May 2024 the county held a ribbon-cutting for a $3.4 million renovation and restoration, which returned the building to the period between 1937 and 1976.[^3][^2] Tours are free, but must be booked by registration on Eventbrite when they are offered.[^2]
