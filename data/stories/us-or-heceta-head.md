Heceta Head Light stands on a high headland on the central Oregon coast, north of Florence in Lane County, named for the Spanish naval officer Bruno de Heceta. The National Register nomination calls it the last major coastal light established in Oregon and the most powerful along the state's seacoast, and the tower, 56 feet (17 m) tall and 206 feet (63 m) above the Pacific, still shows a first-order Fresnel lens. (State Parks gives the tower's height as 56 feet; the nomination's 42 feet is measured from the tower floor to the lantern floor.)[^1][^11]

## The place

The headland carries the name of Captain Don Bruno de Heceta of the Spanish Royal Navy, who commanded an exploring expedition in the Pacific Northwest in 1775.[^1] When the light was planned, the site lay about midway between the Umpqua River and Yaquina Bay lights.[^1] LighthouseFriends, a modern site, says the Light-House Board recommended first-order lights at Heceta Head and Umpqua River in 1888 to close a dark gap of about ninety miles between Cape Arago and Yaquina Head, and that Congress appropriated $80,000 on 2 March 1889.[^9]

The site was remote. In its report for 1891 the Light-House Board recorded that title to the land had been secured, together with a right of way over private property for a wagon road to the nearest public highway, about 7 miles away.[^3] LighthouseFriends adds that a forest fire a few years earlier had left the headland with very little vegetation when the work began.[^9]

## Building the light

The National Register nomination says the station was built about the same time as Umpqua River Light, 28 miles to the south, apparently from the same drawings and specifications.[^1] It dates construction from 1892 to 1894 and gives a total cost of $180,000, well above the $80,000 appropriation reported by LighthouseFriends; the sources do not explain the difference.[^1][^9] The nomination repeats a report that the brick was shipped by sailing vessel from San Francisco to Florence, moved on by barge behind the tug *Quillian* to the mouth of the Siuslaw, and hauled to the site by team and wagon.[^1]

The dwellings, barn and oil houses were finished first: the Light-House Board reported that the contractors completed that contract on 25 January 1893, after a supplemental contract of $3,609.40 for extra excavation.[^4] The extra digging for the tower site delayed the tower contractor, and winter storms stopped his work after the foundation had been laid up to the belt course.[^4] The Board's next report gives the tower as completed on 31 August 1893. The lens and supplies were landed in October and set up in November and December, but the lamps did not arrive from the Staten Island depot until February 1894, and the light was first exhibited on 30 March 1894.[^5]

The tower is conical stuccoed brick with sandstone trim, joined by a short passage to a workroom.[^1] Its first-order lens, with eight bull's-eyes, was made by Chance Brothers of Britain; the nomination notes that most American lighthouses took French lenses instead.[^1] It was first lit by a five-wick coal-oil lamp of about 80,000 candle power, replaced by a gas burner in 1910 and later by electric power.[^1] The quarters were a single dwelling for the keeper and a double house for two assistants, each of three rooms on each of two floors, with a full basement and attic.[^1]

The hillside soon caused trouble. The Board's report for 1895 records that a large landslide endangering the tower and oil house was removed and the bank behind them given a longer slope, a point of rocks in line with the light and Cape Perpetua was cut down to clear the view, and a picket fence went up around the dwellings.[^6]

## The keepers

The USLHS archive's roster, drawn from the keeper registers, lists Andrew P.C. Hald as the first principal keeper (1894-1899), with Eugene M. Walters and John M. Cowan as the first two assistants.[^2] LighthouseFriends says Hald had walked 18 miles to Astoria and then, after a train to Newport, 24 miles down the beach to reach the station, while his wife and furniture came round by sea through San Francisco; it adds that during his years there his wife was seriously ill and a baby daughter died for want of medical attention.[^9]

Olaf L. Hansen was first assistant from 1896 to 1903 and head keeper from 1904 to 1920.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that he and his wife Annie filed a homestead claim at nearby Mercer Lake and started a large family there, and that a one-room school was built at the station for the keepers' children.[^9] Florido "Frank" DeRoy rose through all three grades: second assistant in 1912, first assistant from 1914, head keeper from 1920 to 1925.[^2]

Clifford B. Hermann kept the light from 1925 to 1950.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he earned efficiency stars in 1930, 1931 and 1933 and was the first civilian Coast Guard employee to receive the Albert Gallatin award on retiring.[^9] Roster years for the 1950s and 1960s are uneven; Oswald Allik was head keeper from 1957, and the nomination records that he, the last resident keeper, left in 1963 when the light was automated.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends reports that the second assistant's post was dropped in the late 1930s once electricity eased the work.[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The station's best-documented rescue involved no ship. On the evening of 25 March 1927 a young man, Frederick Huntington, fell 80 feet (24 m) from the cliff above Sea Lion Cave, 2 miles from the station, and was badly hurt.[^7] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin says the keepers were told at 6 p.m. and reached the cave at 7, "the roads being very bad", with a lantern, two flashlights and a small carbide light between them.[^7] Keeper Hermann and second assistant Charles F. Walters, with several other men, followed a trail 8 to 12 inches wide for about 1,500 feet (460 m), on average 300 feet (90 m) above the surf.[^7] The Bulletin says Walters went down the rope first and set the boy's leg, which had a compound fracture; LighthouseFriends says Hermann did. The injured man was lashed to a board and hauled up in two hours, then carried by stretcher to a doctor and on to a train for the hospital.[^7][^9] The Department of Commerce commended both keepers, and the first assistant, Robert R. Bay, who stood the station's watch for 19 hours alone.[^7]

A series of southwest storms struck the Northwest coast from 17 December 1931. At Heceta Head, the Bulletin reports, seas destroyed fences and buried the keepers' extensive gardens under 2 feet of sand.[^8]

LighthouseFriends records that on 12 February 1961 a rock slide cut the power lines and the standby generator failed, so Allik and two Coast Guard assistants lit an Aladdin lamp and turned the lens by hand from midnight until 7:30 the next morning; it also says as many as seventy-five Coast Guardsmen were stationed at Heceta Head during the Second World War as coastal patrol.[^9]

## After the keepers

When the station was nominated to the National Register, the lighthouse and oil houses were Coast Guard property while the keepers' quarters, altered in the 1930s, belonged to the Forest Service and housed a Lane Community College environmental education center.[^1] The nomination lists the razing of the single dwelling among the alterations it describes as made in the 1930s; LighthouseFriends dates its demolition to 1940.[^1][^9]

LighthouseFriends says wear in the lens's rotating mechanism left it leaning nearly six inches, that public objection stopped a Coast Guard plan to retire the lens, and that it was taken out of service in June 2000 and relit on 15 March 2001 after about a year of work.[^9] Later the lighthouse passed to the Oregon Parks and Recreation Department. The department closed it in August 2011 for a restoration of about $1.6 million, which removed cement stucco that had trapped moisture, restored the metalwork and masonry, and repaired the lens drive. It reopened with a ceremony on 8 June 2013.[^10]

## Today

The lighthouse is an active aid to navigation owned by the Oregon Parks and Recreation Department and sits in Heceta Head Lighthouse State Scenic Viewpoint.[^2][^11] A half-mile trail leads from the parking area past the keepers' house to the tower. State Parks lists the ground floor as open 11 a.m. to 3 p.m. from March through October and 11 a.m. to 2 p.m. from November through February, weather and staff permitting; the upper levels are closed, and a day-use parking permit is required.[^11] The former assistant keepers' house is a bed and breakfast run by a concessionaire of the U.S. Forest Service.[^11]
