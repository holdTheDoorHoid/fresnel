Sabine Bank Light was an iron lighthouse built on a sunken caisson in the Gulf of Mexico, about 16 miles off the entrance to Sabine Pass, Texas.[^1][^2] It first showed its light in 1906.[^1][^3] The Lighthouse Service made it unattended in 1922, and in 2002 work began to take down its original tower and replace it with a steel skeleton tower.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Sabine Bank is a shoal about 30 miles long and 5 miles wide, with its top only about 20 feet (6.1 m) under the surface.[^1] It lies in the Gulf roughly sixteen miles south of the Sabine River's entrance, and the river is a natural funnel that directs commerce to Port Arthur.[^1] The river was hard to enter from the Gulf until jetties built in the 1890s gave a deep, stable channel, after which deep-draft ships came to Port Arthur in numbers.[^1]

The bank was a hazard.[^1] In 1888 Congressman William H. Crain proposed a lightship for it, but the district engineer in New Orleans did not think one was needed.[^1] The Light-House Board's 1899 report says an act of March 3, 1899 authorized a light and fog signal on Sabine Bank at a cost of not more than $50,000, though no money was then appropriated for it.[^4] Congress later provided $40,000 under an act of June 6, 1900,[^5][^6] and a further $40,000 under an act of March 3, 1901, to meet the contract.[^6] Meanwhile LighthouseFriends says three vessels, the British steamship Athena, the steamer Venitia and the Italian Fratelli Gazolli, ran aground on the bank between 1899 and 1901.[^1] The Life-Saving Service's table of American vessels that stranded in the decade to 1902 also lists Sabine Bank, Gulf of Mexico.[^7]

## Building the light

Bids for the work were opened on January 8, 1903.[^1] A caisson design was the cheapest, and the metalwork went to the Russel Wheel and Foundry Company of Detroit. The erection contract was awarded to W. R. Taylor, who refused to sign it, so a lighthouse engineer finished the job with hired labor.[^1]

The caisson was towed out on June 23, 1904, by the tender Arbutus and the tug Dello.[^1] On July 21 it sank to its target depth of 20 feet (6.1 m) into the shoal.[^1] It was then filled with concrete, except for a cylinder at the top that held the water cisterns.[^1] Putnam's history of the lights says the caisson of cast-iron plates was built at Sabine, Texas, towed 16 miles to the site, and sunk by the same process used at Fourteen-Foot Bank.[^8] Work stopped as funding was depleted and started again after Congress allocated $12,000 in July 1905.[^1]

On the evening of October 7, 1905, a storm struck the site while the crew were assembling the five-story superstructure.[^1] The workers moved into the open caisson for the night.[^1] Conrad Thompson, who supervised that phase, described it: "There was no sleeping that night and the next day for any of us."[^1]

The tower was lined with enameled brick.[^1] LighthouseFriends says $101,353.67 had been spent when its third-order Fresnel lens first showed on March 15, 1906, and the station was staffed by a keeper and three assistants.[^1] The tower was painted red, and a Daboll trumpet fog signal was run by two five-horsepower oil engines.[^1] The tower measured 50 feet (15.2 m) from the top of the caisson to the ventilator ball above the lantern.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives the height from the base of the structure to the lantern as 72 feet (22 m).[^3] Putnam also records that an oil-vapor lamp later installed at Sabine Bank drew so many birds to the lantern that a bird guard became necessary.[^9]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists 101 keeper and assistant stints at Sabine Bank from 1906 to 1922, and most of them lasted a year or less.[^3] LighthouseFriends says that keepers often served weeks or months at the station before they were relieved, and that the district superintendent in New Orleans complained the service had to pick up men who were not on the eligible roster to fill vacancies there.[^1]

Albert B. Modawell was principal keeper from 1913 to 1917.[^3] The Secretary of Commerce commended him, along with the assistants of his crew, for their work in the 1915 hurricane, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] In October 1916, LighthouseFriends says, Modawell was adrift for more than a day in a small boat with no food or water after his engine failed, and a Coast Guard cutter found him.[^1]

In 1919 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that the Department had commended Ingvald C. M. Ericksen, an assistant keeper, and Charles T. Hansen, a temporary assistant keeper, for keeping the light after the station had sustained serious damage in the hurricane of September 13 to 14, and for putting the station in order afterwards.[^11]

Ericksen was later principal keeper, from 1920 to 1921, and William Collette was the last principal keeper, from 1921 to 1922.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The hurricane of August 16 and 17, 1915 badly damaged the station.[^10][^12] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that the sea washed the hatches off the gallery floor and tore off the iron gallery roof and the stanchions that supported it.[^10] The report on repairs, in the Commissioner of Lighthouses' volume, records that the storm washed the hatches off the main gallery floor about 26 feet 6 inches (8.1 m) above mean high water, flooded the cellar, and carried away the gallery roof and some of the gallery footplates.[^12]

According to LighthouseFriends, the keepers kept the light burning until the lack of drinking water forced them off the station on August 19, and they returned to relight it on August 23.[^1] The Bulletin says that the keepers maintained the light during the hurricane and left the station only when they were compelled to by lack of fresh water.[^10]

Repairs after 1915 ended with a cylindrical wall of cast-iron plates around the gallery, closed in by a reinforced-concrete roof.[^12] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1919 says iron shell plates, davits and footplates were installed, closing in the veranda.[^13] LighthouseFriends says the repairs cost just over $15,000, and that the work helped the tower stand up better in another powerful hurricane in 1920.[^1]

In October 1920, LighthouseFriends says, an assistant keeper was picked up at sea by a Norwegian tanker after two and a half days adrift in a disabled motorboat.[^1]

## After the keepers

On August 22, 1922, LighthouseFriends says, an acetylene lantern was lit in the Fresnel lens and the last resident keepers left the station.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's list of changes for that year includes Sabine Bank's conversion from oil to acetylene.[^14] The 1923 Bulletin describes the change as an unattended acetylene installation that marks the bank "about 16 miles off the entrance to Sabine Pass." It says the oil-vapor light had needed four keepers, and that the saving was about $5,400 a year.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says the station was temporarily occupied as a coastal lookout in World War II. In April 1924 the keepers at Sabine Pass noticed that Sabine Bank's light was not showing on a clear night, and the next day a small hawk still in the lantern room had put out the pilot flames.[^1] In 1960 the light was changed to a 150-watt lamp run from a 32-volt battery.[^1] The Fresnel lens was removed from the lantern in 1971, and the tower was converted to solar power.[^1]

By 2001 the tower was in poor condition.[^1] A Coast Guard notice of March 1, 2001, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, called for the lighthouse structure above the caisson to be demolished and removed, and for a new 55-foot (17 m) steel skeleton tower to be installed on the caisson's main deck.[^1] Work began in late January 2002. The top section of the tower and the lantern room were dismantled and taken ashore, and they are on display at Lions Park in Sabine Pass.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 2002 as the year the light was discontinued.[^3]

## Today

The top section of the original tower and its lantern room are on display at Lions Park in Sabine Pass.[^1] The third-order Fresnel lens is at the Museum of the Gulf Coast in Port Arthur, which LighthouseFriends says is open Monday to Saturday from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m.[^1] The Lighthouse Society's record calls the museum the Gulf Coast Maritime Museum.[^3]

The station stands offshore and is reached only by boat.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record lists it as not open to the public.[^3] The Coast Guard issued the 2001 notice for the work.[^1] It is not recorded in these sources whether the new tower was completed or whether its light is shown.
