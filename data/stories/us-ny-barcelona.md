Barcelona Light stands on a bluff above Barcelona Harbor, on Lake Erie in the town of Westfield, New York.[^1][^2] Its 40-foot (12 m) conical fieldstone tower was finished in 1829, and from 1831 it was lit with natural gas.[^1][^3] The Chautauqua County Historical Society calls it the first public building lit with natural gas in the United States.[^1][^2][^3] The light was discontinued in 1859.[^1][^2] The State of New York has owned the site since 2007 or 2008, and the sources differ on the year.[^1][^3]

## The place

The harbor was later renamed Barcelona.[^1] By 1828 Portland Harbor had just been made an official port of entry.[^1] Warehouses and wharves served small vessels carrying lumber, salt, flour and fish, and the harbor was a rest stop for passengers travelling between Erie and Buffalo.[^1] A portage ran from the harbor to Chautauqua Lake and on to the Allegheny River.[^1] Congressman Daniel Garnsey persuaded Congress in 1828 to appropriate $5,000 for the lighthouse, the historical society says.[^3]

## Building the light

A lot on a bluff overlooking the harbor was bought on 10 July 1828 for $50.[^1] LighthouseFriends names three men as the sellers.[^1] The historical society says the Holland Land Company sold it.[^1][^3]

The contract for the tower and a keeper's dwelling, $2,700, went the following month to Judge Thomas B. Campbell.[^1] It called for eleven patent lamps, eleven 14-inch reflectors and two spare lamps.[^1] Campbell built a 40-foot conical tower of rough-split fieldstone, 22 feet across at the base, and a dwelling of the same stone.[^1] Of the $5,000 appropriated, only $3,506.78 was spent.[^1] The historical society says the work was finished by June 1829.[^3]

The first keeper, Joshua Lane, was appointed on 27 May 1829 at $350 a year.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes a description of him as "a deaf, superannuated clergyman, having numerous female dependents."[^1]

On 1 January 1831 Campbell agreed to supply natural gas to the light "at all times and seasons," for $213 a year, which was the cost of oil.[^1] The historical society quotes the Fredonia Censor of the time as saying the light was "illuminated in the most splendid style by natural carbureted hydrogen gas."[^3] LighthouseFriends reports that, seen from Lake Erie at night, the tower looked like "one complete, constant and unwavering blaze." It does not name the observer.[^1]

## The keepers

Joshua Lane's service was interrupted.[^2] The Lighthouse Society's roster has him in post from 1829 to 1841, from 1842 to 1843 and from 1845 to 1846, with Nathaniel Bird in the gaps.[^2] LighthouseFriends says authorities removed Lane in 1841 for reasons that remain unclear, reappointed him in 1842 and removed him again in 1843.[^1] He returned in 1845 and served until his death in 1846.[^1]

Lane had told his superiors several times about trouble with the gas supply.[^1] Campbell, who supplied the gas, complained about Lane, hoping the keeper would be turned out so that Campbell could draw the pay for running the light.[^1]

Joshua La Due followed in 1846 at the same $350 salary.[^1] Richard Kenyon served from 1849 to 1855, Thomas Taylor from 1855 to 1856, and William Britton from 1856 to 1859, the last keeper.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends says the Great Gale of 1844 destroyed a number of lakeside warehouses and wharves, including those at Barcelona.[^1] The historical society calls it the "Great Storm" and dates it to 15 October 1844.[^3] It says the storm virtually destroyed a larger pier and breakwall that the government had paid for that year, along with the old structures.[^3]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Board's report for 1855 said the harbor was not a proper harbor and that the light was not needed even as a "lake coast light," LighthouseFriends reports.[^1] Even so, the Board fitted a fourth-order Fresnel lens in 1857.[^1] In 1859 the Board discontinued the light, citing the "mutations of commerce and changes of channels or harbors."[^1]

The deed said the property would revert to the Holland Land Company if the light were discontinued. It passed to George W. Patterson, the company's agent, who in 1872 paid the government $500 for the structures.[^1] In the 1880s the keeper's house was enlarged and dormers were added.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the original lantern was likely removed when the light was discontinued.[^1]

The property stayed in the Patterson family for five generations, more than a century in all.[^1] In 1998 Bruce and Ann Mulkin of Fredonia bought it from the estate of Faith Scott.[^1] In 1941 the property was leased to the Bedford Products Company of Dunkirk, which opened the dwelling as a wayside refreshment stand.[^1] In 1943 a great-grandson of George Patterson began a restoration.[^1] On 10 April 1962, a day designated National Gaslight Day, the tower was supplied with natural gas again as a nod to its past.[^1]

The tower and keeper's dwelling were listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1972.[^1][^3] The historical society says New York State acquired them in 2007, and LighthouseFriends says in 2008.[^1][^3]

## Today

The site is a New York State Historic Site, and its grounds are open year-round.[^4] The keeper's cottage, which holds exhibits from the historical society, is open in staffed hours from Memorial Day to Labor Day.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the cottage houses the Westfield Visitor Center in summer.[^1][^3] The 2026 schedule that NY State Parks publishes, as Park Finder lists it, runs daily from late May to 12 October and then on weekends to 20 December.[^4] Park Finder says tower access depends on weather and construction.[^4] LighthouseFriends lists the tower as closed.[^1][^4]

A light is still maintained in the tower, but it is not an aid to navigation.[^1]
