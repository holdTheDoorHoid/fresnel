Point Gammon Light stood on the southern tip of Great Island in West Yarmouth, Massachusetts, just east of the entrance to Hyannis Harbor.[^1][^2] It was first lit on 21 November 1816 and went out in 1858, when a lighthouse on the Bishop and Clerks ledges took over its work; the Lighthouse Society's archive gives 1855 as the year it was discontinued.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The point lies about two miles (3.2 km) north of the Bishop and Clerks ledges, which LighthouseFriends calls treacherous.[^1] The citizens of Dennis, Yarmouth and Barnstable asked Congress for a light, to protect their investments as trade grew around Hyannis.[^1]

## Building the light

The tower was built of stone, conical, sixteen feet (4.9 m) across at its base, with walls twenty feet (6.1 m) high.[^2][^1] The keeper's dwelling was one and a half stories, 16 by 30 feet (4.9 by 9.1 m), and was joined to the tower by a kitchen so the keeper could tend the light without going out in the weather.[^2][^1] The light was first shown on 21 November 1816 at seventy feet (21 m) above the sea.[^1]

Lieutenant Edward W. Carpender, a Navy inspector, reported on the station in November 1838 and found it freshly repaired and in good order.[^1] Five years later, in 1843, the engineer I. W. P. Lewis made his survey and did not find the structure quite so sound. He recorded leaking walls, rotten woodwork and a structure out of repair.[^2][^1] By then a brick superstructure had been added to the tower, bringing its entire height to 25 feet (7.6 m).[^2][^1] The keeper, John Peak, complained that the dwelling was extremely leaky on the east side, and that in an easterly rain the family had to move their beds.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive lists two keepers for the life of the light, both Peaks: Samuel A. Peak from 1816 to 1824 and John A. Peak from 1824 to 1858.[^3] LighthouseFriends gives the first keeper's full name as Samuel Adams Peak, hired at a salary of $300 a year. He died in June 1824, aged forty-one, and his eighteen-year-old son John took over. By 1842 John was paid $350 a year.[^1]

John Peak and his wife, Martha, raised nine children at the light, and two of the sons became keepers.[^2][^1] In 1855 Peak counted 4,969 schooners, 1,455 sloops, 216 brigs and four steamboats passing the light.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that traffic justified a new aid to mark the offshore reef at Bishop and Clerks.[^1]

The archive also lists Benjamin Beall and Duncan M.B. Thaxter under "Contractor", with no years. No source in this story says what that work was.[^3]

## After the keepers

In its 1857 report the Light-House Board judged that the outlying light on Bishop and Clerks reef would meet the needs of navigation, and recommended that Point Gammon be discontinued: "Its discontinuance is therefore respectfully recommended."[^1] Earlier that year a fourth-order Fresnel lens had replaced the lamps and reflectors.[^1] The Bishop and Clerks light first shone on 1 October 1858.[^1] Point Gammon's service ended then, and John Peak moved across to become its first keeper.[^1][^2]

The archive gives 1855 as the year the light was discontinued.[^3] LighthouseFriends and New England Lighthouses give 1858, the year the Bishop and Clerks light first shone.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says a lightship was stationed offshore for a short time from 1855; neither secondary source explains the archive's 1855 date.[^1]

In 1865 the Board found the structures at the discontinued station "in a rapid course of demolition from lawless persons, fishermen and others," and deemed it prudent to have them taken down. Some of the materials went to other stations.[^1] The government, however, left the station mostly intact and sold it at public sale in 1872.[^1]

In 1882 the ornithologist Charles Barney Cory bought Great Island and used it as a game preserve.[^1][^2] At some point he added a taller structure to the top of the tower, designed to facilitate its use as a viewing platform. The old stone dwelling served as a museum for the butterfly collection that Cory and his wife amassed.[^1][^2] In 1914 Malcolm G. Chace, a banker from Rhode Island, bought the island.[^1][^2] The keeper's stone house was dismantled, in 1935 according to LighthouseFriends and in the 1930s according to New England Lighthouses, and its stones were used to build a new house on the island.[^1][^2] In the 1970s a member of the Chace family converted the old tower into a summer residence.[^1]

## Today

The stone tower still stands at the entrance to Hyannis Harbor.[^1] Great Island is privately owned, most of it by the Chace family, and the island and its lighthouse are off-limits to the public.[^1][^2] The family has given up development rights on 266 acres to the Trustees of Reservations.[^2] The tower can be seen from the Hyannis to Nantucket ferry and from excursion boats and fishing charters out of Hyannis.[^2]

The archive records the station as privately owned and not open to the public.[^3]
