Newburyport Harbor Light stands at the north end of Plum Island, where the Merrimack River meets Ipswich Bay.[^1] Two wooden lights went up at the island's north end in 1788.[^1][^8] The white tower there today dates from 1898 and may include parts of one of the originals.[^1][^2]

## The place

Plum Island is about nine miles long from north to south and a mile wide.[^1] The light stands at its northern end, at the mouth of the Merrimack. The lights marked the river's entrance, where the sands and the channel kept shifting.[^1] The nomination says the first towers "were moved quite often, 'sometimes considerable distances,' because of the shifting sands and water of the channel."[^1]

Before there were lights, fires were set on the beach at the north end of the island to guide mariners into the river. That ended in 1783, when the Marine Society of Newburyport put up two day beacons and employed men to display lanterns on them at night, LighthouseFriends says.[^8]

## Building the light

The Massachusetts Assembly approved two lights for the north end of the island on 16 November 1787, LighthouseFriends says. The merchants of Newburyport paid for the work. The lights were first lit on 14 April 1788.[^8]

The nomination dates the two wooden lights to 1788 and calls them the eleventh lighthouse built in the United States. LighthouseFriends calls them the thirteenth.[^1][^8] The two sources disagree on the count.

The nomination says the lights were ceded to the federal government in 1790.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that a year after aids to navigation came under federal control in 1789, President Washington appointed Abner Lowell as keeper.[^8]

By 1842 the towers were in poor condition. The inspector I. W. P. Lewis described two frame buildings, 36 feet (11 m) high, resting on logs and blocks of wood on the sand hills. He found both "dilapidated, decayed, leaky, and out of position" as to the bar channel, as LighthouseFriends quotes his report.[^8]

The nomination records new Fresnel lenses in 1856.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that year the rear tower received a fifth-order Fresnel lens, and that lightning struck the front range tower on 8 August 1856 and burned it. The front tower was not replaced. In 1871 a fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the fifth-order lens.[^8]

The nomination dates a move of the towers, 75 feet (23 m) south, to 1874, and records a move of one third of a mile northeast in 1869.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the towers were moved north from time to time, about 3,000 feet (914 m) in all.[^8] The sources point in different directions and do not reconcile them.

In 1887 the lard-oil lamps in the front light were replaced by mineral oil, and work began on a stone tower, the nomination says.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the switch to mineral oil to 1877.[^8] The channels shifted in 1890, and construction on the stone tower stopped. Jetties were built to keep the channel on one course.[^1] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 lists the Newburyport Harbor Range (front) light among the lights discontinued in September 1890.[^3] LighthouseFriends gives the day as 30 September.[^8]

The present white tower dates from 1898.[^1] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 records that the light moved to a new tower 18 feet (5.5 m) southeast on 20 September 1898.[^5] The nomination says the present light "is believed to incorporate elements of one of the original 1788 twin lights." It calls the tower conical in its chronology and cylindrical in its description, and gives its height as 50 feet (15 m). LighthouseFriends describes a 45-foot (14 m) wooden conical tower.[^1][^8]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists twenty principal keepers from 1790 to 1946.[^2] Abner Lowell (1790 to 1815) was the first. LighthouseFriends calls him the first of three generations of Lowells to keep the light.[^8] His son Lewis followed (1815 to 1823), and then Lewis's son Joseph (1823 to 1833).[^2][^8] Joseph returned for 1866 to 1870.[^2]

Lewis Lowell died on 12 December 1823, LighthouseFriends says. He had been overcome by fumes from a charcoal fire he lit under the lantern to keep the whale oil from thickening. His son Joseph took over his duties.[^8]

Phineas George kept the light from 1833 to 1849, and again from 1849 to 1857.[^2] When he retired in 1857, a notice quoted by LighthouseFriends said he had "entirely lost the use of his right arm" after over twenty winters of storms, watching for vessels in distress and trying to save life and property. LighthouseFriends says his wife's health had broken under the strain of helping him and raising their eleven children.[^8]

Franklin Carleton followed in 1857.[^2] Later keepers included Henry Hunt (1870 to 1882), Elliott C. Hadley (1896 to 1905), Arthur W. Woods (1905 to 1919), George E. Kezer (1924 to 1935) and Edgar M. Wallace (1945 to 1946).[^2]

A 1926 Lighthouse Service Bulletin reprinted the salary list from the report that Alexander Hamilton, as Secretary of the Treasury, made to President Washington on 18 June 1790. The Bulletin calls it the first official report on the lighthouses of this country.[^7] For Plum Island the list reads "Mr. Lowell, with ditto," at an annual salary of 220 dollars.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends says a whirlwind blew both towers over in 1808, and that Congress approved $10,000 in February 1809 to replace them.[^8]

In December 1839 a gale nearly washed one tower away. LighthouseFriends quotes Keeper Phineas George's account, as printed in Niles' National Register, of the tide flowing across the island, covering the meadows with sand and removing many sand hills.[^8]

On 22 December 1839 the Richmond Packet was wrecked. The captain's wife was swept away and drowned. George had left the light only for a few hours and could not get back, so the lights were left dark at the harbor's entrance, LighthouseFriends says.[^8]

The nomination counts more than one hundred shipwrecks near Plum Island from 1874 through 1908.[^1] In 1898 a telephone line was run to the Plum Island Life-Saving Station, LighthouseFriends says.[^8]

## After the keepers

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 records a brick oil house built at the station.[^4] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1896 records a fence built to enclose about two acres of land around the light-house buildings.[^10]

By 1917, the nomination says, the channel had shifted completely. Small range lights were added on the north side of the Merrimack River to aid navigators during the 1920s.[^1]

The 1922 Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists Newburyport Harbor among the lights changed from oil to electric incandescent.[^6] LighthouseFriends gives 1927 for the arrival of electricity, which replaced the kerosene in use since 1877. The two sources give different years.[^8]

The light was automated in 1951.[^2] That year the Coast Guard disposed of 363 by 265.5 feet (111 by 81 m) of land as excess, including the keeper's dwelling, and kept only the land immediately around the tower.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the dwelling later served as headquarters for the Parker River National Wildlife Refuge.[^8]

In 1981 an automatic plastic lens replaced the Fresnel lens in the tower, according to the nomination.[^1] The Friends' website says the Coast Guard still maintains the Fresnel lens, and the Lighthouse Society's record lists a fourth-order Fresnel as the current optic.[^9][^2]

The Friends of Plum Island Light say they were founded in 1994.[^9] LighthouseFriends gives 1995 for the group's formation.[^8] The Coast Guard leased the light to the Friends at a transfer ceremony on 15 June 1996.[^9] In 2003 the Coast Guard deeded the lighthouse and its property to the City of Newburyport.[^9][^8]

## Today

The City of Newburyport owns the lighthouse. The Friends of Plum Island Light look after it and run its tours.[^9] Volunteers give tours on weekends between Memorial Day and Columbus Day. The groups are limited to five people at a time, and tours are booked through an online calendar.[^9] People who climb the tower stairs must be at least five years old and 42 inches (107 cm) tall.[^9]

The grounds have an outdoor kiosk with photographs, maps, stories and a timeline. The lighthouse has no parking of its own, but there is a public parking lot nearby.[^9]
