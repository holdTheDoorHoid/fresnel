Sand Island Light stands on the northeastern tip of Sand Island, the westernmost of the Apostle Islands near the southwestern tip of Lake Superior.[^1][^4] The tower of finely cut sandstone was built in 1881 and set into the corner of a one-and-one-half-story keeper's house. The light was automated in 1921.[^1][^4]

## The place

The Apostle Islands lie off the northeast tip of the Bayfield peninsula. Their first light, at La Pointe, was established in 1852. The Michigan Island light (1857) and the Raspberry Island light (1862) followed, and the nomination calls both "local in character."[^1]

The Outer Island light (1874) and the Sand Island light (1881) were built in direct "response to the growing traffic at the west end of Lake Superior, an increase of activity centering around the bustling Duluth."[^1] Mariners claimed the Raspberry Island light was not clearly visible when approached from the west, and the call for a light on Sand Island echoed that complaint.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress funded the light in 1880, according to LighthouseFriends, and work began on 6 June 1880 under Louis Charles Lederle, who stayed on as keeper after supervising the construction.[^6] The plans resembled those used at the Chambers Island and Eagle Bluff lights. Unlike those brick buildings, the Sand Island dwelling and tower were built of sandstone.[^1] The tower is set into the northwest corner of the dwelling, and the carved rafter ends extend through the wall to support the eaves.[^1] In fiscal 1894 the Light-House Board recorded that "357 running feet of plank sidewalk" had been relaid at the station.[^2]

The light was a fourth-order Henry-Lepaute Fresnel lens, set atop a 42-foot (13 m) tower. It first showed a fixed white light on 25 September 1881.[^6] The Society's record gives 37 feet (11 m) from the base to the center of the lantern.[^7] The focal plane was 52 feet (16 m) above the lake, according to the nomination, while the Society's record gives 56 feet (17 m) above water.[^1][^7]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists Charles Lederle as principal keeper from 1881 to 1892, and Emmanuel Luick from 1892 to 1920.[^7] LighthouseFriends gives Lederle's term as ending in 1891 in its text, and in 1892 in its keeper list.[^6] The NPS says Luick kept the light until 1921, when he was transferred to the Grand Marais light in Minnesota after the station was automated. He retired there in 1936.[^4] The sources do not agree on those end dates.

The roster lists fourteen first assistants between 1902 and 1920, among them Emma H. Kahn, listed as first assistant in 1907.[^7] Ella G. Luick, Emmanuel's wife, is listed as acting assistant in 1903 and 1904.[^7][^4][^6]

Ella Richardson was 16 when she married Luick. The NPS dates the wedding to 1896, when he was 29. LighthouseFriends dates it to 1886, in Rhode Island, when he was 29.[^4][^6] Either way, she had a working role at the station. The NPS says she was "frequently left in charge of the station for days at a time" while her husband went to the mainland for supplies.[^4] LighthouseFriends says she kept the station log and served as his assistant from October 1903 to May 1905.[^6] The marriage did not last. The NPS quotes the keepers' log for 19 May 1905: "Mrs. Ella Luick left for Bayfield on the steamer Barker at 6PM." She never returned to the island.[^4]

Luick married again in 1911. In the NPS's account, his own words are: "In 1911, I married a girl from Iron River and took her to live at the lighthouse."[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A Bayfield dispatch dated 18 September 1885, printed in the Superior Times, says the steamer Prussia, bound for Duluth, "was burned ten miles northwest of the Sand island light house." It puts the fire "at 7 a. m. yesterday," which is 17 September. No lives were lost, the dispatch says, and "the crew were rescued by the light keeper."[^8] LighthouseFriends gives a different account. It dates the fire to 12 September, places it off the island, and says Lederle rowed out in the station's small boat and rescued the crew of ten men and one woman.[^6] The two sources disagree on the day and the place.

On 2 September 1905 the iron-ore carrier Sevona struck a reef off Sand Island and broke in two, according to LighthouseFriends.[^6] The Park Service quotes an unnamed account: "The storm that sunk the Sevona was one of the worst," and says the ship, trying to make York Island, was "grounded on the reef with seven lives lost."[^4] LighthouseFriends says Luick witnessed the end of the wreck but was unable to assist its crew. His own account for 2 September reads: "We could see no life on board or see any distress signal."[^6] According to LighthouseFriends, those on the aft half reached shore in two lifeboats, but the captain and six other men on the forward part had no boat, built a raft that was torn apart by the waves, and all drowned. The wreck's hatch covers were later used to build a cottage that still stands on the island.[^6]

Life on the island was not all storms. By 1918, according to the NPS, more than 100 people lived on Sand Island, most of them Norwegian immigrants who farmed and fished.[^4] Fred Hansen kept a diary from 1913. In July that year he wrote: "Went to Light on a picnic in morning."[^4] Burt Hill lived on the island from 1910 to 1941, and the island's post office closed on 15 May 1916.[^4]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says an acetylene gas apparatus began showing a white flash every ten seconds on 23 April 1921, making Sand Island the first light in the Apostle Islands to be automated.[^6] The Commissioner's annual report for fiscal 1921 lists Sand Island, Wisconsin, among the lights where illumination was improved that year.[^3]

LighthouseFriends says a 48-foot (15 m) steel skeletal tower was erected near the old light in 1933, and that the light was transferred to it.[^6] The National Register nomination describes an open steel skeleton tower within forty feet of the old lighthouse, containing an automated light.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1933 as the year the station was discontinued, and says it is not an active aid to navigation.[^7] LighthouseFriends adds that the Coast Guard removed the steel tower in 1985 and returned the light to the old lantern room.[^6] The sources do not say whether the light shows today.

## Today

The island is part of Apostle Islands National Lakeshore, managed by the National Park Service.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is best seen from the water, or reached by landing on the island.[^6] A two-mile trail runs from the East Bay campsites to the lighthouse, and a steel dock at East Bay is open for public use.[^4] The rock shelf near the lighthouse has mooring cleats, but submerged rocks make landing there hazardous, and the NPS asks for extreme caution.[^4]

Black bears live on the island, and the NPS asks campers and boaters to store food properly.[^4] Several cabins on the island's southeast corner are still privately occupied, and the NPS asks visitors to respect the privacy of the remaining summer residents.[^4]

LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse dwelling and tower are open in season, and that the lighthouse houses volunteers in summer to welcome visitors.[^6] The US Lighthouse Society's record marks the station as not open to the public. The Park Service's volunteer page offers three-week or summer "lightkeeper" posts that include lighthouse tours, but it does not name Sand Island.[^5] The Park Service pages do not say whether the tower can be entered, so check with the park before going.[^7][^4]
