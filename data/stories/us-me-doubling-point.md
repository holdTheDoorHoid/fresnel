Doubling Point Light stands on a granite base in the Kennebec River, off the west side of Arrowsic Island, in Sagadahoc County, about two miles below Bath, Maine.[^1][^3] It was lit in 1898, the northernmost of four lights the government set up on the river that year.[^1] Its tower is a wooden octagon on a granite pier, reached by a footbridge from the shore.[^1][^3]

## The place

The Kennebec was a busy river.[^3] In its report for fiscal 1892 the Lighthouse Board counted "3,137 arrivals" of vessels in the river that year, not counting the steamers that ran daily.[^4] Before the government built lights there, the steamboat and towboat companies hung lanterns on buoys at the turning points and difficult spots.[^3]

The Board's 1892 report asked for a light and fog bell at Doubling Point. It proposed "a red lens-lantern light, showing up and down the river, with a fog bell," and range lights to mark the channel.[^5]

## Building the light

Congress made the money available in 1895.[^6] The Board's report for that year notes "An appropriation of $17,000 was made by the act approved" on 2 March, for "a light, range lights, and fog signal at or near Doubling Point," with other lights on the lower river.[^6]

The land was bought in 1896 from a local owner, Samuel S. Freeman, and the Board's report for the year ended June 30, 1897 says that "Title was acquired to the land needed for a light-house site." The station was to be finished by February 1898.[^3][^7]

The first station was a white octagonal wooden tower, with a one-and-a-half-story L-shaped dwelling about 250 feet (76 m) away, and a pyramidal fog-bell tower about 44 feet (13 m) from the tower. LighthouseFriends says the light and fog bell went into operation on 30 March 1898.[^3] The National Register nomination says that "In 1898 the system was completed and put into operation."[^1]

The tower was moved in 1899.[^3] The Board's report for fiscal 1900 records that the light-station was "Moved 532 feet" (162 m) on 25 September 1899.[^8] The same report says that "A foundation pier of stone masonry was built on the extreme point at the low-water line," and that the tower with its light and fog bell was moved onto it.[^9] A footbridge 130 feet (40 m) long joined it to the shore, and the fog bell was mounted on the tower.[^3][^9] LighthouseFriends says the move reduced the light's height above the river from 33 feet (10 m) to 25 feet (7.6 m).[^3] The nomination says that the tower "was reoriented in 1899 but the original location is not known."[^1]

In 1901 the Board's report says that the $1,620 appropriated the year before "was applied in part to building a boathouse and boat slip," and that "The dwelling was moved nearer the tower."[^10] A fifth-order Fresnel lens replaced the original lantern in 1902.[^2][^3] The nomination says the brick oil house was "added to the complex in 1906."[^1]

## The keepers

Only two men were ever the keepers on site, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] Merritt P. Pinkham kept the light from 1898 to 1931 and had transferred there from Seguin Island Light.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends gives his start as 1889 in one place and 1898 in another, and the roster and the station's first year support 1898.[^2][^3] Charles W. Allen, who had served at Eagle Island Light, kept the station from 1931 to 1933.[^2][^3]

In 1933 the Lighthouse Service decided to run the light from the nearby Doubling Point Range light station, which was a separate station.[^3] The station, except for the tower, was sold in 1935 for $2,200.[^3]

The range lights were a separate station, at the lower end of Fiddlers Reach, and their keepers are not part of this story.[^13] The Lighthouse Service's bulletins name keepers of "the Doubling Point Range light station" in 1912 and in 1928, for rescues and assistance, so a reader who finds those names should not read them as this light's keepers.[^11][^12]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources cited here record no wreck or rescue at the light itself. The rescues and assistance the bulletins record belong to the range station.[^11][^12]

Ice was the light's enemy. LighthouseFriends says that every spring the ice floes came down the Kennebec and struck the foundation of the tower, whose granite blocks were held together by large metal staples. By 1999 the staples had rusted away, and the foundation had become so unstable that the tower might topple into the river.[^3]

## After the keepers

The fog bell was removed in 1980, and its location is unknown.[^3] A 300 mm modern optic replaced the Fresnel lens in the mid-1970s, and the lens went to the Maine Lighthouse Museum in Rockland.[^3] The light was automated in 1988.[^2][^3]

In 1998 the Friends of the Doubling Point Light, a nonprofit, were given stewardship of the light under the Maine Lights Program.[^3] In December 1999 the tower was lifted off its pier by crane, put on a barge, and towed to a yard in Woolwich, where the granite foundation was repaired and filled with concrete. The tower returned to the point on 5 January 2000.[^3]

In September 2023 the wooden walkway collapsed during Maine Open Lighthouse Day. Eleven visitors were injured, and five were taken to a hospital and treated and released.[^3]

## Today

The US Lighthouse Society lists the station as not open to the public, and its owner or manager as the Friends of Doubling Point Light.[^2] The light still works as an aid to navigation, showing a flashing white light every four seconds.[^3]

The dwelling is privately owned, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] Public cruises that pass the lighthouse run from Bath and from Boothbay Harbor.[^3] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 21 January 1988.[^1]
