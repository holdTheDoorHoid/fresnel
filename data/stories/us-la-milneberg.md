The brick tower of Port Pontchartrain light stands at Milneburg, in New Orleans, along the shore of Lake Pontchartrain.[^2] It was finished in 1855.[^1] From 1882 until the light was discontinued, its principal keepers were women: Ellen Wilson, then Margaret R. Norvell from 1896 to 1924, then Minnie E. Coteron.[^1][^2] The sources give the discontinuance as 1928 or 1929, and the tower still stands, on university land.[^1][^2]

## The place

Milneburg grew up as a resort on the lake.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that Alexander Milne, a New Orleans businessman, bought large tracts of land and founded the settlement on the lakeshore. A railroad carried visitors from the French Quarter, and a steam train nicknamed "Smokey Mary" took them down to the water.[^2] The port was also built at Milneburg; LighthouseFriends calls it the lake's first artificial harbor, and says it linked the town to health resorts on the lake's northern shore.[^2]

The first light there was not a government light.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that the railroad company put up a crude lantern, raised on two poles to fifty feet (15 m), to serve the port.[^2] The archive's record gives 1832 as the year the station was established.[^1] LighthouseFriends does not date the railroad's lantern, so it is not clear which light that date refers to.[^2]

## Building the light

In 1834 Congress allocated $5,000 for a light.[^2] The railroad company, LighthouseFriends reports, liked its own higher light better and proposed that the government take the light over.[^2] Three years later a larger sum was set aside, and an octagonal wooden tower, 28 feet (9 m) tall, was built with a revolving chandelier that gave a flashing light.[^2]

Benjamin J. Shane became the first keeper on 15 February 1839, according to LighthouseFriends, and the archive's roster begins with him.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends reports a family tradition that Shane died the next year after tripping over a bucket and falling down the lighthouse stairs.[^2] That account is not confirmed by the other sources read.

In 1854 Congress appropriated $6,000 to replace the old tower at the port, which was beyond repair.[^2] The new tower was brick. It was built about 2,100 feet (640 m) from shore on a concrete pad carried on pilings, and it was completed in 1855, LighthouseFriends reports, with a fifth-order Fresnel lens.[^2] The archive gives 1857 as the year the original lens was installed.[^1] The two sources differ on the lens date. In 1880 the top of the tower was widened and raised by seven feet (2 m) to take a new lantern room.[^2] The archive gives the tower's height as 36 feet (11 m) from base to lantern center and 47 feet (14 m) above mean high water.[^1]

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 noted repairs to the revolving machinery and said that the light was to be changed to fixed white.[^3]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists ten principal keepers from 1839 to 1928, and one first assistant, J. A. Fagot, from 1855.[^1] Charles Fagot kept the light from 1853 to 1872.[^1] The roster does not say how he and the assistant are related, and nothing read connects them.[^1]

Ellen Wilson kept the light from 1882 to 1896, the first of the three women principal keepers.[^1][^2]

Margaret R. Norvell kept it from 1896 to 1924.[^1] Her husband drowned in 1891 while keeping the Head of Passes light.[^2] LighthouseFriends says she was then appointed in his place, with two small children to support.[^2] The Lighthouse Service's bulletin of 1932 says she joined the service in April 1891 as an assistant keeper at Head of Passes, "after the death of her husband," and later served as keeper of Port Pontchartrain and New Canal, so the two accounts differ on her post.[^7] The bulletins call her Maggie R. Norvell; the roster and LighthouseFriends call her Margaret.[^1][^2][^4][^7] She retired in April 1932, after nearly 41 years' service in the eighth district.[^7]

Minnie E. Coteron kept the light from 1924 until its discontinuance.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that she had looked after the Bayou St. John light for eighteen years, and that her parents had kept it before her. She took charge here in 1924, after that light was automated.[^2] After the light was discontinued she stayed on in the dwelling as custodian of the property.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends reports that a hurricane in September 1869 carried away the station's kitchen, capsized its cistern and destroyed the plank walk to the railroad wharf. A new dwelling, kitchen and cistern were built in 1871.[^2]

LighthouseFriends also quotes a 1932 newspaper, the Morning Tribune of 26 June, as saying that in the storm of 1903 Mrs. Norvell's lighthouse was the only building left standing on the lower coast, and that more than 200 people found shelter in her home. That newspaper account is known here only through that quotation.[^2]

The bulletin for 1914 records that on September 16 Mrs. Norvell brought the station a man who had been taken suddenly ill aboard the steamer Hanover, and that she cared for him until he died. She was commended for her action.[^4]

On 29 September 1915 a hurricane reached New Orleans.[^5] The Lighthouse Service's bulletin says there was some flooding in the outskirts and lower sections of the city, from backwater from Lake Pontchartrain.[^5] Its list of damage to Service property includes Port Pontchartrain, along with West Rigolets, Pointe aux Herbes, Bayou St. John and New Canal.[^6]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service's bulletin says Mrs. Norvell kept the New Canal light after she left Port Pontchartrain, and that she was retired from it in April 1932.[^7] Minnie Coteron held the Port Pontchartrain light until its discontinuance, which LighthouseFriends dates to 10 May 1928.[^2] The archive gives 1929.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says little remains of the resort town except the lighthouse.[^2] Lakefront reclamation in the 1930s, LighthouseFriends reports, destroyed much of Milneburg as the shoreline was reinforced; the lighthouse survived, and when the Pontchartrain Beach amusement park was developed in 1939 it served for a time as office space.[^2] That park, considered the South's largest thrill park in the 1970s, closed in 1983 after other parks overtook it.[^2] The land was idle until 1991, when the University of New Orleans bought it from the Orleans Levee District. It is now part of the university's Research and Technology Park.[^2]

## Today

The University of New Orleans owns the lighthouse, and LighthouseFriends says it sits on the grounds of the research park. The grounds are open; the tower is closed.[^2] The archive, however, lists the station as not open to the public, and it gives no current optic and no active navigation role.[^1] The tower should not be entered.
