Red Bay Light Station stands on Saddle Island, just offshore from the entrance to Red Bay, on the Labrador side of the Strait of Belle Isle. It was first lit in 1906 as a harbour light, and its present steel-frame tower dates from 1951.[^1][^2]

## The place

Red Bay is a fishing community on the southern Labrador coast. Saddle Island lies just off the harbour entrance and gives the harbour its shelter.[^1]

The harbour's earlier history is whaling. In the mid-sixteenth century, large numbers of right and bowhead whales drew whalers from the Basque region of Spain and France to the Strait of Belle Isle, where they set up a major whaling port at Red Bay.[^5] Parks Canada counts about seventy years of Basque whaling in the strait, and describes the month-long Atlantic crossing the whalers made.[^5]

After the whales were gone, LighthouseFriends says, Red Bay had a fur-trading post in the eighteenth century.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1906 a fixed white light was set up at the western end of Saddle Island, in a lens lantern on an open framework tower.[^1][^2] It served as a harbour light during open navigation, which typically ran from June through December.[^1]

In 1912 a flat-roofed keeper's dwelling and an octagonal wooden tower went up on the island.[^1][^2] The light became a sixth-order light, with 2.5-second alternating periods of light and darkness, and LighthouseFriends gives its focal plane as 41.5 m.[^1] In 1931 the fuel changed from kerosene to acetylene gas, and the light became flashing white.[^1]

Today the Coast Guard gives the focal height as 36.0 m (118 ft), and Rowlett gives the same figure.[^4][^2] That differs from the 41.5 m that LighthouseFriends gives for 1912, and neither source explains the difference.[^1]

A fog alarm was added in 1936, in a white wooden building with a flat roof.[^1] In 1951 the octagonal tower was replaced by a steel-frame tower with a white daymark.[^1] A new fog-alarm building and a keeper's dwelling were completed in 1961, and a second dwelling followed in 1964.[^1]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends names J. Yetman as keeper from 1906 to at least 1912, and Enos Yetman from 1949 to 1971. No keeper is named for the years between.[^1] Enos Yetman lived on Saddle Island for many years.[^1]

## After the keepers

The keepers' era ended in stages. In 1967 the light was switched from batteries to electricity generated on the station. Commercial power reached the island in 1971, and at that time an electronic fog signal was installed and the station became fully automated.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard's list gives the light as a white flash every five seconds, from a square skeleton tower 6.7 m (22 ft) high. Rowlett gives the tower as 7 m (23 ft).[^4][^2] The nominal range is 15 nautical miles, and the list marks the light as seasonal.[^4] LighthouseFriends says a foghorn sounds a three-second blast every thirty seconds when needed.[^1]

Coast Guard figures from April 2010, printed in the 2011 Senate of Canada report, list one position at Red Bay with a single keeper. Red Bay is not counted among the remote sites.[^3]

Ownership is recorded two ways. LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard owns the lighthouse. Rowlett names Parks Canada as site manager, and Parks Canada describes Red Bay as a national historic site and a World Heritage Site.[^1][^2][^5]

Saddle Island is part of Red Bay National Historic Site. A passenger ferry runs to the island from the town in summer, June through September.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends notes that the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] The light is a working station, so do not climb the tower or go onto the station buildings without permission. The drive from the Blanc-Sablon ferry landing to Red Bay is about 80 km on Route 510.[^1]
