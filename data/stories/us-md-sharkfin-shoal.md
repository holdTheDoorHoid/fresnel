Sharkfin Shoal Light was a six-sided wooden house on a screw-pile foundation in Tangier Sound, Maryland. It was first lit on 1 August 1892. In 1964 the house was taken down, and an automatic light went up on a skeletal tower in its place.[^1][^2]

## The place

The light was built to replace Clay Island light, whose site was being eaten away. LighthouseFriends quotes the Board's 1887 report: "The site of Clay Island light-house is being rapidly washed away".[^2] The Board recommended a screw-pile light and fog signal on Shark Fin Shoal instead, at an estimated $25,000.[^2] Congress appropriated that sum in 1889, and borings were made at the shoal to decide what kind of structure it would need.[^2][^5]

The site took longer to settle than the appropriation. In December 1891 the Governor of Maryland signed an application for the United States to take jurisdiction over the site, which the Annapolis paper located "in Dorchester county, Maryland". The paper placed the shoal between Bloodsworth Island and the north end of Tangier Sound.[^6] The Board's report for the year to June 1892 says the erection was held up while borings showed whether the light could go farther south, where it "might give more aid to mariners".[^3]

## Building the light

Proposals for the metal-work of this light and of one at Greenbury Point went out in January 1890, and the contract was signed in February. The wooden superstructure was begun in April 1890 at the Lazaretto depot in Baltimore.[^2][^5]

On 3 June 1892 the tenders towed the superstructure, the working party and the plant to the site. The screw piles were set, and by the end of June the superstructure was nearly finished.[^2] The light was first exhibited on 1 August 1892.[^2][^5] The house had a fourth-order Fresnel lens, installed that year, and stood 44 feet (13.4 m) above mean high water.[^1]

The Annapolis paper reported on 27 July 1892 that Thomas T. Cole had been transferred from Clay Island "to the Shark fin Shoal, Md.", where he "has been appointed keeper of the station."[^7]

A 1926 obituary in the *Lighthouse Service Bulletin* says that William H. Davis entered the service in 1881 as a carpenter in the fifth district's field working party, and that he assisted in building Sharkfin Shoal. He was keeper of the Lazaretto depot from 1900 until he retired in 1925, and he died in March 1926.[^10]

## The keepers

The archive lists 20 stints at Sharkfin Shoal, from 1892 to 1950: seven principal keepers, twelve first assistants and one Coast Guard entry.[^1]

Thomas T. Cole was the first principal keeper, listed from 1892 to 1903.[^1][^7] A Snow Hill paper of February 1893 calls the keeper "Capt. Cole" (see the next chapter).[^8]

Walter S. Brown was first assistant from 1892 to 1900.[^1] Daniel A. White was first assistant from 1900 to 1901.[^1] An Annapolis obituary of 1922 names Daniel Asbury White as a former "lighthouse keeper at Shark's Fin", and as having also kept the lights at Great Shoal and Thomas' Point. It describes those posts as earlier than his seven years as a watchman at the Naval Academy, which began after he came to Annapolis in 1901.[^11] The archive places him at both stations as first assistant.

Henry C. Sterling was first assistant from 1901 to 1902 and principal keeper from 1903 to 1904.[^1] William R. Schoenfelder was principal keeper for one year, 1904.[^1]

Walter C. Carew was first assistant from 1902 to 1904 and principal keeper from 1904 to 1920, according to the archive.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives his principal's stint as running to 1933.[^2] Carew is the keeper who rescued two men in February 1918 (see below). The Chesapeake Chapter's timeline says he was awarded the efficiency gold star in 1915 and the commissioners' efficiency star in 1918.[^5]

Henry T. McGrath was first assistant from 1914 to 1918.[^1] Ulman Owens was first assistant from 1918 to 1924, and Bertie E. D. Ford from 1924 to 1936.[^1] John D. Elliott was principal keeper from 1933 to 1944, and William J. Kelly from 1944 to 1945.[^1] Raymond C. Peters was first assistant from 1937 to 1950.[^1] Gordy Z. Parks was principal keeper from 1945 to 1950.[^1] The archive also lists Parks and Peters at Great Shoal, the Maryland light on the Wicomico River, in 1950 and 1951.[^12]

The Board's report for the fiscal year 1899 records new model fourth-order lamps and new roofing material for the house.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In February 1893 the Snow Hill paper reported that no light had been shown from the Shark Fin lighthouse for over a week. Navigation was blocked at the time, the paper said. The people of the Straits district were led to believe that Capt. Cole, the keeper, was either very sick or had perished from cold and hunger. The paper added that Cole "has in all probability a full store of food and fuel."[^8]

From 2 to 8 February 1918 Carew rescued two men in a small skiff. The men had left their vessel to get fuel and, caught in drift ice, could not get back to it. He took them to the station and cared for them there until the 8th.[^2][^5] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index lists "Carew, W.C., keeper Sharkfin Shoal, rescue 20".[^9]

## After the keepers

The archive's last principal keeper, Gordy Z. Parks, is listed to 1950. LighthouseFriends says the light was automated in 1950.[^1][^2]

In 1964 the house came down. The Society's record says the old foundation beams were replaced with steel, and a skeletal tower was erected on a battery house on the foundation.[^1] The Chesapeake timeline says an automatic light on a skeletal tower was mounted on the remaining screw-pile foundation.[^5] The Society's record gives 1964 as the year the light was discontinued.[^1]

LighthouseFriends describes how the Chesapeake screw-pile lights were usually converted. The wooden house came off, steel replaced the foundation beams where needed, a concrete platform was poured, a small battery house was built on it, and a metal skeletal tower went on top.[^2]

## Today

The light is not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a modern light is shown from a tower atop the screw-pile foundation.[^2] The Society's record lists the station as not existing and not an active aid to navigation.[^1] The two sources disagree about the present light, and this story does not settle which is right.
