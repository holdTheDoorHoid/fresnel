Seul Choix Point reaches into the northern end of Lake Michigan from the Upper Peninsula, and the brick light with the keepers' house joined to it by a covered passage has stood there since 1895. The name is French for "only choice", and it is pronounced "Sis-shwa". The usual story is that French sailors found the bay behind the point the only shelter along this stretch of shore, though a Jesuit missionary of the early 1900s wrote that local people called the place by an Ojibwa-derived name instead.[^1]

## The place

By 1850 the bay at Seul Choix was, by Lighthouse Friends' account, the most important fishing station on the north shore of Lake Michigan: about 500 men came each summer, though few people lived there all year.[^1] Later the Lighthouse Board looked at the coast as a shipping route. In its 1885 report, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, the Board said that vessels from Chicago and Milwaukee followed this shore in northwest winds, that Seul Choix was a good harbor of refuge with anchorage in three and a half to four fathoms, and that no light stood on the northern shore for about 100 miles between Poverty Island and St. Helena. The growing iron-ore trade out of Escanaba made the route more important, and the Board asked for $15,000 for a light here.[^1] Terry Pepper's account adds that an earlier proposal for a light at Point Patterson, farther east, had failed in the House in 1882 before the Board settled on Seul Choix.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated the money on 4 August 1886.[^1][^2] The land belonged to Archibald Newton, a fisherman from St. Helena Island, and title did not pass to the United States until 17 June 1889 because of inconsistencies in the old deeds, according to Pepper; Lighthouse Friends says only that title came in early 1889.[^2][^1] Bids were then invited twice, and each time the lowest was above the $15,000. In 1891 the Board decided to buy the materials itself and use hired labor.[^1]

Work began on 31 August 1891 and went on until 16 November, in an autumn the lighthouse engineer described as exceptionally stormy, with much time lost.[^3] His report of 17 March 1892 said that the stone found on the spot was "totally unfit" for the foundations, so concrete had to be used instead, and that the permanent tower stood only 20 feet (6 m) high. To have a light ready for the spring of 1892, a temporary tower was built.[^3] Pepper describes it as an open timber frame with a fourth-order lens, put up for about $850; the engineer's own report, in a scan that is hard to read, seems to give about $890 for the temporary tower and its tramway.[^2][^3] A temporary fourth-order light was shown from it on 15 April 1892.[^1]

The money then ran short. Part of the shortfall came from charging the camp outfit and tools, which would be used at other stations, to the Seul Choix appropriation, a point the engineer made himself.[^3] Pepper adds that the unfinished work sat decaying for two years and that the depression of the 1890s made Congress slow to act.[^2] On 18 August 1894 Congress made $5,000 meant for moving the St. Marys River upper range lights available for completing the structures here, and set aside $2,200 to begin a fog signal.[^4]

The work resumed in spring 1895. Bids for materials were opened on 9 March, and the tender *Amaranth* carried the working party and materials to the point. By the Board's report, the dwelling was largely replastered, the oil house finished, and the tower raised from 20 feet 8 inches to 56 feet 4 inches (17.2 m).[^4] Lighthouse Friends gives the finished lighthouse as 78 feet 9 inches (24 m) from base to ventilator ball, of stone and brick with a metal lantern, holding a third-order Fresnel lens made by Henry-Lepaute.[^1] The new tower, 98 feet (30 m) from the old one, showed its light on 15 August 1895.[^5] The Board's text for the light's change from fourth to third order gives the date as 16 August 1896; the same report's table of changes gives 15 August 1895, and the two do not agree.[^5] The steam whistle was first sounded on 10 September 1895.[^1][^5]

## The keepers

The first keeper was Joseph Fountain, listed by the Lighthouse Society's archive for 1892 to 1901.[^7] Lighthouse Friends says he had been keeper at Beaver Island Harbor, was a Native American, and reportedly preferred to live in an old hutch rather than the new brick dwelling.[^1] Assistants Eugene V. Kimball and Patrick J. McCauley arrived with the permanent light in 1895.[^7][^1]

The longest-serving keeper was William W. Blanchard, who came as second assistant in 1902 at $425 a year, was promoted to first assistant in 1904, and was head keeper from 1910 until 1941.[^1][^7] He and his wife Amanda had two sons at the station, Charles and Calvin, and Charles's wife Frances Seaman later recalled the dwelling: spotless gray walls, an oriental rug in the parlor, a wood-burning stove, and a habit of leaving shoes in a box by the door.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1923 lists Blanchard among the keepers awarded the service's efficiency flag.[^10][^13] Lighthouse Friends says his two long-serving assistants, William Hanson and William Davis, shared his first name and were known as "The Three Bills".[^1] The birdhouse built as a copy of the lighthouse, still on the grounds, was made by Hanson's son Willard when he was about sixteen.[^11]

Joseph W. Townshend was head keeper from 1901 to 1910, according to the archive.[^7] Lighthouse Friends says he died in the dwelling in 1910.[^1] After Blanchard came Hanson (1941 to 1945), Ronald W. Rosie Sr. (1945 to 1963), William A. Goudreau (1963 to 1967) and Russell H. Korby (1967 to 1972).[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On a Tuesday night in September 1894, while the station still showed only its temporary light, the schooner *William Home* was off Seul Choix with 579 tons of pig iron, in tow of the steamer *F. R. Buell*. At about 10:30 the deck load shifted, the old timbers opened, and the crew cut the tow line and set sail for shore. The New-York Tribune reported that six of the seven aboard drowned. Anton Minga of Detroit held on to the overturned yawl's keel, was blown onto the beach near the lighthouse, and was found unconscious the next afternoon by the lighthouse keeper, and it was only "with great difficulty" that he was brought back to consciousness.[^6] The newspaper does not name the keeper; Lighthouse Friends identifies him as Fountain.[^1]

The fog signal's work is recorded in the Board's reports: in the report covering 1896 and 1897 the ten-inch whistle sounded about 202 hours and burned about 24 tons of coal, and in the report for fiscal 1900 about 139 hours and 15 tons.[^5][^9] In the winter of 1898 to 1899 the light was among those kept burning, as a Milwaukee-area weekly noted, while in the winter of 1900 the Board reported that the station was closed on 5 February 1900.[^8][^9]

## After the keepers

Pepper says electricity reached the station in 1925, when a diaphone replaced the steam whistle, and that a wing was added to the dwelling for the second assistant. The old barn, which had been made into a dwelling in 1907, was sold and moved to a lake.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives 6 November 1925 for the diaphone and electric lighting and says the barn dwelling was returned to the site in 2006.[^1] The Coast Guard took over the Lighthouse Service in 1939.[^2] The archive lists Coast Guard crews from 1950 until 1972, the year the station was automated with an aero beacon.[^7][^2] The Michigan Department of Natural Resources bought the station on 20 June 1977, and it stood largely unused for ten years.[^2]

## Today

The Gulliver Historical Society was formed on 8 October 1987 to restore the station, and the museum opened to the public on 6 August 1989.[^2] The National Register of Historic Places lists the station under reference 84001846, published on 19 July 1984; Pepper's page gives August 1988, and the two do not agree.[^12][^2] The dwelling was restored and furnished for the centennial in 1995.[^2] Lighthouse Friends reports that the third-order lens left the tower in 1972 and is now thought, according to a sign in the dwelling, to be in a private collection, and that an LED beacon replaced the aero beacon in 2013.[^1]

The lighthouse is owned by the Michigan Department of Natural Resources and run by the Gulliver Historical Society.[^1] The society says the museum is open daily from Memorial Day to mid-October, 10 a.m. to 6 p.m., subject to volunteer guides, with tower tours and keepers' quarters furnished as in 1900 to 1930. A fog-signal-building museum holds local exhibits.[^11] The point is on a gravel road from Gulliver; check the society's hours before going.[^1][^2]
