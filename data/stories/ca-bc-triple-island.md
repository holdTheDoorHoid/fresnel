Triple Island Lightstation is a reinforced-concrete lightstation built on the northwest rock of a three-islet group in Brown Passage, west of Prince Rupert on the north coast of British Columbia. It is still staffed, and its keepers arrive by helicopter.[^1]

## The place

Brown Passage is the channel through which ships from the open Pacific reached Prince Rupert without first going round by Dixon Entrance. In 1913 a concrete-based acetylene beacon was placed on the Triple Islands, which are white rocks about 12 m (40 ft) high.[^1] The Department of Marine and Fisheries' own report for the year ending March 1914 records its steamer *Estevan* sailing north in the spring of 1913 to establish acetylene beacons at "Askew and Triple islands".[^2]

The reason for something larger came on 18 August 1914, when the Grand Trunk Pacific steamer *Prince Albert* ran aground in dense fog on Butterworth Rocks, three miles southwest of the islands. Passengers and crew took to the lifeboats and were picked up by the *Prince John*; during the transfer a baby fell overboard and drowned.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says this wreck convinced the Department that a lighthouse and fog alarm were needed in the passage, and that its chief engineer, Colonel William P. Anderson, visited the following April and chose the Triple Islands.[^1] (The heritage record for the national historic site calls the designer Col. W. A. Anderson.[^6])

## Building the light

The contract, worth $33,500, went to J. H. Hilditch of Prince Rupert, and work began in August 1919.[^1] The Commissioner of Lights recorded that the "combined light and fog-alarm station at Triple island, Hecate straits, was begun in August, 1919, but had to be abandoned before completion, owing to inclement weather in the latter part of November."[^3] The crew lived on a neighbouring rock, linked to the site by a bridge, and blasted the top off the building rock as the tide allowed before filling forms with concrete.[^1]

The federal heritage statements stress how hard the job was. One says that thousands of dollars' worth of material was lost to the conditions on the exposed rock.[^4] The plaque set up by the Historic Sites and Monuments Board puts it this way: "Prevailing westerly gales and high flood tides in the spring and fall made construction of a lighthouse here one of the most hazardous tasks in Canadian maritime history."[^5] The same text says the work was finished on 23 December 1920 "without a single loss of life", and the light was first shown on 1 January 1921.[^5] A different heritage record counts the whole job as seventeen months.[^6]

What Hilditch built was a custom design by the Department's Colonel Anderson: an octagonal reinforced-concrete tower, 23 m (75 ft) according to the plaque, attached to a main building holding the fog alarm and the keepers' quarters.[^4][^5] The basement and first floor held the engines, compressors and air tanks for the fog alarm; the top floor held three bedrooms along the south wall and a kitchen, pantry and storeroom on the north.[^1] The heritage character statement notes that the tower takes up almost the whole islet.[^7]

The Department's reports show the station still being fitted out after the tower was finished: a new aerial tramway, boathouse and engine house in the report published in 1923,[^8] and a re-glazed lantern with storm windows in the next one.[^9] The light was a third-order Fresnel lens showing a double flash, with a diaphone fog alarm giving a four-second blast every thirty seconds.[^1]

## The keepers

Thomas Watkins moved up from Sisters Islets with his wife and two daughters to be the first head keeper. After two years he caught pneumonia and died on 31 January 1923. Unable to attract the attention of any passing vessel, Mrs. Watkins and her daughters kept the light and fog alarm going for twenty days before the lighthouse tender called.[^1] Lighthouse Friends adds that they kept his body on the roof in the freezing weather in the meantime.[^1]

In 1927 Tom Moran came as assistant keeper with his wife Sophie and a baby son. On 22 November 1929 Sophie's clothing was caught by a spinning flywheel on the lower floor and she was badly hurt. Moran nursed her for about a week until the tender *Alberni* took her to Prince Rupert; then, in the station's boat, he was blown north by the gale to Green Island, where his father was keeper. Sophie recovered and came back to the island. The compensation claim Tom filed was refused because she was not a government employee.[^1]

Gordon Odlum, who had grown up in Vancouver and became a keeper after visiting Point Atkinson, arrived in November 1942. He married Jean in Vancouver on 20 September 1944 after a courtship by mail begun with a loaf of bread sent to a bakery, and brought her to the island. The couple stayed until 1952.[^1]

Ed and Eileen Hartt came from Lawyer Island in 1954. When the clockwork that turned the lens began to bind because the pulleys its weight cable ran through were worn, Ed, Eileen and the assistant keeper, Don, spent hours that night turning it by hand; Eileen's combined washing machine and spin dryer, which she recalled lurching about the room "like a dog on a leash", had been sacrificed by morning to build an electric drive for the lens.[^1] The Hartts were the last family at the station. After they left in 1957, four keepers worked rotating month-long shifts.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In March 1959 the assistant keeper Remite Erneste Vargas died on the island. The head keeper had told the Department of Transport on 28 February that Vargas was seriously ill; after a further call on 3 March a tender was sent, but rough seas kept it from landing anyone at the lighthouse for four days. At the inquest in Prince Rupert the coroner agreed with the jury that stormy weather had prevented help from reaching the station, but added that he felt somebody had "slipped up" in how quickly a response was prepared.[^1]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the third-order lens was deactivated in the 1990s, after mercury was declared hazardous, and that a modern beacon placed outside the lantern room now gives the double flash. After a few attempts to de-staff the station, it still has resident keepers.[^1]

## Today

Triple Island is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard. Its light, on the northwest rock of the islands, is listed as Fl(2) W, a white octagonal tower with a red top, 28 m above the water and visible 16 nautical miles.[^10] Keepers change over every twenty-eight days by helicopter, and the station is a pilot boarding station for large foreign ships entering British Columbian waters.[^1] It is a national historic site (1974) and a recognized federal heritage building,[^6][^7] and locally it is known as "The Rock" and "Little Alcatraz".[^4]

The grounds and tower are closed to the public; the lighthouse is best seen from a boat or from the air on the Prince Rupert to Masset flight.[^1] In April 2025 a lightkeeper died while on duty there during a shift change, when only one of the two keepers was on the island; the Coast Guard and Employment and Social Development Canada investigated.[^11]
