Green Bay Harbor Entrance Light stands on a round concrete pier in lower Green Bay, about 3.1 miles northwest of Point Comfort in Brown County, Wisconsin.[^1] It was built in 1934 and 1935 to mark the western side of the channel into the port of Green Bay, and it is still reached by boat.[^1]

## The place

The shallows at the south end of lower Green Bay were the problem. The nomination says that as vessels grew, the shallows became "a problematic obstacle to navigation" for the larger ships that came to the port.[^1] A shipping channel was dredged through the shallows in the late nineteenth century, and it reached "its present length by the early 1930s."[^1]

The Lighthouse Board had looked at the bay earlier. In 1865 it sent a group to Green Bay, and the group identified three places where lights were most needed: Grassy Island, Peshtigo Reef and Chambers Island.[^1] Congress appropriated $25,000 in 1866 for light stations at those three places. Chambers Island Light was built, but the appropriation was withdrawn in 1867 before the others could be built.[^1] Peshtigo Reef, one of the unbuilt sites, got its light in 1936.[^1]

## Building the light

The Bureau of Lighthouses set out in the 1920s and 1930s to replace the Great Lakes light vessels with offshore lights. "Two locations selected for building lighthouses were the entry channel leading to Green Bay Harbor and Peshtigo Reef."[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1936 listed the new stations, and it described Green Bay's as "marking the entrance to the dredged channel at Green Bay where no major aid has been heretofore maintained."[^3]

The first work was "building a wooden crib to serve as the structure's foundation," in 1934.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the pier was towed out into the bay and sunk in position on the afternoon and evening of 5 June 1935. The next morning a self-unloading ship dumped 5,000 tons of crushed stone from Port Inland, Michigan around the crib.[^2] The nomination says construction was completed in 1935 and that the station was then formally established as an aid to navigation.[^1]

The lantern held a fourth-order Fresnel lens. The nomination says it showed "a red light for 4 seconds followed by 2 seconds of darkness," with a 5,000-candlepower lamp.[^1] LighthouseFriends instead describes a steady red light of 6,000 candlepower, so the two accounts disagree about whether the light showed a timed pattern.[^2] The fog signal was an air-powered diaphragm horn that gave two seconds of sound and then eighteen seconds of silence.[^1] A radiobeacon sent the letter K, and a distance-finding station gave a combined sound and radio signal in reduced visibility.[^1] The nomination also notes that the pier is wrapped in steel plates from about five feet (1.5 m) below the water to five feet (1.5 m) above it, which "provides protection from ice damage."[^1]

Green Bay's light was designed to be kept by men on the spot. "Green Bay Harbor Entrance Light was designed to be manned by keepers while Peshtigo Reef Light was designed for remotely-controlled semi-automatic operation without resident keepers."[^1]

## The keepers

The archive's roster names twelve principal keepers between 1936 and 1974, among them Carl Witzmann, the first, who is listed for "Carl Witzmann (1936-1939)".[^4] Theodore Grosskopf followed for "Theodore Grosskopf (1939-1945)".[^4] Two assistant keepers appear for the first years: "Andrew P. Weber (1935-at least 1941)" and "Gregory J. Navarre (1934-1940)".[^4]

After 1939 the work changed hands. The nomination says the light was "manned during the navigation season that extended from April to November." In 1939 the Bureau of Lighthouses merged with the Coast Guard, after which "Coast Guard personnel who served 2-week stints on a rotating basis during the shipping season" took over.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes a couple of Coast Guardsmen who lived in the round part of the tower and alternated two weeks on and two weeks off.[^2] The archive still uses the title "Principal Lighthouse Keeper" for its names after 1939, and LighthouseFriends names Grosskopf as the head keeper in 1943.[^2][^4] The sources do not agree on how the station was staffed, and this account does not resolve it.

LighthouseFriends also reports a visit in the summer of 1959 by a writer for the Appleton Post-Crescent, who found three men on the station. One of them, it says, had brought a lawn mower aboard to repair in his spare time.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends reports an episode in December 1943. Grosskopf and three men were left on the station when bad weather kept them from being taken off.[^2] By the Sunday, their food had run out. A plane dropped supplies on the Tuesday from more than fifty feet up.[^2] The next day John Paetschow, keeper of the Manitowoc Breakwater Lighthouse, led three Coast Guardsmen from Two Rivers over ice in a skiff to bring the men off.[^2] No newspaper or report is cited for the episode, so the account rests on LighthouseFriends alone.

Winter brought a different routine. When the station was unmanned, "the lighthouse displayed an automated light that signaled a red flash for one second followed by 9 seconds of darkness."[^1]

## After the keepers

The nomination says that "Advances in vessel navigation technology" led to the distance-finding station being discontinued in the 1950s, and that shipboard radar had made it superfluous.[^1] The radiobeacon was discontinued in 1988, "although the steel skeletal antenna tower atop the lantern remains."[^1]

The nomination dates automation to 1980.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives 1979.[^2]

The submarine telephone cable to the eastern shore "was subsequently removed," and a new power cable was laid to the western shore, clear of the channel.[^1] The original fog signal was replaced "with modern automated equipment."[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light.[^1][^2] "This property is accessible by boat," the nomination says, and it "requires only periodic visits for maintenance by personnel from the U.S. Coast Guard."[^1]

Both sources describe a red light. The nomination gives the light as red, and LighthouseFriends describes an occulting red light on a four-second cycle.[^1][^2]
