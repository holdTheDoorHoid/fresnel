Sturgeon Bay Canal North Pierhead Light stands at the lake end of the Sturgeon Bay Ship Canal, on the north pier, in Door County, Wisconsin.[^4][^5] It is a pier light rather than a shore tower: a 39-foot (12 m) tower on a fog-signal house at the end of the north pier, placed there to guide ships into a canal that let them cut across the Door Peninsula.[^4][^5][^6]

## The place

A private company began digging the canal in July 1872. Some ships used it from 1880, but it was not open to large vessels until 1882.[^5] Before it, vessels bound for Green Bay had to go around the peninsula. LighthouseFriends calls that route dangerous, and the canal avoided it.[^5]

The canal's owners and mariners wanted a light and a breakwater at the eastern end, but the government was reluctant to help a private venture. Petitions from local businessmen, and a favorable inspection by Commodore Alexander Murray, led the Lighthouse Board to recommend a lighthouse at the lake entrance in 1873.[^5]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 describes the light plainly: a red flasher of the sixth order, which the Board judged too weak and too low for the place.[^1]

## Building the light

Charles Dobson, who had just finished the Dunlap Reef range lights in Sturgeon Bay, supervised the work. Material came by barge in October 1881, and the work was finished in late November.[^5] The first tower was wooden. A wooden walkway ran from it to shore, so that the keeper could reach the light in bad weather.[^5]

The lantern room arrived that year but was not installed until March 1882.[^5] Rufus J. Wright, the first keeper, lit a sixth-order Fresnel lens for the first time on 15 May 1882.[^4][^5] LighthouseFriends prints his name as Rufus M. Wright.[^5]

Dense fog banks often shrouded the canal entrance. In 1884 a fog signal went up on the pier just shoreward of the light, in two buildings that housed the boilers for a steam whistle.[^5]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 gives the canal's 10-inch steam whistles about 381 hours in operation, using about 38 tons of coal and 21 cords of wood.[^2] The report for the year before gives 355 hours and 36 tons of coal.[^7] LighthouseFriends gives a much higher running time, "typically in operation around 540 hours a year," with about fifty tons of coal, and gives no year.[^5] The sources do not agree on how much the whistle ran.

On 10 March 1892 the pier under the firebox of fog signal No. 1 caught fire. The Board's report says the pier under No. 2 caught fire on the 23rd, "disabling both fog signals."[^5][^1]

In 1900 a fifth-order Fresnel lens replaced the original sixth-order lens.[^5] By 1902 the light and fog signal needed major repairs. A crew moved the light to the outer end of the pier in May 1903, and the new combined light and fog signal was tested in early September 1903.[^5]

The sources disagree on what the 1903 tower is made of. The Archive and the FOX 11 report describe cast iron. LighthouseFriends describes riveted steel plate.[^4][^6][^5] The Archive lists the current optic as a 300 mm lens and does not give its type.[^4]

## The keepers

The Archive's roster lists Rufus J. Wright as principal keeper from 1882 to 1886. In June 1886 the Lighthouse Board began a wooden, two-story keeper's house on land the canal company had transferred. LighthouseFriends says Wright was relieved of duty just days before that house was finished, and that no official reason for his removal was ever recorded.[^5][^4]

Charles O. Chapman came to the station in 1884 as first assistant, when the fog whistle added to the work.[^5][^4] The Archive has him as principal keeper from 1886 to 1899.[^4] LighthouseFriends carries him on to 1910. It says that in 1909 a local doctor diagnosed him with stomach cancer, and that he died at the station the following spring, after twenty-six years there.[^5]

His son Claude was assigned to the station as third assistant shortly before Christmas in 1906.[^5]

Conrad A. Stram was head keeper from 1927 to 1933. On 12 June 1928 a fog bank lay about four miles off. Stram wrote of "a whistle, which I figured was a car ferry," and the ferry stayed out in the fog off the canal until about 3 p.m.[^5] LighthouseFriends uses his report to show that a fog signal alone was not always enough to guide a vessel to the canal. Stram told the Coast Guard on duty to have the car ferry office at Menominee radio the captain to run two miles north and come in.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 27 October 1888 the schooner Dreadnaught, of Milwaukee, was trying to enter the canal when she became unmanageable and struck the end of the north pier. The life-saving crew of the Sturgeon Bay Canal station (the report says only "of that name") launched its surf-boat and went to her assistance.[^3] LighthouseFriends says a life-saving station was also built on the property in 1886.[^5]

## After the keepers

In 1930 the light was electrified and the fog signal changed to an air diaphone.[^5] The Archive records automation in 1972.[^4]

In June 2010 the Coast Guard declared the light excess to its needs and offered it at no cost to eligible federal, state and local agencies and nonprofit groups. No qualified steward was found.[^5] An online federal auction followed. LighthouseFriends says it opened on 23 July 2014 and closed on 12 September 2014, with a high bid of $48,500 from Gordon Krist of Paris, Kentucky.[^5] FOX 11 reported that three bidders had placed 21 bids in the last three days, for the same $48,500 final bid, but that in early October 2014 Krist had not yet closed the purchase. The Coast Guard, it said, would keep using the light as an aid to navigation.[^6]

## Today

The Archive lists the light as an active aid to navigation and says it is not open to the public.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the pier is open while the dwelling and tower are closed, and its directions run through the Coast Guard station to the pier.[^5] The approach crosses a staffed Coast Guard station.[^5] LighthouseFriends also says the lighthouse is privately owned, which does not match the Coast Guard's continued use reported in 2014.[^5][^6] The reports read here do not settle whether the 2014 sale closed or who owns the light now. Anyone going should ask the station first, and nobody should climb the tower.
