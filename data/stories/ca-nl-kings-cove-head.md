King's Cove Head Light is a white cast-iron tower near the town of King's Cove, on the Bonavista Peninsula of Newfoundland,[^3][^4][^5] and it has marked that point for ships since 1893 or 1894; the sources differ on the year.[^1][^2]

## The place

In the early 1800s a St. John's merchant, James MacBraire, set up his major fishing premises at King's Cove. During the winter he took several local young men to St. John's to be trained in navigation, and King's Cove developed a reputation for schooner captains who took part in the spring seal hunt and the summer Labrador fishery.[^1] The first official Newfoundland census, in 1836, counted 413 people in King's Cove, when the whole of Bonavista Bay had only about 5,000.[^1]

In 1873 J.C. Shears and other residents petitioned the Newfoundland government for a light on the cove's Western Point. Mariners debated whether a light for Bonavista Bay would be best at Western Point or on Little Denier Island.[^1] In 1887 Inspector John T. Nevill examined King's Cove and Little Denier Island and chose the island, where a light began in October 1888. King's Cove waited five more years.[^1]

## Building the light

The tower came in sections and was joined on the headland.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the cast-iron tower was made by Chance Brothers in Birmingham, England, cost $4,358.41, and was assembled with overlapping riveted joints.[^1] The Heritage Character Statement written for the federal heritage review is less certain about who made it. It says the structure "was manufactured in Britain, or assembled from parts ordered from a catalogue", and that there is "increasing evidence to suggest that Nevill functioned as overall designer."[^2]

The two sources also disagree on the date. Lighthouse Friends and the Rowlett directory give 1893, while the heritage statement says the lighthouse "was built in 1894 under the direction of J.T. Nevill."[^1][^2][^5]

The light was a Swedish Trotter-Lindberg revolving light, the first of its kind used in Newfoundland. The heat rising from the flame turned a screen around the lamp so that the light flashed: twelve turns a minute, two flashes each turn, from a focal plane of 54 metres.[^1]

The tower was painted red at first, with a red wooden store 26.8 metres away. In 1896 the inspector recommended white, because the tower was hard to pick out by day against the dark pines behind it. The tower and store were painted white in 1897, and a two-storey dwelling, also painted white, was built in 1898.[^1]

## The keepers

James Batterton was the first keeper. Lighthouse Friends says he was born in Greenspond, had lived in St. John's for fifteen years, and had served as a coxswain on the S.S. Plover under Captain Samuel Blandford. Shortly after Blandford was elected to the House of Assembly for Bonavista, he appointed Batterton as the first lighthouse keeper, much to the dismay of the local people, who asked, "was there not one man in King's Cove who could light a lamp?"[^1] In 1896 the inspector had noted that a small house beside the tower "would be a convenience to the keeper."[^1]

Batterton died in 1902.[^1] His widow, Annie, died in February 1926.[^6] The Evening Telegram's death notice, as transcribed on Find a Grave, calls her the widow of "James BATTERTON (Lighthouse Keeper)" and lists four sons, a daughter and two stepdaughters.[^6]

His son Philip succeeded him, and Philip and his wife raised seven children at the lighthouse.[^1] A two-storey stable, a pig barn and a double outhouse were added to the station, and the keeper's family cleared land for vegetables.[^1] Philip retired in 1933 and moved his family to St. John's. His older brother, James Jr., who had a home in King's Cove, became caretaker of the light.[^1]

When James Jr. died in 1955, lightkeeping passed to the Maddox family: Thomas Maddox from 1955 to 1974, then Tom Maddox Jr. from 1974 to 1992. Tom Walsh was keeper from 1992 to 2000. Lighthouse Friends says that after 2000 the tradition of lightkeepers ended and the Canadian Coast Guard took over the station.[^1]

## After the keepers

On 14 November 1929 a new and more powerful occulting light began at King's Cove, showing fourteen seconds of light and six of darkness. A fog horn sounded from 1930 to 1940 to warn ships in foul weather.[^1]

In 1931 the light source was changed to acetylene gas. The job of keeper became that of a caretaker at this time, because the acetylene light needed little maintenance, and the light was officially listed as an unwatched light.[^1]

The keeper's dwelling on the point was sold to a family from Keels in 1935 and taken away by them.[^1] The Rowlett directory says the keeper's house has been demolished.[^5]

Batteries powered the light from 1974 to 1984. From 1984 a solar panel charged the two lead-acid batteries that run the light automatically.[^1] The tower was classed as a Recognized Federal Heritage Building in 1990.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the station. Its List of Lights gives the present light as one second of light and three of darkness, a white flash every four seconds, with a focal height of 53.6 metres and a range of 5 nautical miles. The list also gives its operating period as seasonal.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says the current light uses a Seagull 200mm lens.[^1]

The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1][^5] The trail starts at the church in town and runs about 1.7 km on an inner loop, roughly 45 minutes.[^1] The heritage statement asks that the tower's "sound protective paint layer must be maintained," and treats the lantern and its weathervane as a focal point to be kept unaltered.[^2] The Canadian Register of Historic Places describes the tower as one of "approximately 26 lighthouses of prefabricated iron and steel construction, thought to be the only surviving examples of their type in Canada."[^3]
