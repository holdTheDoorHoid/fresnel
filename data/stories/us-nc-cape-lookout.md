Cape Lookout Lighthouse is a 163-foot (50 m) brick tower painted in black-and-white diamonds, standing on the low sand of Core Banks at the southern end of North Carolina's Outer Banks, in Carteret County.[^1] A first lighthouse stood here from 1812, and the present tower has shown its light since 1 November 1859.[^3][^6] In the Park Service's April 2026 update it was wrapped in scaffolding while it was repaired.[^21][^22]

## The place

Early mapmakers labelled the southern tip of Core Banks *Promontorium tremendum*, "horrible headland". The shoals off the point run about ten miles to the southeast, and in the middle of them lie breakers three-quarters of a mile long, in places only two feet under the surface.[^1] The land behind is so low that a ship could be dangerously close to shore before anyone aboard knew it.[^1] The National Register nomination quotes a nautical publication of 1809 that said the cape "cannot be seen more than three leagues in the clearest weather".[^2]

Cape Lookout lies roughly midway between Cape Hatteras and Cape Fear. Congress had authorized lighthouses at those two before 1797 but, as the nomination puts it, "for some reason" left Cape Lookout out.[^2] That changed in 1804, when Congress authorized a lighthouse "on or near the pitch of Cape Lookout". On 18 February 1805 Joseph Fulford and Elijah Pigott transferred a four-acre tract to the United States. The project slowed when J. S. Moore submitted the only bid, and by May it was apparent that construction would not begin that year.[^1]

## Building the light

The Treasury advertised again on 30 November 1810, asking for a wooden octagonal tower around a brick stair well. The contract went in March 1811 to Benjamin Beal Jr., Duncan Thanter and James Stephenson of Boston, and the station was finished in 1812 at a cost of $20,678.54.[^1] The National Park Service describes the result as a wood-frame octagonal tower with a brick stairwell, shingled and painted in horizontal red and white stripes, lit by thirteen lamps with 21-inch reflectors.[^3] A keeper named Fulford, given in the nomination as William, described it as a wooden-framed outer building "boarded and shingled and painted in red and white stripes horizontally" around an inner brick tower.[^2]

Sailors soon complained. A special board that reported on the nation's lights in 1851 described the Cape Lookout light as fixed, with thirteen lamps and 21-inch reflectors, 95 feet above the sea, and called it "one of the most important sea-coast lights" that needed to be raised and improved.[^1] A Navy lieutenant commanding a mail steamer wrote that year that the lights at Hatteras, Lookout, Canaveral and Cape Florida should be "dispensed with" if they were not improved, "as the navigator is apt to run ashore looking for them".[^1] A stopgap of new reflectors and lamps was sent in early 1857.[^1] On 3 March 1857 Congress then appropriated $45,000 for rebuilding with first-order apparatus. The work fell to First Lieutenant William Henry Chase Whiting, a 33-year-old engineer of the Lighthouse Board's Fifth District, who later served as a Confederate general and died in a Union prison in March 1865.[^1][^10]

The first-order lens was made in Paris by Lemonnier, Sauter and Company. Letters about its shipment date from the autumn of 1857, and it seems to have sat in New York until the tower was ready; a January 1859 entry records an "inability to ship" it, with no "Lt. Ships" bound for Beaufort.[^10] Borings for the foundation were under way by April 1858. By mid-September 1859 Whiting reported the tower complete, and Notice to Mariners No. 43, dated 19 September, announced that the new light would show from 1 November.[^10] The brick shaft has walls that thin from about eight feet at the base to about two feet at the top, with a spiral iron stair inside.[^2] The Light-House Board put the light's range at 18 miles in favourable conditions, while National Park Service pages give 15 miles in one place and 18 in another.[^15][^6][^3]

The old wooden tower was not torn down at once. Whiting reported in April 1860 that it had been altered to serve as keepers' quarters, and a January 1869 list of lights still described the "old tower" with its red and white stripes. It seems to have vanished during the 1870s.[^10] The nomination says sand mounds scattered with granite blocks to the north of the light are said to be its ruins.[^2] The park today points visitors to a large sand mound between the lighthouse and the boardwalk, where pieces of concrete and brick mark the foundation of the original tower.[^23]

The tower's look changed in 1873. A Light-House Board directive of that spring reads: "Cape Hatteras tower will be painted in spiral bands, alternatively black and white. Cape Lookout tower will be checkered, the checkers being alternately black and white."[^12] The Fifth District engineer was Major P. C. Hains and the keeper on station was Manaen W. Mason.[^12] The same year saw a new keeper's dwelling, an oil room, a bell line from the house to the lantern and a stove in the watch room.[^2][^12] Fuel changed too, though sources disagree on when. The Park Service's timeline says kerosene ("mineral oil") replaced whale oil in 1873, while the 2008 Historic Structure Report cites a letter-book entry of 26 December 1883 for the switch.[^3][^12]

The light itself kept changing. An incandescent oil vapor lamp arrived in 1912 according to the Historic Structure Report and the Park Service's page on the 1859 lighthouse, though the Park Service timeline places it in 1914, and in February 1914 an occulting device turned the fixed light into one with two eclipses each ten seconds long, in the Historic Structure Report's account.[^12][^13][^6][^3] George Putnam, Commissioner of Lighthouses, wrote in 1917 that in the two and a quarter years before the change "five wrecks were reported, in which the cause was stated to be the mistaking of Cape Lookout Light for some other light", and that none had been reported from that cause since.[^17] Electric lamps, generators and a radiobeacon came on 15 August 1933, raising the candlepower from 77,000 to 160,000. The radiobeacon outfit was second-hand, taken from Lightship No. 72.[^13]

## The keepers

The roster kept by the US Lighthouse Society lists James M. Fulford as first principal keeper from 1812, followed by William Fulford from 1828 and John R. Royal from 1854.[^5] The Park Service's own list dates James Fulford's appointment to 2 June 1812, and its timeline says President Madison appointed him at $300 a year.[^7][^3] On Royal the sources part ways. The Historic Structure Report says he was first appointed to the 1812 station in January 1834 and had run the smaller tower alone since then, while the roster gives 1854 and the Park Service list gives 17 January 1854.[^14][^5][^7]

The bigger tower and first-order lens were too much for one man. On 27 September 1859 two assistants were appointed, A. P. Guthrie and Silas Blount (the Park Service spells it Blunt).[^14][^7] The Park Service says three keepers made it possible to work one 24-hour day followed by two days off, and that at first all three lived in the old tower and its quarters.[^8] Pay before the Civil War was $400 a year for the keeper and $300 for an assistant; after 1869 assistants earned $400 and keepers $700 to $760.[^14]

*General context.* The keepers' logs for Cape Lookout have not been found, but the Historic Structure Report offers the account of a keeper at Bodie Island, farther up the Outer Banks, Lloyd Vernon Gaskill, as typical of the work: he lit the lamp every other evening, watched the light until sunrise, cleaned the lens, and spent further hours painting, mending and writing reports.[^14]

The war broke the sequence. In June 1861 the lens and lamps were taken down and stored in a Beaufort warehouse; the Historic Structure Report says the work of removing and packing the lens fell to keeper Royal and an assistant, Elijah Willis, who was kept on at the station until the end of the year.[^11][^4] Gayer Chadwick was appointed keeper on 24 February 1863, after the Union relit the tower with a smaller lens, and resigned the next year. Royal was rehired in May 1864 and removed with his two assistants on 21 May 1869 in what the report calls an apparent house-cleaning.[^7][^14]

Manaen W. Mason then kept the light until 1876. Among his assistants was Charlotte Ann Mason, second assistant from 1872 to 1875, whom the Park Service describes as the only woman to serve officially as a keeper at Cape Lookout.[^9][^7] The sources do not say how she was related to the head keeper, if at all. The Historic Structure Report says the light went out during an incident in the mid-1870s and that this may have led to the dismissal of Mason and both assistants on 19 August 1876. Its text dates the incident to 1875, but the letter-book entries it cites are from January 1874.[^12] The next two keepers, Melvin J. Davis and William F. Hatsel, were removed in their turn.[^14]

Denard Rumley came on as second assistant in 1878, became keeper in 1880 or 1881, and held the post until 1893, when, the Historic Structure Report says, he died while on duty.[^7][^14] His successor's career shows how people moved up through the ranks. Thomas Clifford Davis Jr. appears on the Park Service list as second assistant from 1869, again from 1883, as first assistant from 1886, then as acting keeper in 1893 and keeper from February 1895 to April 1900.[^7] The roster is full of repeated surnames (Davis, Willis, Royal, Guthrie), but the sources cited here do not trace the family ties.[^5]

By 1900 the Light-House Board was pressing for a better arrangement. "The accommodations for the keepers here are inadequate," it wrote, noting that a single dwelling housed the keeper and both assistants, that "their families can not be with them", and that "at such an isolated and lonely place this is a hardship". It asked for $7,500 for a new building.[^16] A separate principal keeper's house was completed in 1907, after which the head keeper moved into it and the assistants and their families shared the 1873 house.[^9]

Families who did live on the island kept gardens, cows, goats, sheep, chickens and horses, according to the Park Service, but there was no school, so children studied at home or stayed with relatives on the mainland. Until 1899 the keepers could walk to the community of Diamond City on Shackleford Banks.[^8] The Park Service also says the isolation and workload could take their toll.[^8]

Later head keepers included Charles W. Clifton, from October 1909 to about 1930 on the Park Service list and to 1933 on the roster, and Benjamin L. Harris from 1933.[^7][^5] The last name on the roster is John L. Midgett, principal keeper from 1947 to 1950.[^5] The Park Service adds that James Archie Newton, listed from 1939 to about 1945, may have been a Coast Guard officer in charge rather than a civilian keeper.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the early 1860s the war took the light away twice. North Carolina's governor ordered the coast's lights put out in April 1861, and in June the Confederate Light House Bureau called for the lighting apparatus to be removed. The Park Service notes that tales of retreating soldiers smashing lenses are generally untrue; the Cape Lookout lens was carefully packed.[^4][^11] It went to Beaufort, then by a roundabout route to Raleigh, where it had been stored since 22 July 1862; Union troops entered the city on 13 April 1865 and found it, some of its prisms broken or missing.[^11][^4] It left for France for repair on 28 November 1865 and came back in August 1866. Congress authorized $20,000 for repairs to the tower in 1866, and the lens was probably back in place by the end of May 1867.[^4][^12]

In the meantime the Union relit the tower with a third-order lens. The Historic Structure Report dates that to 24 February 1863; the Park Service's Civil War page says 1 March.[^11][^4] On Sunday night, 3 April 1864, a small Confederate party under L. C. Harland crossed into Union-held Core Banks with kegs of powder. The Park Service timeline gives the date as 2 April.[^4][^3] Accounts of the damage disagree. A Confederate report of 9 April claimed both lighthouses were destroyed "so that they never can be repaired again", a second report on 12 April said the old tower had gone but that poor powder had left the new one only disabled, and the Union report of 5 April noted minor damage to the 1859 tower and no word of the old one.[^4] The Historic Structure Report says two kegs were set off at the base of the stair, which shattered the glass and destroyed the entry-level floor, the first landing and about 61 of the iron steps. The New York Times reported on 9 April that the light would probably be out for a week, and on 18 April that "the light now burns as usual".[^11] Temporary repairs, with a wooden stair in place of the iron one, were finished on 27 June.[^11][^4]

A Light-House Board report of 1894 shows the worry over the shoals. It noted that a ship giving the shoals just enough offing to clear them would be ten miles from the light, and that although the light was first-order and "should be seen a distance of 18 miles under favorable circumstances, it may happen during thick or hazy weather that a mariner may fail to see it in time to avoid that line of shoals". The Board asked for a $70,000 lightship.[^15] The Park Service timeline says a lightship was anchored on the shoals in 1904.[^3]

The great hurricane of 17 and 18 August 1899 was among the worst storms to hit the cape. The lighthouse station came through, but the storm destroyed Shackleford Banks and drove many of its people to the mainland for good.[^12]

On 5 December 1902 the barkentine *Olive Thurlow*, bound from Charleston to New York with southern pine, was wrecked in Lookout Bight, near the Cape Lookout Life-Saving Station. Her master had broken his leg on the way and had set a distress signal to get ashore for treatment; the Life-Saving Service reported that one man was killed and the rest of the crew were saved.[^18] Head keeper J. Wilson Gillikin wrote to the Life-Saving Station's keeper from the light station that same day: "The scene was the saddest and most heartrending that I ever witnessed", adding that he believed the station's keeper had "very few equals, and no superiors, along the Atlantic coast".[^19] The life-saving keeper, William H. Gaskill, in turn credited the three lighthouse keepers (Gillikin, Davis and Willis) and several fishermen with helping to haul lines and pull men from the surf.[^20]

In mid-September 1933 a hurricane opened Barden Inlet west of the lighthouse, a change that has since threatened the station with erosion on the inlet side.[^3][^13]

During the Second World War the light was still sweeping brightly in mid-March 1942, silhouetting ships and making them vulnerable to attack, according to the Historic Structure Report. By June the coast had been darkened, and lighthouses served as observation points for submarines and saboteurs.[^13]

## After the keepers

In 1950 the Coast Guard fitted out the ground floor of the tower with generators, batteries and control panels. The light became self-contained, and the resident keepers left.[^13][^3] The nearby Coast Guard station took over watching it.[^3] The Coast Guard sold the 1907 keeper's house in 1957, and it was moved about a mile and a half south.[^9] A development plan of 1962 proposed removing everything on the site except the tower.[^13]

Congress authorized Cape Lookout National Seashore in 1966, and the light station was listed on the National Register on 18 October 1972.[^13] Around the mid-1970s the Coast Guard replaced the first-order lens with two rotating aerobeacons, which still give the 15-second flash.[^13] The date is disputed: the Park Service timeline says September 1975, while the Historic Structure Report calls it undocumented, noting that one author gives 1967 and that a dated photograph shows the change had been made before October 1977.[^3][^13] The old lens went first to Coast Guard headquarters in Portsmouth, Virginia, and in 1994, the Historic Structure Report says, to Southeast Light on Block Island, Rhode Island, where a Park Service brochure says it is still in service.[^13][^25][^3]

By 1988 the masonry between the eighth floor and the lantern had cracked badly, some cracks more than an inch wide. A shoring project in 1989 braced the top of the tower with steel and required cutting several of the original cast-iron steps.[^13] The Coast Guard handed the tower to the National Park Service in 2003; the Park Service timeline dates the ceremony to 14 July, the Historic Structure Report to 14 June. The tower was opened to the public as part of it.[^3][^26] The crowds were so large that more people reached the top in a few days than in the century before, but in early 2008 an engineer's inspection found the tower's circular stair significantly deteriorated and tours were suspended.[^26] The tower reopened for regular climbs on 15 July 2010, for the first time in its history.[^3]

## Today

The Park Service closed the lighthouse after a 2021 safety inspection found problems with the stairs and handrails. A $15.2 million stabilization contract was awarded to Stone and Lime Historic Restoration Services; it covers the stairs, landings, windows and doors, and calls for the exterior paint to be removed to the red brick underneath. The work is expected to take two to three years.[^21] By April 2026 scaffolding 24 decks high surrounded the tower, the Coast Guard had removed the beacon and set a temporary solar-powered LED light on the scaffolding, and the lantern room and watch level were due to be taken apart over the following two weeks and sent to Alabama to be refurbished.[^22] The Coast Guard owns and operates the light.[^21] The tower is closed to climbing for the duration.[^21][^23]

The 1873 keepers' house is a museum run by volunteers, open from mid-March to the end of October, with grounds that were open in April 2026 behind construction fencing.[^23][^22] The light station can be reached only by boat. Passenger ferries run to the light station from the Harkers Island Visitor Center all year, and from Beaufort in summer and on request at other times.[^24] Conditions may have moved on since the last Park Service update, so check with Cape Lookout National Seashore before planning a trip.
