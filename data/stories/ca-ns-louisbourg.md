On Lighthouse Point, on the north side of the entrance to Louisbourg harbour, the first light was lit in April 1734. The site is the first lighthouse location in what is now Canada. The French tower went dark after the siege of 1758, and lights have stood on the point again since the 1840s. The ruins of the French tower stand beside the foundations of the later light, and a concrete tower from 1923 still works there.[^1][^2][^5]

## The place

Before a light, the harbour entrance was marked by a wooden cross that local fishermen set up on the point at the eastern side of the mouth, which was then called Cross Point.[^3] The harbour needed better marking. In 1725 the French transport *Chameau* was wrecked several miles east of the town, with more than 300 people lost.[^3] In 1727 Captain Mechin of the King's ship *Profond* expected a short run from Port Dauphin. It took ten days. Fog and current carried him near the coast west of Louisbourg, and he barely escaped being wrecked. When he reached the town he urged the officials to build a lighthouse.[^3] On 15 November 1727 they met at Cross Point and agreed.[^3]

## Building the light

The engineer Étienne Verrier designed the tower and estimated its cost at 14,000 livres. The civilian contractor François Ganet built it.[^3] Work began in 1730. The tower was built in stages so the mortar could set, and in 1733 about 2.5 m of stone and the lantern were still to be built.[^3] The tower was 22.3 m (about 74 ft) high when finished.[^1][^3]

The first light was cod oil in a bronze basin. A copper ring floating on the oil carried 31 cotton wicks.[^3] In August 1733 a coal-and-wood fire was lit in the tower to welcome the King's ship *Le Héros*.[^3] The lantern glass that came from France was too small, and the official light was not shown until April 1734.[^3] O'Shea writes that the light was reported seen clearly for six leagues at sea, while Verrier gave a more cautious four.[^3] Parks Canada says the flames could be seen for 16 km.[^1]

The tower was rebuilt in 1738 with a fireproof lantern of stone, brick and lead.[^1][^3] The French light was never rebuilt after the siege of 1758 (see below).

In 1842, after Thomas Jost and merchants and shipowners near Sydney petitioned the provincial government, a new lighthouse went up on the same hill, a few yards from the French ruins. It was a square, two-and-a-half-storey dwelling topped by a lantern room, built under Samuel Crawford's direction.[^2][^3] The Parks Canada page dates this second light to 1847.[^1] The 1842 light served until 1923.

The 1923 light is the present tower, and its story begins with the fire of that June (see Storms, below). The Department of Marine and Fisheries chose a reinforced concrete design, drawn in 1917. The contract went to E.M. Dickson of Sydney for $18,100. With inspection costs and the lantern from the Prescott depot, the total for the light and dwelling came to $23,000.[^4] The Department's 1924 report says the new tower and dwelling were built to replace the combined lighthouse and dwelling destroyed by fire, and that the new light "operated on February 1, 1924."[^8] Lighthouse Friends gives 20 February 1924 as the day the light was placed in operation.[^2] The tower is an octagon, painted white, with a red lantern.[^4] The sources disagree on its height. O'Shea gives "almost 55 feet" and, in brackets, 14.5 m, which do not match each other. The Lighthouse Directory gives 17 m (55 ft), and the List of Lights gives the tower 15.4 m high.[^4][^5][^6]

## The keepers

The first keepers on record were soldiers.[^1] In 1733 men named Desloriers, Sans Chagrin, Vadeboncoeur, La Girofflee and La Jeunesse tended the coal-and-wood fire, and O'Shea calls them Louisbourg's first light keepers.[^3] The Lighthouse Friends history names Jean Grenard as the first keeper, a man of about sixty who had moved to Louisbourg from Placentia, Newfoundland. He kept the light until his death in 1744.[^2] O'Shea gives Grenard's full name as Jean Grenard dit Belair, a retired sergeant of the Compagnies franches de la marine, and dates his service from 1733 to 1744.[^3] Lighthouse Friends dates his service from 1734.[^2]

When the 1736 fire broke out, Grenard had shortly before returned to his quarters after checking the light. The fire was blamed on the small lantern and on wicks that may have been set too close together. The engineer Verrier suspected Grenard's negligence, but Grenard was never blamed for the fire.[^3]

During the British occupation after 1745, the British hired Georges DeRoches as keeper. He was one of the French residents who had stayed through the occupation.[^3]

In 1902 a first-order fog siren began work at the station. The Department's report says it was "operated by compressed air" and "was put in operation on February 18, 1902."[^7] The fog alarm had its own engineer. D. A. Campbell ran it from 1902 until 1916, when William Covey replaced him. The two posts were combined only when Covey became keeper of the new light at the end of February 1924. The later head keepers were Wilfred Covey, son of William, M. J. Tanner and Roy Forgeron.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Around midnight on 11 and 12 September 1736 a fire destroyed the wooden lantern. The light went out, and until a new lantern was built a coal-and-wood fire burned on an iron grill on the open tower. The officials reported that it was not as bright as the oil flame, and that it could not be kept lit in spring or fall.[^2][^3]

In 1745 New England troops besieged Louisbourg. They built a battery near the light to fire on the French guns on Battery Island. The light was not damaged by the return fire.[^3] Dudley Bradstreet, one of the New England besiegers, wrote in his journal for 15 July 1745 that he had gone up into the lantern. He called the tower "a magnificent Building" and counted 72 steps to the lantern, as O'Shea quotes the journal.[^3]

The British siege of 1758 damaged the tower. A repair was recommended, but the French light never operated again.[^1][^3]

On 3 June 1923 a fire destroyed the wooden lighthouse of 1842.[^4][^8] Lighthouse Friends gives 2 June as the date.[^2] The Lighthouse Directory dates this fire to 1922.[^5] A temporary light went up at once on the roof of the fog alarm building. It stood 14 m (45 ft) high, showed a white occulting light flashing every 12 seconds with 3 seconds of light, and could be seen 10 miles out to sea.[^4]

## After the keepers

The French ruins were nearly removed to make way for the new tower. Calmer heads and local heritage enthusiasts prevailed, and the ruins were stabilized. They were stabilized again in 1964 and in 1985.[^3] A plaque from the Historic Sites and Monuments Board of Canada went onto the concrete tower in 1926.[^3][^2]

The light was automated in 1990, when the last keepers left.[^1] In 1992 the Parks Canada page records the lighthouse as a Federal Heritage Building, for its place in the history of aids to navigation and for its architecture.[^1]

## Today

The light is still in service and serves the fishing community at Louisbourg.[^1] The Canadian Coast Guard's List of Lights describes it as a long-flashing white light, LFl W 6s, with a focal height of 31.9 m and a range of 13 nautical miles.[^6] The Lighthouse Directory names the Canadian Coast Guard as owner and the Louisbourg Lighthouse Society as site manager. The Louisbourg Lighthouse Society has applied to own the lighthouse.[^5]

Refurbishment of the 1923 tower began in November 2023, with concrete repairs, surface cleaning and recoating.[^1] The site is open and the tower is closed. Parking is at the end of Havenside Road.[^5][^2] The Louisbourg Lighthouse Trail begins near the tower, and interpretive signs at the ruins tell the story of the French light.[^1][^3]
