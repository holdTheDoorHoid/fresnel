The Janes Island station stood at the entrance to the Little Annemessex River in Tangier Sound, Maryland, close to Crisfield.[^1][^2] Its story runs from a light-vessel in 1854 to a screw-pile house first lit in 1867, a rebuilt screw-pile house lit in 1880, and a caisson light set up after ice destroyed that house in February 1936.[^1][^3] The archive dates the loss to 1935.[^2]

## The place

The 1867 report on the district's aids, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, called the screw-pile house "the most eligible form" of permanent structure for stations that had held light-vessels, and said this was "particularly the case in the district in question."[^1] The reasons it gave were the slight elevation of the adjacent shores, their swampy formation, the shallow water and the nature of the bottom.[^1] The same report says that, since the previous report, screw-pile houses had been built on eight stations that had held light-vessels, Janes Island among them, at a total cost of $121,001.[^1] It also gives the Board's aim of replacing light-vessels "wherever practicable" by permanent structures.[^1]

In 1854 the station was a light-vessel off the tail of Jane's bar, at the entrance to the Little Annemessex River.[^1] Its fixed light was 30 feet (9 m) above the water.[^1] The vessel was cream-colored, with her name painted in large black letters on each side, and in fog her bell and horn sounded in turn every five minutes.[^1] A Light-House Board entry for 1862, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, says light-vessels from Cape Henry southward had been "removed and sunk or destroyed by the insurgents," apart from a few exceptions, Janes Island among them.[^1]

## Building the light

A contract and bond for building the light-vessel, made with Fardy & Auld of Baltimore, was forwarded on 15 January 1853.[^1]

On 18 December 1866 the vessel was reported to be leaking badly.[^1] The estimated cost of repairs, more than $8,000, was judged more than she would be worth, so she was stripped of public property and sold at auction, realizing a net sum of $518.55.[^1] The screw-pile house that replaced her was lit on the night of 7 October 1867, and the relief vessel was withdrawn at that time.[^1]

Ice destroyed that house on 20 January 1879.[^1] Congress appropriated $25,000 for rebuilding it on 3 March 1879, and the new house was to follow the pattern intended for Hooper's Straits and for Laurel Point light-houses.[^1] The superstructure had been framed at the Lazaretto depot by August 1880, and its metal work was finished early in November.[^1] The working party and materials left Baltimore on 13 November 1880 aboard the tender Tulip, a hired schooner, with a working scow.[^1] The party reached the site the next day.[^1] The construction platform was finished on 17 November, and the center pile was screwed into position on the 18th.[^1] The light was first exhibited on the night of 20 December 1880, from a hexagonal screw-pile house showing a fixed white light of the fourth order.[^1] In the fiscal year ended June 30, 1899, new model fourth-order lamps were installed.[^6]

## The keepers

The archive's list for the station runs from 1867 to 1945 and names the head keepers and assistants by year.[^2] The first head keeper was Noah M. Lawson, who served from 1867 to 1871.[^2]

The list also includes Nancy W. Lawson, first assistant keeper from 1869 to 1871.[^2] The sources read here say nothing more about her.

Henry S. Moore was head keeper from 1890 to 1918, the longest head-keeper stint on the list.[^2] He had been first assistant in 1884.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1917 names H. S. Moore as keeper of the Janes Island Light Station.[^7]

Luther E. Bozman was first assistant from 1918 to 1919 and head keeper from 1927 to 1938.[^2] Gordy Z. Parks was second assistant from 1927 to 1931 and first assistant from 1931 to 1936, and Robert Bradshaw was second assistant from 1931 to 1934.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In January 1893 the Light-House Board recorded that the fog bell was wrenched from its fastenings by ice.[^5] It was found cracked and unserviceable.[^5] The bell and the boat hoister that replaced them came from the wreck of the Solomons Lump light-house.[^5] An iron landing ladder was put up in May.[^5]

On 13 February 1917 the keeper, Henry S. Moore, gave assistance to occupants of a skiff caught in ice near the station, and gave them food and lodging, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1]

The light and fog signal had stopped because of ice, and was back in operation on 14 February 1918.[^1] The red sector of the light was discontinued on 18 February 1920.[^1]

In April 1928 Bozman, the keeper, assisted the disabled powerboat Secret of Crisfield near the station.[^1]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports that after ice conditions threatened several occupied stations in early February, the Janes Island station was completely destroyed on 8 February 1936.[^3] The keepers had enough warning to reach the mainland safely.[^3] The archive gives 1935 as the year of destruction, which conflicts with the Bulletin's date.[^2] The Evening Star of Washington reported on 13 February 1936 that the structure was drifting in heavy ice east of Tangier and north of Watts Island.[^4] On 9 February an Army Air Corps plane from Bolling Field dropped warning instructions from the Commissioner of Lighthouses at several other occupied stations.[^3]

Lighthouse Friends gives a 1936 entry on ice damage in the Fifth District.[^1] It puts the estimated damage at $237,000 and says that over 250 minor light structures and unlighted daymarks were destroyed by ice.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1936 a new light was set up on a caisson foundation to serve as Janes Island Light.[^1] In 1937 the station was replaced by a 15-foot (4.6 m) diameter steel sheet-pile cylinder, filled with gravel and capped with concrete.[^1] Lighthouse Friends describes the light and fog signal there as unattended: a 375-millimeter acetylene light and a fog bell worked by a CO2 striker.[^1]

The archive's list still shows Alexander S. Asher as head keeper from 1938 to 1945.[^2] That does not fit an unattended light, and the sources read here do not explain the difference.[^2][^1]

## Today

The archive records the 1867 house as no longer existing and says it was destroyed by ice.[^2] Lighthouse Friends describes a modern light shown from a caisson foundation, and the sources read here give no current status beyond that.[^1]

The archive lists the station as not open to the public, and no source read here describes a way to visit it.[^2]
