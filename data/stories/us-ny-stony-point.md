Stony Point Light marks a point of rocky ledges at the eastern end of Lake Ontario, in the town of Henderson in Jefferson County, New York.[^1][^2] A light has been shown from this point since 1838.[^1][^2][^3] The tower that stands there now was built in 1869, and it is a privately owned home that visitors cannot enter.[^3][^1]

## The place

Rocky ledges extend from the point for some distance, and they can be seen beneath the clear water.[^1] Three miles offshore lies the similarly named Stony Island, and four miles beyond it is Galloo Island, which received a lighthouse in 1820.[^1]

On March 3, 1837 Congress appropriated $3,000 for a lighthouse to mark the point. The site was five acres bought from John Littlefield for $100.[^1] The archive's place record also lists the foundations of an 1830 tower on the site. No source cited here explains those foundations or how they relate to the 1837 appropriation.[^3]

## Building the light

The new light was finished and operating by the end of 1838.[^1] Lieutenant Charles T. Platt reported to the Treasury on November 26, 1838, and called it a very useful and important light, kept in excellent order.[^1]

In 1857 the light was fitted with a revolving fifth-order Fresnel lens that needed only one lamp.[^1][^3] In 1868 the Light-House Board's annual report noted that the old dwelling, tower and outhouses were not worth repairing and recommended replacing them.[^1]

Work on the new tower began in the summer of 1869. The stone was quarried near the site, and the job cost roughly $13,000, because the site was hard to reach and labor was expensive.[^1] The sources disagree about the material. The archive's place record gives brick, while LighthouseFriends and the Society's news item both say stone.[^3][^1][^2]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1896 records extensive repairs to the keeper's dwelling, and a new concrete foundation for an oil house, with the metal work for the oil house bought that year.[^4] The next year's report says the metal was erected and the old oil room was fitted up as a living room.[^5] In the year ended June 30, 1900 some 106 feet (32 m) of lead suction pipe was laid to connect the well with the dwelling.[^6]

In 1901 the tower was built up 20 feet (6.1 m), raising the focal plane from 38 feet (11.6 m) to 58 feet (17.7 m) above the water. It was painted white, and the interval between its flashes was cut from two minutes to one.[^1][^3]

## The keepers

Wiley G. Nickles was the first keeper. The archive lists him from 1837 to 1869.[^3] LighthouseFriends gives his birth year as 1802. It says that at 19 he lost both hands and forearms to cannon fire during a Fourth of July celebration at Sackets Harbor, and that hooks were later fastened to his arms.[^1][^2] His wife and children helped with the daily work of filling lamps, trimming wicks and polishing reflectors, but Nickles himself filled out the government reports.[^1]

After Nickles, the archive lists Erastus S. Penney from 1869 to 1871, James S. Dyer in 1871, Penney again from 1871 to 1876, and Newton Smith from 1876 to 1902.[^3] Alden C. Stevens kept the light from 1903 to 1906. The archive also lists Eliza Stevens as principal keeper in 1906.[^3] LighthouseFriends's list of head keepers also gives her, for 1906, but its narrative does not mention her. No source gives her relation to Alden Stevens, if she had one. LighthouseFriends says that Mial Eugene Eggleston transferred to the station on February 15, 1906 and replaced Alden Stevens as keeper.[^1][^3]

Before he came to Stony Point, Eggleston had been a member of the crew at the life-saving station in Charlotte, near Rochester. LighthouseFriends names him in that crew, which was awarded gold lifesaving medals for a rescue on Lake Ontario in December 1902. It says he then accepted a keeper's post at Crossover Island Lighthouse before he moved here.[^1] He kept the light for thirty-six years and retired on April 1, 1942.[^1][^3]

Wylie E. Koepka was moved from Galloo Island in 1942 to replace Eggleston and guard the property during the wartime emergency. He was the last keeper, and he left in November 1946.[^1][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On May 18, 1912 the steam barge Iona caught fire on Lake Ontario, loaded with coal and bound for the St. Lawrence.[^7][^1] The sources disagree on the details. NOAA's page on the wreck puts the fire about 15 miles off Oswego, with 1,000 tons of soft coal aboard. It says that the captain and 12 crew escaped in one boat, which landed at Henderson Harbor the next morning.[^7]

LighthouseFriends gives a different account. It puts the ship 25 miles offshore with 450 tons of coal aboard. It describes hours of rowing in rough water, with no light visible on shore, clouds keeping the crew from steering by the stars, and waves breaking into the boat. After three hours a light was finally seen, and five hours later the lifeboat approached the station. As dawn broke, the survivors' cries woke Keeper Eggleston, who ran to the shore and helped them land. LighthouseFriends counts ten survivors, and says the crew was cared for at the station and left for Watertown the next day. It cites the Watertown Daily Times of May 20, 1912 for this account.[^1] The counts differ: NOAA's captain and 12 crew make thirteen people, against LighthouseFriends' ten survivors.[^7][^1]

## After the keepers

On April 28, 1926 the light's characteristic was changed to alternating red and white flashes, to tell it apart from other lights in the area.[^1] The light was electrified on August 17, 1938.[^1]

Koepka's departure in November 1946 ended resident keeping. The light was automated shortly after, and in 1947 its characteristic changed to a red flash every ten seconds.[^1][^2]

The sources do not agree on when the light went out. The archive's place record gives 1945 as the year the station was discontinued.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the light was discontinued for the season in November 1946.[^1] The old tower's light was replaced in 1959.[^1][^2]

In 1959 a white skeleton tower was built near the shore, with its light at a focal plane of 25 feet (7.6 m), and the old tower's light was discontinued.[^1] The Society's news item describes an automatic light on a steel skeleton tower, installed closer to the shore.[^2]

The station was sold in 1960 for $9,107, according to LighthouseFriends, and the Society's news item says the property was auctioned to a private owner that year.[^1][^2] In 1966 a fire in a nearby barn nearly destroyed the lighthouse.[^1][^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is privately owned. Its grounds, dwelling and tower are closed to visitors, and the Society's archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^1][^3]

The new owners who bought the station in 2002 found the structure was not sound, and they had to gut it, taking off the roof and the second floor, before the restoration could go on.[^1] The grounds are private and closed, so the point is not open to visitors.[^1]
