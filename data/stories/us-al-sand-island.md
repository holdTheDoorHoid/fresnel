Sand Island Light is a brick tower 132 feet (40 m) tall that now rises from a ring of piled stone in open water about three miles off the mouth of Mobile Bay, Alabama, on a spot where an island of some 400 acres once lay.[^2][^3] It is the third lighthouse to stand on the site, and nearly all of its history is a long contest between the tower and the Gulf of Mexico.

## The place

Sand Island lay on the outer bar of Mobile Bay, between Mobile Point on the east and Dauphin Island on the west. Congress authorised an iron spindle there in 1828, which cost $600, was visible for six miles and was finished in 1830.[^3] A proper light followed in 1838. The National Register nomination calls it the second navigational aid for the entrance to the port of Mobile, after the Mobile Point light of 1822, though that count leaves out the 1830 spindle.[^2][^3]

The island was losing ground for decades. The National Register nomination says it was about 400 acres originally and had been cut to less than 100 by 1873.[^2] Lighthouse Friends quotes an 1848 coast survey that found the eastern shore had lost a strip 66 to 100 yards wide along its whole length.[^3] By the Light-House Board's report of 1897 the tower and buildings were "entirely surrounded by water."[^6]

## Building the light

Congress set aside $10,000 for a lighthouse in 1837, and Winslow Lewis built it in 1838: a 55-foot tower with fourteen lamps and sixteen-inch reflectors, finished under budget.[^3] In 1858 an Army engineer, Danville Leadbetter, completed a much taller brick tower of 150 feet with a first-order Fresnel lens, lit in January 1859.[^3] The nomination gives the light's height above the water as 152 feet.[^2]

The Civil War ended that tower. Lighthouse Friends reports that the Confederates took out the lens, that Union blockaders installed a fourth-order lens on 20 December 1862, and that a Confederate party under John W. Glenn blew the tower up with seventy pounds of gunpowder on the morning of 23 February 1863.[^3] The National Register nomination dates its destruction to 1862.[^2] A temporary wooden tower with a fourth-order light followed, and had to be moved twice to escape the retreating shore.[^3]

The present tower was paid for by three appropriations between 1867 and 1873 that together came to $175,000 and also covered a keeper's dwelling and range beacons.[^2] The nomination says building began in 1872; Lighthouse Friends says work began in 1871, stopped in 1872 when money ran out, and resumed in April 1873.[^2][^3] The tower rests on 171 piles under 12 feet of concrete, with granite for doors and windows taken from the ruined earlier tower.[^2][^3] The nomination records a Fresnel lens made in Paris by Barbier and Fenestre in 1871; Lighthouse Friends calls it second order.[^2][^3] The light was first shown on 1 September 1873.[^2][^3] (One line of the nomination's significance statement says "Constructed in 1875," which its own later text contradicts.[^2]) The tower was coal-tarred in 1881, giving it the black daymark seen in old photographs, and a fog bell in a skeletal tower began work on 1 September 1888.[^3]

## The keepers

The USLHS Digital Archive, working from the keeper registers, lists John McCloud as the first principal keeper, from 1838.[^1][^3] It shows no head keeper between Robert Gage, who left in 1861, and Rich O. Hunter, who began in 1864.[^1] The longest stay was Archibald D. Merritt, head keeper from 1866 to 1885.[^1] Hans J.G. Olsen followed from 1889 to 1900, then Niels Nilsen, who had been first assistant since 1898, from 1900 to 1909.[^1] By the twentieth century the station had a head keeper and two assistants.[^1]

Mathias W. Streckert, an assistant in 1903-04, was head keeper from 1909 to 1917.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the station won the district's efficiency pennant for the year ended 30 June 1915, and that after the 1916 hurricane Streckert and Second Assistant Otto H. Beadnell were commended for keeping a light burning when the tower's vibration made the usual lamp unusable.[^3] The same source says Streckert was dismissed shortly after his transfer to Pensacola in 1917, over supplies he was alleged to have over-reported.[^3]

For years after 1906 the keepers had no house at all. The Commissioner of Lighthouses wrote in his estimates that a storm in October 1906 had destroyed the dwelling and "since that time the keepers have used the tower as a dwelling," quarters he called not suitable.[^10] A new two-story dwelling on iron piles, with three bedrooms, was built in 1925 at a cost of $36,749.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

**The hurricane of 1906.** Lighthouse Friends gives the date as 27 September 1906 and says that all those present at the station, First Assistant Andrew Hansen and his wife Emma, drowned.[^3] The USLHS Digital Archive agrees on the date for Hansen.[^19] The Commissioner's later report dates the storm to October, a disagreement the newspapers settle in favour of September.[^10][^17] The Birmingham Age-Herald of 30 September reported a ship's captain's account that the beacons in the bay and all the range lights had been carried away, that the Sand Island lighthouse was "destroyed, only the tower remaining," and that no light was burning.[^17] The Montgomery Advertiser of 3 October listed the Sand Island light among the aids lost, "everything gone except the tower."[^18] The nomination adds that the tower was damaged, much of the lantern glass had to be replaced, and the entrance was moved.[^2] The old south door was bricked up, and a new one was cut on the north side about 30 feet above the ground.[^2]

**Holding the island.** The Light-House Board report for the year to June 1892 said the sea was disturbing the keeper's dwelling, whose foundations were beginning to give way.[^4] The dwelling was moved in 1893 to a spot 783 feet from the tower.[^5] A gale on 6 July 1896 carried away the fog-bell tower, which was rebuilt and the bell reestablished on 13 January 1897.[^7] Congress then authorised a contract, not above $65,000, to rebuild the whole station if the sea made that necessary.[^8] In the year to June 1900 the Board put 3,894 tons of small rock, 3,054 tons of rock weighing three to five tons a piece, and 2,797 barrels of oyster shells around the tower, then another 1,600 tons because the protection kept sinking.[^9] The Commissioner's estimate of 1915 described the brick tower as standing "surrounded by an artificial island of rock, the sand island having washed away," and said the station had no fog signal, although Lighthouse Friends records a fog bell running from 1888 (the sources do not explain the difference).[^10][^3] A contract of the 1917 fiscal year called for 2,700 tons more rock; in the fiscal year 1922 bids for riprap were twice rejected.[^11][^12]

**Lost keepers, 1919.** On the night of 16-17 January 1919 the light was out. A landing party found the station deserted, and the journal showed that Keeper John M. Reynolds and First Assistant William L. Emerson had left on 16 January for Fort Morgan to meet a newly employed assistant.[^13] The district tender searched the Gulf and the south shores of the barrier islands without finding them, and the service believed their launch was either swamped in the breakers west of the station or blown out to sea.[^13] Thorwald Hansen took charge from 1919, and his brother George served as second assistant in 1919-20; Lighthouse Friends says George drowned within a year of joining him.[^1][^3]

**Rescues and later storms.** In the Bulletin for 1924, First Assistant J. R. Linton and Second Assistant A. D. Ward are credited with answering a cry for help on the night of 20 April and bringing the people from a motor boat that had gone ashore in the shoals to the station.[^14] The September 1926 hurricane left the tower and dwelling uninjured, but the boathouse and oil house were partly wrecked.[^15] In 1932 First Assistant J. F. Goodson was commended for towing a broken-down motor boat with three people aboard to safety.[^16]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over the lighthouse service in 1939, and the archive's roster of keepers ends with Edgar H. Orsburne in 1947.[^1][^3] The nomination says the light was fully automated in 1947; Lighthouse Friends says the station was de-staffed in 1948.[^2][^3] Lighthouse Friends tells how Orsburne, in March 1947, set out from Fort Morgan with provisions, was capsized by a storm and spent five days adrift before a cutter found him five miles south of the island.[^3]

The beam was cut from 60,000 to 8,000 candlepower in 1966 when the new Mobile Point light took over, and the light was extinguished in 1971.[^2] The lens went to the Fort Morgan museum, and the 1925 dwelling burned in 1973.[^2][^3]

The Alabama Historical Commission turned down an offer of the lighthouse in 2001 as too costly to save; the Town of Dauphin Island took ownership from the federal government in 2003.[^3] Boulders were rearranged and bound with steel cable, a concrete ring was poured at the base and missing brick replaced in 2008.[^3] A $6 million island-rebuilding project in autumn 2011 put sand back around the tower, but by March 2012 it had drifted west.[^3] The Propeller Club post says the sand was gone again less than a year later, in Hurricane Isaac.[^20]

## Today

The tower belongs to the Town of Dauphin Island, is closed, and stands in open water; it can be seen from Fort Morgan, and the best views are from the water.[^3] A 2026 post by the Propeller Club of Mobile says cracks run down the brick and much of the roof is compromised, that the lighthouse is on the Lighthouse Digest endangered list, and that restoring it in place or moving it to Dauphin Island are both multi-million-dollar choices.[^20] It is not a place to land or climb.
