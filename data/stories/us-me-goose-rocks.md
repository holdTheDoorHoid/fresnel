Goose Rocks Light is a cast-iron "spark plug" tower built in 1890 on a ledge that is awash at half tide, at the eastern entrance to the Fox Islands Thoroughfare between Vinalhaven and North Haven in Penobscot Bay, Maine. The keepers lived inside the tower itself, and for much of its history their families lived ashore.[^1][^2]

## The place

The Fox Islands, more than fifty in all, are said to have been named by the explorer Martin Pring for the silver foxes found there.[^3] In 1888 the Light-House Board described the thoroughfare as "one of the most important passages on the coast of Maine for large fleets of coasting and fishing vessels," and asked for a light and fog bell at Goose Rock to guide vessels into the entrance and past Widow's Island.[^1] The National Register nomination adds the traffic of the day: Vinalhaven had a busy granite industry with two competing firms, and both islands drew summer visitors to new hotels and cottages.[^2] Congress appropriated $35,000 on 2 October 1888, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1]

## Building the light

Work began on 7 May 1890 and ended on 14 November 1890, the Light-House Board reported.[^4] The foundation was an iron caisson set on the ledge and filled with concrete, topped by an iron tower 37 feet (11 m) high. The Board gave the caisson as 26 feet (7.9 m) across and 24 feet (7.3 m) high; Lighthouse Friends says 25 feet.[^4][^1] Metalwork and cement came from Boston and crushed stone from Crotch Island, about eight miles away. The crew lived on Widow's Island and built a wharf, 76 by 32 feet, on the ledge itself.[^1]

The light was a fifth-order lens showing fixed red, with a fixed white sector.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says Keeper Ira D. Trundy and his assistant Leo Gillis lit it on New Year's Eve 1890.[^1] A Coast and Geodetic Survey notice in January 1891 called the light "recently established" and gave the tower as brown, the focal plane as 56 feet (17 m) and the visibility as 13 miles.[^5] Lighthouse Friends adds that ships could run safely past Widow's Island by keeping the white light in view.[^1] Jeremy D'Entremont, on the owners' website, and the US Lighthouse Society record both give the original lens as fourth-order, which the Board's own report and Lighthouse Friends contradict.[^3][^12][^4][^1]

A 1,200-pound (540 kg) fog bell, struck by machinery, was added.[^4] A notice in the Portland paper said it would begin on and after 20 April 1891; Lighthouse Friends gives 30 April.[^6][^1] In June 1894 the Board's observers tested it. The bell hung over the south side of the lantern deck and was struck every 20 seconds; the report called its blow "a heavy blow for a Stevens machine." It carried about 2 miles at right angles to a 10-knot wind, but less than three-quarters of a mile to windward in a 10-knot southwest wind, while to leeward, over tree-covered islands, it was heard 2 miles. Stimpson Island blocked the sound to the east.[^7]

The tower's colour changed twice. A notice of August 1895 ordered it changed from brown to red "to make it a more conspicuous day mark," and the Board's report lists the change as made on 1 September 1895.[^8][^9] In November 1901 the light was to be raised from fifth to fourth order, becoming a flashing red light every five seconds with a flashing white every five seconds in the old white sector.[^10] Lighthouse Friends dates the new lens to 1902, a year later.[^1] About 15 June 1903 the tower went from red to white.[^11]

## The keepers

Goose Rocks was too small inside for families, so it became what Lighthouse Friends calls a "stag" station: the men lived aboard and their families lived in housing ashore, and the keepers were home for eight days a month.[^1] Charles L. Knight, who served here in the 1920s, told the author Robert Thayer Sterling: "My stay at Goose Rocks was pleasant even though it was an isolated station where no one could get out and walk around on dry land."[^1]

The US Lighthouse Society's roster, drawn from the federal keeper registers, begins with Trundy as principal keeper (1890-1893) and Gillis as first assistant (1890-1891).[^12] Angier W. Tapley was an assistant from 1894 to 1896 and then principal keeper until 1899. Albert R. Mills came as an assistant in 1897 and was principal keeper from 1899 to 1917, the longest term the roster shows. Assistants came and went quickly in some years: four men are listed for 1903 alone.[^12] Sources disagree about Myrick R. Morrison, whom the roster has as an assistant from 1921 and principal keeper from 1930 to 1938, while D'Entremont puts his term as principal keeper at 1920 to 1938. They differ over Knight too: the roster has him as principal keeper from 1926 to 1930, D'Entremont as an assistant from 1926.[^12][^3] D'Entremont says Morrison's family lived at West Kent Cove in North Haven.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In December 1940 Roy Manchester, then twenty, was serving as a substitute keeper. Lighthouse Friends reports that his boat apparently exploded while he was crossing from North Haven with the mail. Flames were seen from North Haven that night. The next day Coast Guard men from White Head found a charred part of his 16-foot boat and some of the mail, but no trace of him.[^1]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1963.[^2][^12] The roster for 1962 and 1963 lists a rotation of Coast Guard men under a separate USCG heading.[^12] Jim Woods, who served with the Coast Guard crew about 1959 and 1960, later described a three-man crew with one man ashore at any time. They relied on collected rainwater, hauled the skiff aboard after each trip because of tides of nine or ten feet, and wound the clockwork fog bell every four to six hours in fog.[^3]

After automation, local "lamplighters" were paid to run the fog signal; Alton Calderwood and Elmer Carver, who lived at Little Thoroughfare, are named by the North Haven Historical Society.[^3] The bell gave way to a diaphragm horn in 1969.[^1] In the 1990s no group claimed the station under the Maine Lights Program. When it was offered again in 2004 the only application was rejected, and the General Services Administration sold it at auction. Casey Jordan and Ted Northrop bid $27,000 and formed Beacon Preservation, Inc., which took ownership in November 2006.[^1]

## Today

Goose Rocks is an active aid to navigation with a solar-powered 250 mm optic; the Fresnel lens is gone.[^12][^3] It stands offshore and is owned by Beacon Preservation, a nonprofit. It can be seen from Vinalhaven and North Haven, and the owners rent it to overnight guests.[^3][^1] Jim Woods, writing of his 1959-60 tour, said there was no way aboard but by small boat; visits today are through the owners' overnight stays.[^3]
