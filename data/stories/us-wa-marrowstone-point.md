Marrowstone Point Light stands on a low sand spit at the northeast tip of Marrowstone Island, in Jefferson County, Washington, at the entrance to Port Townsend Bay from Admiralty Inlet.[^1][^2] Its first light was a red lantern on a pole, shown in 1888.[^1][^2] The light a visitor sees today sits on a concrete fog-signal building put up in 1917 or 1918, beside a federal research station and next to Fort Flagler State Park.[^1][^2]

## The place

Captain George Vancouver named the point in May 1792, after a whitish, hardened clay in the bluffs behind it that he called "marrowstone."[^2][^1] The name later spread to the whole island.[^2][^1] The Lighthouse Board reserved ten acres on the point for a light in 1854, in part because of shoals, dangerous rocks, rip-tides and fog.[^2] The channel here is narrow, and Craven Rock lies two miles south of the point.[^2]

## Building the light

The first mark was a post lantern about 15 feet (4.6 m) high, showing a red light.[^2] A contract keeper rowed to the point every few days to polish the lens, trim the wick and top up the fuel.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates its first showing to 1 October 1888.[^1] It was nearly thirty years before the Lighthouse Service replaced it.[^2]

Congress appropriated $3,500 for a fog bell on the point in 1893.[^3] The board's report for the year to 30 June 1894 says a survey of the site had been made and the plans were being prepared,[^4] and the report for the year to 30 June 1895 says the land had been set apart.[^5] The report for the year to 30 June 1896 says the contractors had finished, and that "the station went into commission on April 7, 1896."[^6]

The sources describe the keeper's dwelling differently. LighthouseFriends, quoting the board's notice to mariners, gives "a one and one-half story wooden structure" with a bell tower attached.[^1] HistoryLink calls it a large two-story house.[^1][^2] The 1,200-pound bell was struck a double blow every fifteen seconds in fog, by a hammer worked from a Gamewell striking apparatus.[^1]

The sources disagree about the next changes. LighthouseFriends says an experimental acetylene fog gun replaced the bell on 1 April 1914, and that a new acetylene light went up on a concrete structure on 5 June.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1914 records "bell changed to acetylene gun" and a change "oil to acetylene" at the station.[^7] HistoryLink dates the fog gun to 1913, when it was tried as an experiment.[^2]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses listed a change in the light and fog signal, and a new fog-signal building, at Marrowstone in the fiscal year 1917, at a cost of $713.70.[^8] The report for fiscal 1918 gives "a new light and fog-signal building" costing $1,112.13, a change from acetylene to electric light, and a change in the fog signal "from acetylene gun to third-class Daboll trumpet."[^9] The report for fiscal 1919 says the fog signal went "from an acetylene gun to an air-operated reed horn."[^10] The 1918 Bulletin also says the fog signal "changed from acetylene gun to reed horn."[^11]

LighthouseFriends has a third-class Daboll fog signal installed on 17 December 1917, and an electric light shown from 3 February 1918.[^1] HistoryLink describes a square concrete fog-signal building of 1918 with a three-trumpet Daboll signal, and a small drum lens on a mast on its roof, which it calls the point's "first lighthouse."[^2] The sources differ on the dates and the equipment, and this account does not choose between them. In 1922 the Lighthouse Service also built a sea wall to protect the beach from erosion.[^2]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive roster begins with Osmore H. Morgan, who served from 1896 to 1907.[^12] LighthouseFriends calls him "a seventy-year-old former sea captain," who had been head keeper at New Dungeness Light for the fourteen years before.[^1] HistoryLink gives his name as Osmond Hale Morgan (1826–1907).[^2] He came from Whidbey Island with his wife, Frances, and five children, and he died on 22 June 1907 "while still on duty."[^2]

His daughter, Nettie, lived at the station and helped him.[^2] After his death, LighthouseFriends says, she was "briefly placed in charge of the light and bell."[^1] The archive lists her as keeper for 1907.[^1][^12]

Axel Rustad, a keeper from Ediz Hook, was appointed that same year and served until 1922.[^2][^12] HistoryLink says he came with his wife, Karen, and "their three sons."[^2] LighthouseFriends says the family raised four sons on the point.[^2][^1]

The archive then lists Mortimer Galvin (1922–1926), Jacob Hall (1926–1938), Vivian R. Corrie (1938–1944), Albert Beyer, Jr. (1948), Nicholas L. Sellers (at least 1950) and Blanchard M. Pierce (to 1956).[^12] The archive's "at least 1950" marks a documented span, not an appointment date.[^12]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The earliest wreck these sources describe is the collision of 8 October 1892, when the steamer Premier and the freighter Willamette met in fog off the point.[^2] HistoryLink says the collision killed five people and injured eighteen.[^2]

On 19 July 1903 the side-wheel steamer North Pacific struck Craven Rock in fog and sank.[^2][^13] The San Francisco Call reported that she had "only fourteen passengers," and that the keeper gave the people from her "food and shelter."[^13] HistoryLink gives 40 passengers and crew.[^2] The Call says the Mainlander, which had run ashore at Marrowstone Point and backed off when the tide flooded, took eleven passengers aboard and carried the crew and the rest of the passengers to Seattle.[^13]

The board's report for the year ended 30 June 1897 says the fog bell "was in operation some 585 hours."[^14] HistoryLink says mariners kept complaining that the bell was often inaudible.[^2] The same essay records the steamer S.S. Dode aground on the shoal in May 1907, and the S.S. William Catham beached on the point in fog in 1914.[^2]

## After the keepers

Congress abolished the Lighthouse Service on 7 July 1939 and gave its lights to the Coast Guard.[^2] Civilian keepers kept their jobs until they retired, and were gradually replaced by Coast Guard personnel.[^2] The light was automated in 1962.[^1][^2]

Ownership moved more than once.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says the property was transferred in 1972 from the Coast Guard to the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife.[^1] HistoryLink says it was returned to the Coast Guard in 1972, and that the Fish and Wildlife Service acquired it in 1974.[^1][^2]

A laboratory went up in 1976.[^2] In the early 1990s the US Geological Survey's Biological Resources Division took over the field station and invested about $40 million in new construction and remodelling.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard's Aids to Navigation Branch in Seattle maintains the light.[^2] The optic is a 250-millimetre lens on a mast, 28 feet (8.5 m) above the fog-signal building.[^2] HistoryLink describes its signal as a fixed white light occulting every four seconds, and says it is "visible for nine miles."[^2] LighthouseFriends calls it a flashing white light.[^1]

Neither the lighthouse nor the field station is open to the public, though the light is easy to see from the beach.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the shoreline is open and the tower and dwelling are closed.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's archive, by contrast, lists the station as open to the public.[^12]

Ownership is described differently too. A regional guide lists the light as owned by Washington State Parks.[^15] HistoryLink describes a federal fisheries and biological research station that the US Geological Survey took over in the early 1990s.[^2] LighthouseFriends also gives the USGS as owner.[^1]

The keeper's house, a Victorian residence, now holds the field station's office, library and dormitory space.[^2]
