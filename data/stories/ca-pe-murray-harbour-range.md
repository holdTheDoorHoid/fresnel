Murray Harbour's two range lights were built to guide vessels into the harbour, past a bar across its mouth.[^1] Set one above the other, they gave a single line that a mariner could follow in.[^1] The front light stands on Beach Point.[^6] The rear light is 1,361 m (4,465 ft) away on a bearing of 234°, in a field on private property.[^6][^5]

## The place

Murray Harbour lies a few miles northwest of Cape Bear.[^1] The hazard was the bar across the harbour mouth.[^1] In 1908 the bar was described as about 73 m (240 ft) wide, with about 3 m (10 ft) of water over it at low tide.[^1] A buoy was placed just outside it. From there, a vessel that could see the two lights one above the other was on the line that led to good anchorage and the local wharves.[^1]

Residents asked for the lights. In 1869 John Brooks and other people of the place petitioned the Island's government for a grant to build lights at the harbour entrance.[^1] The Island was not yet part of Canada. It joined Confederation in 1873, after several years of negotiation.[^5]

## Building the light

The first lights were simple. The front light was a white light on a post about 9 m (30 ft) high. The rear light was a white light on the gable of a barn about 1.6 km (1 mi) away.[^1]

A year after Prince Edward Island joined Confederation in 1873, the Department of Marine's General Superintendent of Lighthouses visited and found the lighthouses and lighting apparatus "very inferior".[^1] A new lantern, lamps and reflectors were supplied, and it was proposed to replace the arrangement with two open-framed towers, one on the wharf and one on the beach.[^1] On 22 June 1876 William Mitchell, the Department's agent on the Island, found the lights "not at all sufficient although they were being kept in good order."[^1]

In 1878 Ottawa provided plans and specifications for new range towers, for which $1,500 had been appropriated.[^1] Joseph Egan of Mount Steward built them under an $850 contract. Work began in the summer of 1878 and the range lights were completed that November.[^1][^4]

The Lighthouse Directory gives 1879 for the towers and dates the station's start to 1869.[^5] The Lighthouse Friends history and the Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society's history both give 1878 for the towers.[^1][^3][^4]

In November 1902 the front tower was to be lowered by 3 m (10 ft). The Department's annual report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1902 says arrangements had been made to "reduce the height of the front range tower on Beach point 10 feet in November, 1902, in order that the back light may be more easily distinguished."[^7] The report expected the tower to stand 20 feet (6.1 m) high afterward.[^7] The List of Lights and the Society both give the present tower as 7.2 m (23.6 ft), which does not match that expectation.[^6][^4] The Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society alone says the front tower was moved to the tip of the point in 1908.[^4]

## The keepers

One family kept the rear light for most of a century.[^3] The first name on the rear light's keeper list is James Penny, from 1868 to 1874, a year before the station's 1869 start date.[^2][^1] He kept it again from 1878 to 1897. Malcom McFadyen kept it in between, from 1875 to 1878.[^2] Robert Penny followed in 1897 and kept it until 1944. Robert Curtis Penny kept it from 1944 to 1963.[^2]

The Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society gives the family's service as 1868 to 1966, with three years' exception, and names the last two keepers Robert Penny, Sr. and Robert Penny, Jr.[^3] The two histories agree on the family but differ on when the last Penny left the light: 1963 or 1966. The Society's list gives 1944 as the start of "Robert Penny, Jr.", the year the Lighthouse Friends list starts Robert Curtis Penny, so the two lists appear to name the same keeper.[^3][^2]

The Department's annual report for the fiscal year 1909–10 includes a table of keepers. In it, Robert Penny is listed at the inner light from 11 November 1897, and Lemuel McLeod at the outer light from 21 December 1897.[^8] The rear light is sometimes called "Penny's Light", in a Department list and in the Lighthouse Friends history. The front light was also known as the Beach Light.[^9][^1]

Abraham Daley kept the front light from 1878 to 1897, and Lemuel McLeod from 1897 to 1912.[^1] Lauchlin McNeill followed in 1912 and kept it to 1929. Albert McNeill is listed for 1929.[^1] James Isaac Newton William kept it from 1929 to 1950, and Emerson MacLeod from 1950 to 1963.[^1] The first front keeper, in 1869, is named only as "Lumsden". The sources give neither his first name nor an end date.[^1][^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1897 the timber block that carried the front tower was judged unsafe. As a temporary measure, the block was encased in hemlock boards before the fall gales. Large stones were set in the sand at each corner of the block, with 6-inch posts on top, and the tower's corner posts were bolted to those posts. A new block was planned for the following year.[^1]

In December 2010 a storm swept the front tower off its foundation and into the water. The Coast Guard retrieved it and left it lying on its side on the beach.[^5] The Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society says the storm surge tore the tower from its foundation and badly damaged it.[^4]

## After the keepers

In 1963 the lights were electrified, according to Lighthouse Friends. Emerson MacLeod and Robert Curtis Penny were the keepers then. Both stayed on as caretakers after electrification, at a greatly reduced salary.[^1][^2] The Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society dates the electrification to 1966.[^3][^4]

## Today

After the storm, residents formed the Beach Point Lighthouse Society and petitioned for the tower's restoration.[^5] The two histories give different numbers of signatures: 1,700 in one, more than 3,000 in the other.[^1][^4] On 27 July 2011 a crane mounted the tower on a new foundation.[^5] The Lighthouse Friends history says the new foundation was cement, a short distance from the old one, and gives the cost of the project as $75,000.[^1] The Society says the tower was clad in cedar shingles when it was repaired.[^4]

The Canadian Coast Guard's List of Lights describes the front light as a white square tower with a red top and a red vertical stripe, with a range of 10 nautical miles. It marks both range lights "Seasonal".[^6] The Society describes the light as a fixed red light, visible only on the range line.[^4] The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lights.[^1] The Beach Point Lighthouse Society has submitted a petition for ownership under the Parks Canada Heritage Lighthouse Program.[^1][^4]

Visitors can walk to the front light at Beach Point. Lighthouse Friends reports a sign, seen in 2013, where the road reaches the beach, asking that vehicles not be driven down to the lighthouse; access by foot was still permitted.[^1] The approach over the sand is under water at high tide.[^5] The tower is closed and should not be climbed.[^1][^5]

The rear light stands on private property, reached by Pennys Lane, which is private. The best view is from the Beach Point Wharf or from Wharf Lane.[^2][^5]
