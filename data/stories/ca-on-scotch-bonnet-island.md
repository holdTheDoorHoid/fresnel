Scotch Bonnet Island Lighthouse stands on a small low island about 4.6 km off Prince Edward County, in Lake Ontario, south-west of the larger Nicholson Island.[^1] Its stone tower was first lit in September 1856. In 1959 a 19 m (63 ft) skeletal tower replaced its light, after the stone tower had begun to crumble.[^1] The island is closed to visitors, and landing there is not permitted.[^1][^2]

## The place

LighthouseFriends gives a local legend that a homesick Scottish sailor named the island because its low profile resembled a Tam O'Shanter, the traditional Scottish hat.[^1] The hazard was the shoal to its south. In 1844 the Province of Canada's Department of Public Works reported a shoal there, "upon which the breakers shew themselves in heavy weather," and said that a light there "would add much to the safety of that part of the Lake."[^1]

The Register statement says the light served as "a hazard avoidance light to warn ships travelling through Lake Ontario of the potential danger of the small Scotch Bonnet Island and the nearby shoals."[^2] It also says the light guided steamers and schooners loaded with farm produce, timber, coal and immigrants past the island.[^2]

The Register places the island just west of the "Graveyard of Lake Ontario," the area between Point Petre and Main Duck Island, which it says "saw approximately two-thirds of all Lake Ontario shipwrecks during both the sail and steamship eras."[^2] That is the area's record, not a list of wrecks at this island.

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends quotes the Department of Public Works' report for 1853, which said that "a Light House is being built" on Scotch Bonnet, "or 'Egg Island.'"[^1] The light was nearly finished by the end of 1855. It was not put into operation until September 1856, when the lighting apparatus was taken out to the island and installed in the lantern room.[^1]

The tower was built of limestone. LighthouseFriends describes a circular tower of 16 m (54 ft), with a small keeper's residence attached.[^1] The Register describes a "combined tower and keeper's dwelling" that speaks "to the lightkeeping tradition at remote locations in the nineteenth century."[^2]

In 1861 eight lamps and reflectors in the lantern gave a fixed white light. LighthouseFriends says the lamps burned a mix of whale and vegetable oil, and that 205 gallons were used each year. The lamps were adapted to coal oil in 1864, when whale oil had become too expensive.[^1] A house was built on Nicholson Island in 1862 as the keeper's winter quarters, because the stone dwelling on Scotch Bonnet was fit only for summer use.[^1] A new iron lantern went on the tower in 1882.[^1]

In 1909 a fourth-order, 360-degree French lens replaced the lamps and reflectors. The Department of Marine's report for 1909–10 records: "Scotch Bonnet. A 4th order 360° French lens with a 35 mm. Diamond vapour installation and adjustable iron stand replaces the old catoptric light."[^3]

## The keepers

John Giroux, the first keeper, served three seasons before Samuel Wilson replaced him, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] Before Robert Pye took charge in 1877, none of the six keepers before him had served more than six years. LighthouseFriends suggests that the island's harsh conditions were the likely reason.[^1]

Pye stayed eighteen years, longer than any of the six keepers before him.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes him as a blacksmith. In 1878 the Superintendent of Lighthouses above Montreal, Darius Smith, inspected the station and reported that "Robert Pye is keeper, who has a family of three; he keeps everything in very good order." The same report describes the tower as "a white stone building, 54 feet high from base to vane."[^1]

In 1891, after Pye had closed the station for the season, he "found it exceedingly difficult to reach shore," and said he had "not known so much rough weather during any fall since his appointment fifteen years ago."[^1] He died in 1895 at sixty-nine. LighthouseFriends says many people wanted his widow or son appointed, but the post went to William Farley.[^1]

Later keepers were David O. Spencer (1898 to 1903), Cyrus R. Spencer (1903 to 1913), Benjamin Y. Cunningham (1913 to 1919) and Harold Spencer (1919).[^1] The Department's salary table for 1910–11 lists "Cyrus R. Spencer" with an appointment date of "April 7, 1903," read from the scan by its column position.[^4] The Department's 1912–13 list still names Cyrus R. Spencer at the station,[^5] but its 1913–14 list names Benjamin Y. Cunningham as keeper, appointed 9 May 1912.[^6] This story cannot settle when the change came.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In November 1854, while the tower was being built, the schooner Norfolk of Port Dover went ashore on Scotch Bonnet Island in a gale and was a total loss, according to LighthouseFriends. The captain and all hands, four in number, were lost. LighthouseFriends adds its own opinion that a resident keeper might have been able to help.[^1] The account rests on that one source.

## After the keepers

Lightkeeping on the island ended in 1919. LighthouseFriends says a new light, burning acetylene that it calls compressed in acetone, went into the lantern, the foghorn was discontinued, and the light was visible for one second in every four.[^1]

The stone tower was left standing when the 1959 skeletal tower went up. LighthouseFriends says the new tower replaced the stone lighthouse, which had begun to crumble.[^1] The Register calls the stone building "The ruins of this historic lighthouse," which "present a sharp contrast" to the steel tower of 1959.[^2] In 1979 the island became the Scotch Bonnet Island National Wildlife Area, to protect nesting waterbirds.[^1][^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by Environment Canada.[^1] The Register says visiting the wildlife area "is prohibited to protect colonies of nesting waterbirds," and that the lighthouse site "is not accessible to the public."[^2] LighthouseFriends says landing on the island is not permitted, and that a distant view of the lighthouse can be had from near the end of Highway 20, west of Wellington.[^1]

The Register says the lighthouse is a landmark for annual yacht races on Lake Ontario, and is "highly valued by the community as an integral part of the local heritage."[^2]
