Narraguagus Light stands on the east side of Pond Island, at the mouth of Narraguagus Bay in Washington County, Maine, near Milbridge.[^1][^3] Its circular tower was built in 1853, and through the nineteenth century it was the bay's main navigation light.[^1][^2][^3] The light was discontinued in 1933 or 1934, depending on the source, and the station has been in private hands since it was sold.[^1][^2]

## The place

Milbridge, at the north end of the bay, was a shipbuilding town with a deep harbor.[^1] The nomination adds that the town was also the shipping point for lumber cut upriver, principally at Cherryfield.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says that from the mid-1800s over 150 large ships were launched from the town's shipyards.[^2]

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1897 calls the boat slip "in a bad place for landing", and says ledges surround it and would be costly to remove.[^5] The report for the year ended 30 June 1896 lists a bell buoy on Jerry Ledge in Narraguagus Bay, established 13 August 1895.[^5]

## Building the light

On 3 March 1851 Congress appropriated $4,000 for a lighthouse on the east side of Pond Island, to guide vessels into the bay.[^2] The government bought three acres from Benjamin C. Stanwood, who farmed the island, for $270.[^2]

The sources name different authorities for the work. Lighthouse Friends says the superintendent of lights for the State of Maine supervised the construction of a cylindrical tower.[^2] The nomination names the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers as designer.[^1] The light went into service in April 1853.[^2] As first built, the station was a five-bay dwelling with the tower in its center, like the earlier stations at Grindle Point and Indian Island.[^1] The nomination places the building in the most active period of Maine lighthouse construction, which followed the organization of the Light-House Board in 1852.[^1]

In 1856 a fifth-order Fresnel lens replaced the original seven lamps and their 21-inch reflectors.[^2] The light stayed fixed white.[^2] The tower now stands nearly 31 feet (9.5 m) to the center of the lantern, and the nomination says it was reinforced with brick in 1894.[^1]

## The keepers

The Archive's roster lists fifteen principal keepers, from 1853 to 1933.[^3] Joseph Brown is the first name and served only in 1853.[^3] Wyman Collins replaced him, earned $350 a year and stayed until 1859.[^3][^2]

William C. Gott kept the light from 1893 to 1918, the longest term on the roster.[^3] Lighthouse Friends gives his annual salary as $540.[^2] In 1900 William Dutcher, an early advocate of bird protection, wrote to Maine keepers offering rewards for protecting nesting gulls.[^2] He offered Gott $25 if he would protect the birds and keep people from taking their eggs or shooting the birds until 1 October.[^2] Lighthouse Friends prints the passage addressed to Gott. An island resident, Elizabeth Hitchcock, had a great deal to do with the Gott family.[^2] She remembered Sunday evening hymn singings at the lighthouse, with her mother at a pump organ.[^2] Lighthouse Friends quotes her recollections.[^2]

After Gott came Lester Leighton (1919 to 1924), Alvah Robinson (1924 to 1926) and Charles E. Tracy (1926 to 1933).[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 4 August 1929 the Canadian schooner *Valdare* was dismasted in a violent storm and then wrecked on Petit Manan Bar.[^2][^7] The keeper and first assistant at Petit Manan Light, Pierre A. Fagonde and Earl B. Ashby, found her abandoned that evening but could not salvage her in the rough seas.[^7]

Her four officers and crew reached Narraguagus Light by rowboat.[^2] The Bulletin says they "took refuge" there and were cared for by Keeper Charles E. Tracy until the 16th of August.[^7] Lighthouse Friends says he looked after them for nearly two weeks.[^2] They left on 16 August in the ship's rowboat, bound for Nova Scotia about 95 miles away, with food the keeper had given them.[^7] The British consul general at Boston wrote a letter of thanks, and the Bulletin says the three keepers who helped were officially commended.[^7] Lighthouse Friends says Tracy was commended for his help.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1900 Congress approved $150 to buy land north of the station for a new boat landing.[^6][^2] The old landing was, in Lighthouse Friends' words, "hedged about with ledges."[^2] The old boathouse and boat slip moved to the new land in 1902.[^2]

Earlier work had already changed the station. In 1875 the Lighthouse Board reported that the keeper's dwelling had become "uninhabitable", and a frame house of five rooms was built for the keeper's family.[^2][^1] A fog bell, struck by hand, was added at the same time.[^2] In 1887 the north wing of the old brick dwelling became a workroom, and a cellar and cistern were built beneath it.[^1][^2] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 the Board reinforced the tower walls with brick and provided a new iron lantern deck.[^4]

Lighthouse Friends says the light was discontinued on 21 June 1933, when a lighted bell buoy 575 yards away took its place. Tracy was furloughed for a month or so and then assigned as first assistant keeper at Boon Island.[^2] The nomination says the station was deactivated in 1934, and the Archive gives 1934 as the year it was discontinued.[^1][^3]

The station was sold at auction to Wilbur Dameron in 1934.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says it has passed down through the Dameron family and is believed to be theirs still.[^2] The nomination says the federal government disposed of the complex in the 1930s, and that the lens has been removed.[^1]

## Today

The Archive lists the station as privately owned and not open to the public.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says the grounds, dwelling and tower are closed, and that the light is best seen from the water, where tour boats pass the island.[^2] None of the sources read describes a public way to reach the station on land.[^3]

The nomination describes what stands: a circular brick tower of nearly 31 feet (9.5 m), capped by a ten-sided lantern under a polygonal roof, joined by an L-shaped workroom to a clapboarded keeper's house.[^1] The Archive gives the tower's material as granite blocks, not brick.[^3]

The nomination for the National Register of Historic Places, received in October 1987, names the station as part of the Light Stations of Maine multiple-property listing, and finds it significant under criteria A and C. This copy of the form does not show the Keeper's decision.[^1] It describes the 1.5-acre site as in private ownership.[^1]
