Prudence Island Light stands on Sandy Point, at the east side of Prudence Island in Narragansett Bay, Rhode Island.[^2] Its octagonal granite tower was first built on Goat Island in Newport Harbor in 1823. In 1851 it was taken down, moved to Prudence Island and rebuilt, and it was first lit there on January 17, 1852.[^1][^2] The National Register nomination counts it among the few lights in the country that have served as an active light at two different sites.[^1]

## The place

The light sits on the main shipping route to Providence and Fall River, the nomination says.[^1] It "continues to serve as a guide for ships passing throughout the East Passage of Narragansett Bay en route to the Providence River and Mount Hope Bay."[^1]

The first light stood at the northern end of Goat Island in Newport Harbor.[^1] The nomination says it was first lit on January 1, 1824.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the tower's date as 1823 and says the old light was discontinued in 1842.[^2] The nomination says instead that it was abandoned in 1838, after a nearby and better-placed new light was finished.[^1] The two sources disagree about when the old light went dark. The Newport Harbor Light story covers the Goat Island tower's own history.

In 1850 Congress turned to Prudence Island.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives the appropriation as $3,500, made on September 28, 1850.[^2] The local lighthouse superintendent recommended moving the old Goat Island tower, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The nomination records the decision: instead of constructing a new tower on the island, "the old 1823 light at Goat Island would be taken down and reerected at Sandy Point."[^1]

## Building the light

A one-acre parcel at Sandy Point had been bought earlier in 1851 for $250.[^1] On October 15, 1851, a contract for $900 went to Horace Vaugh, the nomination's spelling, to take the tower down, move it and rebuild it.[^1] LighthouseFriends spells the contractor's name Horace Vaughn and dates the award to October 1851.[^2] The nomination says the old tower was "marked, numbered, disassembled, and then rebuilt on Prudence Island so that it closely resembled its earlier appearance."[^1]

Iron founders I. N. Stanley & Brother of Newport replaced the deck and lantern.[^1][^2] A plaque in the tower reads: "Erected under the superintendence of E. W. Lawton, Collector of the Port of Newport, 1851."[^1] The light was first lit on January 17, 1852.[^1][^2]

The keeper's dwelling was a one-and-a-half-story clapboard house about 200 feet (61 m) west of the tower, joined to it by an elevated boardwalk, according to the nomination.[^1] LighthouseFriends puts the house about 190 feet (58 m) away and says it had six rooms.[^2]

The first optic was a Winslow Lewis apparatus of six lamps with fourteen-inch reflectors. LighthouseFriends says a fifth-order Fresnel lens replaced it in 1855.[^2] The archive lists the original optic as a fifth-order Fresnel.[^3] Beside the light stood a fog bell on a wooden frame, according to the nomination.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the fog bell and its striking mechanism to August 10, 1884.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive's roster begins with Peleg Sherman, keeper from 1852 to 1853, whom LighthouseFriends calls the first keeper.[^3][^2] Henry Diman followed from 1853 to 1855; LighthouseFriends spells the name Dimond.[^3][^2]

Edward M. Spooner kept the light from 1855 to 1862.[^3] LighthouseFriends, citing a newspaper story from March 1856, tells of a wild duck that dashed through the lantern glass and furnished Spooner with his Sunday dinner.[^2]

Thomas J. Cory (1862 to 1875), Isaac Aldrich (1875 to 1886), John T. Clarke (1886 to 1887), Olney Coyle (1887 to 1888), John F. Follett (1888 to 1894), Thomas Burke (1894 to 1898) and Nathaniel Dodge (1898 to 1905) followed.[^3] The archive gives John T. Clarke and Olney Coyle about a year each.[^3]

Martin Thompson kept the light for 28 years, from 1905 to 1933, the longest tenure on the archive's list.[^3] Two indexes to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin record him as a keeper awarded the efficiency flag, first in the 1918 to 1923 index and again in the 1924 to 1929 index.[^5][^6]

Elmer V. Newton (1933 to 1937) and George T. Gustavus (1937 to 1939) followed.[^3] LighthouseFriends says Gustavus had joined the Lighthouse Service in 1910, served at several Massachusetts stations, and came to Prudence Island in 1937.[^2]

After Gustavus, the archive lists lamplighters at the light: Milton H. Chase (1939 to 1943), Sarah A. Chase (1943 to 1951), Milton H. Chase again (1951 to 1958), Roger C. Grant (1958 to 1961), Michael J. Bachini (1961 to 1969) and Marcy Bachini Dunbar (1969 to 1972).[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A hurricane in September 1938 destroyed nearly everything at the station. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin dates the storm to September 21, when it swept the North Atlantic coast.[^4] The nomination says the storm "destroyed all structures on the station grounds except for the tower."[^1]

Five people died. The nomination lists the keeper's wife, her son, two guests and a former keeper.[^1] The Bulletin's account names the keeper's wife and son, a former keeper, and a couple, Mr. and Mrs. Lynch, who had sought refuge in the dwelling.[^4] The Bulletin says the keeper's wife and son "were drowned when the dwelling was finally destroyed."[^4] The keeper himself was saved, though only after being swept more than a quarter mile from the station, the Bulletin says.[^4] LighthouseFriends identifies the retired keeper as Martin Thompson, who had kept the light until 1933.[^2][^3] It says an island resident pulled Keeper Gustavus to safety.[^2]

Years later Gustavus said that his family and houseguests had been "caught like rats in a trap," as LighthouseFriends quotes him.[^2]

## After the keepers

The nomination says that rather than rebuild the keeper's dwelling, the light was automated the following year, in 1939.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives a different sequence: the revolving lens was replaced by a fixed fourth-order lens, and a few island residents tended the light until it was automated in 1961.[^2] The sources disagree on the date. The archive's lamplighter list runs on to 1972.[^3]

In 1961 a diaphragm horn replaced the fog bell on the lantern gallery.[^1] A Coast Guard inspection in September 1980 found the tower in good condition.[^1]

In 2000 the Coast Guard gave the American Lighthouse Foundation a twenty-year caretaker contract for the light.[^2] After local protests, the arrangement passed to the Prudence Island Conservancy in August 2001.[^2][^7] Conservancy members repaired the tower's foundation and applied a new coat of paint in 2002.[^2] In July 2020 the General Services Administration announced that the light was excess to the Coast Guard's needs and available for transfer at no cost to eligible groups.[^2] In 2024 ownership passed to the Prudence Conservancy, and Senator Jack Reed visited the island to celebrate it.[^2][^7]

## Today

The light is an active aid to navigation. Its green light flashes every six seconds.[^1][^2] The nomination gives the tower as 28 feet (8.5 m) high; LighthouseFriends calls it thirty feet (9 m).[^1][^2]

The sources do not agree on who owns the light now. LighthouseFriends' history reports the 2024 transfer to the Prudence Conservancy, but its visitor notes still say the Coast Guard owns the light and leases it to the Conservancy.[^2] The archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^2] Visitors reach the island by ferry from Bristol.[^2]
