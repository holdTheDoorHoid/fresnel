Powles Head Light marks the eastern side of the entrance to Trepassey Harbour, on Newfoundland's southeast coast, west of Cape Race.[^1][^3] Nearly forty years passed between the first proposal for a light there, in 1863, and the light itself, in 1902. Buoys stood in for it in the meantime.[^1][^4]

## The place

Powles Head is the south end of a peninsula that forms the south shore of Trepassey Harbour. A narrow beach joins it to the mainland.[^1] In September 1863 Captain John Orlebar wrote to Governor Alexander Bannerman, proposing a light on Powles Head to guide ships into Trepassey Harbour.[^1]

The case for the light rested partly on the mail. Winter ice often blocked the eastern coast. If the weekly steamers could tie up safely at Trepassey, the post could go overland to St. John's, but no road ran from there toward the capital.[^1]

Orlebar, with Robert Oke, Superintendent of Lighthouses, picked the site, and Oke later drew up the plans.[^1] A road to Trepassey followed soon after, but it took several decades before the light was built.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1895 Richard White, Inspector of Lighthouses, visited Trepassey. He weighed Powles Head against Pounder Cove, which stands on the other side of the harbour entrance, and chose Powles Head. He noted that water was scarce there, but he thought springs might be found near the site.[^1]

White wrote in 1895 that a cast-iron tower, lighting apparatus and a Champion foghorn were on hand and ready for use. Even so the light did not come until 1902.[^1]

The first light was a wooden tower, square in plan, with sloping sides. Tower and wooden keeper's dwelling were both painted white, and a 9.1 m passage linked them.[^1] A fifth-order lens gave one-second flashes of light alternating with one-second dark spells, with its focal plane at 31 m.[^1]

The fog alarm used gun cotton. A four-ounce charge went off every twelve minutes in fog, and when a ship signalled, two quick explosions replied. Lighthouse Friends says gun cotton was chosen because water for steam was hard to get, and that the long gap between shots, kept for economy, made the signal poor.[^1] In 1907 a diaphone replaced it, giving five-second blasts with 112 seconds of silence.[^1]

In 1925 a round iron tower, painted white, replaced the wooden one.[^1][^4] The light's signature stayed the same.[^1]

By 1960 the iron tower had rusted badly. That year a combined light and fog-alarm building, with a square tower on one corner, replaced it.[^1][^3][^4] Two keepers' dwellings were built near the new light in 1961.[^1]

## The keepers

John Devereux was the first keeper, with J. Waddleton as his assistant.[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives no years for either man.[^1] The last keeper it names is Tom Corrigan, who lived at the site for a while after he retired.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Buoys stood in for the light, and they did not last.[^1][^4] A Courtenay automatic whistling buoy was anchored off Powles Head in June 1880. It drifted off that September, was salvaged and moored again, then broke loose and was destroyed.[^1]

A replacement buoy, placed in 1882, was dragged ashore by ice in February 1883. After that it was taken out of service during the winter months.[^1]

On 4 December 1890 the buoy broke from its moorings and went ashore near Cape Mutton. The men who had recovered it held out for $80 before handing it back to the government.[^1]

The buoy went adrift again and was lost in 1895, and plans were made for a light and fog alarm in its place.[^1]

## After the keepers

The station was connected to commercial power in 1970.[^1][^4] In 1972 the diaphone was replaced by an electronic signal.[^1]

In 1990 the station was cut back to a one-person post, and the assistant keeper's dwelling was later removed.[^1] The Newfoundland 3D Lighthouse Gallery says the keeper's dwelling was removed that year, making the station unmanned.[^4]

## Today

The Coast Guard list, dated 24 September 2026, gives the light as a white flash every ten seconds, with a focal height of 30.8 m and a nominal range of 22 nautical miles. The tower is red and white, cylindrical and 2.4 m high. The fog horn gives a four-second blast, followed by 56 seconds of silence.[^2]

Rowlett describes a 9 m square tower with a lantern and a gallery. It notes that the Coast Guard lists a 2.4 m tower, and says the light appeared active in a 2019 photograph.[^3]

Staffing is disputed. Lighthouse Friends says the station is no longer staffed.[^1] The Newfoundland 3D Lighthouse Gallery says it has been unmanned since 1990.[^4] Rowlett describes a 1961 keeper's house as occupied by a keeper, but gives no date for that.[^3] The Fisheries and Oceans brochure, with a page date of 2019-09-24, lists Powles Head among the staffed lightstations.[^5] Those accounts cannot all be current, and this story does not choose between them.

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse. The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1][^3] Lighthouse Friends gives the drive from Route 10 in Trepassey as 6.7 km, the last 4 km of it dirt road.[^1] Rowlett describes an access road of 5 km of gravel, where walking or a four-wheel drive is recommended.[^3] Do not enter the closed tower.
