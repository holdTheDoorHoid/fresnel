New London Harbor Light stands on the west side of the harbor entrance at New London, Connecticut, near the mouth of the Thames River.[^1][^2] A light has been shown on that spot since 1760, and the stone tower that stands there today was built in 1801.[^1][^2] Its National Register form calls it the oldest lighthouse still standing in Connecticut.[^1]

## The place

The approach to the harbor was hard in thick weather.[^4] In its report for the year ended 30 June 1896, the Light-House Board wrote that "the numerous outlying shoals and ledges surrounding the entrance to this harbor make the approach to it dangerous in thick weather."[^4] The same report said the station's location was the problem. The light and fog-signal station was "so far inside the obstructions as to be partially ineffective as an aid for the purpose of safe navigation of this entrance," and the port's commerce had increased since the present light was erected, enough to "change the conditions materially."[^4]

The Board proposed a light and steam fog-signal station on the southwest ledge, on the eastern side of the entrance.[^7] The reports headed the proposal "Black Ledge, New London Harbor."[^7][^11] In the early 1890s the Board estimated the work at $45,000 and recommended an appropriation of that amount.[^7] It repeated the request in report after report. By 1900 it described the recommendation as made in "eight recent annual reports of the Board."[^11] In 1911 the fog signal was moved from this station to the new New London Ledge Lighthouse, a light just offshore.[^1][^2]

## Building the light

The first tower was paid for by a lottery.[^1] "The original lighthouse was funded through a lottery at a time when lighthouses were built individually by local shipping interests," the National Register form says.[^1] In 1760 the colonial legislature of Connecticut passed an act creating a committee to pursue the funding, construction and staffing of a new lighthouse, and thousands of lottery tickets were sold the next year.[^2] Nathaniel Shaw sold the site to the government and served as the first keeper.[^2][^3]

The sources differ on the date of the first light. The National Register form says the first lighthouse on the Sound was established on that site in 1760. LighthouseFriends says the 64-foot (19.5 m) stone tower was finished in 1761.[^1][^2]

When the federal government took over the lights in 1789, the old tower had developed "a crack ten feet long" in its wall, and its height was judged too small to be clearly visible from the west.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the cession of the light to the federal government to May 1790.[^2] Congress appropriated $15,700 for a replacement in 1800. The National Register form dates the appropriation to 5 May 1800, and LighthouseFriends to 7 May.[^1][^2] The contract went to A. Woodward of New London for $16,500, $800 more than the appropriation. Work began in 1800 and the new tower was finished in 1801.[^1]

The new light burned oil lamps with an eclipser, a device that made the light flash.[^1][^2] The National Register form says the lamps and eclipser were installed only five years after the first use in the United States of an "intermittent lighting device" at Cape Cod.[^1]

By 1833 the 1801 tower needed extensive repair.[^1] A contractor, Charles H. Smith, agreed to put in a new stone deck, a new lantern and copper dome, a new stair and a new outer door, for repairs that were to cost $1,500.[^1]

The fixed Fresnel lens came later. The National Register form says that within the decade after 1850 "a Fresnel lens was Installed," and that the Henry Lepaute fixed lens now housed at the station "may be the original equipment."[^1] LighthouseFriends gives 1857 as the year a fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed, and says it remains in the lighthouse today.[^2] The two sources give different dates for the lens.

The keeper's house was replaced more than once.[^1] A new one was contracted in 1818 to Kimball Prince and Lewis Crandall of New London for $1,200, and a kitchen wing was added in 1836.[^1] The present brick dwelling dates from 1863.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists Prince and Crandell as contractors, with no dates.[^3]

The fog signal was improved again and again.[^1] The National Register form records a fog-signal engine in place by 1869. In 1877 it describes a second-class Daboll trumpet in duplicate, operated by a hot-air engine and housed in a brick building 148 feet (45 m) from the tower. The form gives 1,165 hours of fog signal for 1877, and a first-class trumpet was installed in 1883.[^1] In one of the Board's reports of 1891 and 1892, the first-class trumpet "was in operation about 552 hours, and consumed about 10 tons of coal."[^8]

In December 1896 the fog trumpet changed over to oil engines.[^6] The Board's report says the trumpet was "worked by caloric engines in duplicate until December 21, 1896, and since then by Hornsby-Akroyd oil engines in duplicate," and that it ran "some 541 hours during the year."[^6] The new plant used two 3½-horsepower Hornsby-Akroyd engines and air compressors.[^1][^5]

The Board's 1899 report already lists the old fog-signal house as converted into an oil house, and its 1900 report records the same change.[^9][^10] The National Register form dates the conversion to 1900.[^1] The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1900 records an addition to the keeper's dwelling to provide quarters for the assistant keeper.[^10] The National Register form says that before this the assistant's post had been open only to single men, and that the Service thought providing accommodation for married assistants could "attract and train good candidates."[^1] The dwelling was raised one story in 1900.[^1]

## The keepers

The Light-House Board's regulations of 1864 required keepers to keep their premises clean and well whitewashed.[^12] That was a general standard of the service, not a record of what happened at New London.[^12]

Nathaniel Shaw is first on the roster, from 1761 to at least 1771.[^3] Daniel Harris is listed at least from 1775 to 1811, then Griswold Harris (1811 to 1826) and Jeremiah Harris (1826 to 1831).[^3] Terms in the 1840s and 1850s were often short.[^3]

The keepership changed with politics. LighthouseFriends reports that after William Henry Harrison, a Whig, defeated President Martin Van Buren, many Whigs in New London pressed the new Treasury secretary, Thomas Ewing, to dismiss keeper John G. Munn, a vocal supporter of Van Buren.[^2] Munn was dismissed in 1841 and replaced by John Mason on 1 July 1841.[^2] Munn reportedly blackened the reflectors and put the lamps in such disorder that it took weeks for Mason to repair the mischief.[^2] Mason was removed within four months and Munn was reappointed. When Munn died shortly afterwards, Mason was appointed keeper again on 8 December 1841.[^2] The roster shows John Mason, Jr., for 1841, and John Mason, Sr., from 1841 to 1844.[^3]

Charles A. Bunnell kept the light from 1869 to 1889, and Charles A. Bunnell, Jr., served as an assistant from 1872 to 1890. Charles B. Field followed as head keeper from 1889 to 1910.[^3] Assistants rotated through the station: George L. Potter (1890 to 1895), Joseph D. Burke (1895 to 1899), Elmer J. Rathbun (1899 to 1909) and John J. Price (1909 to 1910).[^3]

Theodore DeShong was head keeper for a year.[^3] Joseph F. Woods, who had been an assistant in 1911, held the post from 1911 to 1912.[^3] LighthouseFriends calls Woods the last head keeper of New London Harbor. It says he left on 26 July 1912, and that a custodian was put in charge of the property the next day.[^2] The archive's person record for Woods also lists him as head keeper at Saybrook Breakwater from 1912 to 1918.[^13]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The War of 1812 put the light out.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says the light was "extinguished" during the war, and the National Register form says it was darkened.[^2][^1]

On 30 May 1912, in dense fog, a steam launch from the battleship North Dakota nearly ran onto a ledge about 500 feet (150 m) east of the light.[^2] Woods, in an account LighthouseFriends prints, says he warned her off with the hand horn.[^2] A smaller launch following her "did not seem to hear the horn" and went onto the ledge, which stove several holes in her hull.[^2] The men got into the water and cried for help.[^2] The station had no boat, so Woods got on his bicycle to summon help.[^2] The North Dakota's launch came back, but it had no small boat, so Woods borrowed a rowboat and pulled the men out of the water.[^2]

The fog signal drew complaints as well.[^2] When a new siren went into service in the summer of 1903, residents complained of its "horrible groaning and shrieking," LighthouseFriends reports, while local ship captains liked the sound in a pea-soup fog.[^2] The problem was resolved on 1 February 1906, when the siren was replaced by a Daboll trumpet.[^2]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1912, LighthouseFriends says, and the property was auctioned off as two separate parcels.[^2] The keeper's dwelling next door was sold in 1928 for $21,100.[^2] The National Register listed the lighthouse in 1990.[^2]

The Coast Guard declared the tower excess under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000, and in October 2009 the New London Maritime Society became its legal steward.[^2] The deed was presented at a ceremony on 13 October 2010, and the first guided tours took place in May 2012.[^2] Superstorm Sandy washed out the retaining walls at the base of the tower in 2012.[^2] In 2014 volunteers scaffolded and repainted the tower. The Society had raised $150,000 for the restoration.[^2]

The neighborhood pushed back. In 2014 residents to the north sued the Society over alleged trespassing and disruptive activities, and shortly afterwards the occupants of the keeper's dwelling tried to block land access.[^2] The city's zoning board then decided that the lighthouse could not be operated as a museum in a residential zone.[^2] The Society raised $175,000 in 2015 to build a dock for water access.[^2] A superior court judge ruled in 2018 that limited visits to the lighthouse by land were possible under existing zoning.[^2] In 2019 the zoning board allowed up to twelve tours a week, each with no more than six people.[^2]

## Today

The New London Maritime Society owns the tower.[^2] The keeper's house beside it is privately owned.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that the tower and surrounding land are off-limits to the public except during the Society's special tours.[^2] Those tours are limited by the zoning decision described above, so check with the Society before you plan a visit, and do not go onto the private grounds. Limited views are available from the sidewalk on Pequot Avenue, which runs alongside the Thames River near its entrance from Long Island Sound, and the best views are from the water.[^2]
