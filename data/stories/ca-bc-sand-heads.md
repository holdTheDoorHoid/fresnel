Sand Heads Light stands at the outer end of the Steveston Jetty, a long breakwater on the north side of the Fraser River's entrance, where the river empties into the Strait of Georgia just south of Vancouver.[^1][^2] The station began as a lightship in 1866, and it has alternated between lightships and lighthouses since.[^2][^3] The present tower, on piles, was completed in 2002.[^2][^3]

## The place

The sources describe a river mouth that never stayed put. LighthouseFriends says that about twenty million tons of sediment reached the Strait of Georgia from the river each year, and that the mouth's position kept changing as a result.[^2] In the spring of 1859, petitioners asked for a lightship to mark the mouth of the river.[^2] BC Genesis, which cites Andrew Scott's *Encyclopedia of Raincoast Place Names*, says that gold prospectors demanded a light there in 1859, but that the government did not buy a vessel and anchor it offshore until 1865.[^11]

LighthouseFriends says the vessel was built at New Westminster in 1865 and anchored on the south side of the river's entrance in 1866, under the name South Sand Heads.[^2] Rowlett's directory dates the lightship station to 1866 as well.[^3]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says that Parliament appropriated $14,000 for a lighthouse at the river mouth, and that the contract for building it went to Thomas McKay of New Westminster for $9,500.[^2] Until a lighthouse could be built, the schooner *Eliza* was placed on the station under contract from 4 May 1880.[^2]

The tower was a hexagonal wooden structure, about 15 m (49 ft) from its base to the vane on its lantern, standing on an iron screw-pile foundation. An old fog bell from Race Rocks was hung on it, and a third-order Fresnel lens showed a fixed white light from a focal plane about 16 m (52 ft) high.[^2]

The sources differ on the year. LighthouseFriends says the light began operating in May 1884, and Rowlett gives 1884 as the year the station was established.[^2][^3] BC Genesis gives 1879 for the iron-pile lighthouse.[^11] The sources do not agree, and this story gives both dates.

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends lists the keepers at Sand Heads from 1872. The first named are R. Hicks (1872), James Jolly (1872 to 1880), and Joseph Middleton (1881 to 1884).[^2] John Sproat, appointed as the first keeper, declined the post, and Hugh H. Fraser was put in temporary charge. LighthouseFriends says that Fraser and his assistant, Frederick Williams, drowned after a short time at the light.[^2] Later keepers, by the same list, were R. Westmorland (1884 to 1885), R. Douglas (1885 to 1886), W.P. Daykin (1886 to 1888), John R. Wick (1889 to 1891), and R.S. Jones (1891 to 1892).[^2]

In April 1892 an assistant keeper reached Vancouver to report abuse by a drunken keeper. LighthouseFriends suggests the keeper was probably R.S. Jones. When the assistant tried to escape in the station's rowboat, the keeper woke and threw a sandbag at him from the tower. Captain Lewis of the tender *Quadra* sailed out, removed the keeper, and put two of his crew in charge of the light.[^2]

Hamilton Armour is listed as keeper from 1892 to 1898, though LighthouseFriends' text gives the end as 1897. LighthouseFriends says he helped save the lives of several fishermen.[^2] Thomas W. Harvey (1898 to 1901) and H. Greenway (1901 to 1903) followed.[^2] The Department's keepers' list for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1902 has a "Greenway, H j Sands Head" entry, which matches the station and the years.[^8]

Michael O'Brien kept the station from 1904 to 1924. LighthouseFriends says he had served in the U.S. Lighthouse Service, and that he was in charge when the lightship was placed on station in 1905.[^2] The Department's keepers' lists for the fiscal years ending 31 March 1908 and 31 March 1911 both print "M. O'Brien" against the Sand Heads lightship.[^6][^7] LighthouseFriends says that Edwin Leonard Janes, whom it calls Captain E.L. Janes, had charge of the lightship from 1924 until 1955.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A heavy westerly gale in November 1871 badly damaged the first vessel. During the repairs her fog bell was moved to a place near the crew's cabin, and LighthouseFriends says the keepers were less vigilant in ringing it afterwards.[^2] On 20 April 1878 the South Sand Heads lightship capsized in a heavy gale. She was patched up and returned to her station, but in 1879 she was withdrawn and offered for sale.[^2]

On 24 November 1905 the river lightship broke away from her moorings. The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1906, printed in 1907, says she was replaced on her station at the entrance to the river, with repairs to her garboards and other work, at a total cost of $651.[^5]

On 22 October 1911, during dense fog, a tug the report lists as "No-name" collided with the American steamer *Iroquois* of Seattle off Sand Heads. The tug was a total loss, and no lives were lost.[^10] LighthouseFriends says that Captain Janes told a reporter that the lightship had been rammed by freighters lost in fog on at least three occasions. It says she was driven ashore in 1947 and again in 1955, neither time with loss of life.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Department's annual report printed in 1906 records that the lightship was established on 18 October 1905, that the old schooner *Mermaid* had been bought and converted for the purpose, and that the light on the pile lighthouse "was permanently discontinued" once the lightship's light was shown.[^4] The lightship therefore replaced the lighthouse.

LighthouseFriends says that in 1912 a crew under C. Fischer was sent to dismantle the tower. The tower was so hard to detach from its foundation that it was instead doused with kerosene and burned.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says the Department bought the schooner *Thomas F. Bayard* in 1913 to replace the *Mermaid*, for $4,500.[^2] The Department's report for the fiscal year 1912-13, printed in 1914, lists the purchase of "schooner Bayard to be used as a lightship replacing Fraser River Sand Heads lightship."[^9] LighthouseFriends says she was fitted with a one-inch diaphone fog signal and a submarine bell, and christened *Sand Heads No. 16*.[^2]

In 1956 a new light was placed in service just off the outer end of a rock jetty. It stood on a pier of steel piles encased in concrete, 10.4 m by 14.0 m (34 by 46 ft). LighthouseFriends says that after the lightship's second grounding, repairs were judged too costly, and another lighthouse was built on the north side of the river's entrance.[^2]

The old lightship passed to the Vancouver Maritime Museum in 1978, according to LighthouseFriends. She sank at her mooring on 6 September 2002, and part of her mast is on display at the museum.[^2]

A geological study released in 1992 found that the lighthouse was in danger of collapse, because unstable sediment on the seabed was shifting. The three lightkeepers were reassigned elsewhere, and the light was automated.[^2] The present tower was completed in 2002. Rowlett describes it as a 7.5 m (25 ft) round cylindrical tower, with two equipment shelters, on a platform carried on piles.[^2][^3]

## Today

The light is an active Coast Guard aid, shown as a green flash every five seconds, with a focal height of 13.3 m (43 ft) and a nominal range of 16 nautical miles.[^1][^3]

Rowlett says the site is open but the tower is closed, and that the station is reached only by boat.[^3] Visitors should not try to land on the jetty or climb the tower.
