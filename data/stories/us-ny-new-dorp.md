New Dorp Light stands on a hill above the New Dorp section of Staten Island, a wooden range light that once guided ships through the Swash Channel into New York Bay. It was built in the mid-1850s, the Coast Guard decommissioned it in 1964, and it is privately owned today.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

In 1855 the Light-House Board reported on three ranges to mark Lower New York Bay. One of them was "the range formed by Elm Tree Lighthouse and New Dorp Lighthouse," which marked the Swash Channel.[^2] A ship steered so the two lights lined up, until the Chapel Hill pair came into view, and the second pair then took it past the West Bank.[^2]

The 1967 landmark report says the light, when in operation, "could be seen from ships far down lower New York Bay."[^3]

The land was hard to reach. LighthouseFriends describes the government's right-of-way as a narrow path through the trees, too narrow for a horse and wagon. Keepers had used the road through the Moravian Cemetery, until the cemetery "rescinded that privilege in 1878."[^2]

## Building the light

The sources do not agree on the date. The 1967 landmark report says the building was "Built about 1854," and that the station "was established in 1856."[^3] LighthouseFriends says a contract dated 11 June 1855 was made with a New York builder, Richard Calrow, jr., for six range beacons at $19,124, to be finished by 25 October 1855.[^2] The U.S. Lighthouse Society record gives 1856 for the tower, the keeper's quarters and the first lens.[^1]

LighthouseFriends describes the keeper's dwelling as four rooms on a brick foundation, topped by a six-foot (1.8 m) square tower that rose just over forty feet (about 12 m) above the ground.[^2] The U.S. Lighthouse Society record gives 40 feet (12.2 m) from base to lantern centre.[^1] The original beacon was a second-order range lens showing a fixed light.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the light source changed from oil to incandescent oil vapor in 1907.[^2] In 1924 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists the "Elm Tree beacon and New Dorp beacon (Swash Channel range)" among lights changed from "incandescent oil vapor to electric incandescent."[^5] A second 1924 listing gives the Swash Channel range's earlier illuminant as plain oil, changed to "oil to electric incandescent."[^6] The two 1924 listings disagree about what the light burned before the change.[^5][^6] In 1939 a sixth-order range lens was installed, still showing a fixed white light.[^2]

## The keepers

The first keeper was John B. Fountain, who was appointed at a salary of $400 a year. LighthouseFriends says "he resigned after just a few months."[^2][^1] Philip G. Bender kept the light from 1855 to 1861. LighthouseFriends says he "would mind the light for six years."[^1][^2]

John Wild kept it from 1861 to 1867, and again from 1867 to 1877. The roster also lists Andrew J. Olsen for 1867, between Wild's two stints.[^1]

John F. Langton was principal keeper from 1877 to 1901, the longest term on the roster.[^1] LighthouseFriends spells him Langston, and says that for eleven years after the cemetery road was closed he had to carry supplies to the station by hand or on horseback.[^2]

The roster lists Annie C. Langton as principal keeper from 1901 to 1902. The sources do not say how she came to the post, or how she was related to him.[^1] Joseph Coons kept the light from 1902 to 1908, and Mary C. Coons is listed for 1908.[^1] Theodore DeShong (1908 to 1910) and Henry A. Whaley (1910 to 1917) followed.[^1]

John R. Carlsson was principal keeper from 1917 to 1940.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he was a native of Finland who came to the United States in 1896 and joined the Lighthouse Service the next year. He served on Sandy Hook Lightship and at two other stations before being placed in charge of New Dorp. He died in 1953 and was buried in the Moravian Cemetery, near the light.[^2]

Sidney Z. Gross (1940 to 1944) and Lester Thompson (1944 to 1959) came next, and the roster's last line is Charles Jones, from 1959.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

No wreck at the station itself appears in these sources.

The light was discontinued "as a war measure" in April 1898. The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 gives the date as 28 April, and says it was relighted on 1 August. The report does not name the war. Other lights in the lower bay, including Point Comfort and Conover, were discontinued on the same day.[^4]

LighthouseFriends describes a 1922 newspaper article that blamed "phantom shadows" from an incandescent oil vapour lamp at New Dorp, which it calls the "New Dorp ghost", for a mounted policeman's horse throwing its rider.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service passed the station to the Coast Guard in 1939. The Coast Guard decommissioned the light on 15 June 1964. Its light was darkened, and the windows of the house were boarded up.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the range lights had been replaced by offshore channel markers.[^2]

In 1974 a Staten Island resident bought the neglected property at auction for $32,000, and spent a long time restoring the tower.[^2]

## Today

New York City's Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the light a landmark in 1967. In a letter to the Commission, a representative of the federal government questioned the proposal, saying that "Federally-owned property is not subject to regulation by local law." The commission noted the question of its jurisdiction.[^3]

The lighthouse is privately owned. LighthouseFriends says the grounds, dwelling and tower are closed, that no beacon shines from it now, and that an oil house and a barn also remain on the property.[^2] Do not go onto private land to look at the tower.
