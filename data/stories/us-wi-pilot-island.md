Pilot Island is a 3.5-acre (1.4 ha) island of rock and stone in the Porte des Morts passage, "Death's Door," off the tip of Wisconsin's Door County peninsula.[^1] Lighthouse Friends places it at the eastern entrance to the passage, which links Lake Michigan and Green Bay.[^5] Its brick lighthouse has stood since 1858, and the island is now a bird sanctuary that the public may not visit.[^2][^3]

## The place

The passage had its name before it had a light. The National Register nomination says early French fur traders gave it the name for its wrecks, and that fog often covered the island.[^1] By the 1840s, it says, immigrants arriving to farm the peninsula had greatly increased the number of vessels using the passage, and Congress approved a lighthouse there on 14 August 1848, with $3,500.[^1] That first light went up on Plum Island, about two miles (3 km) west of the later site.[^1][^4][^5] Sources differ on its date: the nomination says a first light was established in 1850, Lighthouse Friends says a stone tower and dwelling were built there in 1848, and the Friends of Plum and Pilot Islands say the Plum Island light was abandoned after only nine years of service, when the Pilot Island light was built in 1858.[^1][^4][^5] Lighthouse Friends says it was soon apparent that the light stood too far inside the passage, and the nomination says it was not considered effective, so a new light was built.[^1][^5]

## Building the light

The island was withdrawn from the public domain by Executive Order on 28 May 1858 for lighthouse use, and the nomination records that it was first called Porte des Morts Island, the light Porte des Morts Light.[^1] The name Pilot Island Light was made official on 1 December 1910, although the 1875 Lighthouse Board report had already used it.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the construction crew arrived in 1858 and had to blast into the island's dolomite to make room for a basement.[^5] The keeper's house was cream-coloured brick, with a square tower rising through the gable at the west end and a decagonal cast-iron lantern holding a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^1][^5] The nomination gives the house as 45 by 32 feet (14 by 10 m), while Lighthouse Friends says 35 feet (11 m) square, and the nomination puts the light 46 feet (14 m) above the water in one place and 48 feet in another.[^1][^5]

The Friends date a first-class steam siren to 1875 and a duplicate building in 1880; in 1894 the sirens gave way to 10-inch steam whistles, first sounded on 18 May 1894.[^4][^11] The Light-House Board's reports give the first-class siren about 226 hours of operation in the fiscal year ended June 1893 (July 1892 to June 1893), and the duplicate 10-inch whistles about 548 hours in the fiscal year ended June 1895.[^12][^13] A brick fog-signal building with compressed-air machinery replaced the older equipment in 1904, according to the Friends and Lighthouse Friends; the nomination dates the brick building about 1900 and marks that date as an estimate.[^4][^5][^1]

## The keepers

William Shurtleff, who had kept the Plum Island light, moved across to Pilot Island in 1858 as its first keeper, with Royal F. Riggins as assistant; the archive lists both as leaving in 1859.[^6][^4] After a run of short terms, Victor E. Rohn kept the light from 1866 to 1876.[^6] Lighthouse Friends says he had been a Union Army lieutenant and arrived with his wife and seven children; the archive lists Jane A. Rohn as first assistant for the same years.[^5][^6] On 4 July 1874, Lighthouse Friends says, a southeast gale kept him from the Fourth of July celebration on Washington Island.[^5] His log entry read: "This island affords about as much independence and liberty as Libby Prison," with the difference, he added, that the guards favoured the island and the chance of outside communication favoured the prison.[^7][^8]

Members of the Knudsen family ran the station for much of the 1880s and 1890s. The archive lists Peter Knudsen as keeper 1883 to 1888, Nelson 1888 to 1889, and Martin N. Knudsen 1889 to 1897; Lighthouse Friends describes them as brothers.[^6][^5] Martin had earlier served as second assistant, from 1880 to 1882, following the death of John Boyce, the previous second assistant.[^6] The nomination reports that Boyce (spelled Boice there) cut his throat on 20 June 1880, and the Friends and Lighthouse Friends describe his death as suicide.[^1][^4][^5] Lighthouse Friends says many people blamed the isolation of the station, while a relative insisted the cause was a failed romantic relationship.[^5] The nomination calls the ten-inch steam whistle, which sounded every thirty seconds in fog, the thing that made the station so trying, and says its vibration killed chicks in the egg and curdled milk within minutes; the other sources used here do not mention this.[^1]

In 1899 Frank Drew became second assistant, and the station held three families at once for the first time. Lighthouse Friends says he built a small shack of salvaged wood so his family could live with him; it was abandoned in 1901, when the first fog-signal building was remodelled as quarters for the second assistant.[^5][^4] In December 1898 assistant keeper Peder H. Pedersen drowned when a sailboat capsized on the way to Detroit Island; his colleague Charles Boshka survived.[^4][^5] Later keepers include Walter Ottosen (1914 to 1920) and Clarence J. Anderson, assistant from 1922 and keeper from 1935 to 1945.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the autumn of 1892 two schooners struck the reef off the island. The nomination calls the fall of 1892, when keeper Martin Knudson saved the crews of the *Nichols* and the *Gilmore*, the most dramatic of the island's rescues.[^1] The Life-Saving Service's medal list records that on the night of 28 October 1892 he waded to a sunken wreck, exposed to the waves and the falling spars, and guided and assisted the ship's company of eight persons to the shore.[^9] The nomination dates the stranding 4 November and says six people were saved; the Life-Saving Service's date and count are used here.[^1][^9] Captain David E. Clow Jr., in a statement Lighthouse Friends quotes, said the keeper "picked his way through the surf along the rocks" in the dark.[^5] The crew of the *Gilmore* (the nomination writes *J. C. Gilmore*, the Friends *J.E. Gilmore*) were already sheltering in the dwelling, and the storm had kept everyone from reaching the mainland until it eased.[^1][^4] Clow said the two crews made big inroads on the keeper's provisions.[^5] The Friends and Lighthouse Friends say Knudsen received a silver lifesaving medal for the *Nichols*; Lighthouse Friends adds a gold medal from a New York benevolent society for the *Gilmore*.[^4][^5]

On 20 January 1913, according to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin, keeper Henry R. Bevry, assistants Alfred L. Cornell and Royal G. Petersen, and laborer Peter Anderson towed a motor boat carrying the mail, frozen in the ice about 10 miles (16 km) from the station, back to the station, and rescued three men in a rowboat who were looking for it. The Bulletin adds that it was reported the keepers were 72 hours without sleep or rest.[^10]

## After the keepers

On 13 June 1962 the Coast Guard removed its people from the island, put in an automatic light and removed the fog signal.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says an individual rented the island until 1981, after which Sea Scouts used it as a camp, and that it passed to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 2007.[^5] The Friends of Plum and Pilot Islands formed in 2008 to preserve the historic structures on both islands, and they work with the Service on repairs.[^4][^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is on the National Register of Historic Places (reference 83004279).[^2] The island is part of the Green Bay National Wildlife Refuge, managed as a colonial nesting-bird area, and it is closed to the public; the 1904 fog-signal building is a ruin.[^3][^14] Lighthouse Friends says the lighthouse can be seen distantly from the Northport to Washington Island ferry.[^5] The Friends report roof repairs in 2025 and 2026.[^2]
