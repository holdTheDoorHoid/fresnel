Punta Tuna is a narrow rock formation on the southeast corner of Puerto Rico, in the municipality of Maunabo.[^1][^4] A Spanish-built lighthouse has stood on it since the early 1890s and still helps ships round that corner.[^1][^2][^4]

## The place

The point is the south-easternmost tip of the island, on the east side of the harbour of Maunabo. The National Register nomination describes the lighthouse as sitting on the tip of a rock whose cliffs plunge about 72 feet (22 m) into the sea, with the Caribbean on the west, south and east.[^1] Its job was to join two lines of lights. It was the most eastern primary light of the island's southern chain and, at the same time, the southern light of the eastern chain, which also included Cabo San Juan and the minor lights at Puerto Ferro, Punta Mulas and Isla Culebrita.[^1]

## Building the light

The Spanish built the station.[^2][^4] The Puerto Rican encyclopedia EnciclopediaPR credits the design to Joaquín Gisbert and the supervision of construction to Adrián Duffaut.[^3] The dates differ between sources. The U.S. Light-House Board's 1901 report says the light "was begun in 1891 and was finished in 1893" at a cost of 26,500 pesos.[^2] The National Register nomination says the lighthouse was constructed in 1893.[^1] EnciclopediaPR says the light began to operate on 18 November 1892,[^3] and the U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive also records 1892 as the year the station was established.[^5]

The building was a one-storey brick-and-stone dwelling with an octagonal tower rising from its centre.[^1][^2] The nomination says it was planned for two keepers, one of a higher class and one of a lower, in two identical sets of rooms: a living room, two bedrooms, a kitchen-dining room, a storeroom and a bathroom on each side of the entrance vestibule.[^1] A cast-iron spiral stair climbs 48 feet (14.5 m) to a cast-iron, copper and glass lantern.[^1] The lens, still in the tower when the nomination was written, is a third-order Fresnel lens made in 1891 by Barbier, Bernard & Cie., with six flashing panels, and it produced a group of two flashes as the whole lens revolved.[^1] A clockwork mechanism driven by a 200-pound weight, hanging down the centre of the stair, turned it.[^1][^4]

The nomination lists changes to the light's characteristics in 1914, 1927, 1935, 1964 and 1970, when its range was increased to 25 miles. It also says the ball-bearing mechanism under the lens was replaced in 1927 and that the clockwork was used until 1939.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that by 1930 the lens was turned faster, and that in 1935 a new reinforced-concrete dwelling was built and the station electrified.[^4] EnciclopediaPR instead dates a remodelling of the building, with a new entrance hall and a concrete roof in place of the brick one, to the 1960s.[^3] The nomination, written about 1980, guesses that the small entrance hall was added "perhaps in the 1950's", so the sources do not agree on when.[^1]

## The keepers

After the Spanish-American War the U.S. Lighthouse Service took over the island's lights. LighthouseFriends dates the takeover to 1 May 1900.[^4] The Light-House Board's report for 1901 records repairs at Punta Tuna that year. The rain-water cistern leaked so badly that the keepers had no water "except such as was brought from a distance", so it was repaired in March 1901; the road to the landing was also graded and repaired.[^2]

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive, drawing on the keeper registers, lists José P. Barrios as principal keeper in 1901, followed by Eulalio Hernández, Julio L. R. Nater and Julio L. Rengel. Guillermo Morris served until 1917, and José P. Castillo served from 1917 to 1935, then Felix R. Giraldet to 1941 and José A. Ramírez to 1945.[^5] Assistants included Gabriel C. Bonilla, Pedro Albino González (1917 to 1920) and Agustín S. Cruz (1921 to 1935).[^5]

One family story is told by LighthouseFriends. Pedro Albino Jr. was born at Punta Tuna in 1919, while his father, Pedro Albino Sr., was assistant keeper there. The father served at six lighthouses in all, and the son joined the Coast Guard in 1946.[^4] In 1947 the son returned to his birthplace as officer in charge with nine men under him. That October a violent thunderstorm passed over, and lightning struck a transformer near the lighthouse, which exploded and left the light and radiobeacon dead. While he waited for a technician he worked from an electronics manual and, by LighthouseFriends' account, had both restored by just after midnight; the commander of the Tenth Coast Guard District sent him a letter of commendation.[^4] This account is from LighthouseFriends alone. The archive's roster does not name him, and lists Domingo Esperanza as principal keeper until 1947.[^5]

## After the keepers

The archive lists Coast Guard crew at the station from 1959 to 1971.[^5] The light was automated in 1989, with an automatic lighting system maintained by the U.S. Coast Guard.[^3]

## Today

The tower was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1981.[^1] When it was nominated, the property was occupied and its access restricted.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that in 2006 the Maunabo community began opening the grounds to the public on a regular basis, and that on 17 October 2012 the National Park Service announced the transfer of the lighthouse to the Municipality of Maunabo.[^4] The Fresnel lens remains in the lantern, but a modern solar-powered beacon is what mariners use.[^4] LighthouseFriends lists the grounds as open from 9 a.m. to 4 p.m., Wednesday to Sunday (holidays excluded), with the tower closed.[^4] That listing is undated, so check with the Municipality of Maunabo before visiting.
