La Pointe Light Station stands on Long Island, a low sandy spit at the mouth of Chequamegon Bay on Lake Superior, in the Apostle Islands of northern Wisconsin.[^1] It began in 1858 as a small wooden lighthouse, built only after the first lighthouse meant for this island had been put up on Michigan Island instead.[^1][^2] What remains today is a skeletal iron tower of 1897, a brick oil house and a triplex of the late 1930s, with the ruins of the first keepers' house in the woods nearby.[^1][^2]

## The place

The station took its name from La Pointe, the old fur-trade settlement on Madeline Island, which the first light was meant to serve.[^2] In 1851 Orasmus Cole lobbied for a lighthouse at La Pointe, and the district engineer, Captain Lorenzo Sitgreaves, recommended a site on Long Island, where the light would be seen by ships coming to the South Channel from the lake.[^1] Congress appropriated the $5,000 that was asked, and the Lighthouse Board secured a 152-acre (62 ha) reservation on 28 April 1853.[^1]

Long Island is a barrier spit of dunes and beaches. Its interior rises only about ten feet (3 m) above the edge of the lake, and the Park Service's inventory says that its Lake Superior shoreline is "constantly reshaped by the natural forces of weather and water."[^1] Sand was to be a recurring problem for the station. Over the years the focus of shipping in the area also shifted from La Pointe to the industrial port of Ashland, and the old lighthouse was eventually replaced by two newer towers.[^2]

## Building the light

The first lighthouse was a mistake that had to be paid for twice. The contractors, Sweet, Ransom and Smith of Milwaukee, followed the direction of Abraham Smolk, the Lighthouse Board's local representative, and built on Michigan Island without Sitgreaves's authorization. Sitgreaves rejected the work, the contractors protested, and in the end they put up a second building on Long Island, at the contractor's own expense by the Park Service's account.[^1][^2] The Park Service describes it as a wood-framed, one-and-a-half-story house with a 35-foot (11 m) square wooden tower rising from its roof, "hastily constructed at minimal cost," and fitted with a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^1] The date is not settled. The Park Service gives 1858 for completion, while the 1979 survey card of the Historic American Engineering Record says the first lighthouse was built in 1856.[^1][^5]

The sand soon undermined it. After only five years the exposed foundation had been compromised by the shifting sands, and in 1869 crushed stone from Raspberry Island was placed around it.[^1] (Lighthousefriends dates the stone foundation to 1864 and the crushed stone to 1868.)[^3]

By the late 1880s the Cleveland Vessel Owners' Association was asking for a fog signal, and the Light-House Board recorded that an act of 1 October 1888 authorized one at $5,500, with the money appropriated on 2 March 1889.[^1][^6] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 says that the building of the signal "was begun early in October and completed in January, 1891," about three-fourths of a mile (1.2 km) east of the light.[^6] The Park Service describes a 22-by-40-foot (6.7 by 12 m) building with two ten-inch steam whistles, which first blew on 3 January 1891.[^1]

The same report argued for moving the light itself. The existing one, the Board wrote, "is not near enough to the inner point to serve as a good guide to clear it, and it is too far from the course of vessels outside to be of the best advantage."[^6] It asked for $10,000 to move the main light and add a small harbor light and fog bell at Chequamegon Point. Congress approved it only on 2 March 1895.[^7][^1]

Work began in 1896. Joseph Sexton, the keeper, wrote in his log that a construction foreman and a work party arrived on 15 July 1896, and the job stopped for lack of money on 30 October.[^1][^8] The sources disagree on the next appropriation. The Light-House Board's report says $1,500 was appropriated by the act of 4 June 1897, while the Park Service gives 14 July 1897.[^8][^1] The new tower was cast by Chamblin S. Scott of Richmond, Virginia, at a cost of $3,912.[^1] Its height is given three ways: the Light-House Board bought "a 60-foot skeleton metal tower," the survey card measures 65 feet 3 inches (19.9 m) to the top of the ventilator ball, and the Park Service says "sixty seven foot."[^8][^5][^1] Sexton's log for 31 August 1897 notes that he helped hoist two cast-iron lantern deck plates, each weighing 1,100 pounds, to the top of the tower.[^1]

The light was established on 11 October 1897, the day the Chequamegon Point tower was first lit.[^8][^1] Sources differ about the lens. The survey card and the Park Service narrative both say that the construction superintendent moved the old lens to the Chequamegon Point tower, while the same Park Service narrative also says a fourth-order lens by Henri Le Paute of France shone from the new La Pointe tower.[^5][^1]

Meanwhile the old lighthouse was raised onto a brick basement and rearranged into two separate dwellings, so that a second keeper could be housed.[^8][^1] The Park Service says that the light stayed lit throughout this work, and a brick oil house was built in the same seasons.[^1][^8] Sexton was not impressed with the results. According to the Park Service, once the masonry was finished on 31 October he complained in his log on 5 November of "No closets or a good pantry," and later entries described holes in the floor and falling plaster.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive of the U.S. Lighthouse Society lists fourteen head-keeper appointments between 1857 and 1965 (John D. Angus served twice), and several men also served at other ranks.[^4] The first was John Haloran, who served from 1857 to 1859, and the same archive lists a Mary Haloran as first assistant in 1857 and 1858, making her one of the station's earliest keepers in her own right.[^4]

Joseph Sexton ran the station from 1889 to 1921.[^4][^9] He had come from Outer Island, where he was second assistant from 1886 and first assistant from 1888.[^9] Lighthousefriends reports that he married his second wife, Sophia, on 5 August 1894, and quotes his log as saying "Returned with my wife," and that Sophia had six children while at the station.[^3] His logs, as quoted there, give the working year in short strokes. On 30 November 1896 he wrote that the steam at the fog signal "was not below 40 # since the first of November." On 8 December 1898 he wrote, "Discontinued light this day. All well. Have not seen a steamer for some days. Glad to shut down."[^3] The keeper's log for July 1897 records chopping the timber down across the point to get the wind to drive the flies away.[^1] On 24 October 1899 he wrote that on the first nice day he would bring his folk to town so that the children could go to school.[^3]

Sexton also gardened where others failed. The Park Service says he planted potatoes and kept a cow and chickens, though he had to build cribs to protect the garden from the eroding shoreline.[^1] His son Ed Sexton, Lighthousefriends reports, was first assistant at Split Rock Light in Minnesota when he and the second assistant, Roy Gill, drowned on 2 October 1910 while rowing for the mail. Sexton's log for 4 October reads, "Heard today that Ed Sexton was drowned. Hope not."[^3][^13]

Most of the 47 assistant stints the archive lists were two years long or shorter, though a few ran far longer.[^4] Knut Olson (spelled Olsen by Lighthousefriends) was head keeper from 1921 to 1941, and James W. Bard from 1941 to 1946.[^4][^3]

The last keeper was Benjamin S. Hudak. Born in Cheboygan, Michigan, in 1896, he came to the service after a medical discharge from the Army and began at Rock of Ages Light.[^10] The archive's record shows him at La Pointe as second assistant from 1928 (Lighthouse Digest says he arrived on 6 February 1930), first assistant from 1930, and then at Marquette Harbor and Outer Island before returning in 1937 and becoming principal keeper in 1946; Lighthouse Digest dates his promotion to head keeper to 1948.[^11][^10] Lake Superior lights generally closed for the winter. The Hudaks lived in Bayfield, and his family joined him on the island in the summers.[^10] Hudak recalled in an oral history that nothing could be grown in the sandy soil, though wild blueberries and cranberries were plentiful, and that he and his wife sold the blueberries by the crate.[^1][^10] In 1959 he told a Minneapolis reporter, according to Lighthouse Digest, "You're away from your family too much."[^10] Hudak died in 1981.[^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources for this station describe weather and ice more than wrecks. On 27 May 1909, Sexton's log says, lightning struck the tower at the fog signal and put out the light at 9:10 p.m.; a lightning rod was placed on the tower the next November.[^3] On 15 April 1903 he left Bayfield at 4 p.m. and reached the station at 10, "had a hard time to get ashore through the ice," had to get into the ice and water up to their waists, and lit the lights at 11 p.m.[^3]

Leaving at the end of the season could be as hard. The Bayfield County Press of 12 December 1919, as quoted by Lighthousefriends, reported that Sexton and his assistants Anderson and Lewis crossed the channel on the ice to Houghton Point pulling a heavy sled, and that Anderson froze four toes.[^3]

The sand caused trouble too. In 1902 the dock was repaired and extended and the fog signal's water intake rebuilt to cut down silt, and in 1911 crews placed three log cribs, each 32 feet (10 m) long and filled with crushed stone, to protect the footings of the Chequamegon Point tower.[^1] The fog signal's work can be counted. The Light-House Board counted it running about 695 hours and burning 39 tons of coal in fiscal 1895, and about 150 hours and 8 tons in fiscal 1897.[^7][^8]

## After the keepers

The machinery changed through the 1920s and 1930s. In 1925 an air diaphone replaced the steam whistle, which the Park Service calls the first in the Apostle Islands; the Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists La Pointe among the stations changed from a steam whistle to a diaphone that year.[^12][^1] A radio beacon was added in October 1927 and the two signals were synchronized in 1931.[^1] An electric lamp was installed in the tower for the winter of 1934 to 1935, and electric lamps were put into service in both towers on 3 August 1937.[^1]

In 1938 a New Deal Public Works Administration project built a triplex to replace the old keepers' quarters, at a cost of about $22,000. The Coast Guard took over the lighthouses in 1939, and the keeper did not fully move in until 1940.[^1] The old lighthouse was then abandoned as a dwelling.[^2] The station was manned until 1964, when the Coast Guard installed automated lights in both towers and the keepers left.[^1] (The same Park Service document elsewhere gives 1968 as the end of the keepers' time and 1965 as when the station was unmanned.)[^1] Hudak said that everything was left in place when the station was boarded up, and that vandals soon found it.[^10]

The Coast Guard took down the fog signal building after 1986, and the foundation remains.[^1] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 4 August 1983 as part of a group of Coast Guard lighthouses on the Great Lakes.[^1] Long Island was added to Apostle Islands National Lakeshore in 1986.[^1]

## Today

The Park Service manages the island, and the Coast Guard maintains the tower as an aid to navigation.[^1] The lighthouses on Long Island are not open to the public, and the island is not served by scheduled tour boats; the Park Service warns that the water around it is shallow.[^2] The tower can be seen from a distance from Madeline Island and from State Highway 13 on the mainland.[^2] The Park Service says the remains of the 1858 lighthouse can be found on the island, hidden in thick vegetation.[^2]
