Saddleback Ledge is a bare granite rock, named for its shape, at the southern entrance to Isle au Haut Bay in Knox County, Maine, between Vinalhaven and Isle au Haut.[^1][^2] A granite tower built in 1839 stands on it; the keepers once lived inside the tower, and the rock has no soil of its own.[^3][^4]

## The place

The ledge sits on the eastern approach to Penobscot Bay. The National Register nomination calls the light the second such station built to guide traffic in and out of the bay, and the only one of six principal aids along that waterway classed as a secondary sea-coast light.[^2] Jeremy D'Entremont records that Congress appropriated $5,000 for a light here in March 1837, after some debate about the site. He quotes Captain Joseph Smith, who reaffirmed the ledge as the best site, writing that there was "no light between Matinicus & Bakers Island, a distance of about forty miles," and that Saddleback ledge was "decidedly the best location."[^3] The LighthouseFriends account puts the purchase of the ledge at $10 in August 1837, with construction beginning in 1838.[^4]

## Building the light

The tower was designed by Alexander Parris (1780-1852), and the nomination notes its similarity to the tower he had designed two years earlier at Mount Desert; it counts it among five known surviving Parris-designed towers or dwellings in Maine.[^2] LighthouseFriends says Parris wrote on the plans in January 1840 that he had personally overseen the work, and that Gridley J. F. Bryant carried out the construction. It gives the cost as $15,000, which the engineer I. W. P. Lewis nonetheless called economical in his 1842 survey.[^4] The sources disagree on the tower's height: about 43 feet (13 m) in D'Entremont's account, 40.6 feet in LighthouseFriends, 42 feet in the US Lighthouse Society's archive.[^3][^4][^1] The quarters were in the tower itself: kitchen, keeper's rooms and a water cistern.[^2]

Two later changes are dated by different sources. LighthouseFriends reports a new lantern in 1855 and a fifth-order Fresnel lens in 1856, replaced by a fourth-order lens in 1914 when the lamp was changed to incandescent oil vapor.[^4] The nomination says the present lantern and deck were fitted in 1883, replacing the originals.[^2] A wooden building, with a boathouse below and rooms for an assistant above, was attached to the tower by 1867, according to LighthouseFriends; the nomination says only that its date is not known.[^4][^2] Lighthouse Board reports show routine work on the landing: the boat slip was extended seaward 40 feet and a 35-foot branch slip added in the fiscal year 1895; in fiscal 1899 the lower 35 feet of the slip was renewed and "two brick cisterns were built"; in fiscal 1900 a new striking machine was installed for the fog bell.[^5][^6][^7] D'Entremont dates a landing derrick with a swinging arm and bosun's chair to 1885.[^3]

## The keepers

The first keeper was Watson Y. Hopkins, who is listed from 1839 to 1849.[^1] D'Entremont writes that he moved in with his wife, Abigail, and seven children; the family of nine shared a living room, two chambers and a cellar.[^3] Hopkins described the place in a statement printed in I. W. P. Lewis's report on the lighthouses he surveyed (LighthouseFriends dates Hopkins's report to 1842, D'Entremont calls it Lewis's 1843 report): "I am obliged to bring my water from shore, a distance of seven miles." The copper spout that caught rain had been ruined by the surf, the iron railing and the privy had been swept away, and "The windows all leak in storms."[^3][^4] In September 1843, a week after Abigail gave birth at the station, the baby fell into the surf while being moved to a boat and was pulled out at once, unhurt.[^3][^4]

Later keepers shared the rock with one assistant, and from about 1880 with two. The archive's roster shows second assistants first listed in 1880.[^1] Life in the tower did not always run smoothly: D'Entremont says first assistant Nathaniel Bowden assaulted principal keeper James H. Orcutt and pointed a loaded revolver at him, which he dates to 1874.[^3] The roster does not fit that date, since it lists Orcutt as principal keeper from 1871 to 1886 but Bowden only from 1880 to 1884, so the date or the account may be wrong.[^1]

One death on the ledge is documented in a contemporary newspaper: a young assistant's. The *Ellsworth American* of 13 September 1905 noted a surprise party for Marnal Newman of Seawall before he left on 16 September for Saddleback Ledge as second assistant.[^9] On Saturday, 11 January 1908 (the date is LighthouseFriends'), the paper reported on 15 January, he left Vinalhaven in the afternoon in a small boat for the station and "nothing further was heard from him." His boat washed ashore at Roberts Head on Sunday afternoon. The paper thought the heavy seas had put him on one of the shoals and filled the boat; he was twenty, unmarried, and had served three years.[^8][^4] The US Lighthouse Society's memorial list names him as an assistant keeper who died at Saddleback Ledge in 1908.[^10] LighthouseFriends adds that first assistant Vurney King was on shore leave and was called back by telephone, leaving principal keeper Jerome Brawn alone on the ledge.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends gives an 1850 inspector's finding that the tower's foundation had been undermined and the place was "dangerous" to live in, and says storms periodically swept away any earth a keeper hauled out for a garden.[^4] An 1896 *Scribner's* article, as quoted by D'Entremont, described the visible vegetation on the ledge as three pea vines, two hills of potatoes and a dozen spears of oats, kept alive in a trash heap.[^3]

In the Great Gale of 27-28 January 1933, LighthouseFriends reprints the keeper's report to the Rockland *Courier-Gazette*: the sea broke the boathouse doors, tore clapboards from the house, took away about 80 feet of the slip and cut the telephone cable. The keeper wrote, "Seas striking the side and roof shook the building so the stove rattled."[^4] In 1927, by the same source, 124 ducks struck the lantern one night; a ten-pound drake broke a pane, damaged the lens and put the light out until the assistants restored it.[^4]

## After the keepers

The station was automated in 1954.[^2][^1] Before that, Coast Guard crews had taken over; in 1946, LighthouseFriends says, they served five weeks on and one off. LighthouseFriends says that on 25 November 1950 squalls kept three men on the ledge with supplies short and the telephone line down, until the icebreaker *Snohomish* reached them on 14 December, when they had about two days' water left.[^4] D'Entremont dates the same episode to 25 November 1951 and calls the vessel a tug.[^3] The attached wooden building lasted until about 1960, when LighthouseFriends says Green Berets blew it up in a training exercise.[^4] The nomination, prepared by the Maine Historic Preservation Commission in 1987, describes the station as consisting only of the conical granite tower.[^2]

## Today

The light remains an active aid to navigation owned by the U.S. Coast Guard.[^4] The US Lighthouse Society's archive records that its optic, installed in 2017, is a solar-powered LED, and that the light is not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that when the Coast Guard offered the lighthouse as excess in 2009 under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, no group seems to have taken it on.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse must be seen by water or air, and notes boat trips that pass it.[^4]
