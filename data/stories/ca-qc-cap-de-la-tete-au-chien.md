Cap de la Tête au Chien is a steep, wooded headland on the north shore of the St. Lawrence in Charlevoix-Est, Quebec, about five miles northeast of Saint-Siméon. A short octagonal concrete tower with an oversized lantern has stood there since 1909, facing Hare Island across the river.[^1][^6]

## The place

In the 1890s the channel north of Hare Island was the one that masters of ocean-going ships preferred, while river pilots disliked it. Lighthouse Friends, drawing on the Department of Marine's reports, relates that Chief Engineer William P. Anderson toured the St. Lawrence lights in 1890, quoted pilots' "remarkable disinclination" to use that channel, and urged that it be lit. A lightship went on White Island in 1893 and Cape Salmon Lighthouse followed in 1894.[^1] Quebec's heritage register says the Department of Marine and Fisheries chose this site to mark the north shore better, as part of an improvement programme that began around the turn of the twentieth century.[^6]

## Building the light

The Department's report for fiscal 1909 said it intended "to erect, during the coming season, a reinforced steel concrete lighthouse tower, wooden fog alarm building, and double dwelling house."[^2] The landing place was "very difficult". To get materials up, the builders blasted roadways from the shore to the building sites and put up an inclined railway on trestlework. They also built a power-house to run trams and a derrick, and a concrete wall across the mouth of a large gorge so that fresh water could be gathered. Work was by day's labour, and $6,433.64 had been spent by the time of the report.[^2]

The tower is octagonal and 11.6 m (38 ft) high, with walls that lean slightly inward, and it was one of the first reinforced-concrete lighthouses in Quebec.[^6] Its lantern is massive, about half the height of the tower.[^6] The focal plane is 63 m (207 ft) above the river, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] The report for fiscal 1910 recorded a third-order dioptric double-flashing light and lantern "erected on a new tower", flashing white.[^3] The same year an officer of the Department, reporting on his inspection tours, wrote that the work under construction at Cape Dogs, the station's English name, "may be considered a model, modern installation."[^4] Lighthouse Friends gives the early light as a flash of a quarter second twice in every five seconds, burning petroleum vapour under an incandescent mantle.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists three keepers: Ludger Bergeron (1909 to 1931), A. Tremblay (1931 to 1932) and Kelly Guerin (1932 to at least 1937).[^1] Department of Marine tables of the 1910s list the station as a light and fog alarm, under the name Cape Dogs.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Ships ran into trouble in the reach below the headland. A Department of Marine casualty report published in 1917 describes the steamer Romera touching bottom on a shoal on the north shore "between cape Dogs and cape Salmon during fog", with small damage; the Wreck Commissioner's court blamed the pilot.[^8] A later report says the steamship Lycaon "stranded May 8th off cape Dogs".[^9] Another notes that the steam barge Rockferry "struck bottom East of Cape Dogs when outward bound" and was leaking badly.[^10] None of these accounts mentions the keepers.

## After the keepers

The fog alarm, housed in a building near the shore, gave a five-second blast every fifty seconds when needed. The present fog-signal building dates from 1950 and the alarm is no longer active.[^1] Lighthouse Friends suggests the light was probably converted to electricity in 1952, when the station's dwellings were electrified. The two houses standing today date from 1957 and 1962 on Lighthouse Friends' account, while Quebec's register puts the keeper's and assistant's houses in 1959 to 1962 and calls the site abandoned since 1988.[^1][^6] In 2011 the lens's rotation mechanism broke, and the Canadian Coast Guard mounted a modern optic on a gallery outside the lantern room with the same characteristic. The Fresnel lens remains inside.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard and the grounds and tower are closed.[^1] The tower, with seven related buildings, is a designated federal heritage lighthouse (Parks Canada's 2016 list of designated heritage lighthouses names it), and Saint-Siméon cited the wider site as a heritage site in 2014.[^11][^5][^6] Parks Canada says that until 2011 the site could be reached only by air and water, and that a hiking trail was built that year.[^5] Lighthouse Friends says the station is best seen from a boat; whale-watching cruises from Saint-Siméon pass in front of it, and it can be glimpsed from the Rivière-du-Loup to Saint-Siméon ferry.[^1] Please treat the station as closed and do not land on it or climb the tower.
