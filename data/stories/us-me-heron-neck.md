Heron Neck Light stands on Greens Island, off Vinalhaven in Maine's Penobscot Bay, where a small brick tower has marked the way into Carvers Harbor and Hurricane Sound since 1854.[^1][^3] It was a one-family station for its whole civilian history, and in 1902 a skipper reported that its fog signal was a dog.[^5][^9]

## The place

The light sits on a bold rock ledge that drops sharply to the sea, at the southeast entrance to Hurricane Sound, the water that frames the west side of Vinalhaven Island.[^1][^2] The National Register nomination says it was set up to guide vessels to Carvers Harbor and Hurricane Sound, and puts it among the aids that served the bay.[^2] Vinalhaven had 1,252 people in 1850, most of whom, the nomination says, probably combined fishing with limited farming; a granite quarry and a summer tourist trade came later in the century.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $5,000 for the light in August 1852.[^3] The station had a cylindrical brick tower joined to a one-and-a-half-story brick dwelling.[^3] The Lighthouse Board's later report dates the dwelling to 1853, and the National Register nomination says the tower was erected that year; the lamp was first shown on 6 February 1854, a fixed red light.[^3][^2][^4] The sources therefore give both 1853 and 1854 for the station. The first lens was a fifth-order Fresnel, which a fourth-order lens by Sautter later replaced.[^1][^3]

The dwelling turned out badly. In its report of 1891 the Light-House Board said its walls had been built "by contract", with so little mortar that many joints looked unfilled, and that driving rain soaked them and kept the house damp.[^4] The board added that the site was underlaid by a sloping ledge over which water flowed, saturating the soil and keeping the cellar wet. It judged the house "unhealthy" and "unsuitable for occupancy in so severe a climate", and recorded the claim that five deaths had occurred in it since 1853.[^4] An inspection report of 1875 had already found the house leaky, with the wall paper "all wet", according to a modern history of the light.[^5] The board asked for $3,300 for a new dwelling in 1891, renewed it in later reports, and the Portland Daily Press of 28 November 1894 repeated the $3,300 recommendation.[^4][^6][^7] A wooden dwelling replaced the brick one in 1895 (LighthouseFriends) or 1895-96 (the nomination), joined to the tower by a covered passage.[^2][^1] The same board's report of 1890 had already recorded that the old stone deck and lantern were replaced by an iron deck and a modern fourth-order lantern.[^3] A stone oil house followed in 1903.[^2]

## The keepers

James Smith, the first keeper, was paid $350 a year.[^3] A modern history of the light calls him James G. Smith, a native of Vinalhaven, who moved to the station with his wife Ann and their children, and says Heron Neck was always a single-family station.[^5] The Digital Archive of the U.S. Lighthouse Society lists him from 1853 to 1861.[^1] John Green kept the light from 1870 until his death in 1883, after which his wife Sara served as keeper for a month before Nathaniel Hartwell arrived.[^5][^1]

Levi L. Farnham, styled Captain, was keeper from 1900.[^3][^1] A newspaper of 1911 spells his name Levi W. Farnham and says he died at Heron Neck after 48 hours' suffering from a paralytic shock; his brother Capt. Frank Farnham of Orland went to the funeral.[^8] When the lighthouse tender *Lilac* called on 17 February 1911, her captain found Farnham's widow and son in charge of the station, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] Joseph Farnham is listed as keeper for 1911 and Fred M. Robbins from 1911 to 1930; no source ties Joseph to the family.[^1] Andrew W. Bennett followed from 1930 and was the last civilian keeper.[^1][^5] The same modern history says an assistant keeper, Clinton Dalzell, married Elizabeth Smith at the lighthouse in 1930, both being natives of Vinalhaven.[^5]

### The fog dog

LighthouseFriends says the early keepers had a bell they rang in answer to vessels' signals in thick weather.[^3] In 1902, however, a skipper told the Rockland Star the station had no fog signal. The story, reprinted in the *Republican Journal* of Belfast that November, has him say the keeper "has no fog signal, so he calls his big dog to answer that purpose."[^9] Lobster smacks rounding the point blew their whistles, and the Newfoundland ran to the point and barked; "the intelligent creature's voice," the skipper said, "is heard at as great a distance as the ordinary fog signal." In fair weather the fishermen blew to call him to the shore and tossed him a biscuit or a chunk of meat.[^9] The 1902 piece names neither keeper nor dog. Later writers name the dog Nemo and say Farnham trained him, and that a second dog, Rover, took over when Nemo retired.[^3][^5] A red whistling buoy marked "H N" was established south-west of the light on 10 July 1902.[^10]

A fog bell was added later: LighthouseFriends dates it to around 1920 and describes a 1,200-pound bell struck every fifteen seconds by weight-driven machinery; New England Lighthouses says it was added in October 1925.[^3][^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1948 the two-masted fishing schooner *Fannie Belle* ran onto the ledges at Heron Neck; nobody was lost, and the vessel was abandoned and later salvaged.[^5]

## After the keepers

The light was electrified in 1948, the year Coast Guard crews replaced the last civilian keeper, and the fog signal became an electric siren.[^5][^1] The light was automated in 1982, ending Coast Guard keepers, and a 300 mm lens replaced the Fresnel.[^1][^3][^5] One Coast Guardsman, Jim Woods, who served there in 1960-61, recalled a crew of three and a rota of three weeks on and one off, with supply runs by skiff round Boiler Point to Vinalhaven.[^5]

On 19 April 1989 a fire, probably electrical, badly damaged the empty keeper's house; the Coast Guard proposed pulling it down, and public opposition led the Island Institute of Rockland to take title.[^3][^5] Congress approved the transfer in November 1993.[^11] The Institute leased the property to a couple who restored the house, and the project is credited with inspiring the Maine Lights Program, under which Maine lighthouses were transferred to communities and groups.[^5][^12]

## Today

Heron Neck remains an active aid to navigation, with the tower, the 1895-96 dwelling and the stone oil house standing, and is on the National Register of Historic Places.[^3][^2] The station is private property and is not open to the public.[^1][^5] LighthouseFriends reports that the restorers received full ownership in 2009 and that public access then ended.[^3] New England Lighthouses says no regular cruises pass the light and suggests a chartered boat or flight, while LighthouseFriends says Isle au Haut Boat Services offers excursions that include it; sources disagree, and the grounds and tower are closed either way.[^5][^3]
