Trial Island is a bare, low hump of rock about 90 m (300 ft) off Oak Bay, at the edge of the narrow, fast Enterprise Channel just east of Victoria.[^1] Ships approaching Victoria harbour rely on it,[^2] and the light on its southern end has been working since 1906, first from a wooden keepers' house with a lantern on its roof and, since 1970, from a slim white concrete tower.[^2][^3]

## The place

The island is said to have taken its name from the Royal Navy: British warships refitted at Esquimalt were sent out to the island and back as a trial run.[^1] Lighthouse Friends describes it as a barren, arid island rising about 24 m (80 ft) above high tide, with tides in Enterprise Channel running six to seven knots, so that experienced mariners enter only when conditions are ideal.[^1]

The Canadian heritage designation traces the original light to the wreck of the American ferry *Clallam* in 1904: planning for it, the statement of significance says, began after that disaster, in waters of riptides and few navigational aids.[^2] The island had already caught vessels. The Department of Marine's wreck returns list the steam scow *Katie*, which "stranded on Trial Island, and was broken up ; a total loss" in a south-west gale on 12 January 1902, and the steamer *Tees*, which stranded on the island's eastern side on 13 February 1904 and was floated off the next day.[^4][^5]

The place also has a memory of an earlier disaster. On the night of 22 March 1895 the steamer *Velos*, towing the barge *Pilot* with stone workers aboard, met a south-easterly gale and the flood tide as it entered Enterprise Channel, lost its rudder chains and was thrown onto the rocks. Lighthouse Friends reports that the *Velos*'s mate, Andrew Christensen, scrambled onto the barge, and that of the men left on the steamer only Captain Anderson survived, carried ashore on Trial Island by the waves; the *Pilot* grounded there and its men got ashore.[^1] The same account says the lighthouse came ten years and several shipwrecks later, although 1906 is eleven years after 1895.[^1]

## Building the light

The Department of Marine and Fisheries' report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1906 reported that "A lighthouse and fog alarm are in course of construction on the southern end of Trial island," with $7,802.60 spent to date.[^6] Lighthouse Friends gives the contractor as George H. Frost and the cost as roughly $12,000.[^1]

The report for the nine months ended 31 March 1907 says the lighthouse "was put in operation on November 1, 1906." It stood on a site 15 m (48 ft) above high water, south of an 80-foot (24 m) knoll near the island's south point, and was "a square wooden dwelling carrying a square wooden lantern on the middle of its cottage roof."[^7] Lighthouse Friends adds that the fog alarm, a diaphone sounding a three-second blast each minute, began work on 1 September 1906, and that a temporary fixed light burned until a fourth-order Fresnel lens arrived from Birmingham, England, and was put in place on 16 November 1908.[^1] The 1906 report had said that a fourth-order dioptric double-flashing apparatus, like the one supplied for Seacow Head, Prince Edward Island, "has been sent to British Columbia for the Trial Island station."[^8] The report for the year ended 31 March 1909 records the change: the temporary fixed white light was replaced by a double-flashing fourth-order light, two flashes in a group every 10 seconds, and a new lantern platform and improved lantern were put on the tower.[^9][^10]

## The keepers

The first keeper was Harold Shorrock O'Kell, appointed on 22 August 1906 according to the department's list (its lists spell him Okell and O'Kell).[^11] Lighthouse Friends says he held a mate's certificate for coasting Canadian waters until a snapped cable broke his shin, after which, on a cork leg, "he served for twenty-five years at the light."[^1] Water came from an 800-cubic-foot cistern under the kitchen and ran so short that the family rowed to town to bathe on weekends.[^1] The department's list for the year ended 31 March 1908 names a second keeper at the station, J. McDonald, paid $450 a year; no appointment date or rank is given.[^12]

Lighthouse Friends lists those who followed: Philip Gresley Cox from 1931, Samuel Avard Dondale (1941 to 1943), William Charles Copeland (1947 to 1950), Russell K. Botham (1952 to 1953), Douglas Howard Franklin (1957 to 1962), then Robert Nagel, Robert W. Noble, Clayton Restall, Iain Colquhoun, Ian G. McNeil (1999 to 2007), Meredith Dickman (2007 to 2019) and John Gilevet from 2019.[^13] Ian McNeil recalled that water was still short in his time: he and his wife filled cans whenever they went to Oak Bay for supplies.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1910 an official, A. E. Roberts, was sent with a boat party to give O'Kell news of his father's death, and bad weather held him from the island for over a day and a half. He reported that "had there been a wreck off the island Friday night, the men on the island could not possibly have launched a boat," and recommended a telephone.[^1] The telephone linked the island into the search-and-rescue network. Forty years later, when the Department of National Defence wanted the underwater cable moved, the marine agent's letter to Ottawa cited a 1944 rescue in which the keeper took five people from a crippled yacht into his home, and a seriously injured passenger was saved because the keeper got medical advice by phone; the line stayed.[^1]

Keepers have gone on watching the channel. In 1997 Keeper Iain Colquhoun saved a kayaker from drowning, and in June 2009 the keeper's lookout saw five kayakers swamped by a powerboat's wake and told the Coast Guard.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1970 a cylindrical concrete tower with lantern and gallery was built a few feet from the dwelling, replacing the original light.[^1] The statement of significance calls it an "apple core" design, a slender cantilever tower anchored by rods that run through its base into the rock, built of quartz aggregate and white concrete so that it needs no paint.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the old lantern room and Fresnel lens were taken down and reassembled in Victoria's Bastion Square.[^1]

In September 2009 the federal government announced that Trial Island and three other British Columbia lighthouses would be de-staffed; a Senate committee recommended in 2010 that the effort stop, and keepers stayed.[^1]

## Today

The light is listed by the Canadian Coast Guard at 13 m, with a green flash every five seconds and a focal height of 28.3 m: "White cylindrical tower with red top."[^14] The heritage designation covers the tower and eight related buildings, among them the 1906 senior keeper's residence, a 1957 junior keeper's dwelling and a 1965 boathouse.[^2] Lighthouse Friends lists a keeper there through at least 2024.[^13] The island is part of the Trial Islands Ecological Reserve, which BC Parks says is closed to the public; landing requires a permit, and there are no docking facilities.[^15][^16] The statement of significance calls the lighthouse highly visible from both land and water.[^2]
