Two wooden lights stand at the head of Baileys Harbor, on the Lake Michigan shore of Door County, Wisconsin. Set one behind the other, they gave a captain a line to steer by: keep the upper light over the lower one and the ship was on the line into the harbor.[^1][^5] The rear light stood in a tower atop the keeper's house.[^1] Both were first lit at the opening of navigation in 1870.[^5] They burned oil, and then gas, until electricity came in 1930.[^1] The historic towers were inactive from 1969 until the Coast Guard returned them to service in 2015.[^5]

## The place

Baileys Harbor is a bay on the Lake Michigan side of Door County, and the range lights stand at its north end.[^1] The National Register nomination dates the harbor's history to about 1847 and calls the harbor "a prominent Door County port, serving passenger and freight ships alike."[^1] Ships loaded cordwood, cedar posts, poles and tanning bark there, but the way in was hard: "Entrance to the harbor, however, was hindered by sand bars."[^1]

Before the range lights, a single light marked the entrance. The nomination says Alanson Sweet built it in 1851, to warn ships off the rocky shoreline and to mark the harbor entrance, and that it "apparently did neither adequately."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the light went into operation in 1853.[^5] The two sources date different things, the building and the start of operation, and they are not reconciled here. On 20 July 1868 Congress authorized moving Sweet's light to Cana Island and appropriated $6,000 for range lights that would guide ships through the sand bars.[^1]

## Building the light

Andrew Breitbach, a Detroit carpenter, built the range lights of wood in 1869.[^1][^5] The nomination, citing the *Door County Advocate*, places one light on the beach and one 60 rods inland. It also records Breitbach's "signature board" on the upper light, dated 21 October 1869.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the work took place in the summer and fall of 1869.[^5]

The *Advocate* reported on 3 February 1870 that the range lights "are ready to be lighted."[^1] The lower light was fixed red, 22 feet (6.7 m) above the water. The rear light was fixed white, 39 feet (11.9 m) above the water, in a tower on the keeper's house.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the lower light used a steamer lens and the rear light a fifth-order Fresnel lens from Paris.[^5]

Both lights burned lard oil, which made them hard to manage in winter.[^5] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1896 the Light-House Board recorded a new brick oil house, "with an iron roof shelving, and doors, and a concrete floor," and a sidewalk "1,071 running feet long" laid through the swamp between the two ranges.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the oil house to 1895 and gives the sidewalk as 1,074 feet.[^5]

The lens records differ. The Board's report for fiscal 1897 says that at the front light "a new fifth-order tubular burner with a parabolic reflector was substituted for the steamer lens."[^3] A list of changes in the same report, however, says the front light was "changed from a sixth-order to a reflector light, June 27, 1897."[^4] The nomination gives the 1897 change as a fifth-order lens, and says the upper light received a fifth-order lens in 1902.[^1] The sources do not agree on what the front light had before 1897.

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists eight principal keepers for the rear light, from 1869 to 1923, and it lists no keepers for the front.[^6][^7] The nomination explains how one keeper could tend both: from the upper light, the keeper could "monitor the lower light."[^1]

Fabian Truedell was appointed the first keeper on 1 December 1869, when he was seventy-four. He resigned in September 1872.[^5] His successor, Marcus S. Shaler, lost his wife Lucy during a typhoid outbreak in 1875. LighthouseFriends says she cared for the sick until she herself died of the disease on 26 January 1875, at forty-three, and that Shaler left the station that April.[^5][^6]

Joseph Harris, Jr., keeper from 1875 to 1881, had been wounded at the Siege of Petersburg in the Civil War.[^5][^6] LighthouseFriends says he founded the Baileys Harbor Literary Club, a debating society, in 1876.[^5] The archive spells one 1881 keeper Hans L. Hansen, and LighthouseFriends prints Hanson. The archive also lists Aaron Burr Caswell for 1882, which LighthouseFriends gives as Burr Caswell.[^5][^6]

Henry H. Gattie came in 1896 from the Sturgeon Bay Ship Canal light station and served longer than any other keeper at Baileys Harbor.[^5][^6] He married Eva Hendricks on 18 February 1901. LighthouseFriends quotes his logbook for that day: "My wedding at 4 PM."[^5] On 25 August 1922 acetylene lights replaced the kerosene lamps in both ranges.[^5] In September 1923 Gattie was transferred to Cana Island Lighthouse, where his job included occasional maintenance trips back to his former station.[^5]

## After the keepers

The keeper's post was eliminated in 1930, when the lights were electrified.[^1][^5] The keeper's house was then leased out, and for a time the Immanuel Lutheran Church of Baileys Harbor used it as a parsonage.[^1][^5] In 1936 the Coast Guard transferred much of the range-light property to Door County.[^1]

The county park commissioners had decided to clear some of the land for a trailer park. Opponents formed The Ridges Sanctuary in 1937 to block the plan.[^5]

In 1969 a metal tower with a single directional light went up just south of the front tower, and the historic lenses were taken out.[^5] The sanctuary took a 99-year lease in 1972, and in the early 1990s it raised over $70,000 to rehabilitate the structures.[^5] The nomination calls the pair "the only example of the range light sub-category of lighthouses found in the state," and says the Baileys Harbor range lights "incorporated the upper light into the keeper's residence."[^1]

On 18 August 2015 three Coast Guardsmen from the Two Rivers station removed the modern skeletal tower and returned the historic lights to service, after 46 years inactive. The front tower shows red and the rear shows white.[^5]

## Today

The lights stand on land Door County owns and leases to The Ridges Sanctuary.[^1] The sanctuary charges a fee to use its hiking trails and boardwalk.[^5] The keeper's house is now the summer residence for seasonal naturalists.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports the grounds open and the towers closed, with the rear light open for tours in summer.[^5] Do not climb the towers. The sixth-order lens from the front tower is on display at the Wisconsin Maritime Museum in Manitowoc, and a lens used in the rear tower is displayed on the site.[^5]
