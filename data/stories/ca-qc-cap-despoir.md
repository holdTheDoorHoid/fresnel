Cap d'Espoir Lighthouse stands on a cliff at the north-east entrance to Chaleur Bay, on the eastern shore of the Gaspé Peninsula just south of Percé.[^4] The station was set up in 1873.[^1] Its present concrete tower dates from 1939, and the light has been automated since 1987 and is still operated by the Canadian Coast Guard.[^1]

## The place

English charts and the Department of Marine called the cape "Cape Despair", a phonetic sounding of the French name, which LighthouseFriends translates as "Cape of Hope."[^4] LighthouseFriends places the light on a rounded point above cliffs that drop steeply into the sea.[^4]

A Meteorological Service report on storm-signal stations, in the Department's annual report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901, says of the cape: "All fishing vessels bound to the banks have to pass this point." Its writer favoured a storm-signal station there.[^6]

LighthouseFriends says a light was first proposed here in 1868, so that fishermen coming from Miscou Bank in thick weather could find shelter.[^4] Parliament voted $1,000 in 1871, but the funds were judged insufficient, and construction waited until more money was found.[^4]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says work began in 1873 and that the light was first shown on 1 September 1874.[^4] The Quebec heritage record dates the station to 1873,[^1] and Rowlett gives 1873 as the year the station was established.[^5] The 1873 date is best read as the year the station was set up; LighthouseFriends alone dates the first showing of the light to 1874.

P. Carroll built the tower under a $2,000 contract.[^4] E. Chanteloup of Montreal was paid $2,223.99 for the lantern room and a revolving light apparatus with six lamps and twenty-inch reflectors.[^4] LighthouseFriends gives the total cost as $5,175.16.[^4]

The first tower's height is not agreed. LighthouseFriends describes a square wooden tower, 12.8 m (42 ft) tall to its weather vane, with its focal plane 27.4 m (90 ft) above high water.[^4] The heritage record calls it a square wooden tower about 5.4 m (18 ft) high.[^1] This story gives both and does not choose between them. LighthouseFriends says the first light was a white flash every thirty seconds, visible for fifteen miles.[^4]

In August 1876 William Barbour, the Department's engineer in Quebec, was sent to find out why the revolving light had failed.[^4] LighthouseFriends quotes his report: he repaired the chain and had the light working again that night.[^4]

In 1878 the keeper, John Beck, found his quarters in the square tower cramped and built an addition to the kitchen, 5.6 m by 3.4 m (18.5 by 11 ft).[^4] A fire in 1883 damaged the tower, and new lamps and reflectors were supplied.[^4] In 1895 Philip Vibert built a storehouse and barn at the station for $296.50.[^4]

In 1913 a quadruple-flash, long-focus reflector went into the lantern, and a building was put up for a diaphone fog alarm.[^4] The light's signal became four flashes every twenty-five seconds.[^4] The heritage record describes the fog-alarm shelter that still stands at the station.[^1]

The old wooden tower was demolished in 1939 and replaced the same year by a concrete tower, the heritage record says.[^1] The Canadian Register statement ties the new tower to the upgrading of Canada's navigation system during the 1930s, and says the concrete tower replaced an earlier wooden one.[^3]

The concrete tower is octagonal, of reinforced concrete, and 14.6 m (48 ft) high, according to the heritage record.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends and Rowlett give 14 m (46 ft).[^4][^5] The heritage record says three floors are joined by a steel stair up to a round metal lantern.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it is a sister design to towers at Cap-de-Bon-Désir, Cap-au-Saumon and Cap-Gaspé.[^4]

## The keepers

John Beck was the first keeper, paid $400 a year, and served from 1874 to 1897.[^4] Charles Bourget followed him from 1897 to 1923.[^4] A Department of Marine statement of Quebec lightkeepers for the year ended 31 March 1908 has "Cape Despair" beside Charles Bourget's name, which fits his service at the station in those years.[^7]

A. Beck served from 1923 to 1936.[^4] J. E. A. Beck is listed from 1936, and LighthouseFriends gives no end date for him.[^4]

The first keeper's house, built about 1874, was demolished in 1959.[^1] The present house dates from the 1960s.[^1] No source here describes the daily work of the keepers beyond the repairs and additions listed above.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends records a tradition that part of Admiral Sir Hovenden Walker's fleet, on its way to attack Quebec, was wrecked at the cape on 11 August 1711, and that all on board were lost.[^4] LighthouseFriends also reports a local tale that fishermen were warned of storms by the apparition of an English ship.[^4] Both are tradition; no source cited here confirms either.

The fog alarm built in 1913 gave a set pattern, which LighthouseFriends describes: "two blasts each minute", each blast 3.5 seconds long, with silence between them and a long silence after the second.[^4]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1987.[^1] The heritage record says the compressed-air tanks for the diaphone were used in the 1970s as containers for oil.[^1]

The keepers' dwellings were sold to private owners.[^4] Rowlett says several light station buildings survive and have been sold.[^5] Both former keepers' houses are now rented as cottages, the heritage record says.[^1] LighthouseFriends and Rowlett describe a four-bedroom keeper's house rented by the week.[^4][^5]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns and runs the light, and the heritage record says it still guides commercial shipping and fishing boats.[^1][^4] LighthouseFriends and Rowlett describe the grounds as open and the tower as closed.[^4][^5] The light shows four white flashes every thirty seconds.[^4][^5] The 1913 signal was four flashes every twenty-five seconds, and none of the sources read says when the period changed.[^4][^5] Rowlett gives its focal plane as 25.5 m (84 ft),[^5] and LighthouseFriends gives its range as seventeen nautical miles.[^4]

To reach it, LighthouseFriends advises driving south from Percé on Route 132 for 16 km, then turning southeast onto Route du Phare and driving 1.3 km to its end.[^4]
