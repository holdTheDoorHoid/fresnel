Eddy Point is a low sandy point on the Nova Scotia side of the southern entrance to the Strait of Canso, the narrow passage that separates Cape Breton Island from the mainland.[^1] A light has marked it since 1851 or 1852, depending on the source. The last lighthouse on the point came down in 2003, and a pair of range lights now guide ships through the strait.[^1][^2][^7]

## The place

The strait joins Chedabucto Bay on the Atlantic with St. Georges Bay on the Northumberland Strait side, and the point sits on the western side of its southern mouth.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the point is low-lying, so the light could not be seen from more than two miles away. A spit of sand and stones reaches about 457 m (500 yards) north-east and sets up ripples in the tidal current off the point.[^1]

In its report for the year to June 1902 the Department of Marine and Fisheries described the channel here. "This buoy and the black can buoy on the opposite shore, off Eddy point, define the safe navigable water in this channel," the report says, so the point was one of the marks defining safe water through the strait.[^3]

The Department's reports also list a Long Eddy Point fog alarm in New Brunswick. That is a different station, and this story is only about the Nova Scotia point.[^6]

## Building the light

Lighthouse Friends says work began on a beacon at Sand Point in 1851, and that the contractor did not finish it that year, so the light did not go into service until 1852.[^1] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society gives the start as 1850 and the year lit as 1851.[^2] The two accounts disagree by a year, and this story does not settle it.

The first light was in a square white building. Lighthouse Friends describes bow windows in its east- and west-facing roofs, which showed lights in several directions, and a large black diamond painted on its southern side so that ships could find it by day.[^1][^2]

In 1893 work began on a new tower. T. M. Crowe of Truro built it against the eastern side of the old light for $800, and Lighthouse Friends says the new light was placed in operation on 1 January 1895.[^1] The society describes the 1895 tower as replacing the original tower of 1851.[^2] In 1922 the light had a fifth-order dioptric lens and a hand fog horn.[^2]

Fog signals came next. Lighthouse Friends says a diaphone fog alarm was placed in 1928 in a building attached to the light, and that a new combined dwelling and lighthouse went up in 1929.[^1] The society has the diaphone in operation from 1930 and a third tower in 1929.[^2] Lighthouse Friends also says a fire in 1937 destroyed the 1929 building and that a fourth lighthouse followed. The society's list does not mention a fire, and it keeps the 1929 tower in service until 1989.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

Joseph Mundell was keeper in 1857 and asked for an outbuilding in which to store oil and other supplies.[^1][^2] Until then he kept the oil in his own outbuilding at some distance from the light, and the wood he gathered for fuel was often washed off the beach by high tides.[^1]

William Welsh was put in charge in 1863. Lighthouse Friends' keeper list gives 1864 as the year he began.[^1] In May 1869 the superintendent of lighthouses dismissed him for repeated neglect, and Joseph Mundell, whom Lighthouse Friends calls likely the previous keeper, was placed in charge in his place.[^1]

Joseph kept the light until 1903, when Edward Mundell took it over.[^1] The Department's list of lightkeepers has "Mundell, Edward Eddy Point" in the report for the year to June 1903,[^4] but its staff table dates Edward's appointment to 28 July 1903, after that fiscal year had ended.[^5] Lighthouse Friends gives his service as 1903 to 1927.[^1]

After Edward Mundell, Lighthouse Friends names A. Carrigan (1927 to 1932), W. Halfpenny (1932 to 1933), V.F. Hindsbee (1933 to 1936) and L. Carrigan (1936 to at least 1937).[^1] The society's list adds a Jim Guptill for 1987 and 1988 that Lighthouse Friends does not give here, so the two lists disagree.[^2]

Lighthouse Friends records the hardships of the years after the Canso Causeway was finished in 1955. It says tides at the point then ran about 28 cm higher than before, and that contractors had removed large quantities of gravel. One keeper described conditions at some point after the causeway was finished. A storm cut a gap about six metres wide and 1.5 metres deep through the northern beach, at high tide the whole road was flooded to a depth of one metre, and the car was marooned at the lighthouse.[^1] The station went down to one keeper in 1977 and was de-staffed in 1979, once the light could be watched from the Coast Guard's Eddy Point Traffic Centre nearby.[^1][^2]

## After the keepers

In 1988 a concrete slab was poured over the foundation of the dwelling, which had been sold and removed, and a white circular fibreglass tower was bolted to it.[^1] The society dates the lighting of that tower to 1989 and an electric lantern to 1992.[^2]

In 2003 the Coast Guard removed the fibreglass tower and set up a pair of range lights on the point, which Lighthouse Friends says guide mariners through the strait.[^1][^2]

## Today

No lighthouse stands on Eddy Point now.[^2] The Coast Guard's List of Lights gives the front range light an occulting green characteristic with an 11-second period, operating 24 hours a day, and places the rear range light 361 m from it.[^7] Lighthouse Friends says the modern range lights can be seen from the point today.[^1] The Nova Scotia society lists the site as reached by car.[^2]
