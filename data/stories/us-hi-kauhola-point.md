Kauhola Point Light stood near the northern tip of the Big Island of Hawaii, in the Kohala country of Hawaii County.[^2][^4] It was first lit in 1897 and guided interisland shipping between Honolulu and Hilo. Its last concrete tower was demolished in December 2009, and a light on a pole took its place.[^2][^5][^8]

## The place

Steep cliffs surround the point, and reefs extend from it for roughly two miles.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin describes the point as a low spur of land, off which a dangerous reef is generally marked by breakers.[^5] LighthouseFriends calls it an important landfall station, and the Bulletin calls the light an important aid for all interisland traffic between Honolulu and Hilo.[^2][^5]

In October 1910 the Hawaiian Gazette reported that land would be condemned for lights in North Kohala, at Mahukona landing and at Kauhola. A right of way to the nearest government road went with the Mahukona land, but the paper said no such provision was made for Kauhola.[^9] LighthouseFriends says several ships were wrecked off the point before the government built its first light.[^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says the Hawaiian Government built a wood-frame, forty-foot (12 m) tower, topped by an enclosed lamp room, with a lens and reflector made by Barbier and Benard of Paris. The light was activated in 1897, one year before Hawaii became a U.S. territory.[^2]

In 1904 the Lighthouse Board took control of the territory's lights. Its report found Kauhola in a dilapidated condition, and noted that no keeper's residence was attached to the station.[^2] At some point after 1904 a lamp on a thirty-foot (9 m) mast replaced the old wooden tower.[^2][^4]

Edward K. Moealoha was keeper from 1907 to 1913.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that when he was forced to spend a night at the light, his only shelter was a small service shed.[^2] A keeper's dwelling was completed on 12 January 1914 at a cost of $2,833, built roughly 475 feet (145 m) south of the light.[^2]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses asked for $19,000 to improve the station in the estimates printed in the 1915 volume. The report said that, because of the station's importance, steps had been taken to change the present lens-lantern light.[^6]

By 1917 a temporary wooden tower carried a flashing white light of 67,000 candlepower.[^4] A Commissioner's estimate says a fourth-order flash lens was installed on a temporary frame tower at the station.[^7] LighthouseFriends describes its lamp as an incandescent oil vapor lamp.[^2]

The station became a one-keeper station on 16 June 1922, after a thermostat alarm was fitted to warn of trouble with the light.[^2]

In March 1933 the light moved into a new tower. The Bulletin says a permanent tower of heavily reinforced concrete, 72 feet (22 m) in height, was completed at Kauhola Point and that the light was moved to it on 16 March. It replaced the wooden structure erected in 1917.[^4] LighthouseFriends describes an eighty-six-foot (26 m), conical tower that cost $17,495.[^2] The two sources disagree on the height, and nothing read here settles it.

The new light flashed alternating red and green. LighthouseFriends says the revolving beacon showed red and green flashes every six seconds.[^2] A liner's second officer reported seeing it from 22½ miles away, and the Bulletin prints that distance.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the autumn of 1929 the Big Island had a seismic crisis. LighthouseFriends says a resident near the station counted 444 earthquakes over three days, and that the swarm broke the mantle of the lamp several times.[^2]

On 25 September 1931 a fire broke out in the lantern room. LighthouseFriends says it was apparently caused by a faulty thermostat, and that the heat broke all the glass in the lantern room. Keeper Sweeney put the fire out before the tower or clockwork was harmed. The lighthouse tender Kukui was sent the next day to help with repairs.[^2]

Demolition News says a 2006 earthquake took about six feet of the point in one go.[^8]

## Keepers

John Hoopii was the first keeper the Archive lists, from 1904 to 1905.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls him "Keeper Hoopii, the first keeper of the light," and says he built a structure beside the tower for more room.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says John M. Sweeney was in charge from 1922 to 1941, save for about six months, the longest tenure at the station.[^2] Sweeney's routine, as R. R. Tinkham, superintendent of the lighthouses in Hawaii, described it, ended at sunrise: "He arises at sunrise and extinguishes the light."[^2]

Sweeney's daughter Lehua McColgan remembered helping with the station's work, including entering weather conditions, earthquakes and the names of passing ships.[^2] The station was electrified in 1932.[^2]

Sidney Estrella was the final keeper, and the Archive gives his service as 1941 to 1951.[^1][^2]

## After the keepers

After a 2007 engineering report, the Coast Guard decided to demolish the 1933 tower. The report estimated that the tower would likely collapse within two to five years from shoreline erosion, and the Coast Guard planned to replace it with a monopole light farther back from the cliff edge.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the 1933 tower stood eighty-five feet (26 m) from the nearest cliff edge when built and was about twenty feet (6 m) from it by 2009. Demolition News gives the original distance as 80 feet (24 m).[^2][^8]

The replacement light was in place by November 2009.[^2]

## Today

Demolition of the 1933 tower began on Friday, 11 December 2009.[^8][^2] Demolition News reports that the Coast Guard had already installed "a new, more modern navigational aid" farther inland.[^8]

When LighthouseFriends visited in 2007, what remained was the headless concrete tower, the ruins of some outbuildings, and the cement piers that had been the foundation of the 1917 tower.[^2] The sources read here do not say what remains today.

The Archive lists the point as not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends, writing before the demolition, says the lighthouse belonged to the Coast Guard, that the grounds were open and the tower closed, and that a gate on the approach road may be locked.[^2] The site is Coast Guard property, and visitors should not go past a closed gate.
