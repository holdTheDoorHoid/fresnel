The Tinicum Island Range Rear Light stands at Billingsport, just north of Paulsboro, New Jersey, on the Delaware River below Philadelphia. It is a cast-iron skeletal tower with a central cylinder and an entrance pavilion at its foot, and the whole structure is painted black. It was lit on 31 December 1880 as the rear mark of a pair of range lights for ships using the river channel.[^1][^2]

## The place

Range lights are paired towers aligned to guide vessels.[^1] The front light is lower than the rear light, which stands some distance behind it.[^1] Mariners seeking the course the lights define align the rear light on top of the front.[^1] The Tinicum Island range guided vessels "along a straight section of the Delaware River navigation channel near Little Tinicum Island."[^1] The National Register nomination says that the Tinicum Island front and rear lights and the Fort Mifflin Bar rear beacon "worked together as a triangulated range, the only one on the Delaware River."[^1] For the dredged channel across Fort Mifflin Bar, ships align the Tinicum Island front light with the Fort Mifflin Bar rear light.[^1]

The sources disagree about when the Delaware's ranges began. The nomination dates the river's range complex to 1876, when the Deepwater Point and New Castle ranges were the first put into operation. It lists the Tinicum Island ranges among those lit in 1880.[^1] A 1938 article in the *Lighthouse Service Bulletin* says that the first ranges on the Delaware were established in 1880, and it names them as the Tinicum Island and Mifflin Bar ranges.[^6] The two accounts contradict each other, and this story records both rather than choosing one.

The original station covered 4.8 acres. Besides the tower, it held a seven-room keeper's dwelling, an oil house, a barn, a privy, a garden and a cistern.[^1]

## Building the light

On 3 March 1879 Congress "appropriated $60,000 to establish the Tinicum Island and Fort Mifflin Bar range lights."[^1] New Jersey's Attorney General approved the deeds and title papers for the purchase of the sites on 28 February 1880.[^1] The bid of John C. Kelly of Philadelphia was accepted for frame buildings at these sites, including the rear light's keeper's dwelling.[^1]

The first money was not enough for the iron tower.[^1] The nomination, citing the Light-House Board's 1880 report, says that "insufficient funding for construction of the iron tower" led Congress to add $15,000 on 16 June 1880, and that the tower was then constructed.[^1]

The cylinder inside the framework is cast iron, eight feet (2.4 m) across, and rises 86 feet (26.2 m) above the ground.[^1] Four cast-iron columns around the tower opening, inside the pavilion, are marked "PHOENIX IRON CO PHILA PA."[^1]

The light was first lit on 31 December 1880, with Benjamin Hannold as its first keeper.[^1][^2] The Light-House Board's annual reports list several small changes after that, and most of them did not affect the skeletal tower.[^1] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1897, the high day mark on the tower was "renewed and raised" to "show above interfering trees."[^4] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901, the tower was "repainted black," and a slatted frame was added on each side just below the watch room.[^5]

## The keepers

The archive's list of keepers for this light begins with Benjamin D. Hannold (1880-1887), followed by Charles B. English (1887-1920), Oscar A. Conard (1920-1923) and John S. Birch (1923-1933).[^3] English's stint is the longest on that list.[^3]

LighthouseFriends names Hannold as keeper of the rear light when the lights were activated on New Year's Eve 1880, but its roster gives his stint as 1881 to 1887.[^2] The two sources differ by a year on when he started, and this story keeps both dates.

The dwelling had its own water supply.[^2] Rainwater from the roof was channelled into a cistern of 3,700 gallons in the cellar, and an open well lay beneath the house as a further supply.[^2] The nomination describes the dwelling as a seven-room keeper's dwelling.[^1]

The nomination ties a keeper's death to this light. It refers to "lighthouse keeper Clyde Harrison's death in February 1965," after which Coast Guard personnel considered various replacements.[^1] The Coast Guard accepted the widow's request to stay in the keeper's quarters until 15 May 1965.[^1] LighthouseFriends, drawing on a family member's recollection, places Harrison at the front light, where the family lived and where he tended the range lights and other minor aids in the area until his death in February 1965.[^2] The archive's list for the front light has Clyde S. Harrison serving from 1944 to 1965.[^7] The nomination therefore ties Harrison to this light, while LighthouseFriends and the archive place him at the front light. This story records that conflict without settling it, and the roster lists him with a note.

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends dates the automation of the rear light to 1933. It says that John Birch, the last keeper, stayed on at the light as a custodian until 1945.[^2]

The nomination dates automation to "around 1967." It also says that the oil house, the keeper's dwelling and the barn were demolished, but it gives no date for the demolition.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the rear dwelling was removed in the 1950s, after it had fallen into disrepair.[^2] The sources give different dates for these changes, and this story does not choose between them.

The light's characteristic changed twice.[^1] In 1908 the fixed red light was switched to fixed white with a red sector, and in 1983 it was switched back to fixed red.[^1]

The Coast Guard repainted the light in 2000 and repaired its handrails.[^2] Tours were suspended in 2017 because of structural concerns, and they resumed in September 2018.[^2] A sports complex of three baseball diamonds and a soccer field now surrounds the rear light.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light and leases it to the Borough of Paulsboro.[^1] The nomination says the light "remains an active U.S. Coast Guard aid to navigation," illuminated twenty-four hours a day by a 1,000-watt lamp.[^1]

The Tinicum Rear Range Lighthouse Society of Paulsboro, New Jersey, is dedicated to preserving the light's history and offers public tours.[^1] The nomination says the tower is "open periodically to the public for tours."[^1] LighthouseFriends says that the grounds are open and the tower is open during tours.[^2]

The nomination says that only the tower survives from the 1880 station.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that the only trace of the front light is a cement pier in the river where its 1908 tower stood.[^2] Because tours were suspended in 2017, check with the Society before going.
