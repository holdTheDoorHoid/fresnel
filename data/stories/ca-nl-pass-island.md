Pass Island Light stands on an island at the entrance to Hermitage Bay, on Newfoundland's south coast. It was first shown in 1879, for a fishing community that lived on the island until the 1970s.[^1][^2]

## The place

The island sits just offshore from the western end of the peninsula that divides Connaigre Bay from Hermitage Bay.[^1] The light was set to mark the entrance to Hermitage Bay. A fixed red light, shown from a corner of the dwelling, marked the rocks and reefs extending from the Wolf Rocks, which lie southwest of the island.[^1]

The light was asked for in a petition. LighthouseFriends, citing the Journal of the House of Assembly, reports that in March 1878 a Mr. Alexander presented a petition from George Penny and residents of Pass Island and other settlements in Fortune Bay.[^1] The House of Assembly agreed, plans were drawn, and a contract was let.[^1] The community held out against early offers of resettlement and was moved, mainly to Hermitage, in 1974.[^1]

The name also appears in another kind of record. The United States Life-Saving Service's annual reports list "Pass Island, Hermitage Bay" among the places where American vessels stranded. Both the report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1902 and the one for 1907 carry the entry.[^6][^7] Each table gives a count for each place, by fiscal year. It does not name the vessels, and it does not say that any ship struck the light.

## Building the light

The lighthouse was completed in 1878, but its light could not be shown until 1879, because the necessary materials had not arrived from Scotland in time.[^1]

The first station was a rectangular two-storey dwelling, with a lantern room on one end of its flat roof. The domed lantern had triangular glass panes and showed a fixed white light from a fourth-order lens, at a focal plane of 86 m (282 ft).[^1][^2]

In 1879 the inspector, John T. Nevill, tested the two lights from the water. He went round the island slowly by steamer to judge the double light, then ran out in line with it to find where the two lights merged into one. He judged the red light a useful danger warning.[^1]

A one-storey, flat-roofed fog-signal building went up in 1914. Its alarm was driven by air compressed by an oil engine, and it gave one four-second blast every ninety seconds.[^1] In 1917 an octagonal wooden tower with sloping sides replaced the first lantern house, joined to the dwelling by a short covered way.[^1][^2]

The Coast Guard's list gives the present tower as a white skeleton tower, 9.4 m (31 ft) high. Rowlett gives 9.5 m (31 ft).[^4][^2] The light shows a white flash every ten seconds, with a red light as a secondary light.[^4] The fog signal stands on its own skeleton tower, with a white daymark and a black horizontal stripe.[^4]

## The keepers

T.W. Bulley was hired as the first keeper.[^1] In 1885 the inspector called him a model keeper, and reported every part of the station, inside and out, in good order, with everything from the coal store to the lantern room clean.[^1]

In 1889, while carrying oil to the lantern room, Bulley slipped and fell against the lens. The damage was judged not serious.[^1]

In 1894 the inspector removed a few boards and found rot beneath them. He reported the south and east sides in very bad condition. In 1895 a carpenter found the tower decayed and unsafe, and the tower repairs were put off until the following year.[^1]

LighthouseFriends lists the keepers as T.W. Bulley (at least 1885 to 1903), Theodore Gaulton (1904 to at least 1912), William Carter (at least 1921) and Harry Carter (at least 1935 to at least 1945).[^1] In 1921 William Carter kept the fog alarm, with his son Chesley as assistant. By 1935 William had retired but still lived at the station. Harry, another son, was then in charge, with a brother-in-law as assistant.[^1] The 1945 census, as LighthouseFriends reads it, still shows Harry as keeper of the fog alarm, with his father living with the family.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard's list still shows the light as a white flash every ten seconds, with a range of 12 nautical miles.[^4]

Coast Guard figures from April 2010, printed in a 2011 Senate of Canada report on lightkeepers, list four staffed positions at Pass Island on a 28-day rotation. The report counts Pass Island among five remote Newfoundland lightstations.[^3] Its remote sites are those with no year-round road access, and 29 of the 50 staffed lightstations in the two regions are of that kind.[^3] The same report also describes all 23 staffed lightstations in Newfoundland and Labrador as automated, meaning their navigation aids can function in the absence of a lightkeeper.[^3] Fisheries and Oceans Canada describes the province's lightstations in general: most lightkeepers now live in neighbouring communities and commute to work. That is a provincial description, not a record of this station.[^5]

LighthouseFriends says the island should be visible from the end of the road beyond Seal Cove, that the grounds are open, and that the Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse.[^1] Rowlett says the island is accessible only by boat, and that the station's buildings had fallen into ruin by 2008, though a fog signal was still active.[^2] This is a working Coast Guard station on a remote island, and the sources give no official visitor arrangement. Do not land there uninvited. The newest staffing figure the sources give is from April 2010.[^3]
