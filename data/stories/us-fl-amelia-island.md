Florida's oldest standing lighthouse is a white, stuccoed brick tower on a knoll above Egan's Creek, about a mile from downtown Fernandina Beach and half a mile from the Atlantic. It began its working life on another island: the tower was taken down on Cumberland Island, Georgia, and rebuilt here in 1839.[^1]

## The place

By the 1830s the channel into the St. Marys entrance had shifted southward, and the Cumberland Island lighthouse, built in 1820, was losing its value as a guide. In 1834 Francisco Pons, writing for the merchants and mariners of Fernandina, asked Florida's delegate in Congress for a light on Amelia Island in its place, arguing that the old one could not be seen when entering the bar. Congress approved the new light in March 1837.[^1]

The government's agent, Archibald Clark, chose the highest point of land on the island, a bluff nearly sixty feet (18 m) above sea level, and in April 1838 signed a deed for six acres there with Mary Fernandez, the widow of Domingo Fernandez.[^1]

## Building the light

The Treasury awarded the contract on 25 October 1838 to Winslow Lewis of Boston, who had built the Cumberland Island tower eighteen years earlier, for $7,000. It covered a keeper's dwelling as well as the tower, and called for the old lighthouse to be removed and rebuilt on the new site. In December 1838 the naval inspector George Hollins pressed for speed, calling the Cumberland tower "not in very good order; this light but little use, the channel having shifted," as the nomination quotes him.[^1]

The National Register nomination says Lewis finished rebuilding on 4 March 1839. The tower was 50 feet (15 m) tall and, with its lantern, 64 feet (19.5 m). The lantern held fourteen revolving lamps with fifteen-inch reflectors.[^1] A descriptive summary in the Light-House Board's report volume for 1893-94 gives a different figure, calling it a white brick tower 58 feet high.[^5] The tower's walls were built with hollow cavities, a technique the nomination says was common among contractors, and it calls this the only hollow-wall lighthouse left standing from Florida's territorial era.[^1]

The Light-House Board made its own changes. A third-order Fresnel lens was installed in 1856; the nomination says the pre-war lens came back after the Civil War and within several years was replaced by another third-order lens, made in 1868 by Henry-LaPaute of Paris. In 1881 the Light-House Board's annual report, as the nomination quotes it, said the old lantern "was too small for the third order lens which it inclosed," and a new one was set up. During the work the main light was shown from a temporary wooden pyramid carrying a fourth-order light, and the board gave notice that it would be back in the old tower from 1 July 1881.[^1] A brick oil house followed in 1890, and in October 1903 a Barbier and Benard lens was installed along with a revolving mechanism designed by Colonel D. H. Heap, giving a flash every ten seconds.[^1] Electric power replaced the oil-vapor lamp (installed in 1915) with a 500-watt bulb in 1933.[^1]

The main light also anchored a system of smaller lights marking the shifting bar. By 1856 three beacons stood at the northeast edge of the island, and in 1858 a rear range light was added; the system lasted until the jetties of the 1880s. A gale in 1899 destroyed the range structures.[^1]

## The keepers

Amos Latham, a Connecticut native and Revolutionary War veteran, moved from Cumberland Island with the light and was keeper until his death in 1842.[^1] The Digital Archive of the US Lighthouse Society lists Edmund Richardson (1842 to 1849), George W. Walton (1849 to 1854), Horace D. Vaughan (1854 to 1857) and James W. Woodland (1857 to 1859) after him.[^2] The nomination says Walton was removed in 1854 for speaking "in terms most intemperate and bitter" against President Franklin Pierce.[^1]

Woodland's story runs through the Civil War. The nomination says he kept the light until Florida seceded and then served as the Confederacy's keeper until Confederate authorities took the lighting apparatus away to Madison, Florida. His pay, $400 a year from the federal government, was cut to $300. His wife, Winifred Woodland, was appointed assistant keeper in 1860, the only woman the nomination records as officially serving as a keeper here in the historic period.[^1] Federal troops occupied Fernandina in March 1862, but the light stayed dark; in 1864 the Light-House Board reported steps taken to re-establish it.[^1]

Dewayne W. Suydam was assistant in the early 1870s and keeper from 1880 until failing health ended his service in 1891.[^1] Charles W. Grimm followed, a German-born former Navy man who kept the light through the Spanish-American War and the 1903 lens change.[^1] The Digital Archive dates his term 1892 to 1905, the nomination 1891 to 1906.[^1][^2]

Then came the O'Hagans. Thomas P. O'Hagan, born in Brooklyn to Irish immigrants, joined the Lighthouse Service in 1880 and came to Amelia Island in 1905.[^1][^3] He retired on 30 June 1925, at 66; the Lighthouse Service Bulletin called his record "meritorious" and said he "was held in esteem by the officers in charge of the district."[^3] He and his wife raised twelve children, three of whom became keepers.[^1] His son Thomas J. O'Hagan was assistant from 1912 and keeper from 1925 to 1954, the longest tenure at the station.[^1] By marriage the family was related to Amos Latham.[^1]

In the spring of 1939 Helen O'Hagan, the younger Thomas's wife, wrote a poem for the lighthouse's hundredth anniversary that includes the lines "Around this Tower my children play, my husband is its keeper."[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

During World War I, wrecks and groundings in the shoals of Nassau Sound led to a red sector, a pane of red glass the full height of the lantern, facing southeast.[^1]

In the Second World War the light kept burning, since its brief flashes were needed for navigation. Thomas O'Hagan joined the Coast Guard in June 1941, with a fast launch for tending the smaller aids on the Amelia River and at Tiger Island. Six Coast Guard recruits kept a round-the-clock watch from the gallery, and a short-wave radio linked the station to Mayport. The nomination says no vessels were sunk off Amelia Island by submarines, and that O'Hagan made no rescues.[^1]

## After the keepers

Thomas O'Hagan retired in 1954, and his successors stayed about two years each.[^1] The Coast Guard automated the light in 1970, ending 131 years of resident keepers; Otho O. Brown was the last.[^1][^2] The Coast Guard put the lighthouse on its disposal list in 1998, a non-profit formed to restore it, and on 28 March 2001 the City of Fernandina Beach took ownership.[^1]

## Today

The tower is owned by the City of Fernandina Beach; the Coast Guard, with Auxiliary volunteers, maintains the light, which still shows as an aid to navigation.[^4][^2] The grounds, at 215 O'Hagan Lane, are open for viewing on Saturdays from 11:00 a.m. to 2:00 p.m. Inside access is by a city-run tour, offered on the first and third Wednesdays of each month with advance registration; the city says the tour does not allow visitors to climb the stairs.[^4]
