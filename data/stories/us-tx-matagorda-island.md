A black cast-iron tower stands on the northeast end of Matagorda Island, a barrier island in Calhoun County, Texas, six miles offshore from Port O'Connor and reached by boat. It was built to mark Pass Cavallo, the gap that let ships into Matagorda Bay. Its first tower was lit in 1852. The Civil War, the sea and the hurricanes took turns at it, and the tower now standing is a rebuild on a new site, first lit in 1873.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Pass Cavallo lies between Matagorda Peninsula and Matagorda Island. Ships bound for the port of Indianola, which one history calls the second busiest in Texas after Galveston, had to come through it.[^2] The county historical commission's narrative says the federal government built a full-scale lighthouse here in 1852 because of increasing marine traffic through the pass, on 7.5 acres at the extreme northeast end of the island.[^3]

## Building the light

Congress set aside $15,000 for Texas lights on 3 March 1847, but over five years passed before the lights became a reality. The land was bought by 1848, and the state's governor did not sign the cession of jurisdiction until 29 September 1851.[^2] The Baltimore firm of Murray and Hazlehurst then cast the iron sections for the 55-foot (17 m) tower. The materials reached the island in the summer of 1852, and the iron tower, with a brick lining, rose that season.[^2]

Sources differ on the first lighting. LighthouseFriends and the Calhoun County Museum's timeline both give 31 December 1852, when Keeper James Cummings lit a revolving set of reflector lamps.[^2][^4] The county narrative says only "December of 1852".[^3] They also differ on the daymark, which LighthouseFriends dates to the first year of service and the county narrative to spring 1854.[^2][^3]

To make the light carry farther, Murray and Hazlehurst added 24 feet (7.3 m) to the tower, and a third-order Fresnel lens replaced the reflectors.[^2][^3] The county narrative has the contract in 1857 and the work done in 1858; the museum timeline dates the extra height to 1857 and the lens to 1859; LighthouseFriends says the lens was lit in July 1859.[^3][^4][^2]

The war interrupted the light. The county narrative says Confederate troops removed the lens in December 1862 and tried to blow the tower up with an explosive charge, which damaged six of its cast-iron panels.[^3] In 1865 a temporary three-story wooden tower was set up to show a light. The county narrative calls its lens fourth-order; LighthouseFriends and the museum say fifth-order.[^3][^2][^4] LighthouseFriends adds that the keeper's family lived in this tower and that it leaked badly.[^2]

The sea was also closing in. By 1866 the surf threatened the damaged tower, and it was taken apart, its pieces stored on the island.[^2] A new site about two miles inland was bought; LighthouseFriends has workers arriving early in 1872 while the county narrative says reconstruction began in 1873; the work stalled on 31 July 1872 when the $20,000 appropriation ran out, then resumed the following May with another $12,000.[^2][^3] The museum's timeline dates the move to 1870 and says a new third-order lens was ordered in July of that year from Sautter and Company of Paris.[^4]

The Lighthouse Board's notice of 2 July 1873, printed in the New York Herald, announced that the light in the old tower would be extinguished on 1 September and a third-order white flashing light shown from the new tower, visible 15 nautical miles. It described the tower as cast iron, in the shape of a truncated cone, painted black.[^5] The county narrative confirms the date, 1 September 1873.[^3] The notice's figure for the distance between the two towers is damaged in the scan, so the histories' "two miles" cannot be checked against it here.

The Light-House Board's reports record upkeep in the years that followed. The report volume labelled 1899 lists the light as third-order, flashing white every ninety seconds, and records a 310-foot wharf and a boathouse built at the station.[^6] The report volume for 1900 records the tower, dome and gallery scraped and painted black, a copper-wire net put round the lantern against flying birds, the keeper's dwelling gallery rebuilt and the wharf extended to deep water.[^7]

Sources give the new tower's height in different ways: 84 feet to the centre of the lantern in the lighthouse society's archive, 92 feet above sea level in the county narrative, and a light 91 feet above sea level in LighthouseFriends.[^1][^3][^2]

## The keepers

The lighthouse society's archive lists a long roster of head keepers and assistants for the station between 1852 and 1956.[^1] The first, James E. Cummings, served 1852 to 1853; descendants of his family came to the 2004 ceremony at the tower.[^1][^2] James R. McCreary followed, from 1853 to 1861, with Henry Westerman as first assistant.[^1]

In the years after the war the archive lists Almon Reed as keeper for 1866 to 1869 and again 1871 to 1872. Emily Reed is listed as first assistant for 1868 to 1869, and Nancy S. Reed for 1869.[^1] The sources cited do not say how they were related.

Joseph Forrestier is listed as first assistant from 1886, then as head keeper from 1888 to 1911, a term of 23 years by the archive's dates.[^1] A small cemetery east of the tower holds the graves of Adeline Forestier and William Henry Forestier, described as the wife and infant son of Keeper Joseph Forestier. LighthouseFriends also reports a stone for Theodore Olsen, keeper from 1913 until his death, which it lists as December 1918.[^2]

Heinroth, listed as keeper from 1918 to 1946, was followed by Arthur B. Barr, who had been his assistant from 1938.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls Barr Heinroth's son-in-law and dates the handover to 1948; the archive's roster dates it to 1946.[^2][^1] Barr remembered the island's worst feature as the rattlesnakes, and the keepers were issued antivenin in case anyone was bitten.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The station took the Gulf's weather. LighthouseFriends reports that the hurricane of 19 to 20 August 1886 drove the keepers and families into the tower, where the water rose four feet, shook a bull's-eye panel of the lens loose and severely damaged the dwelling.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports that a hurricane on 18 August 1916 damaged the station among others in the district.[^8]

On the night of 12 to 13 October 1918 Keeper Olsen wrote that the air was calm and the bugs "so thick around the lantern that they obscured the light". He had screen wire over the ventilators and still had to shovel the insects out of the lantern and off the gallery.[^9] After the hurricane of 13 to 14 September 1919, Heinroth was commended for keeping the light, saving the station motor boat and caring for the Coast Guard station crew who took refuge in the tower.[^10]

The keepers also helped boats in trouble. Heinroth helped float a grounded tug on 10 November 1921, and Assistant Keeper Charles M. Teller towed a disabled launch to Port O'Connor on 17 May 1923.[^11][^12]

On 28 June 1929 the keeper had gone to town for mail and supplies, about 14 miles from the station, when an unexpected tropical storm overtook him on the way back, estimated at 75 to 90 miles an hour. Three men from the upper end of the island took shelter in the tower overnight, and the assistant keeper helped with rescue work using a plane operating near the Coast Guard station. The Bulletin says the station suffered only minor damage.[^13]

## After the keepers

Electricity reached the island in 1956, and the station was automated soon after. Barr stayed on as civilian custodian, checking the area's other lights, until he retired in 1966, says LighthouseFriends.[^1][^2] The Coast Guard removed the Fresnel lens in 1977 intending to close the light, but public protest kept a lesser light going until 1995.[^2][^3]

After the light went out in 1995 the tower was left abandoned.[^3] LighthouseFriends says a Houston oilman, dismayed by its condition in 1997, and the county judge recruited friends to form the Matagorda Island Foundation, while the county narrative dates the foundation to 1998.[^2][^3] The county narrative says the light was relit at midnight on 31 December 1999, and that a $1.23 million renovation was celebrated in 2004; LighthouseFriends dates the celebration of the first phase to 11 June 2004 and puts the state transportation grant at $905,000.[^3][^2]

## Today

The tower stands within the Matagorda Island Wildlife Management Area, which the county narrative says is reachable only by boat or plane.[^3] LighthouseFriends reports that the tower is three miles from the island dock, that its interior is closed to the public, and that the grounds are open.[^2] Visitors need to check with the island's managers before planning a trip. Ownership is described two ways: LighthouseFriends says in one place that the Coast Guard handed the tower to the Fish and Wildlife Service, which leased it to Calhoun County, and in another that Texas Parks and Wildlife owns it.[^2] The archive records the aid as active with a solar-powered optic of 250 mm.[^1] The Fresnel lens is on display at the Calhoun County Museum in Port Lavaca.[^2] The tower was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1984.[^3]
