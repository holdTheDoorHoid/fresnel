Grand Traverse Light stands at the tip of Michigan's Leelanau Peninsula, where ships rounding the point turned between Lake Michigan and Grand Traverse Bay. The present brick dwelling-and-tower has been there since 1858, and in the 1980s it was rescued from decay and opened as a museum.[^1][^2]

## The place

Vessel masters travelling between the Manitou Passage and the Straits of Mackinac made a turn off the northern end of the peninsula, and the same point marked the entrance to Grand Traverse Bay. The museum's history page, drawn from Terry Pepper's lighthouse writing, says a light was first recommended for Cat's Head Point in 1849.[^3] Congress appropriated $4,000 on 28 September 1850,[^2] and a presidential order of 30 June 1851 set aside 58.75 acres of public land for the station.[^2] The light was known early on as Cat Head Light, from the point beside it.[^2]

## Building the light

Sources disagree about the first station. Lighthouse Friends says building began in late 1851 and describes a 30-foot (9 m) conical brick tower beside a two-room keeper's dwelling, with a fixed light from six lamps and 14-inch reflectors first shown in September 1852.[^2] The museum's history page says work began in spring 1852 and describes a squat rubble-stone tower with Lewis lamps, and says only that keeper David Moon first appeared on district payroll records on 7 September, so the light was probably shown soon afterwards.[^3]

Both accounts agree it did not last. Lighthouse Friends says the station was found "poorly built and poorly situated," useful to vessels entering and leaving the bay but of little service to those on Lake Michigan.[^2] The old buildings were pulled down in 1858 and a new building went up on higher ground: a Milwaukee cream city brick dwelling with a short square wooden tower on its roof ridge.[^2][^3] The museum's account puts a fifth-order Fresnel lens in that tower, in a cast-iron lantern, with a focal plane of 103 feet (31 m).[^3] The US Lighthouse Society archive instead lists the light at 40 feet (12 m) above the water, so the height is unsettled.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the lens was upgraded to a fourth-order Barbier and Finestre lens in 1870, which the Light-House Board called "a very necessary and decided improvement."[^2] The same page also says, earlier, that a fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the light source in 1857, which does not fit that account.[^2]

Later additions are recorded in the Light-House Board's reports. A two-story barn, 18 by 24 feet (5.5 by 7.3 m), is recorded in the report for 1891.[^4] The museum's page places a barn of the same size in 1880, while Lighthouse Friends says 1891.[^3][^2] An addition to it is recorded in the report for 1895.[^5] In 1895 the Board argued that a fog signal at Grand Traverse was "deemed necessary to navigation"; that wording is as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, and the report of the next year renewed the request for $5,500.[^6][^2] Congress appropriated the money in the act of 1 July 1898, and the signal was contracted in January 1899.[^7] The contract date is given as 20 January in the Board's report and by Lighthouse Friends, and as 10 January by the museum.[^7][^2][^3] The ten-inch steam whistle, in duplicate, went into service on 20 December 1899, in a buff pressed-brick building roofed with red metal tiles.[^8] The Board's report for 1900 says the whistle had run about 183 hours and burned about 18 cords of wood.[^8] Lighthouse Friends says 1904 was the busiest year on record, at about 318 hours and 49 cords.[^2]

The whistle meant more work than one keeper could do, and the dwelling was altered to hold two families. Lighthouse Friends dates this to 1900 and the museum to 1901.[^2][^3] The National Register lists 1858 and 1900 as the station's significant years.[^11]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society archive lists 23 principal keepers between 1852 and 1972.[^1] David Moon came first and resigned within about a year; Philo Beers, a former deputy US marshal, took over on 15 April 1853.[^2][^3] The archive gives his term as 1853 to 1857.[^1] Lighthouse Friends and the museum both repeat a story of raids by followers of James Strang, the "king" of Beaver Island, who were said to have taken Beers's nets on one occasion and station supplies on another; the museum calls the accounts "reportedly" and relates that Beers once drove off a party that tried to carry off the Fresnel lens.[^2][^3] No dated record of these raids appears in the sources read. Beers's son Henry J. Beers was keeper from 1859 to 1861, and the two used material from the old keeper's dwelling to build a house in Northport.[^1][^2]

Priscilla Poncher appears as principal keeper in 1873, between Henry R. Shetterly (1862 to 1873) and John C. Hall (1873 to 1874); the archive gives only the year.[^1] Peter Nelson then served from 1874 to 1890, and George W. Buttars from 1890 to 1918.[^1] In 1869 the district inspector found the station in good condition except for some woodwork and doors that had shrunk from improperly seasoned lumber.[^3]

The assistant's post turned over quickly after it was created: the archive lists at least three first assistants (Oscar E. Dame, Nels A. Nelson and Hector Stebbins) for 1907 alone.[^1] Dame also served again from the late 1920s; Lighthouse Friends says Bobbie Ditzler, whose great uncle he was, became first president of the friends group that grew into the museum.[^1][^2] The archive gives his second term as 1928 to 1938; Lighthouse Friends says 1923 to 1938.[^1][^2] Between 1918 and 1923 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index records an efficiency flag awarded to the station and to keeper Reynold W. Johnson.[^10] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1921, the Commissioner's report credits Johnson and assistant E. C. Johnson with helping the launch *Dolphin*, which "had been blown ashore."[^9]

James McCormick kept the light from 1924 to 1938, having moved from South Fox Island in 1923 according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1][^2] His son Doug, then eight, grew up there until he enlisted in the Coast Guard in 1935. He helped his father polish the lens and trim the wick, played along the shoreline in summer and skated on the frozen water in winter.[^2] McCormick's stonework survives on the grounds, including a stone birdhouse, a planter and steps.[^2] Bette McCormick Olli, whom Lighthouse Friends calls one of "John and Mary McCormick's" twelve children (the keeper on the roster is James), wrote a pamphlet, *The Way It Was*, about life at the lighthouse.[^2]

The light was electrified in 1950.[^2] The archive lists Coast Guard personnel at the station from 1955 onward.[^1] On 24 December 1967 keeper John Marken (the Lighthouse Friends text also spells the name Marten) stood the midnight to 8 a.m. watch, left with his wife that day, and the couple were later found dead in their car, which had left the road about half a mile from the station.[^2] Coastguardsman Marion Lee Hancock, on duty, acted as officer in charge until William A. Staples arrived on 4 February 1968.[^2][^1]

## After the keepers

The fog signal's boilers and whistle were removed in 1933 and replaced by a diaphone driven by compressors; a 440-volt compressor followed in 1953.[^3][^2] In 1972 the tower light was replaced by an automated beacon on a skeletal steel tower, and the last Coast Guard personnel left.[^2] The empty buildings fell into disrepair until a local group formed in the mid-1980s (1984 by Lighthouse Friends, 1985 by the museum) to restore them.[^2][^3] The museum says it reopened the station on Memorial Day 1987; Lighthouse Friends says half the building opened in 1986.[^3][^2] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 19 July 1984.[^11] Ownership passed from the Coast Guard to the Michigan Department of Parks and Recreation in September 2003.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse sits inside Leelanau State Park, 8 miles (13 km) north of Northport, at the end of Lighthouse Point Road.[^12] The museum says it is open seasonally, from April to December, and that visitors can walk through the restored keeper's dwelling and climb the tower; it is closed in winter, though the park and grounds stay open and a Michigan Recreation Passport is required.[^12] A keeper program, begun in 2004, lets visitors stay in the assistant keeper's quarters.[^2]
