Prince Shoal Light (Phare du Haut-fond Prince) is a red-and-white steel-and-concrete tower rising straight out of the St. Lawrence at the mouth of the Saguenay, south of Tadoussac Bay in Quebec's Haute-Côte-Nord. It replaced a line of lightships, the last on the St. Lawrence, and its best-known day is the Christmas storm of 1966, when the sea broke into it.[^6][^4][^1]

## The place

The shoal that gives the light its name lies at the entrance to the Saguenay River. According to Lighthouse Friends, it was named after the Prince of Wales, whose ship H.M.S. *Hero* ran onto it in August 1860 on the way to open the Victoria Bridge at Montreal; the source says the shoal was not on the Admiralty's maps before that.[^1] The Department of Marine's report published in 1906 describes two patches, with the mark moored on the south edge of the more westerly one in 4 fathoms (about 7 m) of water.[^2] Until 1905 the danger was marked by a buoy. On 4 August 1902 the middle-ground can buoy on Prince Shoal was replaced by a steel gas buoy with a Pintsch lantern, painted in red and black bands with the name in white letters.[^3]

## The ships

At the opening of navigation in 1905 an iron lightship, formerly stationed at the Lower Traverse, replaced the gas buoy and was moored in the same position.[^2] The Department's report describes a red hull lettered "Prince shoal" and "No. 7" in white, a red ball hoisted on the mainmast by day and a steam fog whistle during thick weather.[^2] A submarine bell was added; it struck the number 7 every twenty-two seconds, and the report says Prince Shoal was the last of five lightships in eastern Canadian waters to receive the new aid.[^2] Jean Cloutier, writing on the lightships of the St. Lawrence, dates the vessel's stay at Prince Shoal from 1906 to 1956, a year later than the Department's report, which says the opening of navigation in 1905.[^4][^2]

By 1909 the Prince Shoal, Red Island and White Island lightships were kept in position under contract, the keepers receiving $3,000 for the season for providing and maintaining crews, while the department supplied fuel, light and engine supplies and made repairs.[^5] According to Lighthouse Friends, when White Island Reef Lighthouse began operating in 1956, Lightship No. 20 was transferred to Prince Shoal and No. 7 was withdrawn.[^1] Cloutier counts the era of lightships on the St. Lawrence as 134 years, from 1830 to 1964, ending with the arrival of the permanent pillar at Prince Shoal.[^4]

## Building the light

The Canadian Register of Historic Places says construction of the tower started in 1961, in response to the year-round opening of the seaway the year before, and that it was designed to replace a seasonal lightship.[^6] Lighthouse Friends records that designs were drawn up in 1961 and that the hourglass-shaped structure was built at the Champlain Drydock in Lévis; workers called it the "yo-yo".[^1] On 6 August 1963 the steel caisson left Lévis under tow by four chartered tugs for a 105-mile (about 170 km) trip downriver. After fifty hours of fog and storms it reached the shoal, where its valves were opened and it was sunk onto a prepared foundation, then filled with crushed rock and cement.[^1] The accommodation level and the light tower itself were added on site in 1964, and the light was reported to have been activated that summer.[^1] The register describes a steel and reinforced-concrete structure built from a custom Department of Transport plan, with a waisted caisson, a disc-like accommodation unit, a slender offset light tower and a helicopter pad on the roof.[^6]

## The keepers

The keepers on duty in 1966, according to Lighthouse Friends, were head keeper Roger Lagacé, Claude Fraser and the newest of them, Yvanhoé Gagnon, who had the night watch.[^1] Lighthouse Friends adds that Gagnon had been a cook at the Hôtel Tadoussac before joining the service, and that after the storm he alone stayed on the lights, later serving on the North Coast and at Anticosti Island.[^1] A fourth man took part by radio: Jos. Therrien, keeper at Pointe Noire, who wintered in Tadoussac and spoke to Prince Shoal by radiotelephone.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Christmas storm of 1966 is the event that made the station known. By Lighthouse Friends' account, Gagnon was preparing the keepers' Christmas dinner when a north-easter began to rise; Lagacé, coming on at 4:00, noticed the change. The anemometer was registering gusts to its maximum of sixty knots.[^1] At dawn a wave broke open the station's steel doors, and sea water doused the furnace burners below.[^1] Lagacé raised Therrien on the radio, and Therrien in turn reached the Coast Guard's district officer in Quebec, but help could not be sent in the weather.[^1] Waves then broke windows in the living level and Lagacé's room, at high tide the waves were breaking over the helicopter pad, and spray froze on the light tower into an ice shell 15 to 20 cm (six to eight inches) thick.[^1] The three men were taken off by helicopter to Tadoussac, at 15:30 by Lighthouse Friends' account, once the river had calmed. Lighthouse Friends speaks of a twenty-four-hour battle, the register of two days of pounding; the sources do not agree on how long it lasted.[^1]

The Register of Historic Places says only that high waves, fierce winds and snow battered the tower for two days, that it was badly damaged, and that it survived conditions far harsher than it was designed for.[^6] Lighthouse Friends adds that the vibration tore the xenon lamps from their mounts and the light stayed dark until a temporary light was lit on 11 January 1967.[^1] The keepers went back the next spring, but after fifteen days Lagacé left lightkeeping, and Fraser left a few months later for a Coast Guard ship.[^1]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends reports that Prince Shoal was automated in 1987, leaving Île Rouge as the only staffed light in Quebec.[^1]

## Today

The tower is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard and is closed to visitors.[^1] The Coast Guard's List of Lights gives it as "Haut-fond Prince", a white cylindrical tower with red bands, showing a white flash every 2.5 seconds at 24.6 m above the water, with a nominal range of 18 nautical miles, year round.[^7] The register's statement of value cites the 1966 storm and describes the tower as one of several light towers in the area visited by tour groups.[^6] It can be seen from a distance from Tadoussac or Pointe Noire, and whale-watching boats from Tadoussac and Baie-Sainte-Catherine pass by it.[^1]
