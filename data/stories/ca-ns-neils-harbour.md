Neil's Harbour Light stands on Neil Head, a rocky promontory on the east side of the entrance to a small cove on the Atlantic coast of Cape Breton Island.[^1][^2][^3] The Department of Marine and Fisheries built its square, tapered wooden tower in 1899 for a village whose boats needed a light to find their way back safely, and the building is now a heritage lighthouse.[^1][^3]

## The place

The cove had been a seasonal fishing base in the 1850s and 1860s, and by 1899 a permanent fleet was working from it.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says Scottish and French settlers fished there in the 1700s, and that Neil MacLennan, a merchant based at Westmont, brought dry goods to the cove by boat and dealt with the fishermen there. The fishermen began calling the place Neil's Cove.[^2] Between 1871 and 1891 the population of Neil's Harbour and nearby New Haven rose to 430, with an influx of Newfoundland fishermen.[^2] By the 1880s the village had a school and was linked by coastal steamships to Sydney.[^1]

## Building the light

A description the Department published in 1899, which Lighthouse Friends prints, gives a square-plan wooden tower with sloping sides, painted white and topped by an octagonal iron lantern painted red. It stood 10.4 m (34 ft) from its base to the lantern's ventilator.[^2][^3] The tower stood on ground 14 m (46 ft) above high water and 20 m (65 ft) back from the edge of the bank.[^2] The contractor was P. McFarlane of Baddeck, under a contract of $725.[^2] The light was fixed red, 22 m (73 ft) above high water, and visible 8 miles from all points of approach by water, the description says. The illuminating apparatus was dioptric, of the seventh order.[^2]

## The keepers

Angus A. Buchanan was hired as the first keeper on 14 August 1899, at an annual salary of $150.[^2] The Department's staff table for the year ended 31 March 1911 still lists A. A. Buchanan at Neil Harbour, appointed 14 August 1899.[^5] According to the register, he was the only lightkeeper in Nova Scotia who sat in the provincial legislature.[^1]

In 1910 Buchanan was given a hand-operated fog horn, and he was required to sound it when vessels signalled during periods of limited visibility.[^2] The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1911 records the station's entry as "Neil harbour, east coast, Cape Breton Island. — Hand fog horn at lighthouse."[^4]

George Sweet, Jr. replaced Buchanan in 1911 and was in charge of the lighthouse for the next twenty years.[^2] Walter Bragg was keeper from at least 1936 until 1956, and by Lighthouse Friends' account he had been keeper for over twenty years when he lost his job.[^2]

## After the keepers

On 2 November 1956 a 200-watt electric bulb replaced the oil lamp, and six days later Bragg was out of a job.[^2] From 1964 a time clock switched the light on and off, with a battery-operated 12-volt light for power failures.[^2]

The village's volunteer fire department sold ice cream from the lighthouse from 1999, until Scotty Hatcher took over the operation in 2003.[^2] The Neil's Harbour-New Haven Community Development Association has run the ice cream shop since 2016.[^2]

The lighthouse was recognised as a heritage lighthouse on 12 February 2015, under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act.[^3][^1] Lighthouse Friends says the building was transferred that year to the community development association.[^2] In "This Lighthouse Matters", a 2015 online vote among Nova Scotia lighthouses, the lighthouse came second in the Low Tide category and won $10,000. The prize paid for rotten wood in the lighthouse to be replaced and a leak in the lantern room to be repaired.[^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard's List of Lights gives the tower's light, on the headland at the east side of the entrance, as a white long flash every six seconds, 17.9 m (59 ft) above the water and visible 9 nautical miles.[^6] The list marks it seasonal.[^6] The same list gives a separate red light, a flash every four seconds, at the outer end of the breakwater, visible 3 nautical miles.[^6] The list gives the tower's height as 10.0 m, where the register and the 1899 description give 10.4 m (34 ft). The sources do not explain the difference.[^6][^3] The 1899 description gives a fixed red light. No cited source dates the change.

Ownership is not settled in the sources. Lighthouse Friends says the building went to the community association in 2015, but the same page's visitor notes still say the Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse.[^2] Visitors reach it by Lighthouse Road, which turns off New Haven Road from the Cabot Trail.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and that the tower has served as an ice cream shop in the summer.[^2] The register lists its current function as an eating or drinking establishment.[^3]
