Rocky Point Lighthouse is a small red-and-white iron tower on the south side of the entrance to Harbour Breton, a fishing port on the south coast of Newfoundland. It is the second tower on its point: a wooden light of 1873 burned, and its cast-iron replacement has marked the harbour mouth since 1881.[^1][^2]

## The place

Harbour Breton lies on the northern side of Fortune Bay, and Lighthouse Friends describes it as one of the oldest and largest fishing centres on the south coast of Newfoundland.[^1] In the nineteenth century the London firm Newman and Company, later Newman and Hunt, made it their Newfoundland headquarters and, in that account's words, controlled commercial life in the community.[^1] A fisheries officer who visited in 1871 found about 100 people employed there, exclusive of fishermen, salting, drying and packing fish for Europe, Brazil and the West Indies.[^1] The light was meant to serve that trade. Lighthouse Friends calls it one of the first out-harbour lights designed for the fishing trade on the south coast.[^1]

## Building the light

The first tower went up on Rocky Point in 1873: a square wooden tower 4.3 m (14 ft) tall, painted white with red corners and topped by an octagonal lantern whose sides alternated red and white.[^1] The Town of Harbour Breton's heritage statement credits its establishment to Inspector of Lighthouses John T. Neville (spelled Nevill elsewhere).[^2] Its first keeper, William Lorenzen, was paid $48 a year.[^1]

In 1874 the inspector Austin Oke found the first tower "very leaky" and ordered it puttied and painted.[^1] The light began as a fixed red light. In 1875 it was changed to white, with a red light added to mark Harbor Rock, the end of a ledge running out from the point.[^1] The Canadian Coast Guard's current List of Lights includes a Harbour Rock light buoy.[^4]

In June 1881 the wooden tower was destroyed by fire.[^1][^2] A cast-iron tower, ordered from James Angel of Victoria Iron Works in St. John's, was erected the same year, painted in horizontal red and white bands and fitted with a lantern of sixteen triangular panes.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends puts the light 20.5 m above high water.[^1]

## The keepers

The sources name few people, and they do not wholly agree. Lighthouse Friends lists William Lorenzen (1874 to at least 1878), G. Rideout (at least 1886 to 1891), and Albert Jensen (1892 to at least 1912), with William Jensen recorded in at least 1935.[^1] The town's heritage statement says Albert and William Jensen served in the early 1900s, and that Albert Jensen "actually died at the light tower in 1907 after drinking wood alcohol."[^2] That date does not fit Lighthouse Friends' "at least 1912" for Albert, and which is right is not settled here.

The light was hard on its lens. In 1877 the eighth-order lens was damaged when the lamp's chimney glass broke during the night; the inspector did not blame the keeper, because his pay was not sufficient for a nightly watch, nor did his instructions "require a continuous nightly watch." The same accident happened in 1879, and in 1884, in the iron tower, which also had no dwelling, another incident with a lamp broke the lens.[^1] After the 1881 fire the light was more satisfactory, chiefly, according to the inspector, because "better attendance on the part of the new keeper has been secured by a small addition to his salary."[^1] The following year, 1885, a small addition to the store at the light became a bedroom for the keeper.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1931 an acetylene gas light was installed and a formal keeper was no longer needed; a caretaker was paid to change the gas cylinders and see that the light stayed lit.[^1] The town's heritage statement says the light ran on kerosene in the early 1900s, and that the tower is now solar powered and fully automated.[^2]

The tower was designated a municipal heritage site by the Town of Harbour Breton in 2009, and a second Canadian Register entry also describes the lighthouse.[^2][^3]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard List of Lights gives a white flash every 4 seconds, 16.2 m above water, visible 9 nautical miles, from a red and white cylindrical tower.[^4] The heritage statement for the lighthouse calls it the oldest structure in the town and its largest tourist attraction, with a short trail and lookout around it and interpretive signs.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says a 0.4 km path leads from the end of Route 360 on the south side of the harbour to the lighthouse, and that the grounds are open and the tower closed. It names the Canadian Coast Guard as owner, while our record lists the Town of Harbour Breton as agency, so ownership should be confirmed.[^1]
