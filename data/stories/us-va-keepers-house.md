Old Point Comfort Light stands just outside the red brick walls of Fort Monroe, in Hampton, Virginia, where the entrance to Hampton Roads opens between the mouths of the James, Nansemond and Elizabeth rivers.[^2] Its octagonal stone tower was built in 1802 and 1803, depending on the source.[^1][^2] It is still an active light.[^1] It was automated in 1972, according to the Society's record, or in 1973, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1][^2]

## The place

A navigational beacon stood on Old Point Comfort as early as 1775, when LighthouseFriends says John Dams, caretaker of the ruins at Fort George, was paid an annual 20 pounds to tend a light there.[^2] After the War of 1812 the federal government built Fort Monroe on the point. Congress appropriated $6,000 in 1837 to move the lighthouse inside the fort, but the work was deferred at the last minute, and the tower remains outside the walls.[^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says an 1798 law set aside $3,050 for a light at Old Point Comfort.[^2] Between 1800 and 1801 Congress appropriated another $5,000 and engaged Elzy Burroughs to complete the octagonal stone structure.[^2] Burroughs completed most of the work during 1803.[^2] The tower's height is given differently in the sources: LighthouseFriends says 54 feet (16 m), and the US Lighthouse Society record gives 40 feet (12 m) from base to lantern center, or 48 feet (15 m) above the water.[^2][^1]

Ten oil lamps in reflectors were set in the lantern, burning 486 gallons of oil a year. LighthouseFriends says they could be seen from fourteen miles at sea.[^2] The Society's record gives eleven lamps, with 14-inch reflectors.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes a note from President Jefferson judging Eli Vickery the best man for the post, and says Vickery was appointed first keeper on 11 February 1804.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society record dates the station's establishment to 1802.[^1] The two dates are not reconciled in the sources.

In 1854 a fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps and reflectors, which LighthouseFriends says cut the oil use to a tenth of the old amount.[^2] The Society's record dates the original lens to 1855.[^1] On 20 July 1855 a fog bell was mounted on a wooden tower beside the lighthouse.[^2]

## The keepers

The Society's keeper roster runs from Eli Vickery in 1804 to 1972.[^1] It lists 25 principal keepers and seven first assistants, and it names Coast Guard personnel without a position after 1950.[^1] Paul D. Luke kept the light from 1806 to 1819, and John Luke followed him from 1820 to 1844.[^1]

The roster names women as keepers in several places. Amelia Dewees is listed as principal keeper from 1857 to 1861, and Ellen M. Cropper as first assistant from 1867 to 1871.[^1] Mary S. Hozier is listed as principal keeper for 1918, the year Elijah A. Hozier's stint ends.[^1] The roster gives her a single year and does not say how she and Elijah A. Hozier were related; the sources used here do not say.

William Roscoe Davis (LighthouseFriends prints William R. Davis) was keeper from 1870 to 1878.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says that early in the war he escaped from a plantation in Hampton, then rescued his mother, wife and seven children from other plantations, and that they sought refuge with him inside the walls of Fort Monroe.[^2]

John Bradford Jones kept the light from 1878 to 1908, the longest tenure at the station.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls him a former slave, and says his thirty years as keeper were the longest at the light.[^2] On the night of 31 August 1886, Jones noted that his clock stopped at 9:55 p.m., when the station felt the effects of the major earthquake centered at Charleston, South Carolina. LighthouseFriends gives Jones's account: the shocks lasted about two minutes and were accompanied by a rumbling noise.[^2]

Elijah A. Hozier kept the light from 1908 to 1918.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources read describe no storm that damaged the tower itself. The Jack Tars and Royal Marines of the War of 1812 landed at Old Point Comfort and used the tower as an observation post, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2]

Fort Monroe stayed in Union hands throughout the Civil War. LighthouseFriends says the tower was undisturbed while most east coast lights were damaged or put out. President Lincoln landed at the fort's wharf in 1862 to watch Union troops take Norfolk, and Confederate President Jefferson Davis was imprisoned in an artillery room near the light station.[^2]

On 11 May 1931 a violent electrical storm, with hail, crossed Old Point Comfort in the afternoon. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports that the keeper picked up hailstones "as large as ordinary hens' eggs," and that the only damage at the station was the breaking of two windowpanes.[^8]

## After the keepers

The keeper's dwelling was rebuilt in 1891 or 1892, depending on the source. The Lighthouse Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1892 says that a working party made repairs in April, May and June of 1892, when the old house, too far gone to repair economically, was rebuilt with new outbuildings.[^3] LighthouseFriends dates the new house to 1891 and says the keeper's dwelling was connected to the fort's water supply in 1893.[^2] The Society's record gives the keeper's quarters a date of 1900.[^1]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1893 says that in June 1893 about 50 cart loads of earth were used to grade the ground in front of the dwelling, and that about 300 running feet (91 m) of galvanized iron pipe were "laid to connect the station with the water supply at Fort Monroe."[^9]

In June 1896 the old wooden fog-bell tower was removed and replaced with an iron structure, and an iron oil house was erected.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the oil house and the iron bell tower were both added in 1896.[^2]

In July 1900 the tower's lantern and gallery were painted, and old brick in the tower was replaced with new work laid in cement mortar. The Board reported this under the year ended 30 June 1901.[^5]

In 1917 an electric striker replaced the mechanical fog-bell striker.[^2] In 1918 the light's illuminant was changed from oil to electric incandescent, which LighthouseFriends says increased the light's intensity.[^6][^2]

The light's colour has changed several times. The Board's list of changes records a colour change on 10 March 1899.[^10] LighthouseFriends says the light was fixed white at first, changed to fixed red about 1900, showed a white sector briefly in 1905, and then went back to all red. The automated signal now shows red and white, with the white sector covering 133 degrees.[^2] The two sources do not agree on the dates, and this story does not settle them.

In 1936 a photoelectric control was added to the fog signal.[^2] A beam fired every two minutes from Fort Wool onto a cell at the station, and if the beam failed to arrive, the fog bell sounded.[^2] In 1954 a Coast Guard officer in charge fitted 250-watt bulbs to five poles on the balustrade around the lantern room, to set the light apart from the hotel and attraction lights along the shore. LighthouseFriends quotes a writer who lamented that it made the light look like "an amusement park ride."[^2]

The Society's record gives 1972 as the year of automation. LighthouseFriends says the keeper's dwelling housed two Coast Guard families until automation in 1973.[^1][^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light. The grounds are open, and the dwelling and tower are closed.[^2] The light is reached through Fort Monroe National Monument. The Army closed the post in September 2011, and two months later the fort was designated a national monument and transferred to the National Park Service.[^2][^7] Visitors reach it through the monument's entrance on McNair Drive.[^2] The tower is painted white with a dull red domed roof, and a plaque on the tower marks it as a Virginia National Landmark.[^2]
