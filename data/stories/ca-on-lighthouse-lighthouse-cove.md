The Thames River lighthouse stands at Lighthouse Cove, on the Lake St. Clair shore of Essex County, Ontario, where the Thames River runs into the lake.[^1][^4] Its stone tower, about 17 m (55 ft) high, was built to mark the river mouth, and members of one family, the Cartiers, kept the light from 1838 until 1950.[^1]

## The place

The mouth of the Thames needed a light. In November 1836 Alexander Chewett and seventy-three other inhabitants of the Western District petitioned the Provincial Parliament of Upper Canada for one, and the following March the Assembly granted £1,000 for a lighthouse to mark the river.[^1] A light had stood there before. A heritage listing by the Municipality of Lakeshore says the lighthouse replaced a wooden one destroyed in 1812, and the historical marker on the site says a frame structure was destroyed by fire in the later years of the War of 1812.[^3][^4]

Claude Cartier, who became keeper in 1838, had served in that war. He was born in the parish of Saint-Michel-d'Yamaska, in what is now Quebec, and learned the trade of tailor at Quebec as a youth. He enlisted in the Canadian Fencibles in 1810, was present at the capture of Ogdensburg, New York, and was left with a severe leg wound that later affected his health. After the war he settled in Upper Canada, and from about 1831 he ran a popular inn, the Chatham Hotel, at Chatham on the Thames.[^2]

## Building the light

The sources do not agree on when the stone tower was built. Lakeshore gives 1815, the marker and the Lower Thames Valley Conservation Authority give 1818, and Rowlett's directory dates the tower to 1818, notes a reconstruction in 1973 to 1975, and gives the station's start as 1810.[^3][^4][^9][^8] LighthouseFriends puts the stone tower in 1837. The Dictionary of Canadian Biography says the lighthouse was still under construction in 1837, when Cartier petitioned for the post of keeper.[^1][^2]

A square wooden front-range tower was put up on a pier in 1845, to stand with the stone tower.[^1] By 1878 an inspector described the main light as a white stone circular tower 9 m (30 ft) high from base to vane, with an iron lantern holding five base-burner lamps. Brick was later added, raising the tower to about 17 m (55 ft). In 1897 a seventh-order lens replaced the lamps and reflectors, and a fifth-order lens went into the main lantern in 1909.[^1]

The Department of Marine kept up the works. Repairs to the pier and lighthouse at Thames River were completed on 12 July 1905.[^5] A later report, printed in 1909, says surveys were made of the lighthouse reserves at Presque Isle and on the Thames river, to regulate the campers on these government properties.[^7]

## The keepers

Claude Cartier became keeper in 1838 and moved into the lighthouse in 1840.[^2] He held the post until his death at Chatham in July 1855.[^2] His son Thomas took charge of the light after him and kept it until 1880.[^1]

After Thomas died, his widow Emma looked after the lights for four years, and then her son, Henry Joseph Cartier, was appointed official keeper.[^1] LighthouseFriends lists Emma as head keeper from 1880 to 1884 and Henry Joseph from 1884 to 1928.[^1] The Department's keeper list for the Thames River station in its 1906 report also carries the name H. J. Cartier.[^6] In April 1903 fire destroyed the keeper's dwelling. Henry Cartier was awarded $250 for the provisions and money he lost, and a temporary house was put up for the family.[^1]

Henry's brother William C. Cartier kept the light from 1928 until his death in 1950, and the Cartier family's keeping ended then.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends lists two later head keepers, C. W. Riberdy from 1950 and Armand Jacobs until 1966.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In April 1868, during a violent storm on Lake St. Clair, Thomas Cartier and a younger brother risked their lives to reach the scow *China*. The scow had become unmanageable and filled with water, and her deck load had been swept overboard. The two brothers took her captain, Charles Parker, and four crew into their small boat.[^1] A Chatham newspaper of February 1870, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, credited Thomas Cartier with saving eleven lives in the previous twelve years.[^1] In 1872 the government gave him an inscribed gold watch for saving thirteen lives, and in 1876 it paid him $50 for aiding vessels caught on Lake St. Clair.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1966 the lighthouse was replaced by an automatic light.[^4] Rowlett's directory records the light as inactive from 1966 to 1974.[^8] LighthouseFriends says the navigational lights in the range lights resumed operation in 1974.[^1]

The Lower Thames Valley Conservation Authority obtained the lighthouse by a 1972 grant and restored it in 1973, taking the tower down stone by stone and rebuilding it on a restored foundation.[^4][^1][^8] Lakeshore lists it among its designated heritage properties and calls it one of the three oldest lighthouses still standing.[^3]

## Today

The tower stands in a one-hectare Lighthouse Conservation Area owned by the Lower Thames Valley Conservation Authority.[^9][^1] The area is open for day use all year, from 8 a.m. to sunset.[^9] The grounds are open and the tower is closed to visitors.[^1] Rowlett's directory says the site is leased to the Coast Guard for a search and rescue station.[^8] In 2021 a cylindrical mast near the boat landing at the river entrance showed a flashing red light, and the old lighthouse was no longer active.[^1]
