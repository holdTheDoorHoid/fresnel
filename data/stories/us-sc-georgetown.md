Georgetown Light stands on North Island, at the mouth of Winyah Bay in Georgetown County, South Carolina, about 12 miles (19 km) southeast of the town of Georgetown.[^1] The National Register nomination calls it the oldest active lighthouse in the state.[^1] It marks the entrance used by the ships that carried the rice and indigo of the Georgetown district.[^2]

## The place

Ships bound for Georgetown passed between North Island and South Island to enter Winyah Bay, then ran about fourteen miles upstream to the town.[^2] A local-history article says that many ships had run aground trying to make the port, and that merchants feared the missing light sent trade on to Charleston instead; that is the article's own claim, not a figure from a report.[^4]

In 1789 Paul Trapier, a Revolutionary War patriot, gave a tract of land on North Island for a light.[^2] The new federal government did not build on it for years. Congress appropriated $5,000 on 21 February 1795, a sum that went unspent, and appropriated $5,000 again on 19 March 1798, with $2,000 more.[^2]

## Building the light

The sources do not agree on when the first tower was finished. The National Register nomination says work began in 1799 and that the light was completed and lit in 1801.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the 72-foot (22 m) cypress tower was finished in 1800, with a two-story keeper's dwelling and a tank for whale oil.[^2] A South Carolina Maritime Museum article, reprinted from a local newspaper, says Congress accepted a bid of $6,250 in 1798, that Daniel Steven, superintendent of the Charleston lighthouse, was in charge of construction, and that the lamp was lit in December 1800.[^4] The same article says newspapers across the state reported a "full and brilliant light."[^4]

A storm in 1806 ended the life of the wooden tower; the museum article calls it a gale, possibly with hurricane-force winds, in August of that year.[^2][^4] Congress appropriated $20,000 on 10 February 1807 for a replacement built to "secure its future safety," and the money had to be appropriated again in 1810.[^2] The contractors were Thomas Walker and James Evans of Charleston, who reportedly used enslaved labour.[^2] Dates for the brick tower differ too: a marble plaque over the door lists 1811, while the National Register nomination and the museum article give 1812.[^2][^1][^4] Inside, the stairs and the central post are cut from solid stone; the outer walls are brick.[^1]

By 1850 the light came from eleven lamps with fourteen-inch reflectors, and in 1854 a fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced them.[^2] The Confederates used the tower as an observation post to watch Union ships, and a Union landing party took it in May 1862.[^1] The repairs came in 1867. The tower was raised to 87 feet (27 m) with a new lantern, a new fourth-order lens, and a new two-story dwelling measuring twenty-four by twenty-six feet, with a cistern and boathouse.[^2] The nomination says the light was rebuilt in 1812 and again in 1867, "presumably" because of war damage; LighthouseFriends and the museum article tie the 1867 work specifically to Civil War damage.[^1][^2][^4]

## The keepers

The archive of the U.S. Lighthouse Society, drawing on the keeper registers, lists 73 lines of keepers, assistants and Coast Guard crew between 1800 and 1984.[^3] John Berbant was appointed first keeper on 5 March 1800 and died within a few months; John Shackleford then took charge.[^2][^3] Samuel N. Marsh (1829-1853) has the longest single span among the head keepers on that list.[^3]

Daniel J. Knight was keeper in 1886 (the archive lists 1886-1887).[^3] On the night of 31 August 1886 he was upstairs in bed when a noise like thunder came from the east and the house began to shake. A clock in the dwelling stopped at 9:57 p.m., the time of the first of eight shocks; what he felt was the Charleston earthquake, which did only minor damage on North Island.[^2] Thomas Patrick O'Hagan followed him, promoted to keeper here in 1887 from earlier posts at Sullivans Island and Charleston, and later moved on to Mosquito Inlet and Amelia Island, Florida.[^8][^3]

Joseph W. Grisillo, a native of Austria, kept the light from about 1920 (the archive says 1919) until his death.[^2][^3] He had been an assistant keeper at Hunting Island, Cape Romain, Tybee Island and Charleston. LighthouseFriends says his family lived mostly in Charleston so the children could attend school. It cites a Georgetown Times article of 29 December 1933, which it says reported him found dead in a chair in the keeper's dwelling, with the coroner ruling natural causes; the newspaper itself was not read.[^2] A 1925 issue of the Lighthouse Service Bulletin, reporting events of June and July, commended Grisillo and assistant keeper G. C. Ellis for putting out a fire on 27 June in the Delco light and store room of the Navy radio compass station on the lighthouse reservation, using three extinguishers belonging to the lighthouse.[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The fiscal-year 1894 report of the Light-House Board describes a storm of 11 to 13 October 1893 that came ashore near Georgetown. "The principal injuries" were at Georgetown and Oak Island light-stations, with the tide rising "several feet" on the Georgetown tower.[^5] The same report says a new boathouse and 700 running feet of picket fence were built here that year.[^5] In the fiscal year ended June 1899 a war telephone line was built to join the station to a telephone station on South Island, which connected to Georgetown.[^11]

In 1909 a dwelling was built on South Island for a keeper tending lights there. In 1916 the Lighthouse Service decided to consolidate the stations near Georgetown, and it moved that dwelling to North Island.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported that the whole house, with chimneys and piazza and weighing about 115 tons, was moved across Winyah Bay.[^6] The Bulletin describes the last stage, after the tow across the bay, as a roll of 400 feet to the new site on the Georgetown reservation, and says the keeper's family occupied the dwelling during the operation.[^7]

In the fiscal year ended June 1922 the lighthouse tender Palmetto helped the schooner Phoebe Crosby, aground at the entrance to Winyah Bay, and took her captain and seven crew to the light station, where Grisillo sheltered the crew.[^9]

On 5 November 1938 an explosion rocked a boat on Winyah Bay carrying assistant keeper George C. Ellis and Gabriel Jackson, a keeper in charge of river lights near Georgetown. Both men jumped overboard. Ellis could not swim and drowned; his body was not recovered. Jackson drifted about two miles before a towboat picked him up after two hours; LighthouseFriends says he was burned over ninety percent of his body and spent just over a month in hospital.[^2] The archive lists Ellis here 1925-1938 and a Gabriel N. Jackson as head keeper 1934-1939.[^3]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the station stayed a family station until a fire in 1968 destroyed the two dwellings, after which Coast Guard crews rotated through. The Maritime Museum article says the fire was in 1958.[^2][^4] The two accounts cannot both be right, and the sources here do not settle it. The light was automated in 1986 and the crew of six reassigned; it was the last lighthouse in the state to lose its keepers.[^2]

The Department of Juvenile Justice leased the seven-acre site afterward for a marine-institute programme, which was dropped after a short time.[^2] A fifth-order Fresnel lens from the light is on display in the South Carolina Maritime Museum in Georgetown.[^2][^4] The sources differ on when it left service: the museum article says the light was magnified through it until 1999, while LighthouseFriends says it was removed from the tower in 1986, shown at the Coast Guard station in Georgetown, moved to Miami in 2001 and returned to Georgetown in 2014.[^4][^2] Both sources give a fourth-order lens for the 1854 and 1867 installations, so the fifth-order lens was presumably a later replacement; neither says so.[^2][^4]

## Today

When the National Register nomination was written in 1973, Thomas A. Yawkey owned the land, leased to the Coast Guard, and the island could be reached only by boat across Winyah Bay.[^1] Under Yawkey's will, South Island and North Island, except the lighthouse acreage, passed to the South Carolina Heritage Trust in 1976; the lighthouse property was added to the preserve in 2001.[^2] The Tom Yawkey Wildlife Center restricts public access to protect the sanctuary and offers tours by appointment only.[^10] LighthouseFriends lists the tower as closed and says the light is best seen by boat.[^2] Visitors should treat the tower as closed and should not land on the island without an arranged visit.
