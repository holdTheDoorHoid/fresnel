Rockwell Light, also called Bray's Point Light, is an octagonal tower at the corner of Lake and Bayshore Streets, on the north side of the mouth of the Fox River where it empties into Lake Winnebago at Oshkosh, Wisconsin.[^1][^2][^3] A private owner had it built to mark his harbor and the river entrance.[^1] A later history says the point had shallow reefs just offshore, and that the owner first tried and failed to win a government light there, so he paid for the tower himself.[^4] For decades it appeared on the Light Lists.[^1]

## Building the light

George A. Rockwell, an Oshkosh architect educated at Cornell, drew the plans in 1909. He died suddenly, at 41, once the final drawing was complete.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory says it is called the Rockwell lighthouse after its architect.[^2]

The sources do not agree on the year. LighthouseFriends and the Lighthouse Directory give 1910 for the completed light; IPL and Penberthy's history give 1909.[^1][^2][^3][^4] They also disagree on the tower. LighthouseFriends describes an octagonal concrete lighthouse. The Lighthouse Directory describes a 42-foot (13 m) octagonal brick tower with an ornate open iron lantern.[^1][^2] The focal height is disputed too. LighthouseFriends puts the red light 26 feet (7.9 m) above low water, while the Lighthouse Directory gives a focal plane of about 49 feet (15 m).[^1][^2]

IPL says Bray built it with his own funds as a private aid to navigation.[^3]

General context, not this light's own history: from 1907 federal law required anyone who set up or maintained a light as an aid to navigation in the navigable waters of the United States to get the Commissioner of Lighthouses' permission first. Private aids were "entitled to the same protection as is afforded the aids maintained by the Lighthouse Service."[^5]

On this light, IPL says that in 1917 it was "the only government-licensed inland light", but none of the sources read gives the license or its date.[^3]

## The keepers

William Bray, born in Oshkosh in 1880 and later a state senator, bought several parcels on the point in 1907 and 1908, when the land was fairly isolated.[^1] He fixed up an old house on the point and added a private harbor for his boats. His yacht Glyndwr won the 1905 championship of the Inland Lake Yachting Association, and he was commodore of the Oshkosh Yacht Club. He entertained President William Taft at the point in 1911.[^1]

Penberthy says that after building it, Bray took on the keeper's role and lit the lamp each night himself.[^4] After he sold his lighthouse and home on the point, he moved to Oregon, where in 1921 he bought a lumber company. He died in southern Oregon in 1946.[^1]

IPL says that in 1917, when it was called Thompson's Point Lighthouse, Mrs. Thompson was appointed as its keeper.[^3] IPL gives no first name for her and does not say which Thompson she was. LighthouseFriends does not mention a keeper in those years. It says the point was sold in 1917 to Harry C. Stutz, passed to Frank H. Wheeler, and was bought by John C. Thompson, an Oshkosh attorney, in 1918.[^1] The two accounts disagree about who held the point in 1917.

The Light Lists name the operators after that. LighthouseFriends says the Cook & Brown Lime Company ran the light in the 1920s and early 1930s; John C. Thompson is listed as operator in 1934; A.J. Coffey, superintendent of Cook & Brown, is listed as responsible for the light from 1942 to 1947; and John Buckstaff, who bought the point in the mid-1940s, is listed as operator from 1948 until at least 1965.[^1]

## After the keepers

Ownership changed several times after 1917. Harry C. Stutz, maker of the Stutz Bearcat sports car, bought the point that year.[^1] Thompson, an Oshkosh attorney, bought it in 1918, reworked the house on the point and restored the light.[^1]

Accounts differ on when the light went dark. IPL says it was no longer lit during the late 1950s.[^3] The Lighthouse Directory says it was deactivated in the 1950s and fell into disrepair.[^2] Penberthy gives the same decade.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the operator was listed until at least 1965, and that the light no longer appears on the Coast Guard's Light List.[^1]

The Lighthouse Directory says new owners restored the light and reactivated it in 1986.[^2] IPL also dates the restoration and relighting to 1986, and Penberthy credits the owners of the property with the work.[^3][^4]

A summary on us-lighthouses.com says that once shipping on Lake Winnebago grew, the federal government took over the light's operating costs.[^4] It gives no date, and the other sources read here do not mention it.

## Today

Rockwell Light is privately owned. LighthouseFriends says the grounds and tower are closed, and that the light can be seen from the street at the corner of Lake Street and Bay Shore Drive.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory says the same about the grounds and tower, and adds that the lighthouse can be photographed from the street nearby.[^2] Penberthy asks visitors to respect the private property and not trespass.[^4]

The Lighthouse Directory calls the light active and privately maintained.[^2] IPL's copy, archived in 2020, says it continues to show a fixed red light today.[^3] The sources do not agree on whether it is lit now.
