Sandy Hook Light is a whitewashed, eight-sided tower on the sandy peninsula at the mouth of New York harbour, in Monmouth County, New Jersey. It was first lit on 11 June 1764 and is the oldest lighthouse still standing in the United States, according to the National Historic Landmark nomination.[^4][^1]

## The place

Sandy Hook is a low spit of sand that reaches north from the Navesink Highlands. Thomas Pownall, an Englishman who sailed in during 1755, described the neck of "low sandy hills covered with cedars and holly" and wrote of the Highlands, "This was the first land of America that I saw."[^1] Ships bound for the harbour had to follow a narrow, curving channel around the tip of the Hook, and in 1761 a number of vessels were wrecked on the unseen sandbars and shoals around it. The losses led forty-three New York merchants to petition the colonial assembly of New York for a lighthouse.[^1]

## Building the light

The New York assembly passed an act to raise up to three thousand pounds by lottery "for building a Light House".[^1] A committee bought four acres of the Hook from its owners, the Hartshornes, on 16 May 1762.[^1] Lighthouse Friends credits the mason Isaac Conro of New York with the work and adds that a second lottery was held in 1763; the National Historic Landmark nomination spells the builder's name "Contro".[^5][^4] The tower was lit on the evening of Monday 11 June 1764. The *New York Mercury* described it a week later as octagonal, with a base 29 feet (8.8 m) across and a top 15 feet (4.6 m) across, nine storeys and 103 feet (31 m) from bottom to top, with an iron lantern roofed in copper and "48 Oil Blazes".[^1] The 1966 nomination gives the height as 105 feet and calls the tower brick, whereas Lighthouse Friends describes rubblestone and the archive rubblestone with a brick interior.[^4][^5][^6] The colony paid for the operation of the light with a tonnage tax of three pence a ton on ships entering the harbour.[^1]

In the Revolution the tower became a military prize. A patriot party carried off eight copper lamps and four casks of whale oil in March 1776, and in June Lieutenant-Colonel Benjamin Tupper tried to knock the tower down with artillery; after an hour of fire he reported that he "found the walls so firm I could make no Impression".[^1] Loyalists then fortified the building, and the British held it, as the "Lighthouse Fort" or "Refugees Tower", for most of the war.[^1] Ambrose Serle, secretary to Admiral Lord Howe, called it "a stinking Edifice" because of the whale oil and the soldiers stationed in it.[^2] The tower came under the federal government in 1789, under the act that took over the nation's lighthouses.[^5][^2]

The lighting changed over the years. By 1852 the Light-House Board recorded eighteen 21-inch reflectors with Argand lamps, fitted in 1842.[^5] A Fresnel lens shipped to America in 1840 for Sandy Hook proved slightly too large for the lantern and went to the Navesink Highlands instead.[^1] The present third-order Fresnel lens went in during the 1850s: the National Park Service gives 1857, when an iron lantern was added, and Lighthouse Friends gives 1856.[^1][^5] The Park Service dates the interior brick lining and iron spiral stair to the renovation the Light-House Board reported in 1863; Lighthouse Friends puts them in 1857.[^1][^5] A new double dwelling for the keepers was built in 1883, the last of five keepers' quarters on the site.[^2][^5]

## The keepers

The first keeper whose name the archive records is John Seward, from 1784; Matthew Ely, who followed in 1792, was appointed by President George Washington.[^6][^2] At first the keeper was responsible only for the tower, and after 1817, when two more beacons went up on the Hook, he had three lights and 32 lamps and reflectors to tend.[^1] In its report of January 1852 the Light-House Board remarked that the keeper, David Patterson, paid a helper out of a small raise, and that Sandy Hook had one keeper where the Navesink twin lights had five.[^5] Assistants were assigned from 1854 according to Lighthouse Friends and the archive's roster; the Park Service's tower page says 1857.[^5][^6][^1]

Charles W. Patterson took over from Uriah Smalley on 21 March 1861 and stayed twenty-four years.[^2] He had been turned down by the army for medical reasons and then applied for a lighthouse appointment.[^3] His sister Sarah Patterson Johnson joined as an assistant in May 1867.[^2] The archive lists her service as 1867 to 1882, while the Park Service says she resigned with her brother in 1885 and then taught the soldiers' children at the Sandy Hook Proving Ground until the Army cleared civilians from the Hook in 1898.[^6][^2] She wrote to her father that "the sand and cedars never change".[^2] Charles and his wife raised three sons and a daughter while living on the Hook, and his son Trevonian was later keeper of the Sandy Hook life-saving station.[^2][^3] A New York Times reporter who visited in 1870 called Sandy Hook the "meanest" place a man could be stationed, with more mosquitoes and flies than anywhere of its size on the continent.[^2]

Samuel P. Jewell became an assistant in 1869 and succeeded Patterson in 1885; he stayed until 1909, and the Park Service calls him the longest-serving lighthouse keeper.[^6][^2] In 1885 Patterson was paid $600 a year and Jewell $450.[^3] A mortar burst at the Army's proving ground in July 1903 and rained iron over the Hook, and wrecked Jewell's bicycle.[^2] The tower was wired to the electric-buoy plant on the Hook and, according to the Park Service, lit by incandescent electric lamps on 9 May 1896 (the keepers page says the tower was first electrified in 1894[^2]); when the plant closed in September 1903 the light went back to oil.[^3] An oil-vapour lamp was installed in November 1906 and at first needed Jewell up the tower's 108 steps again and again in a night.[^5][^2] In April 1909 the *New York Sun* reported that he had gone on sick leave and that his doctor said he would never return to duty; he died in 1913.[^3][^2] His last assistant, Howard Poe, succeeded him and held the post until 1930.[^2][^6] The Park Service says electricity returned to the tower, for good, in 1925, and that this made a keeper living on the spot less necessary.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that on 23 August 1933 a storm blew down the wires that carried current to the main light and to Sandy Hook Point Light at 6.30 p.m.; the keepers put the auxiliary oil lights in service an hour and ten minutes later and they burned all night.[^7] A November gale reported in the Bulletin in 1935 cut the shore at the station back by 18 feet (5.5 m).[^8] The nomination says the light stayed lit throughout its history except during the blackouts of the Second World War.[^4]

## After the keepers

By 1941 the lighthouse was in Coast Guard hands, and the archive's roster of keepers ends with Gustav Axelson in 1947 to 1949.[^2][^6] The tower was declared a National Historic Landmark on 11 June 1964, the 200th anniversary of its first lighting.[^1] The sand has kept building northward: in 1764 the tower stood 500 feet (150 m) from the tip, and it now stands about a mile and a half (2.4 km) from the northern end.[^1] Lighthouse Friends reports that it closed to visitors in 2017 and reopened in June 2018 after repainting and new roofing.[^5]

## Today

The tower stands inside Gateway National Recreation Area. The Coast Guard maintains the light, an automated third-order Fresnel lens showing a fixed white light visible for nineteen miles on clear nights.[^1] The 1883 keepers' quarters next door serves as the Sandy Hook Visitor Center, and the Park Service lists tours of the lighthouse, first come, first served, from 1 p.m. to 3:30 p.m. Friday through Monday.[^9] Check the Park Service for current tour dates before you go.[^9]
