Grosse Point Light is a tall brick tower on a low bluff beside Lake Michigan in Evanston, Illinois, some 12 miles (19 km) north of Chicago.[^4] It was lit on 1 March 1874 as the landfall light for Chicago's harbor, and it still carries its original second-order Fresnel lens, now as a private aid to navigation.[^2][^1]

## The place

The point gave the light its name long before there was a tower. The National Register nomination notes that Jacques Marquette's diary mentions camping at "Grosse Pointe" on 3 December during his 1674 expedition toward what would become Chicago.[^1] By the 1880s the water off the point was among the busiest in the country. The nomination reports that Chicago saw 20,000 arrivals and departures of major vessels in 1888, in a shipping season of only eight months.[^1] LighthouseFriends, citing the *Evanston Index*, says that 8,787 sailing vessels and 3,566 steamers passed Grosse Point during the navigation season of 1885, and that a vessel coming south from the Straits of Mackinac would steer for the point and then follow the shore into Chicago.[^2]

The same water was dangerous. The nomination counts no fewer than 28 shipwrecks between 1851 and 1873, a figure it takes from a book on North Shore wrecks, and the Light-House Board's 1894 description calls the light "of great use to vessels going to and from Chicago."[^1][^4] The best-known loss came before the light existed. The nomination says the passenger steamer *Lady Elgin*, returning to Milwaukee from a political rally in Chicago, collided with the lumber schooner *Augusta* in September 1860, and that nearly 300 people died.[^1] The station's own brief history, by Donald Terras, says estimates today come close to 400.[^10] According to the nomination, the federal government was petitioned for a lighthouse soon afterward, but the Civil War interrupted the project.[^1]

## Building the light

The first idea was to reuse an old tower. LighthouseFriends reports that in 1870 the Light-House Board recommended moving the cast-iron lighthouse on Chicago's North Pier to Grosse Point, where "it would there serve what it was intended for, to mark the approach to Chicago." The plan was rejected, and Grosse Point got a new tower instead.[^2]

Congress appropriated $35,000 in 1871, and a site 100 by 550 feet (30 by 168 m) was bought for $1,200.[^2] The Board's 1894 description of the station says it cost $55,000 to establish, and does not explain the difference.[^4] The designer was Orlando Metcalfe Poe, a Civil War general who had become engineer of the Upper Lakes Lighthouse District in 1870.[^1] Poe drew up the plans in 1872. Bids were opened on 13 August of that year, and excavation began in September.[^2] By November the stonework of the keepers' house had risen to ground level.[^2]

The tower rests on piles and concrete. The nomination gives a foundation of concrete and wooden piles to a depth of 30 feet (9 m), while LighthouseFriends describes ninety-one piles driven fifteen feet (4.6 m) down and capped with a four-foot concrete footing; the two accounts are not easy to reconcile.[^1][^2] The tower has two walls with an air space between them, the outer one inclining to give the conical shape. The nomination gives 22 feet (6.7 m) "around" at the base, tapering to 13 feet 3 inches (4 m) at the parapet, and LighthouseFriends reads the first figure as a diameter; a 141-step iron spiral stair climbs inside.[^1][^2] The nomination gives its height as 113 feet (34 m) from the ground to the ventilating ball.[^1] The Board's report of 1894, by contrast, calls it a conical brick tower 90 feet (27 m) high, and the Lighthouse Society's archive records 90 feet from the base of the structure to the center of the lantern, so the two figures may be measuring different things.[^4][^3]

The lens is the station's claim to fame. The nomination says it was made by Henri LePaute in Paris in 1850, the first of five second-order lenses to enter service on the Great Lakes.[^1] LighthouseFriends adds a 1924 newspaper account that the lens was one of three bought in France for $10,000 each in 1860 and that, with the Civil War, the other two were meant for Florida; that date does not match the nomination's 1850.[^2] The nomination gives 1 March 1874 as the day it was first shown.[^1] The Board's 1894 description gives the characteristic as a fixed white light varied by a red flash every three minutes.[^4] LighthouseFriends says three panels of red glass were turned around the fixed lens by a clockwork driven by a sixty-pound weight hung inside the tower walls.[^2]

The nomination describes the brick keepers' quarters as a duplex, its two halves mirror images, each with a full basement, seven rooms, an attic and a brick woodshed that doubled as a root cellar.[^1] A covered passageway joined the house to the tower and to a fuel room, which was later used as the keepers' office and workroom.[^1] The head keeper lived in the south half. According to LighthouseFriends the first assistant had four rooms in the other half and the second assistant the remaining three.[^2]

Two small brick fog-signal buildings were added on the edge of the bluff in 1880, and LighthouseFriends says a second assistant keeper was appointed that year to handle the extra work.[^1][^2] They first held steam sirens. The nomination and LighthouseFriends say 10-inch steam whistles replaced the sirens in 1892.[^1][^2] The Board's report for fiscal 1892 says the change came on 9 April 1891, although its report a year earlier still lists steam sirens running 239 hours at the station.[^6][^5] In 1894 the Board described the signal as a blast of five seconds followed by silent intervals of twenty and forty seconds.[^4] In the report for fiscal 1900 the Board records a 50-foot (15 m) flagpole, a new metal fence along Sheridan Drive and a new oil house, and the following year the fog-signal houses were given sound deflectors.[^7][^8] LighthouseFriends says the deflectors were meant to reduce disturbance to the neighbors.[^2]

In 1914 the weathering brick was sheathed in concrete: LighthouseFriends reports scaffolding going up and a cost of $2,679, and George Putnam's 1917 book on the Service describes the tower as repaired with concrete and cement mortar over steel reinforcement.[^2][^9]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the National Archives' keeper registers, lists ten different principal keepers over the life of the station, matching the nomination's count.[^3][^1] Charles B. Woodford was the first assistant in 1873-74, and Charles H. Boynton was head keeper from 1874 to 1880.[^3] In 1894 the three keepers were paid $675, $500 and $425 a year, and the station burned about 781 gallons of mineral oil annually.[^4]

Staff did not always stay long. The archive's lists name some sixteen different second assistants between 1880 and 1890, several of them for a single year or less.[^3] One surviving complaint shows what that cost the head keeper. On 26 July 1888 Anthony Hagan wrote to the district inspector that his second assistant, a Mr. Palmer, had quit work at 10:30 that morning, telling him he did not care for the lighthouse.[^2] Hagan asked for an assistant "that will obey my orders," in LighthouseFriends' transcription.[^2] LighthouseFriends notes that Hagan was removed from office less than two weeks later.[^2]

His replacement stayed for thirty-six years. Edwin J. Moore had been second assistant at Grosse Point from 1883, left in 1885 to take charge of the Calumet Pierhead light, and came back in 1888 as head keeper.[^2][^3] The nomination calls him the most notable of the station's keepers and describes a stern, authoritarian man who took the title "Captain" instead of keeper.[^1] It adds that Grosse Point had a reputation within the Service as one of the best-managed lighthouses on the Great Lakes.[^1] Moore died in 1924. The nomination says he was seventy-three; LighthouseFriends says seventy-three in one paragraph and sixty-nine in another, and gives the date as 1 March 1924.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends quotes a newspaper of 3 March 1924 that he asked his wife, "Is the light all right, mother?" as he collapsed minutes after climbing down from lighting it.[^2]

Others served for many years too. Isaac N. Blanchard was first assistant from 1899 to 1917.[^3] John T. Trucker joined as second assistant in 1916, was first assistant from 1917 to 1932 and then head keeper until 1934; between them Oscar H. Knudsen was head keeper from 1924 to 1932.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The keepers were part of a wider system. The nomination says it was common for them to work alongside the Life-Saving station at Northwestern University, and gives one example. In August 1885 the schooner *Jamaica* sprang a leak in mid-lake and her captain steered for Grosse Point, but stranded her on shoals about five miles (8 km) from the tower, where she was seen from the lighthouse. The Life-Saving crew was summoned and, the nomination says, rescued everyone aboard.[^1]

The reports give some measure of the fog. The steam sirens ran for 239 hours in fiscal 1891, and the sirens and whistles together for 436 hours in fiscal 1892, using about 28 tons of coal.[^5][^6] The whistles ran 343 hours in fiscal 1900 and 277 hours in fiscal 1901.[^12][^13] The total varied a good deal from one year to the next.

## After the keepers

The dates for the end of the staffed station are not settled. The nomination says the beacon was electrified in 1923, and that the government removed the steam whistles from the fog buildings in 1922; Terras's brief history also gives 1923 for electrification.[^1][^10] LighthouseFriends instead says the station was electrified and the fog signal discontinued in 1934.[^2] The nomination says the Lighthouse Service first tested a photoelectric switch to turn a beacon on and off here, citing a 1934 letter and a Milwaukee newspaper of that year; Terras dates the device to 1934.[^1][^10] The nomination says the station lost its keepers and was automated in 1935, and that the passageway was removed by the government then.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the last keeper left in 1935 and that the north wing of the duplex was also taken down.[^2]

Ownership changed hands soon afterwards. Terras says the site passed to the Lighthouse Park District in 1935, and the nomination says the district has run it since the light was deactivated.[^10][^1] LighthouseFriends adds that the City of Evanston acquired the station in 1935 and the tower itself in 1945.[^2] The light was put out in 1941, when lighted buoys were placed seven miles offshore. It was relit in February 1946 as a private aid to navigation.[^1]

Between those dates the tower had one more job. The nomination says that in 1944 two Northwestern University physicists working for the National Defense Research Committee used it to send and receive infrared signals while refining photocells for the Navy.[^1] After the war came the rebuilding. A preservation plan submitted in 1978 was based on the original 1872 blueprints, the passageway was reconstructed in 1984 to the original specifications, and in 1993 the missing north wing was rebuilt.[^1][^2]

## Today

Grosse Point Light stands in a small park at Central Street and Sheridan Road.[^11] It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1976 and designated a National Historic Landmark in 1999.[^2] LighthouseFriends lists the City of Evanston as owner and the Lighthouse Park District as manager.[^2] The light shows two flashes every fifteen seconds.[^2]

The park grounds are open year-round. The interpretive center and tower are open on summer weekends: June through September on Saturdays and Sundays at 2, 3 and 4 p.m., weather permitting, with no more than eight people per viewing and an admission fee. Children under eight are not allowed in the tower, and the visitor page says the facility is closed the week of Independence Day and Labor Day weekend.[^11] The climb is 141 steps.[^1] The south half of the duplex was still a residence for a resident keeper when the nomination was written in the late 1990s, and the museum is in the north half.[^1][^2]
