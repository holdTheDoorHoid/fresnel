Maugher Beach Lighthouse stands at the tip of a long sandy beach on the west side of McNabs Island, at the inner entrance to Halifax Harbour. Its light was first established in 1828, on a British fort, and since 1941 it has come from a concrete tower.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

McNabs Island is the largest island in Halifax Harbour.[^2] Roughly halfway along its western shore, a rocky beach projects into the harbour for several hundred metres.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the beach takes its name from Joshua Mauger, who cured fish there in the 1750s and 1760s. The sources also spell it Maugers, Maughers and Meaghers.[^2][^3]

The light was wanted for the approach. Ships that reached Sambro Island at night had to stand off, because nothing guided them into the inner harbour.[^2] A House of Assembly committee said in 1826 that the harbour should be "as easily made at night as in the day time."[^2] The legislature voted £1,500 for a lighthouse that year, according to the Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society.[^3] A light near the island's northwestern end was put into operation on 31 March 1903, in line with the Maugher Beach and Chebucto Head lights, to form a range leading ships in.[^7]

Before any light, the beach was a place of punishment. Brian Kinsman's 1995 history of the island says that from the 1780s into the 1810s the military kept gibbets at the seaward end, where the bodies of executed men could be seen by all ships entering or leaving the harbour.[^4] The sources disagree about the mutiny that brought bodies there. Lighthouse Friends says six men of HMC Columbine (a boatswain, three seamen and two marines) were tried and executed at Halifax after an attempted mutiny on 1 August 1809, and that their bodies were hung on the beach.[^2] Kinsman says four men were executed on 18 September 1809 for an August mutiny aboard HMS Columbine, and were left on the gibbets.[^4] Kinsman also records a tradition that Peter McNab, whose home was near, once cut the bodies down.[^4]

## Building the light

After the War of 1812 broke out, it was decided to protect the harbour with a fortification at the seaward tip of the beach, and work began there on a Martello tower.[^5] Kinsman dates the start to 1815, while Lighthouse Friends and the Preservation Society say 1814.[^4][^2][^3] Kinsman says the tower was not finished until 1828, thirteen years after work began; the Preservation Society says it took fourteen years.[^4][^3]

In 1826 the province asked for a light. The Lieutenant Governor then persuaded the military to let a lantern room sit on top of the tower instead of a separate lighthouse.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says Colonel Gustavus Nicolls, commander of the Royal Engineers at Halifax, thought that plan more desirable.[^2] The arrangement carried a condition: the light would be removed if the military needed the tower.[^2] Kinsman says a third storey was added in 1828 to house the light.[^5] Lighthouse Friends says the light went into operation in April 1828.[^2] A Parks Canada historian, quoted by the Preservation Society, was sceptical of the arrangement: "Whether the two functions were compatible was never tested, fortunately perhaps."[^3]

In 1851 Abraham Gesner, the Nova Scotian who had invented kerosene, tested it at the light.[^3] Lighthouse Friends gives May 1851 for the permission, and the Preservation Society gives 1851 and credits keeper David George's careful records of the fuel with helping to establish it.[^2][^3] Kinsman dates a month of government control under Gesner to December 1852, and says: "It is said the experiment was so successful that mariners veered off course to witness the amazing spectacle."[^4] The sources give different years for the same test.

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends names the early keepers, J. Bolser (1830 to 1836) and D. McGlinn (1840 to 1846), and then David George, who kept the light from 1846 to 1873. It says he served there longer than any other keeper.[^2] In 1853 a separate keeper's dwelling was built at the station. The sea sometimes broke over it in autumn and winter gales, and the keeper would then stay in the base of the stone tower.[^2]

Edward Horne came over from Peggy's Point in 1873. In 1875 a double row of piles, about 180 m (600 ft) long, was driven along the beach. Horne reported that without them the sea would have breached the narrowest part of the beach and carried away the dwelling.[^2] His pay rose from $400 to $450 in 1876, to cover the extra work of tending the bell, and to $500 in 1879 so that he could hire an assistant.[^2]

James Doody (1896 to 1903) and William Iceton (1903 to 1915) followed.[^2][^3] The 1906 keeper table lists "Iceton, Wm" at the Mauger Beach Light and Fog Alarm, appointed 8 July 1903 at $800 a year. The 1909 table lists "Wm. Iceton, sen" at Mauger's Beach.[^11][^10] Thomas Conrad kept the light from 1915 to 1936.[^2][^3] The roster after that shows keepers coming and going through the 1940s and 1950s, several of them for only a year or two.[^2][^3]

The Preservation Society says the Coast Guard considered the station an isolated post, because of its exposure and difficult winter access, so two keepers were kept there for mutual support until automation. Without water, telephone or electricity until the 1970s, they relied on rainwater from basement cisterns and periodic radio contact.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A hurricane on 12 October 1871 seriously damaged the tower. The old mortar had to be cut out and the stones repointed with cement.[^2]

In 1872 the Norwegian bark *Tordem Skjold* was wrecked near the light. The Preservation Society says she lost control while tacking into the harbour and passed too close to the lighthouse, which took the wind out of her sails, but her crew scrambled ashore. For years the keepers displayed her signboard.[^3]

A winter storm in 1931 tore most of the outer wall off the south side of the old tower, which was then repaired.[^2][^3] In 1940, according to the Preservation Society, the British warship HMS *Revenge* ran down and sank HMCS *Ypres*, one of the small gate vessels that tended the anti-submarine nets from Maugher Beach to York Redoubt. No lives were lost.[^3] The Preservation Society also recalls a McNabs Island resident who, watching waves break over the keeper's roof, once saw the beach outhouse float past.[^3]

## After the keepers

A Neptune fog trumpet was placed in operation in April 1889, and the fog bell was moved to Georges Island.[^2] In 1901 an iron lantern room was set on the tower, and a fifth-order Fresnel lens replaced the old lamps.[^2] In 1903 the light was changed from fixed white to flashing white, and a 25 cm (ten-inch) whistle replaced the reed horn.[^6]

In 1906 the tower was raised about 3 m (10 ft), and a third-order lens was installed in a new lantern room. A fog alarm building was built the same year.[^2][^8] The diaphone foghorn was first mounted on the lighthouse.[^2] By the inspection of 11 January 1907 it had been moved to the fog-alarm building.[^9] Freeman and Griffin of Isaac's Harbour built a double dwelling and boathouse in 1913.[^2][^3] In the year to March 1922 new crib protection work was done at the beach and old work repaired, and the foundation of the tower was protected with ballast covered in concrete.[^12]

The present concrete tower was built in 1941, which the Register of Historic Places links to the wartime upgrades in Halifax Harbour.[^1][^3] The old granite walls took until 1945 to demolish, and substantial ruins still stood in 1948.[^3] Boulders were added to the breakwater in 1957.[^3] The fog alarm building was pulled down in 1975, and the foghorn moved to the tower, where it was silenced in 1993 after 104 years.[^2][^3]

The Coast Guard placed Hector G. Lowe in charge in 1970. Lighthouse Friends says he was told in 1973 that his job would end within six months, but he stayed until full automation in 1983.[^2] The Preservation Society dates his hiring to 1973.[^3] In 1987 the breakwater was rebuilt with large granite boulders, and the old duplex was burned.[^2][^3] In 2003 Hurricane Juan breached the sand barrier between the ocean and the tidal pond. The Preservation Society says the lighthouse has been harder to reach along the beach since.[^3]

## Today

The Preservation Society says the Government of Canada owns the light, and Lighthouse Friends says the Canadian Coast Guard does.[^3][^2] The Coast Guard's list describes a white light that shows equal periods of light and dark every four seconds, 17.4 m above the water, with a range of 10 nautical miles, from a white octagonal tower with a red top.[^13] The Register gives the range as 15 nautical miles.[^1] The Preservation Society gives the present light as flashing yellow since 1992, a different characteristic from the Coast Guard's list.[^3][^13]

The island is reached by boat.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says the tower is closed and the grounds are open, that the usual road to the light has washed out, and that visitors should allow at least two hours on the island. It also notes a decent view of the light from York Redoubt National Historic Site in Fergusons Cove.[^2]
