Ashtabula Harbor Light is a steel lighthouse with iron plating, standing on a concrete crib at the end of a detached breakwater in Lake Erie, about half a mile off the Ohio shore.[^1][^17] It is the third lighthouse built for the harbor, and the one still in use.[^2]

## The place

Ashtabula, in Ashtabula County, is a Lake Erie port at the mouth of the Ashtabula River. The first lighthouse there was a hexagonal tower of 1836, set on a wooden crib about 40 feet (12 m) square and joined to the east pier of the river by a ramp.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives Samuel Miniger as the first keeper on record, burning sperm whale oil in eight lamps; the preservation society's history names a Captain Bigelow as the first keeper, with seven lamps.[^2][^3] The sources do not settle who tended the light first.

A second, square pyramidal tower of 30 feet (9 m) went into service on the west pierhead when navigation opened in 1876, because new dock facilities were being built. It was moved 471 feet (144 m) toward the end of a lengthened pier in 1882.[^2] In 1891 Congress appropriated $4,700 for range lights and a steam fog signal at the harbor.[^4][^2] The range lights on the pier are covered under their own entries; this story follows the lighthouse that replaced the 1876 tower.

## Building the light

The third lighthouse was begun in 1904 at the end of a new breakwater, after the river was widened and a breakwall built. Lighthouse Friends describes a square, two-story iron structure with a short cylindrical tower rising from the middle of a hipped roof; a fixed fourth-order red light and a compressed-air siren were established on 3 June 1905, and the steam whistle on the old west pier was discontinued.[^2] The Society's history adds that the river-widening left the 1876 tower sixty feet from the pier, so that it looked like a "floating lighthouse."[^3]

Larger works followed. The River and Harbor Act of 1910 authorised a longer west breakwater and a new east breakwater to enclose an enlarged outer harbor.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses dates the act that appropriated $45,000 for rearranging and rebuilding the harbor's aids to navigation to 22 October 1913, and says acetylene flashing lights on the east and west pierheads were established on 17 July 1914.[^5] Lighthouse Friends gives 14 October 1913 for the same appropriation.[^2]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported that the steel lighthouse was moved about 1,800 feet (550 m) from its old place on the breakwater to a new pierhead of 40 by 60 feet (12 by 18 m). The foundation, a timber crib filled with reinforced concrete and stone, was the work of the Army's Engineer Department, and the Lighthouse Service raised a reinforced-concrete base on it to a height of 16 feet. A one-story addition of 20 by 24 feet (6 by 7 m) gave room for the fog-signal plant, and the interior was fitted with brick lining and plaster, a tile floor in the power room and hardwood floors in the quarters.[^6] Lighthouse Friends puts the distance at about 1,750 feet and says the light was first shown at the new site on 31 August 1915; the Commissioner's report instead gives 21 September 1916 as the day the principal light went into commission, and the Society's history also dates the move to 1916.[^2][^6][^3] The sources do not agree on the year.

The new light was a fourth-order revolving lens floating on a bed of mercury, showing a white flash every five seconds at 14,000 candles from a focal plane 51 feet 5 inches (15.7 m) above the lake, visible 15 miles by the Commissioner's count.[^6] The fog signal was a compressed-air diaphone, one two-second blast every 20 seconds, which went into commission on 26 March 1917.[^6][^7] The Commissioner gave the cost to 30 June 1918 as $42,429.72 against the $45,000 appropriated.[^8][^5] Lighthouse Friends says the light was electrified and a radiobeacon added in 1929.[^2]

## The keepers

The government bought two lots at the harbor on 2 August 1871 and began a wooden dwelling the next spring; an addition in 1898 turned it into a duplex for the head and assistant keepers' families, according to Lighthouse Friends, while the US Lighthouse Society's archive dates the keeper's quarters to 1896.[^2][^1] The Society's history says civilian keepers living in the Walnut Boulevard house worked the light until 1915, alternating duties at the lighthouse and making relief trips by boat.[^3] After the move, the Commissioner reported that three keepers had quarters in the main building while on duty, with a dwelling ashore holding two families.[^6]

The register-based roster in the US Lighthouse Society's archive names head keepers beginning with Joseph F. Crawford (1905-1909), followed by Thomas H. Holtan, Hector V. de Grasse, Ralph H.B. Scobie (1915-1921), Frank L. Sellman (1921-1929), Francis E. Comerford (1929-1932), John E. Brophy and Eugene A. Ray (1937-1950).[^1] The preservation society's own partial list names Fayette E. Walworth, appointed 6 February 1894 and resigned for his health on 1 November 1905, and Charles W. Anderson as his successor until 1915, which does not match the register-based roster for those years.[^3]

The roster shows keepers working up through the ranks of a station that had a head keeper and up to three assistants. Francis Comerford was second assistant from 1919, first assistant from 1926 and head keeper from 1929; Eugene Ray went from third assistant in 1927 to head keeper in 1937, and Thomas Baker from third assistant in 1929 to first assistant in 1937.[^1] Clara J. Crawford appears on the roster as first assistant in 1907.[^1]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists Ashtabula, Ohio, with R. H. B. Scobie as keeper, as the station that won the tenth district's lighthouse efficiency flag for the calendar year 1920.[^9] The Bulletin reported that on 14 June 1926 Comerford and Edward B. Eger, assistant keepers at Ashtabula, rescued a man who was attempting to swim across the gap near the station, and listed them among those officially commended.[^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Bulletin printed a letter from the master of the steamer Harry Yates about making Ashtabula on 18 December 1927. Coming from Long Point, the steamer met a 40-mile gale from the west with very heavy snow, and off the harbor the master could not pick up the fog whistle. He knew the car ferry Ashtabula lay inside with a wireless set, but its operator could not be raised, so he asked Cleveland to have the man found; he was reached at home, and calls from him every two minutes gave the steamer a good radio-compass bearing. The master went in on that bearing and picked up the lighthouse, and he wrote that the fog whistle was sounding but could not be heard over the storm.[^11]

Ashtabula's lighthouse was also struck by a ship in 1927: the Canadian Steamship Lines steamer Gleneagles ran into it and drove it back six inches on its foundation, with no one hurt, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^2]

The winter of 1928 is the best documented. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that mild weather had left open water on the station's three exposed sides when a gale from the west set in on 20 January 1928 with the temperature falling steadily; sea and spray froze onto the crib and buildings until the whole structure was encased in heavy ice.[^12] The two keepers on duty were storm- and ice-bound. The storm ended on the 22nd, and the other two keepers, who were ashore, set out to relieve them; they had to go more than two miles to find a safe crossing over the ice. On arrival they found the station a solid mass of ice, between two and five feet thick on its two most exposed sides. The men inside had thawed open the east door and dug a trench of nearly 40 feet with pick and crowbar to reach the side of the crib, so that a ladder could be put up to the breakwater. The Bulletin lists plugged exhaust pipes and an ice-encased lantern among the difficulties, and says a considerable part of the riprap protection was carried away.[^12] The Bulletin reported that a storm on 1 April 1929 swept Lakes Erie and Ontario, and that the most extensive damage to lighthouse property was to the riprap at the Ashtabula West Breakwater, which it said had been substantially repaired about a year before.[^13]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the Coast Guard manned the lighthouse until 1973, when it was the last manned light on Lake Erie; the same year the keepers' dwelling ashore was deeded to the City of Ashtabula.[^2] The Society's history agrees on 1973 and adds that although the light itself was electric, the foghorn still needed a keeper's attention.[^3] A modern optic was installed in 1995, and the fourth-order Fresnel lens was placed on exhibit at the Ashtabula Maritime Museum, which had been dedicated in 1984.[^2] The Society says the lens on the light from 1959 was made in France in 1896, whereas the Commissioner's 1918 report describes the lens installed in 1916 as a fourth-order, six-panel lens; the sources do not say whether these are the same lens.[^3][^6]

The Ashtabula Lighthouse Restoration and Preservation Society was formed in 2003. It applied for the lighthouse under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, and ownership was transferred to it on 6 May 2007.[^2] The lighthouse is on the National Register of Historic Places (reference 83001943).[^14]

## Today

The lighthouse is still an active aid to navigation.[^15] The Society owns it and says its restoration is nearly complete.[^16] The county tourism office lists it as open by appointment, with seasonal tours that depend on weather and on wind and water conditions on Lake Erie.[^17] It stands about half a mile offshore, and the local harbor association says it can be seen close up only by boat.[^15][^17] Lighthouse Friends warns that the breakwater is very uneven and potentially dangerous to walk, so a visitor should not try to reach the light on foot.[^2] The keepers' house on Walnut Boulevard is now the Ashtabula Maritime Museum, and Lighthouse Friends says a distant view of the lighthouse can be had from it.[^3][^2]
