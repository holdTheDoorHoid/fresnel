Hams Bluff Light stands on a hilltop about 360 feet (110 m) above the sea, at the northwest tip of St. Croix, in the Saint Croix District of the U.S. Virgin Islands.[^1] The Danish Lighthouse Service built its steel tower in 1912 and 1913. A newer light now shines from a tower beside it, and the historic tower serves as a daytime landmark.[^9][^10][^4]

## The place

The bluff is an elevated headland at the northern end of Maroon Ridge, about four miles north of the town of Frederiksted.[^1] St. Croix was then part of the Danish West Indies.[^1] The Danish government studied what the Panama Canal's opening might mean for the islands. It appeared likely that the canal would bring more ships through the passage between St. Thomas and St. Croix, and the government decided to build two seacoast lights to mark that passage.[^8] The nomination says traffic through that channel rose somewhat but not significantly, and that most maritime traffic passes through straits in the Greater Antilles.[^8][^9]

In 1912 the Danish Lighthouse Service bought 22.5 acres at the northwestern end of St. Croix from J. W. Blackwood, for 2,812.50 francs.[^9] The official change of sovereignty came in March 1917, and the lighthouses passed to the United States with the other Danish government property.[^7][^11]

The sources disagree on who ran the lights after the transfer. The nomination says the U.S. Navy operated the Virgin Islands lighthouses from 1916 until after the First World War, and that the Bureau of Lighthouses took over their ownership in 1919 and assigned Lighthouse Service personnel to operate them.[^7][^9] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1918 says the military governor formally handed the care and maintenance of the aids to the Lighthouse Service on 16 August 1917.[^12]

## Building the light

Construction began in late 1912 and was complete by mid-1913. The light station was officially established on 15 July 1913. Its first keeper was A. L. F. Madsen.[^9] The Lighthouse Society's archive gives 1915 as the year the station was established, but the nomination's 1913 date is used here.[^9][^15] The auxiliary buildings stood on the lower ground to the west: two concrete dwellings for the keeper and assistant keeper, a storehouse and an oil house.[^9][^2] Another passage places the oil house on the bluff top, 75 feet west of the lighthouse.[^4]

The tower is a two-story conical steel tower topped by a lantern, about 30 feet (9 m) tall, standing on a concrete platform.[^1] The optic was a classical fourth-order Fresnel lens made by Barbier, Bénard & Turenne of Paris. An incandescent oil-vapor lamp by AB Lux of Stockholm lit it, rated at 39,000 candlepower.[^3] The light's original pattern was two half-second flashes, a 4.5-second gap and then 14.5 seconds of dark. The lens turned once every 20 seconds on a circular carriage.[^3]

The turning was driven by clockwork. A 150-pound weight hung in a 17-foot drop tube, and the nomination says the mechanism ran for 2.5 hours before the weight had to be wound back to the top.[^3] The original optic could be seen from 25 nautical miles in clear weather.[^9] The lighthouse was first painted all white. By the middle 1930s the lantern was black and the tower had a black band at its base.[^3]

## The keepers

The nomination names A. L. F. Madsen as the first keeper, when the station opened in 1913.[^9] The station was staffed by resident keepers from 1913 until August 1974.[^2][^10] The keeper on duty worked from the watch room on the tower's second story. It was furnished with a table, a chair, a clock and a telephone for communication with the keepers' dwellings.[^4]

The USLHS archive's roster for the station lists principal keepers, assistants and other staff from 1919 on. It names Peder M. Pedersen from at least 1921, Felix R. Giraldet from at least 1930, and Juan Ramos Ferran from 1935 to 1942. Cayetano Valle served as first assistant from 1920 to 1922. Henry Pedro is listed as lamplighter from at least 1921, and James Hotchison as laborer from at least 1919.[^15]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The keepers displayed storm signals from a flagpole at the station buildings when needed.[^2] The nomination describes the site as elevated terrain exposed to powerful storms, and says the Danish steel design was well suited to that exposure.[^6] It also records deterioration from storm damage and corrosion.[^5]

A National Register review sheet says the lighthouse's deterioration was made worse by Hurricane Irma.[^13] The Virgin Islands Legislature's letter, attached to the nomination, credits the lighthouse's survival of "the catastrophic storms of Irma and Maria in 2017" with adding to its importance.[^14] The sources record no wreck or rescue tied to the light.

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over the station in 1939, when the Bureau of Lighthouses was abolished.[^10] The optic was electrified in 1941 by a 2,500-foot (762 m) commercial power cable from the keepers' dwellings. That cable was used until it failed in 1978 and was replaced.[^3]

In 1949 the original Fresnel lens was replaced by an 18-inch Crouse-Hinds lantern, which the nomination says could be seen for 24 miles in clear weather.[^3] Resident keepers ended in August 1974, when the beacon was automated.[^10] A National Register review sheet gives 1970 as the end of manned service instead.[^13] In 1981 the Coast Guard transferred 22.25 acres of the station to the Navy and kept the 0.25 acre around the lighthouse.[^2][^10] The Navy later passed its part to the Army. The buildings on the lower ground were demolished, and a modern building there is now used by the Virgin Islands National Guard.[^2][^10]

In 1991 the beacon was changed to batteries recharged by solar panels, and in 1998 a Vega marine beacon replaced the rotating lens.[^3][^10] In 2011 a steel skeleton tower was built beside the lighthouse. An LED beacon on top of it now carries the light, and the optic inside the old lantern was removed.[^10][^1] The current light shows two white flashes every 30 seconds. Its focal plane is 394 feet (120 m) above the sea, and it is visible for 14 miles in clear weather. The nomination does not say whether those are statute or nautical miles.[^10]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the lighthouse and a 0.25-acre circle centered on it.[^1] The National Register listed Hams Bluff Light on 16 September 2019.[^17]

The nomination says the lighthouse is accessible on foot for public visitation.[^5] A hiking guide describes a route along the outside of the National Guard facility's fence to "a gate in the fence (usually closed)," and then up the trail.[^16] Do not pass a closed gate or cross the facility's fence. The sources do not say whether visitors may go inside the tower, so do not climb it. The Legislature's letter says hikers already visit the site, and it asks for the lighthouse's conservation and restoration.[^14]
