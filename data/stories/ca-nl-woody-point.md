A small white wooden tower with a red top stands on a grassy headland at the south arm of Bonne Bay, beside the old west-coast "capital" of Newfoundland, surrounded by Gros Morne National Park.[^1][^2][^4]

## The place

Woody Point sits on the western shore of the south arm of Bonne Bay. Lighthouse Friends calls it the early "capital" of the West Coast of Newfoundland: a centre of law and government with a government Bond Store where every vessel had to clear customs.[^1] The federal heritage statement traces the region's importance to the Treaty of Utrecht of 1713, which let France fish this coast but not settle it, so that it became known as the French Shore. British settlement began around 1800, when Bird and Company built a trading post for furs and salmon at Woody Point, and the herring, cod and lobster fisheries made the town important enough to justify a light in 1919.[^2]

## Building the light

The first tower went up in 1919, a square, pyramidal wooden tower on land bought from George Parsons for $150, painted white with a red lantern. Its acetylene gas light flashed red twenty times a minute.[^1] The site is called Curzon Head in the Lighthouse Friends and Heritage NL accounts;[^1][^3] the federal heritage statement calls the headland Crawley Head,[^2] and the sources do not say which is right.

Fire marked the light's early years, and the sources differ on how. Lighthouse Friends says a fire that swept the town in 1922 destroyed fifty-eight structures and spared the lighthouse, and that the acetylene light was later damaged by fire and replaced in 1923 by a fixed red kerosene light shown 13.7 m (45 ft) above high water.[^1] Heritage NL also dates the fire damage to the light to 1923, after which kerosene was used,[^3] while the Lighthouse Directory says the first lighthouse was damaged by fire in 1922 and largely rebuilt.[^5] The Parks Canada statement mentions only the town's major fire of three years after 1919 and says the lighthouse survived.[^2] The sources do not settle the year or how much was lost.

A second wooden tower with an integrated lantern room stood by 1952.[^1] The present tower was erected in 1959, when the light became battery-operated.[^1][^3] The Parks Canada statement describes a square, tapered wooden tower 7.3 m high, with a superimposed gallery and a square wooden lantern, on a concrete foundation. The statement calls this "tower with a superimposed gallery" one of seven variations on a design the Department of Marine and Fisheries preferred, and says towers of this kind are sturdy, movable, economical and easy to erect from local materials and expertise.[^2]

## The keepers

Only a thin record survives. Lighthouse Friends lists Richard Sheppard, Rod Noel and Fred Parsons as keepers, the last for 1949 to 1959.[^1] Heritage NL names Fred Parsons as the last official lightkeeper, who retired when the 1959 tower was built; William M. Parsons then served as caretaker of the light until 1966.[^1][^3] The sources do not date Sheppard's or Noel's service.

## After the keepers

Today's light is listed by the Canadian Coast Guard as Woody Point Light, a white pyramid tower with a red top, 7.3 m high, seasonal, showing a long flash of 2 seconds and eclipse of 4 seconds in a 6-second period, 13.7 m above the water with a range of 4 nautical miles.[^4] Another directory describes it as a red flash every 4 seconds and a 6 m tower;[^5] the Coast Guard list is the official figure for the characteristic.

## Today

The tower was designated a heritage lighthouse by the federal government in September 2016,[^5] and the Town of Woody Point has made it a municipal heritage site, valued for its setting and as a landmark visible from town and from the water of Bonne Bay. A stylised image of it appears on the town's logo.[^3] Parks Canada notes it is reached by a short path from town and is much photographed, including by passengers arriving by cruise ship.[^2] The lighthouse is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard. The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1][^5]
