Cap-de-Bon-Désir Lighthouse, the Phare de Bon-Désir, stands on a rocky point on the north shore of the St. Lawrence, about 3 km (2 miles) east of Grandes-Bergeronnes in the La Haute-Côte-Nord region of Quebec.[^3] Its first light, a steel tower lit by acetylene in 1941, gave way in 1958 to the octagonal concrete tower that still shows its light.[^1]

## The place

The point lies in the Saguenay–St. Lawrence Marine Park, where whales come to feed close to the shore.[^5] The park's interpretation centre says visitors can watch belugas, rorquals and seals from the land.[^5] LighthouseFriends describes the cape as a rich feeding ground, where cold water rises to the surface at the meeting of the Saguenay and St. Lawrence rivers.[^3]

The light was built for ships. In 1941 the Pilots Corporation asked the Department of Marine and Fisheries for an aid at the point, because navigators were increasingly choosing the north-shore channel.[^3]

## Building the light

The first tower, built in 1941, was a steel skeleton tower 9.4 m (31 ft) high with an acetylene lamp. The Quebec record says the light worked automatically on acetylene gas.[^1][^3] Traffic in the north-shore channel grew sharply between 1945 and 1955, and LighthouseFriends says a fog horn and a stronger light were judged necessary.[^3]

In 1957 the Forestville contractors Euclide Tremblay and Camille Dufour were engaged to build the present tower. It was finished the next year at a cost of $53,749.[^3] The Quebec record describes an octagonal tower of reinforced concrete, with two Fresnel lenses.[^1][^2] The present tower is 10.7 m (35 ft) high, according to the Quebec record, LighthouseFriends and the Routes des phares listing.[^1][^3][^6] The Coast Guard list gives 14.3 m for the same tower.[^4]

The new light came into service on 10 October 1958. Its optic was a DCB-10 dioptric apparatus: a 500-watt electric bulb set between two ten-inch Fresnel lenses.[^3]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends names Esdras Beaulieu as the caretaker of the 1941 tower, without giving dates.[^3] When the new station opened, the head keeper was Gilbert Fraser, a Second World War veteran who had served at Île Rouge before coming here. He kept the station from 1958 to 1975.[^3] LighthouseFriends names three assistants who worked with him: Léonid Lavoie, Lucien Dionne and Charles-Eugène Bouchard. It gives dates only for Bouchard.[^3]

In 1975 Bouchard was transferred to Île Rouge, and Marcel Ouellet was appointed head keeper. Ouellet remained the station's only keeper until it was de-staffed in 1982.[^3]

The fog signal was a type F diaphone. When needed, it sounded five-second blasts every forty seconds, using air compressed by diesel engines.[^3] LighthouseFriends reports that the fog signal ran for 919 hours in 1964 and for 1,832 hours and 37 minutes in 1967.[^3] No wreck or rescue at this point is named in the station record or in the sources cited here.

## After the keepers

The Quebec record dates the start of automation to 1971 and its completion to 1982.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the first new equipment to 1972: a visibility meter that switched on the fog horn when needed, a generator and a battery bank.[^3] The sources differ on the first year.

In 1971 an electric foghorn, set just seaward of the light, replaced the diaphone. It sounded for four seconds each minute.[^3] The fog signal was deactivated in 1997.[^3]

## Today

The light is still in service.[^1] The Coast Guard list gives its characteristic as a white flash every six seconds, with a focal height of 46.4 m (152 ft) and a range of 18 nautical miles.[^4] LighthouseFriends gives the focal plane as 44.5 m (146 ft).[^3] The two figures differ.

Parks Canada runs an interpretation centre at the site.[^5] LighthouseFriends says it occupies the head keeper's house, with displays on lighthouses and marine life, while the assistant keeper's house holds a small shop.[^3][^5] Parks Canada says entry is free for children and charged for adults, and that hours change with the season.[^5] LighthouseFriends lists the grounds as open and the tower as closed, so visitors should not climb it.[^3]
