Greens Ledge Light is a cast-iron tower standing in open water at the southwest end of the Norwalk Islands, in Long Island Sound off Rowayton, Connecticut. Lit in 1902, it replaced an older stone lighthouse on Sheffield Island, and it still shows a light today.[^10]

## The place

Greens Ledge is a submerged rock ledge on the western approach to Norwalk Harbor.[^1] A local legend, repeated by LighthouseFriends, says it was named for a pirate called Green who sailed with Captain Kidd and was chained to the rock after his execution; the sources give it only as legend.[^2] Before the ledge had a light of its own, the harbor was served by the lighthouse on Sheffield Island, an 1868 tower that had itself replaced a masonry tower of 1826.[^1]

In the 1890s Norwalk Harbor was being improved by federal appropriation for more commercial traffic, and the route past the islands was a hazard to vessels of deeper draft.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for fiscal 1896 proposed a light and fog-signal station on Greens Ledge, estimated at $60,000, saying the improvements were needed because of "the important commercial interests of this port" and the harbor works the government was carrying out.[^3] The nomination for the National Register says Greens Ledge and Peck Ledge were proposed to Congress that year.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $60,000 for a light and fog signal at Greens Ledge in an act approved on March 3, 1899.[^4][^1] The Board's report for that year records only a preliminary survey of the site; the next says plans and specifications for the metalwork and the lighthouse were being printed.[^4][^5] The Philadelphia Construction Company took on the foundation and the tower in 1900.[^1] LighthouseFriends puts its price at $29,780 and says it sublet the cast-iron foundation cylinder to the Bing Foundry & Machine Company of Philadelphia.[^2]

The design was the cast-iron tubular foundation that Lighthouse Board engineers had developed in 1873: curved plates bolted into rings, lowered onto the site and filled with concrete. The nomination calls Greens Ledge a typical example of a prefabricated conical cast-iron tower on such a foundation.[^1] The Board's report for fiscal 1901 says that on January 30, 1901 the fourth section of the foundation cylinder was complete and filled with concrete, and the fifth and part of the sixth had been erected and fitted in place.[^6] The nomination notes photographs from 1901 showing the lower courses assembled on a wharf at Wilsons Point, then the cylinder placed at the ledge, then building on site that fall.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the setting of the cylinder to May 21 and 22, 1901, and the finished work to October 31, 1901, with some 1,500 tons of riprap piled round it.[^2] The January and May dates do not obviously agree, and the report does not say where the sections were standing in January.

Inside, the first floor of the tower was a kitchen. The second story held two rooms, and the nomination suggests that the smaller one, a bathroom, may be a very early example of indoor facilities in a conical cast-iron lighthouse. The lantern is seven feet (2.1 m) across.[^1]

The sources disagree about the first lens. The nomination says a fifth-order lens showing a flashing red light went in during February 1901, and that three months later a fourth-order lens replaced it, showing a fixed white light with a red flash every fifteen seconds.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the temporary fifth-order lens to February 14, 1902, with the fourth-order lens following in mid-May.[^2] The nomination itself dates the station to 1902, and the Digital Archive of the US Lighthouse Society gives 1902 as the year of the original fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^1][^7] LighthouseFriends adds that the Sheffield Island light was discontinued once Greens Ledge was lit, and that the fog signal ran about 400 hours in a typical year.[^2]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the keeper registers, lists William H. De Luce as the first head keeper (1902 to 1904) and Henry M. Bevedret as the first assistant (1902 to 1903).[^7] New England Lighthouses has De Luce staying until 1908.[^8] The archive shows a head keeper and a first assistant from the start, and a second assistant from 1920.[^7]

The best-known story from the station is the abandonment of 1910. The archive lists John T. Kiarskou as head keeper from 1909 and Leroy C. Loughborough as his assistant.[^7] On the first or second of March (LighthouseFriends says the first, New England Lighthouses the second), Kiarskou took the station's only boat ashore, saying he would be back with supplies and to cash his assistant's paycheck.[^2][^8] He did not come back. LighthouseFriends says he went to a hotel and went on a drinking binge; New England Lighthouses says he gave himself up to the Bridgeport police and was discharged.[^2][^8] The two accounts differ on the amount of the paycheck, $49.69 or $44.69.[^2][^8]

Loughborough stayed at the light. According to LighthouseFriends, fog set in for nearly four days, and he had to run the fog signal engines as well as the light and go without sleep. Supplies ran low, and he and his dog went on half rations until the last stew was shared on the ninth day.[^2] New England Lighthouses says instead that he lived on potatoes and dog biscuits, with only boiled salt water to drink.[^8] The lighthouse tender *Pansy* came out, and he was found unconscious on the floor near the fog signal, with the dog beside him.[^2] "I feel ten years older and my hair has grown gray," Loughborough told those who took him ashore.[^8] He was taken to his father's house at Point Judith, Rhode Island, and died in February 1911, aged 27.[^2][^8]

His brother George E. Loughborough was assistant from 1910 to 1912.[^7] LighthouseFriends says that on March 1, 1912 George went ashore for the morning, found a telegram that his aunt was dying, and did not return; newspapers ran stories of a keeper starving in a lighthouse, but the tender *Tulip* found the head keeper, William T. Locke, with food to last to the end of March. George was dismissed after failing to return by March 20.[^2]

On December 30, 1916, according to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin, head keeper William F. Rhodes and assistant keeper Frank L. Thompson helped put out a fire on an oyster boat near the station.[^9] Thompson was in trouble himself the next winter. Both LighthouseFriends and New England Lighthouses tell of an afternoon in 1917 (New England Lighthouses says February) when he rowed back from shore with supplies and was caught in the ice. LighthouseFriends adds that he drifted with the tide toward open sea, and says a tug from the Bridgeport Towing Company, sent out after a $50 fee was agreed, found him about 10:30 that night.[^2][^8]

The archive lists Edward Steiger as head keeper from 1942 to 1944, with Coast Guard names appearing from 1943.[^7] New England Lighthouses prints a family account, sent in by the granddaughter of a Coast Guardsman there from 1943 to 1947, of two men who followed a channel cut through the ice, were stranded, fell through while trying to walk to shore, and were rescued after people on shore heard their shouts.[^8] After the war the archive shows a string of Coast Guard crew in the 1960s, and the last head keeper it gives clear dates for, Howard S. Matsuba, from 1968 to at least 1969.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Greens Ledge Light Preservation Society says the base had been damaged by storms and winter ice over its first three decades, and that granite from the excavation of Radio City Music Hall in New York was used to build up the riprap around it, dating this to 1933 in one place and 1932 in another on its own pages. It also says the hurricane of September 21, 1938 sent waves as high as the fifth-story window.[^10] New England Lighthouses says the tower developed a tilt over the years, especially after that hurricane, and LighthouseFriends reports that keepers complained of generators shaking the furniture across the room to its low side.[^8][^2]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard automated the light and fog signal in 1972, and the Fresnel lens gave way to a modern rotating optic.[^2][^8] In May 2015 the lighthouse was declared excess to the Coast Guard's needs and offered under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act; when no suitable owner came forward it went to auction.[^2] The Society says it sold on September 15, 2016, for $150,000, to the highest of four bidders, a family that gave it to a newly formed nonprofit; New England Lighthouses agrees on the price, while LighthouseFriends gives a high bid of $130,000.[^10][^8][^2] The Society says it took the quitclaim deed from the General Services Administration on May 17, 2017, began structural restoration in April 2018, and substantially finished it in May 2023, at a cost of about $2 million raised privately.[^10]

## Today

Greens Ledge Light was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on May 29, 1990.[^1] It is owned by the Greens Ledge Light Preservation Society and is still an active aid to navigation, with Coast Guard equipment in the lantern.[^10] It is an offshore lighthouse, reached by boat, and it is not open for landing on your own. The Society runs public tours on a limited number of dates from late spring to early autumn, for ages 8 and up, and rents the tower for overnight stays of up to six guests on a handful of nights a year.[^12][^11]
