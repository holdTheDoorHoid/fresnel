Lincoln Rock Light stood on a reef in Clarence Strait, off the west coast of Etolin Island in southeastern Alaska.[^2] The first lighthouse, a wooden house on a concrete pier, went up in 1903. Storms had damaged it by 1910, and a replacement dwelling and fog signal went up on the neighboring Lincoln Island.[^1][^2][^8] The Coast Guard withdrew its lighthouse personnel from the rock in 1968.[^2]

## The place

Clarence Strait is a major thoroughfare for vessels that choose the sheltered inland passage between Ketchikan and Wrangell over the open Pacific. The rock lies in the channel between Etolin Island and Prince of Wales Island.[^2] Navy Lieutenant Commander Albert S. Snow named the rock for Abraham Lincoln in 1886.[^8] President McKinley reserved the site for lighthouse purposes by executive order on 1 July 1901.[^2]

The Douglas Island News of 30 September 1903 said the reef "is on the main steamship route" and was submerged "about ten feet at high tide."[^3]

## Building the light

A contract was signed on 12 March 1902. The contractor lost all his lumber, one small steamer and a barge while taking materials to the site, and stormy weather forced him to stop work in August 1902.[^2]

The Douglas Island News reported in September 1903 that heavy seas had repeatedly carried away the wooden frames set in the reef to hold the wet concrete. It said a contractor who had lost a year to storms "gave up in disgust and publicly announced that Lincoln Rock could not be conquered."[^3] The paper said nearly fifteen hundred yards of concrete had been used by then.[^3] The Lighthouse Board annulled the contract in June 1903 and had the station built by hired labor.[^2]

A Notice to Mariners published in September 1903 described the new structure: a fixed white light of the fourth order, to show on or about 1 December 1903, and a Daboll trumpet fog signal.[^2] The archive gives the focal height as 57.75 feet (17.6 m) above mean high water.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive lists 99 keeper and crew entries at the rock, from 1903 to 1968.[^1] Carl E. Peterson was the first head keeper, from 1903 to 1904.[^1] A Wrangell paper of 22 December 1910 reports that "Keeper Peterson has gone from Sentinel Island to Lincoln Rock," without a first name.[^9]

Norval S. Douglas was head keeper from 1912 to 1920, and Gardner Tibbetts was first assistant until 1915.[^1] In December 1914 the two men gave food and help to people in need, as described below.[^6]

On 9 July 1923 a keeper's log recorded the USS Henderson passing with President Harding aboard. LighthouseFriends quotes the entry: "Did not put flag out as sun was not up yet and President would be asleep."[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A storm on 28 November 1909 swept over the lighthouse.[^2] The Wrangell paper of 9 December 1909 refers to the "big storm of November 28," and reports that an assistant engineer of construction for "the lighthouse service of this district" came as far as the rock and gave "the necessary instructions for temporary repairs."[^4] His plans called for "the entire lower story of the lighthouse" to be built of concrete.[^4] LighthouseFriends says an Army ship, the Paterson, picked up the keepers and took them to Wrangell.[^2]

On 4 April 1910 another storm sent seas into the lower floor, and the crew was trapped on the second floor.[^2] The keepers left the rock in December 1910, after a temporary light had been set up.[^8]

On 2 December 1914 Douglas and Tibbetts gave food and help to two men "whose launch had been wrecked." On 5 December they gave food to a trapper "who was without money or provisions."[^6]

Congress appropriated $25,000 by the act of 4 March 1911 for rebuilding and improving the light and fog signal.[^5] The new station was placed in commission on 10 October 1912, and the 1915 report says the work was entirely completed in the fiscal year to June 1915, with $24,774.49 spent in total.[^5] CoastView gives 10 October 1911 as the completion date instead.[^8] This story follows the official report's 1912 date for commissioning, and the disagreement is not resolved by the sources read.

The new dwelling and fog-signal building went onto Lincoln Island, leaving only the light on the rock.[^2][^8] LighthouseFriends puts the island about 1,650 feet (500 m) east of the old site; CoastView gives about 1,300 feet (400 m).[^2][^8] The Service's reports called the island station the "Lincoln Rock Fog Signal Station."[^5][^7]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over Alaska's aids to navigation in 1939. LighthouseFriends says four Coast Guard personnel were typically assigned to each lighthouse after that.[^2]

In 1947 a large generator took over from the earlier equipment, and a submarine cable was laid from Lincoln Island to the rock.[^2] CoastView says a skeletal light tower was added in 1944.[^8]

By the late 1960s the Coast Guard had decided to automate the light and remove its staff.[^2] The lighthouse personnel left on 20 March 1968. A battery-operated light was left on the rock, and the station was discontinued that year.[^1][^2] The facilities on Lincoln Island were demolished, leaving concrete foundations.[^2]

## Today

The 1903 station is gone. CoastView says that only the building foundations remain, and that a modern light on a steel skeleton tower is active on the rock.[^8]

The tower belongs to the Coast Guard, and the archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says the modern light is best seen by boat.[^2]
