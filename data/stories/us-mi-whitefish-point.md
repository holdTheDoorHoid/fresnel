Whitefish Point is a sandy point at the southeastern end of Lake Superior, the corner that ships turn when they enter or leave the lake. A light has shone from it since 1849, and the cast-iron tower that stands there now dates from the early 1860s.[^1][^2][^3] Since the 1980s the site has also housed a shipwreck museum.[^2][^15]

## The place

The Great Lakes Shipwreck Historical Society describes the point as a turning point for vessel traffic in and out of Lake Superior, and as the eastern end of an 80-mile (129 km) stretch of shore, west to Munising, known as the Shipwreck Coast. By the society's count at least 200 of the lake's 550 known major wrecks lie near the point.[^15] In 1870 the Light-House Board said why the place mattered, in words LighthouseFriends quotes: "Vessels bound either up or down the lake run for this light, and in foggy weather without an efficient fog signal both delay and risk are encountered."[^2] By the 1890s the Board was also describing the danger the other way round: ships bound down the lake all tried to make Whitefish Point, and "a slight variation in their course from the nearest point of departure will run them ashore near Crisps Point," the reason it proposed a station there.[^7]

The Committee of Commerce was told to look into lighthouses on Lake Superior, Whitefish Point among them, on 13 January 1846, and Congress voted $5,000 on 3 March 1847.[^2][^3] A surveyor laid out a reservation of 115.5 acres, which Terry Pepper describes as "cranberry bog and sand dunes."[^3] Horace Greeley, passing through that summer, wrote in the *New York Tribune* that "every month's delay is virtual manslaughter," as Pepper quotes him.[^3]

## Building the light

The contract went to Ebenezer Warner of Sandusky, Ohio, in August 1847. Stone came up in 1848 by schooner from Tahquamenon Island, and the work was finished on 1 November 1848.[^3][^2] The tower was a stone one, but the sources disagree about its size: LighthouseFriends gives just over 42 feet (13 m), Pepper 65 feet (20 m).[^2][^3] Thirteen lamps with 14-inch reflectors made a fixed white light. Because the building season had ended, the decision was made to hold the lighting until the 1849 navigation season.[^3] The station cost $8,298, $3,298 more than Congress had voted, and Warner petitioned and was paid the difference on 3 March 1849.[^2][^3]

A fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps in 1857, according to LighthouseFriends and Pepper; the US Lighthouse Society archive lists the original lens as a third-order Fresnel installed that year.[^2][^3][^1] The sources read do not settle which is right.

The Light-House Board asked for a new tower in 1859.[^2] The replacement was prefabricated cast iron, an open frame of four legs around a central cylinder. Its stairs ran up the cylinder, and a covered passage from the second floor of the keeper's dwelling led in.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends dates the work to a 1860 contract and a finish in spring 1861; Pepper has the contracts awarded in 1861 and the light in service late in 1862.[^2][^3] The old stone tower came down, though the old dwelling stayed standing for years.[^3]

Fog was the next problem. A fog signal had been asked for as early as 1863.[^2] A steam whistle was set up in 1872 by LighthouseFriends' account and 1871 by Pepper's.[^2][^3] A submarine bell was added off the point in 1912.[^2][^9] The Light-House Board's own reports give numbers for the work it did: in the fiscal year ended June 1894 the 10-inch whistle ran about 401 hours and burned about 25 tons of coal, in the year to June 1895 about 469 hours and 29 tons, and in the year to June 1896 about 477 hours and 37½ tons.[^5][^6][^7] Pepper reports that the busiest year was 1907, with 694 hours and 43 tons of coal.[^3]

The light changed in the 1890s. In June 1893 it went from fixed white to fixed white with a red flash every twenty seconds, and the station was given an oil house.[^4] A second assistant keeper was added from 1894 to cope with a flashing light and a steam whistle.[^2][^1] The Board's reports for the fiscal years 1895 and 1896 record the keeper's dwelling being altered and enlarged and the tower, brown until then, being painted white to make it a better daymark; LighthouseFriends dates both to 1895.[^6][^7][^2] On the Board's account the light was changed to a white flash every 5 seconds on 13 April 1896. Pepper says red.[^7][^3] Plans and materials for rebuilding and enlarging the fog-signal house are recorded the same year, and LighthouseFriends dates a new, larger building to 1896.[^7][^2]

## The keepers

The station was hard to hold on to. LighthouseFriends counts eight early keepers who all resigned, only one lasting beyond four years; Pepper counts seven resignations in the decade after the first appointment.[^2][^3] The US Lighthouse Society roster names Jonas A. Stone for 1848 to 1849, then James B. Van Rensselaer, but Pepper says James A. Starr was appointed first, on 10 October 1848, and resigned the following May.[^1][^3] In 1850 the district superintendent, Henry B. Miller, recommended a fence around the property to keep cattle away from the buildings.[^2][^3]

Later head keepers stayed far longer. The roster lists Charles A. Kimball from 1883 to 1903 and Robert Carlson from 1903 to 1931, twenty-eight years.[^1] An Alonzo L. Kimball was first assistant from 1883 to 1895; the sources read do not say whether the two were related.[^1] Second assistants came and went: the roster lists sixteen different men in that post between 1903 and 1913.[^1]

In 1887 the keeper fell out with the fishing firm of Endress & Sons, which was setting nets and driving stakes just off the reservation. When the keeper put out a net of his own, LighthouseFriends says, the firm drove stakes on either side of it. The inspector took advice from a US attorney, who held that the firm had no right to drive stakes within a mile of the shore, and on 13 September 1888 the inspector reported that he had removed them with the tender *Warrington*.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In July 1899 the steamer *Cumberland* went ashore on the point in fog. The Life-Saving Service's table says the Vermilion Point crew, about ten miles away, arrived when the weather cleared and found the lighthouse tender *Marigold* trying to haul her off; the surfmen ran lines all day, and the steamer came off without apparent damage at about 5.40 p.m. The district inspector wrote to praise them, even though the wreck was "a long distance from the life-saving station."[^8]

On 13 September 1914 the launch *Ora Endress* capsized off the point. The *Soo Times* of Sault Ste. Marie, 19 September, as quoted in the *Lighthouse Service Bulletin*, said that Keeper Robert Carlson telephoned a life-saving station about nine miles away, then called for volunteers; of 20 or more men, only Mike Nolan and Fred Kinnear stepped forward. The keeper of Crisp Point light, who was a passenger on the launch, wrote that seven of them got into the launch's towed lifeboat, but two men would not leave the wreck's bow until Carlson reached them, supplied oars and took the two off.[^10] The paper called it a feat "one which one sailor in a thousand could have accomplished." The eleven men were fed and clothed at the light station, and Carlson was commended by the Secretary of Commerce.[^10][^11]

On the night of 28 February 1925 the keeper reported an earthquake shock: the dwelling could be seen rocking east and west, and the window curtains swung 4 to 6 inches. He found no damage to the lens, lantern or tower.[^12]

In April 1933 two fishermen were carried off on an ice floe in Whitefish Bay. Keeper Harry F. House's report says the light was lit on the nights of 10 and 11 April for the Coast Guard, fishermen and aircraft searching for them. LighthouseFriends adds that the Coast Guard surfboat sent after them was crushed between two ice fields and left four of its men stranded on the ice. On the 12th House set out in his own motor boat, which had to be dragged over the crushed ice on shore to open water, and by his report reached four men on the ice beside a sunken motor boat. By his account they all got into his boat and headed back for the point. The Secretary of Commerce commended him.[^14][^2]

The *Bulletin* also recorded a use of the signals in 1930: the radiobeacon and fog signal were sounded together so that a ship's master could time the gap between them and work out distance. One master approaching the point checked his distance for more than 12 miles with an error of no more than a quarter of a mile.[^13]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard set up a lifeboat station on the reservation in 1923, and the Lighthouse Service itself joined the Coast Guard in 1939.[^2][^15] Pepper says the two crews worked independently until 9 April 1947.[^3] The lifeboat station closed in 1951 and the Coast Guard took its last people off the site in 1970; the light was automated in 1971.[^2][^15][^1] Pepper says the Fresnel lens was taken out in 1968 and replaced with a modern beacon.[^3] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1973.[^15]

The Great Lakes Shipwreck Historical Society took on the property from 1980 at the township's request. It held a Coast Guard licence from 1983, opened exhibits in 1985, opened its museum building in 1987, and received the site by Act of Congress in October 1996.[^15] LighthouseFriends adds that the society recovered the bell of the *Edmund Fitzgerald*, lost in November 1975 about 15 miles (24 km) from the point.[^2][^15]

## Today

The light is an active aid to navigation.[^1] The Great Lakes Shipwreck Museum occupies the site; its visitor page says it opens daily from 1 May to 31 October, 9 a.m. to 5 p.m., and that the visit includes the 1861 keeper's quarters, the 1923 Coast Guard surfboat house and the *Fitzgerald* gallery with the ship's bell.[^16] The page does not list the tower among them, so check with the museum before assuming it can be climbed. The station is reached by road, 11 miles (18 km) north of Paradise, Michigan.[^16]
