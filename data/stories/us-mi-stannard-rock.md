Stannard Rock Light stands on a submerged reef in Lake Superior, 24 statute miles (39 km) from the nearest land, and was long described as the lighthouse farthest from shore in the United States.[^1][^4] A newspaper feature of 1905 found four men living on the rock to tend the light and the fog whistle, with Marquette, Michigan, the nearest port.[^10]

## The place

The reef is named for Captain Charles C. Stannard of the American Fur Company, who is said to have charted it in 1835.[^2] When the Lighthouse Board first urged a mark for it in 1866, it described the shoal as lying about 23 miles from Manitou Island Light and rising only two and a half to three feet above the water. It called the rock "the most serious danger to navigation in Lake Superior," and said that, being so far from land, it was seldom seen and "much dreaded by all navigators." That wording is the Board's, as LighthouseFriends quotes it.[^2] Congress gave $10,000 on 2 March 1867 for a day beacon; the work was done in the summer of 1868, and a stone-and-iron beacon was built on the reef.[^2] In 1917 George R. Putnam, then Commissioner of Lighthouses, wrote that this stone beacon was "still standing."[^4]

The case for a lighthouse grew with the traffic. In 1871 the district engineer, Orlando Poe, tied it to the rise of commerce between Duluth and the lower lakes, and in 1873 Congress paid for surveys.[^2] A report from the keeper at Manitou Island Light, as LighthouseFriends retells it, counted only five fog-free days near the reef in June 1874, which helped make the case for a fog signal as well as a light.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $50,000 on 3 March 1877 to start the work.[^2] The Board's plan followed its earlier work at Spectacle Reef in Lake Huron.[^3] In 1889 Arnold Burges Johnson wrote that the Board had reused the steamer and the plant from Spectacle Reef, and that the Stannard Rock light had cost about $300,000.[^3] Putnam gave the same cost and the same dates, 1877 to 1882.[^4]

According to LighthouseFriends, the contractors set up a depot called Stannardsville on Huron Bay, built a wooden protection crib there in the summer of 1877, and towed it to the reef in August 1878; the crib was set in position on 7 August.[^2] Inside it they built the foundation: a pier of concrete in a wrought-iron cylinder, 62 feet (19 m) across, finished in August 1880 at 23 feet (7 m) above the lake.[^2][^14] The Commissioner of Lighthouses later described it as standing 35 feet (11 m) above bed rock.[^14] The tower itself was limestone, brought from Ohio and cut at Stannardsville, and the 33 courses were laid between 4 July and 31 August 1881.[^2]

The work crews went back in May 1882, cleared ice that still covered the pier and the lower part of the tower, and put in the staircase and lantern.[^2] On 25 May 1882 a Wisconsin newspaper announced that after 1 July a second-order light "flashing white" every 30 seconds would be shown from "Stannard’s Rock."[^5] A New-York Tribune page of 5 December 1882, badly damaged in the digitised text, appears to report in a summary of the government's year that the new light-house on Stannard's Rock was lighted on 4 July.[^6] LighthouseFriends gives the same date for the first showing of the light, with a second-order Fresnel lens by Henry-LePaute of Paris carrying twelve flash panels.[^2]

Sources differ on the numbers. LighthouseFriends puts the tower at 78 feet (24 m) tall, with the light 102 feet (31 m) above the lake.[^2] Johnson, writing in 1889, gave roughly 101 feet for the structure and said the light was visible about 18 miles.[^3] The Light-House Service Bulletin of 1924 said the tower was built in 1880, which is the year the foundation pier was finished; a Bulletin of 1922 dated the pier's iron sheathing to 1882.[^14][^12] The National Register of Historic Places lists 1882 as the significant year.[^19]

Distance from shore is also given differently. Johnson said 28 miles from shore; Putnam said 24 statute miles; the 1924 Bulletin said the nearest land, the end of the Keweenaw Peninsula, was 27½ miles away.[^3][^4][^14]

## The keepers

The United States Lighthouse Society archive lists 137 appointments at the station from 1882 to 1961, from the keeper registers of the National Archives.[^1] The first head keeper, John Pasque, had been at Sturgeon Point, Lake Huron; LighthouseFriends says he took the higher-paying post at Stannard Rock and left after one season for Michigan Island.[^2] John Armbruster, of Outer Island, ran the station for a month before going back.[^2][^1]

Then came three Priors. James Prior was head keeper from 1883 to 1888, George Prior from 1888 to 1893, and William H. Prior from 1893 to 1896.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls them brothers, and the archive's roster shows George and William each serving as an assistant at the rock before taking charge.[^2][^1]

Edward T. Chambers held the post from 1896 to 1908.[^1] In 1905 a newspaper feature, reprinted from the Milwaukee Sentinel, called the rock "the lonesomest spot" on the lake and named Captain Chambers "of Mackinac Island" as its keeper.[^10] It said that in the early days the keeper had only one or two assistants, and that later there were four men, two on duty at all times.[^10] The station could be reached from one side only, in smooth water, and supplies were hauled up by derrick.[^10] According to LighthouseFriends, in November 1904 the contractor's tug failed to collect Chambers and his three assistants on schedule; they learned of Theodore Roosevelt's election five weeks late, had eaten all their provisions, and had spent a night making a canvas sail for the yawl before the tug Ward arrived on 14 December.[^2] This account rests on LighthouseFriends alone.

Lawrence T. Pedersen served as an assistant from at least 1917 and as head keeper from 1921 to 1930.[^1] The longest service of all was Elmer A. Sormunen's. The archive shows him as second assistant from 1935 to at least 1939, first assistant from at least 1942 to 1956, and head keeper in 1956 and 1957.[^1] LighthouseFriends tells of a spring trip in his first year when he and Louis DeRusha left Marquette in a 40-foot motorboat on 29 April, missed the station in snow because of a faulty compass, and took 48 hours to get back to Marquette.[^2] Louis I. Wilks was head keeper from 1936 to 1956.[^1] Under the Coast Guard, according to LighthouseFriends, keepers got a week of leave each month, and the lake trout fishing was good.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Light-House Board's annual reports counted the hours the steam whistle ran: about 144 hours in the report for 1893, 144 again in 1894, and 156 in 1895.[^7][^8][^9]

On 6 November, in a year the Bulletin does not state but which LighthouseFriends gives as 1921, a flock of ducks struck the lantern and broke two of the upper panes; glass fell through the lens room and four ducks were killed.[^11][^2] When the keepers returned for the 1922 season they found that the winter's ice had torn off almost all the wrought-iron sheathing around the concrete pier.[^12] The Bulletin reported that the plates were half an inch thick, and that the concrete behind them was in an excellent state. It suggested that heavy ice built up on the pier early in the winter and gripped the plates when a storm struck.[^12] The Commissioner's estimates for the fiscal year 1924 proposed $30,000 for a new shell.[^13] The repair, finished in 1924, used radial anchor trusses and steel plates bolted over a new belt of concrete three feet thick.[^14]

By the summer of 1928 the station had a radiotelephone link to Marquette. In a letter printed in the Bulletin of January 1929, the Marquette keeper described how it had been used on 28 July (1928, going by the date of the issue), when the keeper at Stannard Rock reported a fishing boat that seemed to be disabled; the next day a boat was reported missing from Munising, and it was picked up about 15 miles out.[^15] The Marquette keeper said he often read the morning paper's important items to the Stannard Rock keepers, and gave them the weather before they set out for the rock.[^15]

The Bulletin of January 1930 printed the head keeper's account of the late-November storm of 1929, which it described as one of the worst on Lake Superior, with temperatures of 10 degrees below zero.[^16] The head keeper wrote that in 30 years on the Great Lakes it was the worst weather he had known. On the 29th, he wrote, "the worst gale that I have ever known at the station" sent spray over the dome every minute; he tied down the vapor lamp with wire, and the lantern glass became a solid mass of ice.[^16] The steam hose ran night and day to thaw the horns and pipes so the fog signal could sound. The aerial wire came down three times under the weight of ice.[^16] The keeper noted that ice overhung the pier by six to eight feet.[^16] The report says the station's practice was to make no trips to shore in the last two months of the season.[^16] LighthouseFriends names the head keeper as Pedersen.[^2]

The worst event came in 1961. According to a press photograph caption in the Boston Herald-Traveler, an explosion at about 9:30 on a Sunday night in June left one man missing and another injured, and the outside world learned of it only when the Coast Guard cutter Woodrush came by around midnight.[^17] LighthouseFriends dates it to 18 June 1961 and says it was fed by propane and gasoline in the upper pier, killing the head keeper, William A. Maxwell, while Richard Horne, Oscar Daniels and Walter Scobie survived and waited three days for the Woodrush.[^2] Scobie, who was 22 and a third-class engineman, later recalled being thrown out of his bunk on the fourth deck.[^18] The accounts differ on the hour: the Coast Guard's 2015 article describes a morning, while the 1961 caption and LighthouseFriends say night.[^18][^17][^2] The archive's roster ends with Maxwell's name and the year 1961.[^1]

## After the keepers

After the explosion the Fresnel lens was removed and automatic equipment installed, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The Lighthouse Society's archive lists the light as no longer an active aid, while the Great Lakes Evaporation Network describes it as automated in 1962 and still kept as an active aid by the Coast Guard; the sources disagree and the present status of the light is unconfirmed.[^1][^21] The tower's lens went on display at the Marquette Maritime Museum in 1999, and the pedestal followed the next year.[^2]

LighthouseFriends reports that the lighthouse was made available under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000 in May 2012 and was deeded to the Superior Watershed Partnership in November 2015.[^2] The National Register of Historic Places lists it (reference 73000953), with 30 March 1973 as the date in the National Park Service's record.[^19]

## Today

The Superior Watershed Partnership owns the lighthouse.[^21] It describes it as a year-round observation station, with remote instruments used by researchers in the United States and Canada, and says a renovation costing two million dollars is needed.[^20] A research team led by a scientist from Environment and Climate Change Canada placed an eddy-covariance station on top of the tower in 2008.[^21] The Society's archive lists the light as not open to the public.[^1] It stands on open water, and the Coast Guard crew that took a former crewman out in 2015 said that a trip needs a three-to-five-day window of weather.[^18] Visitors should treat it as a landmark seen from a boat, not a place to land.
