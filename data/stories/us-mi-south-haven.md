South Haven Light stands at the end of the south pier, where the Black River meets Lake Michigan at the town of South Haven, in Van Buren County.[^1][^2] Some light has marked this harbor entrance since the late 1860s, though the sources date the first one differently. The Lighthouse Board's tower was first lit in 1872, and the metal tower that stands there now went up in 1903.[^1][^2][^3] The Historical Association of South Haven has owned the light since 2012. It is not open for tours, because of liability concerns.[^1][^9]

## The place

The Black River was navigable for six miles inland, but its mouth was the problem.[^1] In August 1838 Lieutenant James T. Homans of the U.S. Navy inspected the western lakes' lights and recommended two parallel piers at the river's mouth. A fine schooner had stranded upstream when a bar formed at the river's mouth.[^1] By 1868 piers bracketed the entrance, and, according to LighthouseFriends, private enterprise was keeping a beacon light on the south pier.[^1] The federal nomination for the harbor's piers says that a beacon with a fifth-order lamp was erected on the south pier in 1871, and it cites an 1880 county history for that date.[^3] The sources disagree about when a light first stood there, and this is the first of several disagreements in this story.

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says the Lighthouse Board completed a dwelling and pierhead tower at South Haven in 1872, using a $6,000 appropriation.[^1] The Board bought a bluff parcel above the river and built a two-story frame keeper's dwelling there, with seven rooms and a bath.[^1] The first tower was square, wooden and pyramidal, with an octagonal cast-iron lantern on top. Its fixed red light, produced by a fifth-order Barbier and Fenestre Fresnel lens, was first shown on 10 May 1872.[^1] The archive lists a fifth-order Fresnel lens, installed in 1903, as the tower's original optic.[^2] That disagrees with LighthouseFriends, which dates the first fifth-order lens to 1872.[^1]

The light was moved more than once. LighthouseFriends says the tower was moved 120 feet (37 m) lakeward in 1888, and that a gas machine inside supplied its light.[^1] The Board's report for the fiscal year that ended on 30 June 1901 records the South Haven Pierhead light station as "Moved 250 feet to outer end of extension of south pier July 4, 1900" (about 76 m).[^4] LighthouseFriends dates the same move to 1901 and gives the distance as 249 feet (76 m).[^1]

In October 1903 a ten-man crew came aboard the lighthouse tender Hyacinth to erect a new metal tower and remove the wooden one, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] The light in the new tower was first exhibited on 13 November 1903.[^1] The archive gives 1903 as the tower's year and describes the material as cast iron.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes its walls as steel plates over metal lath.[^1] The federal nomination's amended items place a cast-iron cylindrical light of 1903 on the north pierhead, and a modern light of 1965 to 1980 on the south pierhead.[^3] The Board's 1901 report and LighthouseFriends place the pierhead light on the south pier, so this story follows them.

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists Captain William P. Bryan as the first keeper, from 1871 to 1873.[^2] LighthouseFriends says his two years were the shortest tenure of any keeper of the light.[^1]

James S. Donahue kept the light from 1874 to 1909, longer than any other keeper.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends says that a minie ball at the Battle of the Wilderness in 1864 cost him most of his left leg.[^1] In 1880 Donahue built a 300-foot handrail out to the pier's elevated walkway, to help him reach the tower safely.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes his logbook entry for 3 July 1875, which records his wife's death: "my wife died this afternoon at 4 p.m., of lung disease."[^1] When the metal tower went up in 1903, LighthouseFriends says, Donahue set the old wooden tower up in his yard to preserve it as a relic.[^1] The Life-Saving Service's 1891 medal list records a silver medal for Donahue, dated 20 May 1890, for rescues from drowning.[^5]

Louis N. De Diemar kept the light from 1909 to 1913, when Jesse T. Brown took over.[^2] Brown's time included the 1916 rescue described below. John Langland, Jr., followed from 1919 to 1932, and Robert G. Young from 1932 to 1940.[^2] In 1940 Young was transferred to Kewaunee, Wisconsin, and the local Coast Guard station took over responsibility for the light, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] Richardt G. Hansen was first assistant from 1934 to 1937.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The medal list describes the rescues only as made "at various times during the years 1875" to 1889. It does not name the vessels or the dates.[^5] A Lighthouse Service Bulletin from 1916 reports that on 6 July Keeper Jesse T. Brown assisted in rescuing two women who were in danger of drowning in Lake Michigan near the light station.[^6]

LighthouseFriends says that in fog, mist or snow the keeper had to stay in the tower and sound a hand-operated, bellows-style horn.[^1] The horn was replaced on 18 September 1913 by a 1,200-pound bell.[^1] On 25 March 1937 an air diaphone replaced the bell.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over control of the country's lighthouses in 1939.[^1] The harbor's range lights changed as well. In 1916 a fifty-foot (15 m) skeletal tower was set up 267 yards (244 m) shoreward of the pierhead light to form a range.[^1] In 1923 the Lighthouse Service changed the range lights from oil to electric.[^7][^1] A 1924 issue of the Bulletin says that while the illuminant was being changed, one electric flood light was added to the front range tower.[^8] LighthouseFriends says two flood lights were installed on the outer tower in 1923.[^1]

The elevated walkway on the south pier came from Calumet Light in Illinois and was installed at South Haven in 1925.[^1] A light was established on the outer end of the north pier in 1949.[^1]

In 1989 a preservation committee began fundraising to save the tower and catwalk, which were threatened by budget cuts.[^1] The Corps of Engineers owns the catwalk and licenses it to the City of South Haven. The Michigan Maritime Museum took over its stewardship in 2003.[^1]

The Coast Guard declared the pierhead light excess to its needs in 2010. The light was awarded to the Historical Association of South Haven, and the deed transfer was held on 9 August 2012.[^1] The association's "Save the Light" campaign raised more than $300,000 in 2015.[^1] Mihm Enterprises of Hamilton, Michigan, restored the interior in 2015 and 2016. A $60,000 matching grant from the Michigan Lighthouse Assistance Program paid for the exterior.[^1]

## Today

The Historical Association of South Haven owns the light and is based at the old Hartman School Building on Hubbard Street.[^1][^9] Its website says the pierhead light is not open to the public for tours.[^9] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's place record lists the light as open to the public, which disagrees with the association's statement.[^2] The association offers "sneak peeks" at the lower level during HarborFest and Labor Day weekend.[^9]

The keeper's dwelling in South Haven now houses the Marialyce Canonie Great Lakes Research Library of the Michigan Maritime Museum. LighthouseFriends says a good view of the pier light can be had from the street near the dwelling.[^1]
