Colchester Reef Light stands on a stone pier at the southeast edge of Colchester Reef, a shoal in western Lake Erie about six kilometres (four miles) southeast of Colchester, Ontario.[^1][^10] Its light was first shown on 3 November 1885. The wooden lighthouse that first carried it was replaced by a skeleton tower in 1954, and the station has been automated for most of its life.[^1][^10]

## The place

Before there was a light there was a lightship. LighthouseFriends traces a vessel on the reef to 1848, when the Hackett brothers of Amherstburg laid a keel for a new lightship there.[^1] The Department of Marine's report for 1869, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says the Hacketts had kept her on the reef for three years on subscriptions from ship owners and underwriters, and that they had asked the government for help.[^1] Parliament agreed in 1871 to pay an annual subsidy of $500, raised to $700 by 1876.[^1]

The reef was a hazard. LighthouseFriends says it was the site of several vessel strandings in the 1800s.[^1] In April 1880 a Chicago paper reported that work on calking the lightship was being pushed along on Bois Blanc Island, and that she would be going out within days.[^2]

## Building the light

In 1880 Parliament appropriated $5,000 towards a lighthouse on a pier on the reef. Tenders for the work came in, seventeen of them, from $15,500 to $37,555. The contract went to Frederick T. Thomas, a civil engineer from Ottawa, and with the Department's consent the work was transferred to George Wilson.[^1]

Wilson's foundation was to be an iron caisson, with stonework built up inside it and protected by a cofferdam.[^4] Two newspapers printed in September 1883 give different accounts of the loss of the caisson that August. The Mower County Transcript of Lansing, Minnesota, reported the iron caisson "destroyed by the severe storm on the 20th," with a loss of $10,000.[^3] The Watertown Republican of Watertown, Wisconsin, said George J. Wilson claimed a loss of about $10,000, and described the mishap: after the caisson was pumped out, "the cofferdam gave way," and the weight of the water crushed in the caisson, which was built of rather light iron.[^4] LighthouseFriends also says a storm destroyed the caisson.[^1] The sources agree on the loss and disagree on the cause, and the story does not choose between them.

Wilson gave up the work, and a contract for $17,465 went to John E. Askwith of New Edinburgh to finish the light.[^1] A new caisson was towed out from Amherstburg in late July 1885. The light began operating on 3 November 1885. The Department of Marine described it as "a fixed white dioptric light of the third order," elevated 22 m (72 ft) above the lake, on a circular stone pier with a hexagonal wooden tower painted white. The lightship was withdrawn once the light was lit.[^1]

A fog bell, tolled every fifteen seconds, was added in 1886.[^1] During the 1890 and 1891 season twelve crib-work piers were placed around the foundation to take the pressure of ice and waves, at a cost of $9,560.42.[^1]

## The keepers

Christopher Robson was hired as the first keeper, but about six months later he was replaced by John Manson.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates Manson's charge of the light from 1885 or 1886, and says he stayed twenty-six seasons, until he retired in 1911 at seventy because of failing eyesight.[^1] The Department's statement of keepers for 1906 lists "Colchester Reef, Light and Fog Bell" against the name Manson, John, with an appointment date of 1 May 1880.[^9] That date is five years before LighthouseFriends' start, and the sources do not reconcile the two.

Manson was born in Scotland and came to Canada as a child. He went to sea young, and was mate of the lightship Dunscomb, which was stationed at Bar Point for many years, before he was given charge of Colchester Reef when that lightship moved in 1885.[^1] On his retirement the Lake Carriers' Association praised his long and faithful service, as LighthouseFriends reports. The association also recalled that once, returning from the village of Colchester in a gale, his small boat swamped and he swam the last stretch to the lighthouse. On another occasion a boat carrying him, his wife and two babies overturned, and the association said all four were saved.[^1]

Fred Malott came from Pelee Passage Lighthouse to take charge at Colchester in 1911, and kept the light until 1916.[^1] John B. Knapp began his long service there in 1916, and was keeper until 1936.[^1] In December 1933 Knapp and his assistant, S. Bock, were stranded at the lighthouse after closing the station for the season, because the ice was too thin to walk on and too thick for a boat to get near.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Lake Erie took the lightship more than once. In the late fall of 1880 her owners asked permission to withdraw her, but were to keep her on station. She was then driven from her station, cut in two by ice floes and sunk, and a passing steamer rescued her crew.[^1]

The lightship was on station again under George Campbell of Windsor, who had been paid $1,440 to keep a vessel there for the 1881 season.[^1] On the night of 12 November 1883 she foundered.[^5] LighthouseFriends says it was in a gale.[^1] The Rock Island Argus of Illinois printed the wire report the next day. It said the "Colchester Reef lightship foundered last night" and that "Capt Forrest was the only man on board, and he is supposed to be drowned."[^5] LighthouseFriends says Captain James S. Forrest, aged sixty-three, and another man drowned.[^1] The sources disagree on how many men were lost.

## After the keepers

In 1905 the crib-work was replaced by steel and concrete protection, and the wooden bell tower by a steel one.[^1][^6] The Department's report for that year says the work was under the direction of Mr. Fred Forster, at a cost of $4,955.24.[^6] The breakwater was repaired by day labour in 1907 for $705.50.[^7] In 1909 a 55 mm diamond vapour installation replaced the duplex lamp.[^8]

The station was automated in 1936. The bell was discontinued, and the light changed from fixed white to flashing white.[^1] In 1954 a skeleton tower replaced the old wooden lighthouse. LighthouseFriends describes it as red, with a white oval daymark, and says it was later cantilevered off the side of the pier to make room for a helicopter landing pad.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory describes the tower as painted red with a horizontally slatted daymark.[^11] The Coast Guard's current list calls it a white skeleton tower.[^10]

## Today

The Coast Guard lists the light as entry 620, near the southeast edge of the reef. It shows a red flash every four seconds, from a focal height of 21.6 metres, and the list gives the tower as 12.4 metres high, standing year round.[^10]

The station is closed to visitors, and the reef can be reached only by boat. The Lighthouse Directory lists the site and tower as closed, with the Coast Guard as owner and site manager.[^11] Do not plan a landing on the reef.
