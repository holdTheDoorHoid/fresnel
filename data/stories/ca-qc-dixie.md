Dixie Sector Light, on Lake St. Louis, a widened stretch of the St. Lawrence River just west of Montreal, replaced the front light of a pair of range lights that the Department of Marine set up there in 1915.[^1][^2]

## The place

Lake St. Louis is a widening of the St. Lawrence River, and vessels crossing it in those years were passing between the Lachine Canal and the Soulanges Canal.[^1] Before 1904 the lake was marked partly by lightships.[^3][^5] The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1900 says the names of the three lightships "have been painted on their topsides".[^3] The report for the year ended 30 June 1904 says that the services of the three lightship keepers "were dispensed with", and gave the seven lights at the lower end of the lake to the Lachine keeper.[^5]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends dates the range to 1 September 1915.[^1] It quotes the Lake Carriers' Association's report for that year, which describes two fixed white catoptric range lights, set to mark the axis of the ship channel between Windmill Point gas buoy 98S and Lightvessel No. 13.[^1] The front light is exhibited 12.2 m (40 ft) above the water from an enclosed white wooden tower. The rear light is 25.3 m (83 ft) up, on a brown skeleton steel tower.[^1]

The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1916 lists the Dixie work as "Erection of range lights, front, wooden tower, back, steel skeleton tower", done by day labour, at a cost of $7,923.05.[^6] LighthouseFriends gives the same $7,923.05 for range lights erected by day labour under J. D. Weir's supervision.[^1]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends names the keepers. Albert LeBlanc was hired as keeper of the front range at $320 a year, and Ed. Massie was paid the same to care for the rear light. H. Massie is listed for the front light in 1917.[^1] The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1917 records that "a small dwelling was erected at Dixie's back range for the keeper".[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 6 June 1903 the steamer Keefe of the Wolvin Line touched in the main channel of Lake St. Louis, "at a spot 200 feet south of gas buoy No. 98 S", which marked the north side of the channel east of Windmill Point.[^4] The Department's report gives two figures for the buoy's move. One passage says it "was moved to a new position about 300 feet south of its former location".[^4] A later passage, which also reports "boulders outside the gas buoy not known before", says "the buoy was moved 100 ft. south".[^4]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says a sector light replaced the front range in August 2018, and that the Coast Guard confirmed the rear range light was dismantled in July 2019, with a warning light placed on its pier.[^1]

The Coast Guard's list describes the Dixie Sector Light as a "Square skeleton tower", 3.4 m high, with the characteristic "Iso R W G 6s": an isophase light in red, white and green sectors.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard's list still carries the light, southwest of Île Dixie.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes the front light as a cylindrical tower and says the light is still active, showing quick-flashing green lights over the lake.[^1] The Coast Guard list gives a square skeleton tower with a white cylindrical daymark, not a cylindrical tower, and an isophase light with red, white and green sectors. This story reports both descriptions and does not settle which is current.[^1][^2]

LighthouseFriends says the Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, with grounds open and the tower closed. It names Parc du Millénaire, reached from Avenue Dorval, as a place with a view of the lights.[^1]
