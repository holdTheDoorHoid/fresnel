Smith Island is a roughly fifteen-acre scrap of grass and bluff at the eastern end of the Strait of Juan de Fuca, and for almost a century it carried a brick lighthouse built about 200 feet from a western bluff that kept eroding. The Coast Guard abandoned the old house in 1957, and the last piece of it went over the edge in 1998.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The island lies between five and six miles west of Whidbey Island, about a dozen miles north of the entrance to Admiralty Inlet.[^2] The National Register nomination describes it as surrounded by shoals and thick kelp beds, in a fog belt where visibility drops to about ten miles for roughly two months of every year.[^1] It was first named Blunt's Island, after a midshipman of the Wilkes Expedition, and became Smith Island by usage; at low tide a narrow spit joins it to little Minor Island.[^3]

## Building the light

Smith Island was among the second batch of West Coast lighthouses, and its fourth-order light first shone on 18 October 1858.[^3][^2] The Cape Cod-style building was a masonry dwelling roughly 25 by 35 feet with the tower rising through its roof; the nomination says the light was engineered by Hartman Bache and built under the supervision of the U.S. Engineers, with walls over 14 inches thick.[^1][^3] It stood about 200 feet from the western bluff.[^1][^2]

Sources differ on the lens. HistoryLink says a Fresnel lens replaced the original light in 1885, while LighthouseFriends says a new revolving fourth-order lens arrived in 1895.[^2][^3] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1895, which covers July 1894 to June 1895, records that at Smith Island "the new lens and revolving apparatus were placed in position and are in good working order."[^6] The same report notes that a lens-lantern light was shown from 5 to 15 July 1894 during repairs to the regular apparatus, though it does not say the two are connected.[^6]

## The keepers

The first keeper was John Vail (1859 to 1860); his wife appears in the archive roster as first assistant Mary Jane Vail.[^4] In May 1859, as HistoryLink tells it, Haida war canoes were spotted off the island a few days after settlers at Port Townsend had jailed a Haida party; the head keeper and his wife left, but assistant J. R. Applegate stayed to tend the light. A group of Haida landed and shot at him, and he returned fire and wounded one of them. Some wags dubbed the affair the "Battle of Smith Island," and HistoryLink says the settlers hyped it more than it deserved.[^2] The nomination also tells the story and says an Indian was seriously wounded.[^1]

DeWitt Dennison came as assistant in 1880 and became head keeper in 1881, bringing his wife and six children.[^2] LighthouseFriends reproduces entries from the station logbook of his years. On 1 November 1882 the lens stopped revolving; the entry says small pieces of broken glass had jammed the revolving shaft, and that he "called my daughter to keep the lens turning" while he repaired the machinery.[^3] He died in October 1891, and his son Frank became acting keeper and then keeper. The USLHS roster shows Frank as head keeper from 1892 to 1898, and LighthouseFriends says he resigned in 1898, returned in 1904 as assistant and was transferred in 1905; HistoryLink says only that he remained on the island until 1905.[^2][^3][^4]

The nomination calls Bernard Meagher perhaps the most famous keeper. On 2 September 1917, the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported, he helped transport an officer from the disabled steamer *Samson* eleven miles to the nearest telephone.[^7] The nomination says he rowed there and back so the light was lit by nightfall.[^1] On 28 February 1920 he rescued the crew of the burning launch *Lucy Bell* and landed them the next day where medical aid could be had, and in the fiscal year 1922 he provided food and lodging to five crewmen of a hydroplane that had landed at the station during a storm.[^8][^10] His wife Harriet is listed in the roster as a laborer from at least 1919 to 1925.[^4] On 1 April 1925, at about 8:30 a.m., after noticing the peculiar action of a small launch that had come up on the beach, she went aboard and found two people unconscious from asphyxiation.[^9] Meagher had a stroke in March 1925, and LighthouseFriends says Harriet and her son kept the light for several months.[^3]

On 29 December 1939 head keeper Edwin G. Clements went out in the station rowboat to relight the beacon on Minor Island and did not come back. According to LighthouseFriends, when Clements had not returned by midnight assistant Charles Bearman radioed the Coast Guard cutter at Port Townsend; the rowboat was found cast up on the shore the next morning and Clements's body was not recovered until 6 January 1940.[^3] HistoryLink calls it a stormy night, says the boat was found flipped over on the beach the next day, and says the body was found the day after that, which does not fit the January date.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The nomination lists three sailing vessels grounded on or near the island and later salvaged: the *Coquimbo* in 1862, the bark *Alcalde* in 1894 and the schooner *Minnie A. Caine* in 1901.[^1] HistoryLink says the *Caine* was driven ashore on Christmas night 1901 in a gale, and her crew stayed on the island into the new year.[^2] On 24 July 1936 assistant keepers Dwight Southmayd and Orval Risdon rescued four men whose boat had run aground during high seas near the station.[^11]

The shore itself was a threat to the station. In 1878 the Lighthouse Board, as quoted by LighthouseFriends and HistoryLink, warned that the sea was "rapidly eroding the west face of the island" and that the bank stood only about 160 feet from the dwelling.[^2][^3] In the fiscal year ended June 1892 the board's report records an iron oil house built and a replacement for the beacon on the sand spit that had washed away.[^5]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard abandoned the old building in 1957 because erosion of the bluff was undermining it, and a skeleton-tower light was established nearby.[^1][^3] HistoryLink dates the first pieces falling to 1969; the USLHS archive says the front of the structure fell in the 1980s.[^2][^4] LighthouseFriends reports photographs showing the foundation at the cliff edge in 1975, just over half the building standing in 1982 and about a quarter in 1993; the last portion fell in 1998.[^3]

## Today

The island is part of the Smith and Minor Islands Aquatic Reserve, and LighthouseFriends says it is closed to the public year-round, with visitors required to stay 200 yards offshore.[^3] The Coast Guard still maintains an automated flashing light there.[^3] The old lighthouse's lantern room went to Jim Gibbs's private Skunk Bay Lighthouse in 1959, and a second Fresnel lens from the station is displayed at the Museum of History & Industry in Seattle.[^3] A National Register of Historic Places nomination for the station is dated 6 April 1978.[^2]
