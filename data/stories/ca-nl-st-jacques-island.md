St. Jacques Island is a pair of grassy hummocks joined by a low neck, just off the harbour mouth of St. Jacques on the north shore of Fortune Bay, Newfoundland. A 12 m (39 ft) white cast-iron tower has stood on its cliff, lit since 1908, and the station's best-documented story is of a December storm in 1963.[^1][^2]

## The place

St. Jacques grew in the mid-1800s, when families from Grand Bank moved there for the herring fishery that supplied bait to the Grand Banks schooners. By 1891 the firm of Burkes and Young had a prosperous bank fishing business at St. Jacques, which was also the parish seat for the Roman Catholic communities of inner Fortune Bay; a convent and school was established there in 1894.[^1] A lighthouse was already under consideration in 1885, when Austin Oke visited to look at sites. In 1894 Inspector John T. Nevill reported that residents had repeatedly asked for one and judged the island the best place: easy to reach, useful for making the harbour, and with a commanding range over the bay.[^1]

## Building the light

Lighthouse Friends says the tower was erected in 1907, with a keeper's dwelling, at the southeast end of the island; the Parks Canada statement gives 1908 as its build date.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends gives its lens as a fourth-order Fresnel from Chance Brothers of England, showing a white light that was steady for seven seconds and eclipsed for three, from a focal plane about 40 m (131 ft) above the sea because of the cliff-top site.[^1] The Parks Canada heritage statement, as reproduced by the local campaigner Alex Hickey, describes the tower as sections of cast iron prefabricated in England and assembled on site, with the seams filled with lead and caulking.[^2] P. O. Burke, the first keeper, showed the light for the first time in the spring of 1908.[^1]

A landing boom was installed in 1918. A fog alarm was arranged in 1928 and began working in 1929, housed in a flat-roofed engine house and powered by air compressed by an oil engine; it gave a two-second blast every 45 seconds when needed. The light was changed to fixed white in 1930, and a new dwelling was built in 1950.[^1] The Canadian Coast Guard's list of lights today gives the characteristic as a long flash, white, every 6 seconds, 39.9 m above the water, with a range of 16 nautical miles.[^4]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists P. O. Burke from 1908 to at least 1912, then Isaac Burke (at least 1916), Joseph Edgar Penney (at least 1929 to at least 1935) and Hubert Myles (at least 1963). In 1935 Penney's assistant was Thomas Harry Young.[^1] Hickey describes Isaac Burke as a father of eight.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In July 1916 Isaac Burke watched the yacht *Caribou* capsize in Fortune Bay. Lighthouse Friends says he rowed his dory to St. Jacques for help, and Dr. Conrad Fitz-Gerald took his medical schooner *Albatross* out to search. While searching off Belleoram Back Cove it was cut in two by the mail steamer *Hump*. Lighthouse Friends says Burke "presumably" died in the collision; Hickey says he likely died on impact. The two men from the *Caribou* were never found. Dr. Fitz-Gerald and two brothers from St. Jacques who were aboard were thrown into the water and survived.[^1][^3]

On Thursday 19 December 1963 a storm arose that evening that, Lighthouse Friends says, brought 100 mph winds for two days and coated the side of the Fiander dwelling with seven inches of ice. Assistant keeper Eric Fiander was in charge because Keeper Hubert Myles was ashore on leave; Myles's 17-year-old nephew Hughie was on the island. On Friday morning Fiander made his radio call to the Department of Transport office at Burin, and the two men went out to check the station. After lunch they headed down the cliff with rope to secure a boat shed that the wind had toppled on the beach between the island's two lobes. They did not come back. That night Fiander's wife Katherine, alone with their three small children, judged the ice-coated lifeline to the generator building too dangerous to follow, and the lighthouse and dwellings were dark. The next day she tried the generator and could not start it. About noon on the day after that, two friends from Coombs Cove arrived looking for the men. The account says high-water marks on the cliffs showed the sea had swept them away. Hughie's body was recovered on the beach the next day; Eric Fiander's was never found.[^1] Lighthouse Friends lists a December 1964 *Newfoundland Woman* article by Cassie Brown and Katherine Fiander among its references.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1968 staffing changed to a four-person rotating shift, and one of the two dwellings was sold and removed from the island.[^1] The lighthouse was declared a Recognized Federal Heritage Building in 1989.[^1]

When the federal government offered surplus lighthouses to others, neither the province nor the municipality showed interest. Alex Hickey formed the non-profit St. Jacques Island Heritage Corporation, which submitted a business plan to Fisheries and Oceans in 2013. After three years of negotiation, an agreement signed over the lighthouse, outbuildings, the keeper's residence and the island to the corporation.[^2] The corporation then won designation as a heritage lighthouse under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act. By 2019 it was one of sixteen in Newfoundland and Labrador.[^1]

## Today

Besides the tower, a 1960 dwelling and later generator and equipment buildings stand on the island; the sources differ on whether the generator building dates from 1960 or 1970.[^1][^2] A floating dock was installed in 2019, and the corporation plans to turn the keeper's dwelling into a tourist rental and artist retreat to fund the tower's upkeep.[^1] The island is reached only by boat. Lighthouse Friends lists the grounds as open and the tower closed, and names the Coast Guard as owner, which conflicts with the transfer to the heritage corporation described above.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends says the island is visible from the town of St. Jacques-Coomb's Cove, but because the lighthouse is on the island's southern side a boat is needed to see the station.[^1]
