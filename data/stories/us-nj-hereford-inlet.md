Hereford Inlet Light stands on the south side of Hereford Inlet, in North Wildwood, New Jersey, between Five Mile Beach and Seven Mile Beach.[^1][^6] It is a wooden house in the "stick style," with the tower rising from the building. It was built in 1874 to mark the inlet, and it is open to visitors today.[^1][^7]

## The place

The inlet was a route for coastal shipping, and it was a hazard. The nomination quotes the Lighthouse Board's 1871 report recommending a light at the entrance, "of the fourth order," as "a good harbor of refuge for small coasting vessels." The nomination says such a light was felt to be important to the coal trade, and to small steamers on the Delaware River Bay.[^1] The Friends group writes that strong currents and shifting sandbars near the entrance caused frequent groundings and shipwrecks.[^6]

The nomination describes the water in the inlet.[^1] "The white water off the inlet makes the need of the lighthouse and lifeboat station obvious." It also says some sections of the inlet are "only two feet deep" (0.6 m).[^1] It records one vessel driven off the inlet fifteen times between 25 December 1787 and 6 February 1788 before it could get in.[^1] The Life-Saving Service had a station on the inlet, Station #36 in the Fourth District.[^1][^12]

## Building the light

The nomination says Congress appropriated $25,000 in 1872 for the land and the building.[^1] In 1873 the site was chosen, and the New Jersey legislature granted the federal government jurisdiction over the property.[^1] The Friends group dates the authorizing act to 10 June 1872.[^6]

Construction began on 8 November 1873 and was completed on 30 March 1874, the Friends group says.[^6] The nomination dates the building to 1874, along with facilities for a boat.[^1] The Friends group credits the design to Paul J. Pelz, the Lighthouse Board's chief draftsman, and says he later became known as the designer of the Library of Congress. The attribution comes from the Friends group alone.[^6] The Friends group says a Notice to Mariners announced the light on 11 May 1874.[^6]

The sources disagree on the tower's height. The nomination says the tower is 55 feet (16.8 m) high and the light is 53 feet (16.2 m) above the water.[^1] The Friends group gives 49½ feet (15.1 m) for the tower and 57 feet (17.4 m) for the light's elevation.[^6] The Friends group says the light can be seen for 13 nautical miles on a clear night; the nomination says 13 miles at sea.[^1][^6]

The Board's report for the fiscal year 1896 says the dwelling was overhauled, the sandy site was partly regraded and refenced, and a detached brick oil house was erected. The same entry says preparations were made to change the light from fixed white to flashing red and white.[^2] The nomination dates that change to 1897.[^1] The Board's report for the fiscal year 1899 lists a flagstaff, a telephone and a set of signal code flags furnished at the station.[^3][^1]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists ten lines of service at Hereford Inlet, from 1874 to 1961.[^4] The first keeper, John Marche, drowned when his boat capsized on the way back from the mainland. The Friends group says he had been in the post less than three months.[^6] The nomination, which spells his name March, dates the drowning to 1875.[^1] The archive lists Marche for 1874 alone, and also lists a John Nickerson for 1874.[^4]

Freeling H. Hewitt was next, a Civil War veteran and former merchant seaman who came from Cape May Court House, according to the Friends group. He kept the light for forty-five years, until 1919.[^4][^6] The Friends group says he held the first formal religious services in the Wildwoods, in the lighthouse parlor.[^6]

William H. Hedges kept the light from 1919 to 1925.[^4] His wife, Laura Hedges, is listed as principal keeper from 1925 to 1926. The archive describes her as the wife of keeper William H. Hedges, gives her birth year as 1869, and records the cause of her termination as "Temporary." No other woman appears on the station's roster.[^5][^4]

Ferdinand Heizman kept the light from 1926 to 1939.[^4] He was followed by Robert L. O'Neal, George L. Baker, Newman F. Bowden and, last on the roster, Bruce R. Bolon, who served from 1960 to 1961.[^4] The Friends group says the last live-in Coast Guard keeper left in 1961.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 2 November 1883 a schooner of Camden, New Jersey, carrying a crew of five men and a cargo of pig iron, staves and carboys, stranded on the south bar of Hereford Inlet.[^12] The Life-Saving Service's report for the fiscal year ending 30 June 1884 says the Hereford Inlet station crew discovered the schooner's situation immediately and went to her assistance.[^12]

In 1916 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists a whistling buoy established at Hereford Inlet.[^9]

In February 1927 the beach at Conover Beacon and at Hereford Inlet was also washed away, the Bulletin reports.[^10] The Bulletin's index for 1918 to 1923 lists storm damage to the Hereford Inlet light station on page 117, without a date.[^11]

The Friends group says a severe storm in 1913 undermined the foundation, and that the building was moved 150 feet (46 m) west to its present site.[^6] The group's FAQ dates the storm to September 1913, and its history page dates it to August.[^6] The nomination instead explains the distance from the sea as the result of later landfill.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over the lighthouse service in 1939, according to the Friends group.[^6] In 1964 an automatic optic was placed on an iron skeletal tower behind the old lighthouse, and the lighthouse was closed. The property went to the New Jersey State Marine Police, and the building was boarded up for eighteen years.[^6] The 1976 nomination lists the owner as the State of New Jersey's Bureau of Marine Police.[^1]

The lighthouse was registered in the state and national registers in 1977.[^8][^1] In 1982 the New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection leased stewardship of the lighthouse to the City of North Wildwood, and part of the restored building opened to the public on 1 July 1983.[^6] In 1986 the modern automatic light was moved into the lantern room. The restored fourth-order Fresnel lens was later put on display on the second floor.[^6]

## Today

The city's official site says the lighthouse is open to the public and "continues to function as an active navigational aid maintained by the United States Coast Guard." It lists opening hours of 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. daily, with the last tour at 4 p.m., and free admission.[^7]

The Friends' older FAQ says the stairs have 69 steps to the lantern room, and that visitors count 56 of them because the lantern room is closed to the public.[^6] The city's site does not say whether the climb is open now.[^7]

The newsletter *The Bay Run*, published by the Delaware Bay Lightkeeper Friends, says the city took over the lighthouse at the end of 2017 and gave the volunteer Friends group notice to be out by the end of that year.[^8] It says city employees moved the Friends' items to a storage shed, and that court proceedings had not returned them by the time of writing. It quotes Mayor Patrick Rosenello as saying that certain Friends members had "negligently disregarded" the city's concerns about the lighthouse's operation.[^8] The city's own account appears here only as the newsletter prints it.

The older Friends website still describes the Friends as its operator, while the city's site now credits the City of North Wildwood.[^6][^7]
