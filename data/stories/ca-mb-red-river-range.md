The Red River range lights marked the mouth of the Red River in Manitoba, where the river runs into the south end of Lake Winnipeg.[^7] The first pair was lit on 2 September 1895.[^1][^2] The second set, built in 1913 or 1914, was in service until 1992.[^1][^2] Its front tower now stands on the grounds of the Marine Museum of Manitoba, in Selkirk.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The Red River flows north and, on its final stretch, through southern Manitoba.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it was a key trade route for the Hudson's Bay Company and helped settle British North America.[^1] Where it meets the lake it empties through several mouths in a delta.[^1] The lightship that first marked the mouth was placed at the eastern mouth and later moved to the western one, which carried the best water. In 1893 the eastern channel became the deepest.[^1]

The first aid at the mouth was a lightship. LighthouseFriends says it was built by John and Charles Short for $2,468 and placed in 1880, in Lake Winnipeg at the mouth of the river. It was a scow-shaped vessel with a small tower and a fixed white light 6.4 m (21 ft) above the water.[^1] The Selkirk Museum also calls it the first light placed on Lake Winnipeg at the mouth of the river.[^2]

## Building the light

After the lightship was beached at the western mouth, it served as a shore light until 1893. Then its lantern was set on a pole in the eastern channel.[^1] LighthouseFriends says an inspection by the Department of Marine's chief engineer in August 1894 led to a pair of range lights at the mouth of the eastern channel. They were lit on 2 September 1895.[^1] The Museum gives the same date and says the Department decided in 1894 to build two lighthouses.[^2]

LighthouseFriends prints the description published for the lights in 1895. It says of the back tower: "The back tower is built on a pile foundation in a hay marsh on the east side of the channel."[^1] The tower was a square wooden building, 14.3 m (47 ft) from its pile foundation to the vane, with a red lantern and a white body. A red stripe about 1 m (3 ft) wide ran down the side facing the channel. Its fixed white light was 14 m (46 ft) above the summer level of the lake, and the description says it should be visible 12 miles from all points of approach by water.[^1] The front tower stood on a pile foundation on the shoals on the west side of the channel, about 1,100 m (3,600 ft) from the back tower. It was 8.2 m (27 ft) high, with a fixed white light 7 m (23 ft) above the lake.[^1][^2]

The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1912 records an inspection of the aids on the Red River and Lake Winnipeg in February 1912. It does not say what the inspection found.[^5]

The towers were replaced in 1913 or 1914, and the sources disagree on the year. LighthouseFriends says new range lights were built in 1913 and that M.J. Egan oversaw the work, at a cost of $3,091.[^1] The Selkirk Museum says the two buildings were replaced in 1914, with Egan overseeing.[^2] Rowlett gives 1914 for the front light.[^3] The new front tower was a square wooden tower of about 8.2 m (27 ft), with a dioptric lens. The rear light was a steel skeletal tower with an enclosed wooden top.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

Joseph Monkman was the first keeper of the lightship. LighthouseFriends says he was paid $250 a year and served three years, from 1880 to 1883. The Museum gives the same years.[^1][^2]

William Hughes followed him. LighthouseFriends says he had charge of the lightship from 1883 and kept the range lights until 1924, a span of forty-one years.[^1] The Museum has him succeeding Monkman in 1883.[^2] The Department's keepers' lists give a different date. For "Red River Range," they list William Hughes with an appointment date of 12 February 1892, in the reports for the years ended 30 June 1911 and 1914.[^4][^6] The lists do not say which post the 1892 date refers to. The 1911 list also puts Hughes against Black Bear Island, with the same date, while the 1914 list gives that station to D. Matheson.[^4][^6] The sources do not reconcile the 1892 date with the 1883 start. LighthouseFriends also gives 1885 in one passage.[^1]

In August 1925 the Winnipeg Tribune printed remarks by Hughes, as LighthouseFriends quotes them from that newspaper. He said: "Some storms, of course, but they were the only excitement I ever had." He added: "Just the lights have to be tended at night."[^1] LighthouseFriends also says that James Aikins, the Lieutenant Governor of Manitoba, awarded Hughes the Imperial Service Medal soon after he retired. The medal was for public servants with a quarter-century or more of service.[^1]

Roderick E. Spence succeeded him in 1924 and served until at least 1937.[^1][^2] A newspaper article that LighthouseFriends relays, without naming it, describes Spence's family of twelve children, his ability to talk at length, and his skill at catching catfish, a hobby that paid.[^1] The Museum says Hughie Reed replaced Spence as keeper. The sources give no dates for Reed.[^2][^1]

## After the keepers

In 1992 the back tower of the 1913 or 1914 set was demolished. The front tower was moved to Selkirk, and modern towers went up at the entrance to the river that year.[^1][^2][^3] Rowlett describes the front tower as "Inactive since 1992 (a decorative light is displayed)."[^3]

The modern range lights served until 2019. In that year the front light was discontinued and the back light was renamed the Red River Mouth Light.[^1][^2]

The Canadian Coast Guard's current list shows the Red River Mouth light at the south end of Lake Winnipeg. It is a flashing white light, every four seconds, with a focal height of 17.2 m and a range of 5 nautical miles. It is on a skeleton tower with a white trapezoidal daymark and an orange stripe. The light is seasonal, and it is obscured between 072 and 312 degrees.[^7]

## Today

The front tower of the 1913 or 1914 set stands on the grounds of the Marine Museum of Manitoba in Selkirk. Rowlett lists the museum as owner and site manager.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1]
