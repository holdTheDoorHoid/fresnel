A granite tower rises from a rock off Cape Elizabeth, Maine, that is barely visible at high tide, at the northern entrance to Portland Harbor. Ram Island Ledge Light went up in 1905 after three wrecks and groundings between 1900 and 1902 made the case for a light there, and it has been automated since the end of the 1950s.[^1]

## The place

Ram Island Ledge is a narrow reef, about a quarter of a mile long, that runs southwest from Ram Island and marks the northern side of the outer approach to Portland. It lies a little over a mile from Portland Head Light.[^2] The National Register nomination describes the hazard as "the hazardous and oftentimes submerged shoals" on that side of the harbor entrance.[^1]

Before there was a lighthouse there were beacons. LighthouseFriends dates an iron spindle at the ledge's southern edge to 1855 and a 50-foot wooden tripod to 1873, a structure that the open ocean washed away at least three times.[^2] The Light-House Board's report for 1899 records, among the beacon work of the year ended 30 June 1899, that the tripod on "Ram Island Ledge beacon" was rebuilt.[^3]

The case for a real light rested on trade and on a stranding. The Life-Saving Service's report for fiscal 1900 describes a steamer that stranded at night on Ram Island Ledge in a strong gale with heavy rain; her crew and passengers chose to stay aboard rather than face the surf in the life-saving crew's boat.[^4] LighthouseFriends identifies the steamer of February 1900 as the *Californian*; the National Register nomination calls her the *California*.[^1][^2] The report's passage does not name her, but its service table for the same weeks lists the British steamer *Californian* under Cape Elizabeth for 25 February 1900. According to LighthouseFriends, the First District's inspector and engineer wrote in 1901 that grain shipments through Portland had grown from about a million bushels in 1895-96 to more than fourteen million in 1900-01, and recommended a light and fog signal on the ledge to guide ships between Witch Rock and the ledge.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress acted on 28 June 1902, authorizing a light and fog signal at a total cost of $166,000 and appropriating $83,000 to start.[^2] The National Register nomination gives the same figures and notes that the work had not begun before the British schooner *Glenrosa* struck the reef in a heavy fog on 22 September 1902, followed less than three months later by the fishing schooner *Cora and Lillian*.[^1] The Life-Saving Service's annual report lists the *Cora and Lillian* among the casualties for 8 December 1902, at Ram Island Ledge, in the district of its Cape Elizabeth station, and describes a schooner there that was fast on the ledges and "went to pieces, becoming a total loss" after a tug failed to float her and a southwest wind rose on the 10th.[^5] The report's text does not name the vessel in that passage, so the match to the *Cora and Lillian* rests on the date and place in the casualty table.

LighthouseFriends gives the construction record: the government paid $500 to two Cape Elizabeth families for title to the ledge in March 1903, and a contract with the Bodwell Granite Company of Rockland supplied granite from Vinalhaven. Work began on 1 May 1903, the first stones were set on 9 July, and the crew slept in temporary quarters on Ram Island, about 600 yards away. Further appropriations of $50,000 (1903) and $33,000 (1904) followed, the last course of stone was laid by July 1904, and the lantern came by rail from Atlanta that fall.[^2]

The light was shown for the first time on 10 April 1905.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends reports a third-order Fresnel lens made in Paris by Barber, Benard & Turenne, turning on a mercury bath to give two flashes every six seconds, and a fog bell struck once every ten seconds, placed in operation on 28 August 1905.[^2] An iron landing pier built that spring and summer from surplus funds was not in the original plans.[^2] The nomination describes a conical granite tower with the keeper's quarters and storage inside its walls, a skeleton pier on the west side, and a round lantern with diamond-pane windows; it gives the height as 77 feet (23 m) from base to the middle of the lantern. LighthouseFriends gives 70 feet (21 m) to the lantern deck, and the two figures measure different things.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the keeper registers, lists William C. Tapley as principal keeper from 1905 to 1921, with Edward T. Merritt and Wellington M. Latham as his first and second assistants.[^6] LighthouseFriends says Tapley began as an assistant at Saddleback Ledge in 1886 and had kept Goose Rocks and Deer Island Thorofare before coming to the ledge.[^2]

During his years there, lobstermen of Casco Bay complained that keepers had an unfair advantage because they lived on the fishing grounds. In reply to an inspector's inquiry, Tapley wrote, as LighthouseFriends quotes him, "I have never owned or hauled a lobster trap, I have not time and do not care to fish."[^2]

John B. Dewyea followed as principal keeper (1921-1929), then Leroy L. Myers (1929-1939) and William A. Clark Jr. (1939-1942), with a succession of assistants, mostly for a year or two each.[^6] The archive's roster ends in 1956 and names Coast Guard personnel in the 1950s.[^6] Edgar W. Lovatt, listed as an assistant from 1913 to 1925, has the longest run of any assistant in that roster.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The ledge turns up in the official lists of assistance rendered. In June 1914 the Lighthouse Service's monthly bulletin recorded that the tender *Hibiscus* helped pull the schooner *Harriet C. Whitehead* off Ram Island Ledge.[^7] A Commissioner of Lighthouses report from the early 1920s credits First Assistant Edgar W. Lovatt with picking up an adrift launch, towing it to the station and, with Keeper Dewyea's help, returning it to its owner, and credits Dewyea with piloting two men to port who had lost their way in fog without a boat compass.[^8] LighthouseFriends places that in 1922 and describes Dewyea's earlier near-thirty years at Halfway Rock.[^2]

On a foggy night in August 1927, LighthouseFriends writes, a motorboat struck the ledge and its occupant set a mattress on fire with gasoline to draw the keepers' attention. Assistant Keeper Frank William Alley saw the signal and took the man into the tower for the night.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1924-1929 lists a rescue at the station (page 214) and a vessel assisted by its keepers (page 268), without dates.[^9]

## After the keepers

A power cable laid between Portland Head and the ledge in late 1958 allowed the light to be automated, and the last resident Coast Guard crew left on 14 January 1959, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The National Register nomination says only that the light was automated "sixty years" after its first lighting, which would point to about 1965; the 1959 date is better documented.[^1][^2] The station was converted to solar power in 2000, and LighthouseFriends reports that care of the tower was licensed to the American Lighthouse Foundation in October 2005.[^2]

The Coast Guard declared the lighthouse excess to its needs. It was offered at no cost to government bodies and nonprofits, and no one took it, so the General Services Administration opened an online auction in June 2010.[^2][^10] Bidding started at $10,000 and did not heat up until September; the price jumped from $30,000 on 1 September to $190,000 when the auction closed on 14 September 2010. The *Portland Press Herald* called it one of the priciest lighthouses put on the market in the Northeast.[^10] The *Press Herald* and *Mainebiz* both reported that the leading bidders said they wanted the light to stay in Maine or in local hands.[^10][^11]

## Today

Ram Island Ledge Light was sold at federal auction in 2010 and is privately owned.[^2][^10] The Coast Guard said it would continue to maintain the automated light and fog signal.[^10] The nomination treats the whole rock as one property with the tower.[^1] LighthouseFriends lists the lighthouse as privately owned, with grounds and tower closed, and best seen by boat.[^2] The *Press Herald* noted that the tower can be approached only in calm seas.[^10] LighthouseFriends describes a modern 300 mm optic showing two white flashes every six seconds, with an automatic foghorn sounding once every ten seconds when needed.[^2]
