A white wooden tower with an eight-sided lantern, joined to a fog-signal house, stands on Point Elliott in Mukilteo, Washington, a few steps from the ferry landing for Whidbey Island.[^1][^4] It was lit on 1 March 1906, was built to a plan its architect used for several Pacific Northwest stations, and still shows a fourth-order Fresnel lens that flashes two seconds on and three seconds off.[^1]

## The place

The point has had several names. The National Register nomination says that for centuries it was a gathering place and trading centre for Snohomish and other tribes, and that the Lushootseed word behind "Mukilteo" has been rendered as "good camping ground", and also as a "narrow passage" or a "throat".[^1] Captain George Vancouver came ashore on 31 May 1792 and called the place Rose Point for its wild roses; in May 1841 Lieutenant Charles Wilkes of the U.S. Exploring Expedition renamed it Point Elliott.[^1] The nomination says the Point Elliott Treaty of January 1855, in which 22 Puget Sound tribes ceded their lands, "may have been" signed here.[^1] White settlers arrived in 1858, and Jacob Fowler and Morris Frost founded a trading post and settlement.[^1]

By the turn of the twentieth century, traffic in Possession Sound had grown to the point of danger, with several near collisions, and a few Mukilteo citizens joined the Everett Chamber of Commerce in lobbying for a light.[^1] In 1902 the Treasury Department told the House of Representatives that the Light-House Board judged a light and fog signal at "Muckilteo Point" would help vessels bound for Everett and the smaller boats running up Possession Sound and Saratoga Passage, and added: "The tides are very strong and deceiving at Muckilteo Point. Some time ago one of the large San Francisco vessels narrowly escaped running ashore there in a fog."[^1] The Board put the cost at no more than $22,000, and Congress appropriated that sum on 9 January 1903.[^1] The finished station cost $27,000.[^1]

## Building the light

The station was designed by Carl W. Leick, who from 1889 worked as a draftsman in the engineering office of the Thirteenth Lighthouse District; the nomination counts Mukilteo among the last of several similar wood-frame stations he designed.[^1] Construction began in 1905 and, according to the nomination, took eight months.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the opening of bids to 30 June 1905 and the start of work to August.[^4] The complex had a combined tower and fog-signal building, two keepers' dwellings on either side of it, an oil house, and a windmill over a well that also pumped fresh water to the town.[^1]

Sources disagree about the tower's height. The nomination calls it a 38-foot (12 m) octagonal wooden tower, although its summary gives the lighthouse a height of 35 feet (11 m); HistoryLink says 30 feet (9 m), and the USLHS archive records 30 feet from base to the centre of the lantern and a light 33 feet (10 m) above the water.[^1][^3][^2] Wood was unusual: the nomination says most lighthouses of the period were brick or concrete.[^1] The fog signal was a Daboll trumpet driven by compressed air, its brass horn poking through the back wall of the building; the Mukilteo Historical Society's timeline instead names a Cunningham diaphragm horn.[^1][^6] The nomination says the note carried up to eight miles, sounding every 16 seconds.[^1]

The Seattle Times, as the nomination quotes it, called Mukilteo "without exception the most up-to-date and thoroughly equipped station on the Sound", and the next day the Everett Morning Tribune ran the headline "Best Light House in Entire Puget Sound District", reporting that on the first night "dozens of people curiously watched the glint of the lamp".[^1] A paper in the San Juan Islands noted on 10 March that the lighthouse "is now completed and in operation".[^9] The first entry in the station's guest register is dated 16 March 1906.[^1]

The lens was a fourth-order Fresnel, turned by a clockwork weight.[^1][^6] LighthouseFriends says the original revolving lens came from Barbier and Bénard of Paris.[^4] Electricity reached the station in 1927. The nomination says the fourth-order lens now in the tower, made in France in 1852, was brought to Mukilteo then; HistoryLink explains that an attempt to adapt the original lens to an electric bulb failed, so the Lighthouse Service exchanged lenses with New Dungeness.[^1][^3] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1914 had already listed Mukilteo among the lights changed from oil to incandescent oil vapor.[^12]

## The keepers

The first keeper, Peter N. Christiansen, was a Norwegian who had been a sailor for 21 years, ten of them in the U.S. Navy, before joining the Lighthouse Service.[^1] The nomination and HistoryLink say he joined in 1893; the Mukilteo Historical Society and the Turn Point keepers' history put his appointment as assistant keeper at Turn Point, on Stuart Island, in 1894.[^1][^3][^5][^7] He was promoted to keeper there in 1898.[^7] A Friday Harbor newspaper reported in April 1906 that he had been "transferred to the Mukilteo lighthouse, one of the best stations on the Pacific coast".[^8] HistoryLink says the posting was partly a reward for his part in saving the crew of the tug Enterprise, aground at Turn Point in a winter storm in February 1897.[^3]

He arrived with his wife, Theodine, and four children.[^1] The Historical Society's account of his career notes that, unlike a duplex at Turn Point, Mukilteo gave each keeper a two-storey house of his own, close to a town with schools and shops.[^5] It describes the work in the oil-lamp years: lighting the lamp before sunset, climbing the tower every three to four hours in the night to refill it and adjust the clockwork, trimming the wick, keeping the glass clear of smoke, tending the fog-signal compressor, and keeping the dwellings spotless for an inspector who could arrive unannounced.[^5] Christiansen worked with eight assistants over nineteen years; the first, David O. Kinyon, came from Destruction Island and stayed about a year.[^5] The archive's roster also lists Theodine Christiansen as first assistant in 1918.[^2]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1925 reported that on 4 September Christiansen had rescued a boy who was adrift on a couple of planks near the station.[^10] A month later, on 5 October 1925, he died, presumably of a heart attack, after helping unload a large shipment of coal for the station.[^1] The Historical Society says Theodine kept the station running for a short time until his successor arrived.[^5]

Edward A. Brooks followed and served until he retired in 1937; the nomination says he came from New Dungeness, with his wife Anna and four children, while the Turn Point history records an earlier Edward A. Brooks as assistant there from 1898 to 1902 who "later was named Keeper at Mukilteo".[^1][^7] Ray E. Dunson came from Smith Island in 1937 and was keeper when the Coast Guard took over the Lighthouse Service in 1939.[^1] From then to 1979 the Coast Guard kept at least three men at Mukilteo, standing 24-hour watches; the nomination says Vivian R. Corrie, in charge from 1946, set the record for the longest tour of the Coast Guard years. It puts the end of his tour at 1960; the archive's roster ends his time as principal keeper in 1958.[^1][^2] The station went back to a single caretaker when it was automated; the last Coast Guard caretaker, Boatswain's Mate Kurtis Betz, served from 1986 to 1990.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources read for this station name no wreck at the point after the light was lit. The one rescue they record is Christiansen's, of the boy in 1925.[^10] In the week of 17 to 24 December 1933 a succession of gales and extreme high tides struck the whole Pacific Northwest, with winds as high as 90 miles an hour at some stations. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that West Point and Mukilteo stations were flooded to some extent around the station buildings by the high tides and heavy surf, that no serious damage was reported, and that protective works for them had been provided for.[^11] The nomination dates the station's seawall, which turned a marshy tideland into the lawn and garden, to 1934.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard automated the light and fog signal in July 1979, and the first caretaker of the automated station was Charles Milne, 1979 to 1982.[^3][^2] LighthouseFriends tells of the fog sensor installed in 1981: it kept sounding the horn on clear nights, annoying neighbours, until the Coast Guard worked out that sun or moonlight reflecting off the white seawall was fooling it, and the wall was painted black.[^4]

In 1954 Washington State Parks acquired 17 acres next door, and the Coast Guard gave up an acre of swampy land from the station's corner, leaving 1.46 acres; Mukilteo State Park opened in 1957, mostly as a parking lot for a boat ramp.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the acre's transfer to 1973.[^4] Around 1960 the Coast Guard announced it would replace the Fresnel lens with a modern beacon, and local protests kept the lens (the nomination places this "in the 1960s").[^1][^6] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 21 October 1977.[^1]

In 1990 the Coast Guard offered the city a free long-term lease and permission to run a museum; the lease was finalised in October 1991, and the Mukilteo Historical Society began giving tours, in the spring of 1992.[^1][^3][^6] The Coast Guard kept the two dwellings for its own people until 1996. Ownership of the whole station passed to the City of Mukilteo in the spring of 2001, and a new National Register nomination covering the oil house, garage, seawall and other pieces was prepared in 2008.[^1][^6]

## Today

The City of Mukilteo owns the station, the Mukilteo Historical Society runs it as a museum, and the Coast Guard still operates the light and fog horn as aids to navigation.[^1] The historical society says the lighthouse and gift shop are open on Saturdays and Sundays from 1 pm to 4 pm, April through September, and that the grounds are open year round.[^13]
