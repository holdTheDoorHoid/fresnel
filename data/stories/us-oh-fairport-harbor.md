Fairport Harbor Light stood on a bluff above the mouth of the Grand River, on Ohio's Lake Erie shore, from 1825 until 1925. The sandstone tower built in 1871 still stands, and since 1945 the tower and the keeper's house have held a marine museum.[^1]

## The place

The harbor sits where the Grand River meets Lake Erie. LighthouseFriends says the settlement was first christened Grandon, on land deeded by the Connecticut Land Company, and took the name Fairport from its reputation as the finest port on Lake Erie.[^1] LighthouseFriends counts 2,987 vessels passing through the harbor in 1847 alone.[^1]

The Board listed the front and rear beacons on the east pier, at the harbor entrance, as aids of their own, separate from the bluff light. This story follows only the bluff light.[^6]

The Life-Saving Service also had a station at Fairport. Its report for the fiscal year ending 30 June 1878 records that the removal of the Fairport life-boat station had been assisted by a donated site, given through the kindness of Hon. H. B. Payne of Cleveland.[^11] The 1880 report lists the station as Station No. 7, Ninth District.[^12]

## Building the light

In 1825 a notice in the *Painesville Telegraph* asked for bids to build a lighthouse and keeper's house. It was signed by A. Walworth, collector of customs for the Cuyahoga district, and set out the materials, the foundation, the height and even the size and shape of the windows.[^1] Hiram Wood and the builder Jonathan Goldsmith contracted for the work at $2,900. Goldsmith had left the cellar out of his estimate, and Walworth contracted him to build it for an extra $174.30.[^1]

In the fall of 1825 the lighthouse and its dwelling were finished, and a fixed white light fueled by whale oil was first shown. LighthouseFriends gives the lighthouse a height of fifty-five feet (17 m).[^1] Within ten years the foundation had settled so far that it had to be replaced, and within thirty years the tower was encircled with wire hoops to keep it from toppling.[^1]

In 1869 Congress appropriated $30,000 for a new tower and keeper's house. A light was shown from a temporary tower on 10 December 1869, so that the old tower could come down. The new contractors drove piles, capped them with concrete and laid a limestone base. An act of 12 July 1870 halted the work and returned about $9,000 of the appropriation to the Treasury. Congress gave $10,000 more on 3 March 1871 to finish the tower and the dwelling.[^1]

The third-order Fresnel lens from the old tower went into the new one, and the new tower first showed its light on 11 August 1871.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes a sandstone tower with a brick-lined interior, sixty-eight feet (21 m) tall, and a red-brick dwelling of one and a half storeys joined to it by an eighteen-foot (5.5 m) covered passage.[^1] The Lighthouse Society's place record gives the tower's height as sixty feet (18 m) to the lantern, and describes it as a conical tower of sandstone and brick.[^3]

Later work appears in the Board's reports. In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892, water from the city works was piped into the keeper's dwelling, and a hand rail some seventy feet (21 m) long was placed in the tower.[^4] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1895, a roof went over the covered way, the fence around the grounds was replaced, and extensive repairs were made.[^5]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society's roster begins with Samuel Butler, listed for 1825 to 1833.[^3] The Historical Society's keeper page calls Butler the first keeper and says he was a staunch abolitionist who used the lighthouse as a stop on the Underground Railroad.[^2] That is the Society's own account. The roster does not mention it, and the Society gives no dates and no names for the people who were said to have been hidden.

Elijah Dixon followed Butler in 1833, and Butler returned from 1841 to 1845. After that the head keepers changed often: Thomas Greer, Isaac Spear, Hendrick E. Paine, Olmsted Baker, Halsey H. Baker and James McAdams each served only a few years.[^3] George F. Rogers was head keeper from 1865 to 1871, and the Society calls him the last keeper of the original tower.[^2][^3]

Joseph C. Babcock, whom the Society describes as a Civil War veteran, became the first keeper of the 1871 tower.[^2] He was head keeper from 1871 to 1881, first assistant under George L. Riker from 1881 to 1900, and head keeper again from 1900.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he retired in 1919 at the age of seventy-six, and that his son Daniel took over.[^1] Daniel I. Babcock had been first assistant from 1901 to 1919. The roster has him as head keeper until 1925, but LighthouseFriends says he served until 1926.[^3][^1]

George L. Riker was head keeper for nineteen years, from 1881 to 1900, after two years as first assistant. LighthouseFriends says he died of a heart attack on 27 August 1900, and that Mrs. Riker stayed in the keeper's house until 23 January 1901.[^1] Other first assistants on the roster include James Matchett (1900 to 1901) and John B. Leavy (1919).[^3] The roster names no women keepers for this light.[^3]

Jonathan Goldsmith, who had built the 1825 light, applied to be its keeper in 1841, and LighthouseFriends says the post went to someone else.[^1]

The roster runs on after 1925 with more names, among them Frank S. Huntington, Frank LaRose and John E. Brophy. The Historical Society's page says Huntington served shortly after the new breakwater light was established.[^2] The Archive's roster for the West Breakwater light gives Huntington, LaRose and Brophy the same stints at that light. This story does not count them as keepers of the bluff light.[^3][^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 17 November 1879 a schooner of Buffalo, New York, bound from Erie, Pennsylvania, tried to make the harbor during what the report calls a "blinding storm of rain and sleet," with a northeast gale. It missed the piers and was driven ashore west of the harbor, about half a mile (800 m) from Station No. 7. The keeper and crew placed the surf-boat on its wagon, took it to the beach opposite the wreck and launched it into the surf.[^12] The Service's report is for the fiscal year ending 30 June 1880, so the wreck fell in November 1879.[^12] The report places it west of the harbor and does not connect it with the bluff light.

The Board's reports also record a southwest gale on 21 November 1892, when the steamer *Canisteo*, towing the schooner *Stewart*, slightly damaged the wooden beacon at the outer end of the east pier while entering the harbor. That beacon is a separate aid, not the bluff light.[^6]

## After the keepers

The breakwaters at the harbor mouth were completed in 1913.[^1] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' estimates, printed in the volume for 1915 to 1922, proposed the change that followed: "It is proposed to discontinue the present main light on the bluff and to construct a new light with a fog signal on the pierhead."[^7] On 12 June 1917 Congress appropriated $42,000 for a new combined light and fog station on the west breakwater pierhead, as LighthouseFriends describes it, and the Commissioner's report records the appropriation for aids to navigation at Fairport Harbor.[^1][^8]

LighthouseFriends says part of that money was to pay for demolishing the stone lighthouse, but townspeople mounted a campaign to keep it. Letters of protest from groups including the Daughters of the American Revolution, a temperance society and a Kiwanis club persuaded the Secretary of Commerce to leave the obsolete lighthouse standing.[^1]

The new light was first lit on 9 June 1925. LighthouseFriends says it replaced the bluff light after about a century.[^1] The Lighthouse Service's Bulletin for 1933 says the old light was discontinued on that date, but that its tower still remained and was still owned by the Lighthouse Service. It also reports that the Men's Civic Club of Fairport had placed a bronze tablet on the old lighthouse, reading "Built 1825-Rebuilt 1871."[^10]

The Fairport Harbor Historical Society was founded to preserve and celebrate the town's nautical heritage.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the Society obtained government approval to make the lighthouse and its keeper's house into a marine museum.[^1] The museum opened in 1945, and LighthouseFriends describes it as the first such museum in the country.[^1] The museum also has the third-order Fresnel lens from the tower, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1]

## Today

LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is owned by the Fairport Harbor Marine Museum, and that the grounds are open and the dwelling and tower are open in season.[^1] The Historical Society's keeper page gives the museum's hours as noon to 6 p.m. on Wednesdays, Fridays, Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays, from Memorial Day to 30 September 2026.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives a different schedule: 1 to 6 p.m. on Wednesdays, Saturdays, Sundays and holidays, from Memorial Day weekend until the third weekend in September. The two differ, so check before you go.[^1]

The National Register record, which uses the name Fairport Marine Museum, is dated 5 November 1971.[^13]
