The Sheet Harbour Passage range lights are a pair of small wooden towers in the Halifax Regional Municipality of Nova Scotia, built in 1915 to guide ships from the sea into Sheet Harbour Passage.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Sheet Harbour Passage runs between the mainland and Sober Island, which forms the eastern side of the entrance to Sheet Harbour.[^1] A village of the same name lies on the mainland beside the passage, and Lighthouse Friends gives its population as 150 in the early 1900s.[^1] Before the range lights, the passage's official light was a fixed red light shown from a window of James Wambolt's dwelling from 28 September 1887, at a height of 12.8 m (42 ft) above high water.[^1]

With the range lined up, a ship could pass between the east end of Fishery Point Shoal and the western side of Eastern Shagroost.[^1] In its report for the year ended 31 March 1909, the Department of Marine recorded a bell buoy established at Fishery Point, Sheet Harbour Passage.[^6]

## Building the light

Lighthouse Friends gives 15 August 1915 as the day the pair of range lights was established.[^1][^2] The towers are square and pyramidal, of wood, painted white with a red vertical stripe on the range line.[^3] Rowlett gives the front tower as 8 m (27 ft) and the rear as 9 m (29 ft).[^3] The sources disagree here: Lighthouse Friends says the rear tower was identical to the front one, while Rowlett gives it a metre more height.[^1][^3] Lighthouse Friends gives the front light a height of 15.5 m (51 ft) and the rear light's focal plane as nearly 23 m (75 ft).[^1][^2]

The Department's report for the year ended 31 March 1916 lists, under Sheet Harbour, the erection of wooden range light towers by contract at $1,792, and a separate line of $303.75 for inspection and other costs.[^4] Lighthouse Friends names the contractor as A. Balcon of Halifax, paid that sum for the pair of towers, and gives $70.74 for title searches and buying the sites; the Department's table has no land cost for Sheet Harbour.[^1][^4]

## The keepers

James Wambolt tended the 1887 light from his own house. Lighthouse Friends gives his service as 1887 to 1911, and the Department's keepers' list for the year ended 31 March 1909 gives his appointment as 11 May 1887.[^1][^8] Lighthouse Friends says the Wambolt family cared for the range lights for many years.[^1]

George Wambolt took charge in 1911.[^1] The Department's list for the year ended 31 March 1915 shows a "G. W. Wambolt (temp.)" at Sheet Harbour passage, appointed on 25 December 1911; the list gives initials only, so it is probably George Wambolt.[^5] Arthur Wambolt followed in 1915, and William A. Wambolt in 1917; the span given for William runs to at least 1923.[^1]

After the lights were electrified, a caretaker was hired. Laurie A. Spears held the post at $75 a year until his death in 1957, and caretakers looked after the lights until 1967.[^1] Lighthouse Friends records that Spears's daughter remembered going out with her father in the morning, by horse and wagon, to help blow out the lamps.[^1]

## Today

Both towers belong to the Canadian Coast Guard.[^1][^2] Rowlett lists both as "Inactive since 2024".[^3] Lighthouse Friends and Rowlett both describe the site as open and the tower closed.[^1][^2][^3]

The Coast Guard's Atlantic list of lights, buoys and fog signals, December 2025 edition, has an entry for a light and bell buoy at Sheet Harbour Passage but none for the range towers.[^7] Whether the range lights still show is not settled by these sources.
