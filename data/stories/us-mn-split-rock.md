Split Rock Lighthouse stands on a headland above Lake Superior on Minnesota's North Shore, in Lake County.[^1][^8] Its brick tower and three keepers' houses went up in 1909 and 1910 on a coast that had no light between Grand Marais and Two Harbors, and the station went into commission on July 31 or August 1, 1910, depending on the source.[^2][^1] Two months later both of the station's assistant keepers drowned on a mail run, and their bodies were never found.[^2][^3] The station was closed in 1969; since 1976 the Minnesota Historical Society has run the site, and it became a National Historic Landmark in 2011.[^4]

## The place

An official description of the station, in the National Archives clippings, says the station was meant to mark a point thought dangerous, between the Grand Marais and Two Harbors lights, where odd magnetic pulls on the compass and the lack of dependable soundings made navigation hard in thick weather.[^1] The 1969 National Register nomination blames the iron: cargoes of high-grade ore and ore deposits in the lake basin, it says, swung the compass needle far from true north, and more than one ship trusting its compass ran aground.[^5]

The wish for a light here was old. LighthouseFriends reports that Congress had set aside $6,000 in 1856 and $15,000 in 1866 for a light at Beaver Bay, a few miles up the shore, but that William F. Raynolds, who visited in 1868, concluded: "The light is not needed for local purposes, nor is it required by the general wants of commerce."[^2] The National Register nomination says it was 1907, after the Mesabi Iron Range had been working for more than five years and mariners had made constant demands, before Congress authorized a light.[^5]

The Minnesota Historical Society ties the station to a storm. On November 28, 1905, it says, a single gale damaged 29 ships on Lake Superior, about a third of them U.S. Steel's, and the Society counts 36 lives lost; it adds that a delegation led by the steel company's president went to Washington to lobby for a light station.[^4][^6] Pepper's history gives a larger toll: over 200 deaths in back-to-back storms between November 27 and 29.[^3] In January 1907 J. H. Sheadle, vice president of the Lake Carriers' Association, wrote to the congressional Committee on Interstate and Foreign Commerce asking for "a light-house of the second order, with fog-signal attachment" near Split Rock. "There is at present no light-house on the north shore of Lake Superior between Grand Marais and Two Harbors," he wrote, "and it is extremely difficult to locate Two Harbors in a fog or storm." He listed the steamer Lafayette, the barge Madeira, the steamer Spencer and the barge Pennington as total losses in the previous two years.[^2]

The site is a headland the description calls Stony Point, 127 feet (39 m) above the lake and 7.63 acres in extent; other sources put the cliff at 124 or 130 feet.[^1][^5][^2] In Terry Pepper's history the Light-House Board named the station after the Split Rock River, three miles to the southwest; the Historical Society says the lobbyists in Washington were the first to use the name Split Rock for the place, and it became the station's name.[^3][^4]

## Building the light

Pepper reports that Congress approved the station by resolution on February 26, 1907, and followed with a $75,000 appropriation on March 4; an official report extract gives the act of March 4, 1907, for "a light and fog-signal station at or near Split rock, near Beaver Bay, Lake Superior."[^3][^1] Buying the land was slow: an official report extract says the matter of acquiring title by condemnation was in the hands of the district attorney, and with title secured late in 1908 the engineer Ralph Russell Tinkham drew up the plans.[^1][^3][^2] Pepper says a Duluth firm, L. D. Campbell & Son, supplied the thirty to fifty men who did the building.[^3]

There were no roads. The nomination says all the men and materials had to be brought over the lake and hauled up the face of the 124-foot (38 m) cliff; the description says the tender brought supplies by boat and a derrick with a hoisting engine raised them up the cliff.[^5][^1] Work began in June 1909 (an Eleventh District paragraph in the same file says June; the description's cost paragraph says March 1909), stopped for the winter on November 21, 1909, and resumed about May 1, 1910. The lighting apparatus was received from the general light-house depot on May 20, 1910.[^1]

A report extract written in mid-1910 expected the light and fog signal to be ready to go into commission on August 1, 1910, and the later description of the station says that is when the station went into commission.[^1] Pepper says the light was shown for the first time on the night of August 1.[^3] LighthouseFriends and the Historical Society's library guide say July 31 instead, as does the Society's page on the beacon, which calls July 31 the anniversary of when the beacon was first lit; the 1969 nomination says only August 1910.[^2][^7][^18][^5] The station cost $72,541, which was $2,459 less than Congress had given.[^1][^2]

The description lays out a brick station. The tower is octagonal and trimmed with concrete, its concrete footing reaches 2 feet 6 inches (0.8 m) below grade, and a steel skeleton is built into the brickwork. Beside it are a separate brick building for the fog-signal machinery, a concrete oil house and three two-story brick keepers' houses, all linked by concrete walks. Every house had a cistern in its cellar, with a hall, living room, dining room and kitchen on the ground floor and three bedrooms and a bath upstairs, and each had a frame barn. A boathouse at a log crib pier 900 feet (274 m) west of the lighthouse sheltered the keepers' boats, and stairs led up the bank from the pier.[^1]

The records disagree about the light itself. A report extract written in mid-1910 said the station was "to be a third-order light, showing a single white flash every ten seconds," and the US Lighthouse Society's archive, LighthouseFriends and the Historical Society's page on the beacon all describe a third-order, bivalve Fresnel lens.[^1][^8][^2][^18] The later description of the station says second-order: two panels, each of seven refracting and thirteen reflecting prisms, estimated at 1,200,000 candlepower, with its focal plane 168 feet (51 m) above the lake and visible 22 miles in clear weather. Pepper's history gives the same second-order figures.[^1][^3] George R. Putnam's 1917 book lists Split Rock as a two-panel lens 39 inches (1 m) in diameter, turning once in twenty seconds, at 220,000 candles.[^9] Sheadle had asked in 1907 for a second-order light.[^2] These figures cannot all be right, and we have not found a document that settles which are.

The fog signal was announced as a first-class compressed-air siren sounding a two-second blast every twenty seconds; as described after completion it was a pair of 6-inch automatic sirens, worked by compressed air from two 22-horsepower gasoline engines.[^1] Pepper, who calls the original signals diaphones (the Historical Society's timeline says the siren was converted to a diaphone only in 1936), repeats a story that the signal unsettled horses five miles away at Beaver Bay and left guests at Slater's Hotel asking what animal was making the noise.[^3][^10]

The station changed in modest steps. A concrete tramway replaced the derrick in 1915 and 1916.[^3][^10] The Historical Society's timeline records that it came down in 1934, when the station was given a truck and the second assistant was made laborer and truck driver; that the fog signal's gas engines gave way to diesels in 1932 and its siren to a diaphone in 1936; and that the station was electrified in 1940.[^10] LighthouseFriends adds a 1,000-watt electric lamp then; Pepper dates the change to 1941.[^2][^3]

## The keepers

Orren "Pete" Young, the first head keeper, took command on July 20, 1910.[^3] Pepper says he was 52, had entered the Lighthouse Service in 1901 as second assistant at Huron Island, and had been at Grand Island East since 1908.[^3] The Minnesota Historical Society calls him a one-time sailor whose lighthouse career began in 1901 on the Michigan side of Lake Superior, and says his wife and four children spent summers at the station but never lived there year-round; in his later years the family had a year-round home in Two Harbors, 20 miles to the south.[^11] He retired at the mandatory age of 70 in 1928, the Society says, though LighthouseFriends writes that he resigned.[^11][^2]

Franklin J. Covell succeeded him. The roster has Covell at the station as second assistant in 1913-1916 and again in 1922-1923, as first assistant from 1924, and as head keeper from 1928 until he retired, at 70, in 1944.[^8][^11] When he was promoted, the family stayed in the middle house rather than move one door over to the head keeper's.[^11]

For the station's first two decades the keepers did not winter there. Covell's daughter, Ileana Covell-Meyers, recalled one thing she disliked as a child: closing the light on December 15, when the family packed up and left until April 15, with schools changed twice a year and a cold house to return to that took days to thaw. In 1931 the keepers were allowed to stay all year, and she called it a joyous year.[^2][^11] LighthouseFriends, which says she fondly remembers her time at the station, prints a list of its contrasts: gales that drove spray over the headland and built ice an inch thick on the east windows, frozen water pipes and ink bottles, lightning that knocked down curtains and set the dining-room telephone dancing, and "Thunderous waves pounding the caves beneath the cliffs so hard the entire rock quakes."[^2] The same list has summer nights quiet enough to hear fish jumping far below and a hand-wound victrola playing records newly arrived by boat from Chicago; in her own account, in winter her father played the violin and sang for the family by the pot-bellied stove.[^2]

The highway along the lake reached the station in 1924, and with it came visitors by car.[^4] In 1935 Covell's superiors told him, "This is a show station in the Lighthouse Service and visitors should be encouraged to visit the Lighthouse and see the lens and other mechanism at stated periods which are indicated on the sign placed at the entrance of the station."[^2] (General context, not Split Rock's own record: the 1881 *Instructions to Light-Keepers* told keepers to be "courteous and polite" to visitors who followed the rules and to show them what was of interest at times that did not interfere with their duties.[^12] Pepper quotes a similar rule in his Split Rock history.[^3]) A keeper's letter printed in the Lighthouse Service Bulletin said that in the 1937 tourist season "we registered 19,230", fewer than a third of the people who came, and estimated that 60,000 visited in July and August alone.[^13] LighthouseFriends reports 27,591 signatures in the log for 1938 and that a fourth worker was added in summer to handle the crowds.[^2] A Bulletin article of 1939 said that Split Rock had in recent years been the most visited lighthouse in the United States, with as many as 28,000 names in the visitors' register in a single year, and that the keepers believed many more entered the grounds without climbing the tower.[^14]

The Lighthouse Service became part of the Coast Guard in 1939.[^5] According to the 1969 nomination, the Coast Guard cut the crew to two men in the early 1940s, and the Historical Society's timeline has a 1942 entry saying that when the Navy took over the Coast Guard in the war, lightkeepers became "commanding officers" (the entry does not say this applied at Split Rock).[^5][^10] Robert E. Bennetts, head keeper from 1947 to 1961, is called the last civilian keeper by the Historical Society.[^10] LighthouseFriends says he wrote in the logbook on January 31, 1961, the day he retired, that he had served 38 years, 3 months and 14 days, and then handed the records and equipment to his successor, Boatswain's Mate 1st Class John L. Comeau.[^2] After that the head keeper's post turned over quickly: the roster lists four more men between 1962 and 1968, and eight Coast Guardsmen under a separate heading for 1960 to 1968.[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Two ships from the 1905 storm came to grief within a few miles of where the light would stand. In winds of 70 to 80 miles an hour, LighthouseFriends says, the steamer William Edenborn lost the barge Madeira from her tow line while the two were moving along the lake's western shore. The Madeira ended up on Gold Island, where nine of her ten crew reached shore; the Edenborn was pushed into the mouth of the Split Rock River, split in two, and lost one crewman.[^2] The Historical Society dates the Madeira's loss to about 3 a.m. on November 28, says she struck at "Gold Rock," and credits seaman Fred Benson's actions with saving all but one of the crew.[^6]

On Sunday, October 2, 1910, just over two months after the station went into commission, First Assistant Edward F. Sexton and Second Assistant Roy C. Gill left the station in a small rowboat to fetch the mail from a lumber camp at the mouth of the Split Rock River.[^3] Seven years to the day, First Assistant Harry Thompson recorded the event in the station logbook. As LighthouseFriends prints his entry, the two men "left the station in row boat at 12:20 to go to Split Rock for mail and did not return" (Pepper has 12:30 p.m.). Young could not leave the light unattended overnight; at 8 a.m. he went to look for them and "found the boat about 2 miles from the light afloat bottom up about 20 rodds from shore. That shows that both men drowned."[^2][^3] Pepper says Young's log recorded searches for the bodies every day for two weeks, and that they were never found; he adds that Sexton's wife, who was pregnant, and their small child left the station on October 6 aboard a motor launch sent for them from Two Harbors.[^3] Lee Benton arrived as the new first assistant on October 15 and James W. Taylor as second assistant on October 18.[^3][^8]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of October 1, 1923, reports the light being seen far beyond its listed range. On the night of August 4, it says, Lighthouse Service officers crossing Lake Superior from Outer Island, Wisconsin, to Grand Marais on a lighthouse tender saw Split Rock from about 52 nautical miles away (the note gives 60 statute miles). The weather on that night and on a second crossing three nights later was "clear and cold," the note says, and the lights were all distinctly visible; it adds that none of the lights' listed visibilities exceeded 22 miles.[^15]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the light was decommissioned by the start of 1969 and the station was passed to the General Services Administration to be disposed of.[^2] The nomination, dated April 1969, says the Coast Guard no longer needed the lighthouse and had taken its personnel away, and that the General Services Administration held the property for disposal.[^5] The Historical Society's timeline records that the site was placed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1969.[^10] After two empty years the 7.6-acre property passed to the State of Minnesota on March 1, 1971, and the Department of Natural Resources dedicated Split Rock Lighthouse State Park on May 2 that year.[^2][^7] The Historical Society has administered the site since 1976; its keepers page gives 1975 for the transfer, while its history page says the state took the site in 1971 and handed over administration in 1976.[^11][^4]

The Society has restored the site to how it looked in the early 1920s, before the highway, when the station could be reached only by water.[^4] The site grew from 7.6 to 25 acres in 1980, the same year the head keeper's dwelling was restored; a visitor center opened in 1986.[^10] LighthouseFriends adds replica foghorns on the fog-signal building in 1979 and a greatly expanded visitor center in 2003.[^2] Lee Radzak, manager of the site from 1982, led the latest restoration work on the lighthouse and fog-signal building in 2008 and 2009.[^11]

## Today

Split Rock Lighthouse is a Minnesota Historical Society historic site in Split Rock Lighthouse State Park, at 3713 Split Rock Lighthouse Road, Two Harbors.[^4][^17] It is open to the public.[^8][^16] LighthouseFriends says the Minnesota Department of Natural Resources owns the property and the Society manages it.[^2] It is no longer an aid to navigation.[^8] For October 20, 2026 to January 4, 2027, the Society lists it as open Monday and Thursday through Sunday from 10 a.m. to 4 p.m., closed Tuesday and Wednesday. An $8 grounds pass does not include the lighthouse or the keeper's house; the page lists a separate Keeper's Tour, $25 with a 20 percent discount for members, limited to ten people and offered on set dates. Hours change with the season, so check the Society's page before going.[^16]

The Society says the lantern still holds the original French-built Fresnel lens, turning on its original clockwork.[^17] Each year on the evening of November 10 the lens is relit for a few hours in memory of the ore carrier Edmund Fitzgerald and her crew of 29, lost in 1975; LighthouseFriends notes the sinking happened at the far end of the lake, and that the ship had passed the working light many times.[^2] Pepper says the ceremony began in 1985, and the Society's page for the 2026 event says tickets are offered and the lighting is live-streamed.[^3][^19] LighthouseFriends says the concrete piers of the old tramway can still be seen beside the stairway down to the water.[^2]
