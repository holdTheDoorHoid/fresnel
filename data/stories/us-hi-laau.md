La‘au Point Light stands on the southwest tip of Moloka‘i.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls it the first light in the Hawaiian Islands built to mark a landfall for ships entering a channel.[^1] The 1912 Light Station Report gives 2 January 1882 as the date the station was first established.[^2]

## The place

In 1878 the American ship H. V. Carleton ran aground on the western end of Moloka‘i.[^1] The channel to Honolulu, 25 miles wide, had a light on the Oahu side, at Diamond Head, and none on the Moloka‘i side.[^1]

In 1880 the Hawaiian Legislature appropriated $2,500 for a lighthouse on the island's western side.[^1] The Lighthouse Service's 1936 history later listed the light among those "established prior to 1890."[^3]

Rudolph William Meyer chose the point, the southwesternmost tip of the island.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he thought it remote and barren, but the most visible place for a light, and he reported that it could be seen from nearly ten miles.[^1] The 1912 report gives the distance by road from Kaunakakai as "about 20 miles," over a rough, hilly and rocky road.[^2]

## Building the light

The government had planned a stone tower, but "no readily available materials could be had near the site," and Meyer had proposed wood.[^1] The 1881 contract, given to H. Hackfeld and Company of Honolulu, called for stone, and local workers gathered stones to haul to the site.[^1] By December 1881 the work was finished.[^1]

The light was a fixed white fourth-order Fresnel light, fifty feet (15 m) above the sea.[^1] A Notice to Mariners published by December 1881, as LighthouseFriends prints it, said the light could be seen from a vessel's deck in clear weather for eleven miles, and that the tower was painted white and the lantern red.[^1] Within three years Meyer and the keeper admitted that the original lamps worked poorly with the Fresnel lens, and two replacement lamps, sent in 1884 and 1885, performed poorly or leaked.[^1]

In 1902 the stone tower was rebuilt and repainted. In 1906 a 35-foot (11 m) wooden tower replaced the stone one, and stones from the old tower went into the new foundation.[^1]

The government bought the site in 1911.[^2] The 1912 report, made by A. E. Arlidge, superintendent, gives a deed dated 25 November 1911 for a 51-acre site of mountainous, rocky ground.[^2] In June 1912 the station was built anew: the old tower was destroyed, and the light was lit on 15 June 1912 from a skeleton pyramidal iron tower above a cast-iron service room.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes the 1912 tower differently, as a wood trestle tower built on the bluff behind the earlier station.[^1] The light was on acetylene gas, with an occulting pattern of one second of light, two seconds of eclipse, one second of light and six seconds of eclipse.[^2] Its gas came in four compressed-gas containers, exchanged for recharged ones about every 220 days.[^2] The Lighthouse Service's list of changes for 1912 puts the point among the lights changed from fixed to flashing or occulting.[^4]

## The keepers

John Warren Burrows was appointed keeper before the light was finished, at sixty dollars a month. He worked through December 1881 to prepare it, and his salary rose to seventy-five dollars a month in 1882.[^1] The keeper's quarters stood next to the light and served as his home.[^1] The Lighthouse Society's roster lists him as principal keeper from 1881 to 1898.[^5]

In July 1891 Burrows wrote to the Minister of the Interior, a letter that LighthouseFriends prints. He said he had been keeper for nine and a half years at $75 a month "without rations." From that he paid an assistant $25 a month, and he had to bring the oil from Kaunakakai, "20 miles each way," keeping a stock of pack animals. He asked for $100 a month. The request "was rejected, despite lobbying on his behalf by Meyer." In the same letter he wrote that "the landing is never to be depended on from one day to another."[^1]

Water was never dependable. LighthouseFriends says a reliable source was never found, and casks of water came to the point by lighter.[^1]

Burrows lived at the station with his Hawaiian wife and several children, and he served until his death in 1898.[^1] His son, John R. Burrows, "a young man of twenty-six," took over as keeper.[^1] The roster lists him from 1898 to at least 1912.[^5][^1] The roster also lists Elia H.N. Kanahele as a laborer from 1908 to at least 1912. LighthouseFriends lists him among the keepers.[^5][^1]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the station was automated in 1912, under the Lighthouse Service.[^1] In 1937 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported an "improvement of unwatched lights" in Hawaii. At La‘au Point the change was a new acetylene installation: a Dalen gas-mantle lantern with a DBKE-200 flasher, which raised the light from 390 to about 1,640 candles.[^6]

## Today

The Lighthouse Directory describes the light as active, a white flash every 2.5 seconds from a 20 ft (6 m) post with a diamond-shaped black and white daymark. The site is open, and the tower is closed.[^7] LighthouseFriends describes the light in 2022 as a flashing white light on a pole near the point, at 151 feet (46 m) above the surrounding water.[^1] The Lighthouse Society's place record gives "Existing: No" and "Active Aid to Navigation? No," which do not agree with LighthouseFriends or the Directory.[^5] The Directory gives no route to the point, and no other source read says how it is reached today.[^7]

On access, the 1912 report says the point can be landed on in calm weather, and that the shore is rough and volcanic and "very dangerous during heavy seas."[^2]
