Bush Point Light is a 20-foot (6 m) concrete pyramid on a low point on the southwest shore of Whidbey Island, near Freeland in Island County, Washington. It stands across a narrow passage from Marrowstone Island, in Admiralty Inlet.[^1][^2][^3] The Lighthouse Board set up a post light on the point in 1894.[^3] The present tower was not finished until 1933, and the Coast Guard is listed as its owner.[^1][^2]

## The place

LighthouseFriends calls the passage between Bush Point and Marrowstone Island the narrowest in North Puget Sound.[^1] The Bureau of Lighthouses' detailed estimate for the station calls the point low, with strong and irregular currents, and says serious collisions had happened between Bush Point and Point No Point in fog "on account of the echo which can be obtained from it."[^4]

Before there was a government light, the Farmer family marked the passage. LighthouseFriends says they hung a kerosene lamp from a wooden gallows each night to aid mariners.[^1]

## Building the light

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 lists a tubular-lantern post light at Bush Point, dated 10 May 1894.[^3] The station has no keeper's quarters.[^5][^6]

After the 1908 grounding of the USS *Colorado* (see below), the Lighthouse Board asked for $41,600 for new or improved lights and fog signals at six points, one of them Bush Point.[^1] The point stayed marked by a post light for years.[^1] The Shipmasters' Association of America petitioned for an aid there in September 1918, and the Bureau of Lighthouses later asked Congress for $46,000 for a light and fog-signal station at or near Bush Point.[^4] In 1930 $10,000 was allotted for an automatic acetylene light.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the tower, built of reinforced concrete, was not finished until 1933, and that the whole project cost $6,197.[^1][^6]

## The keepers

The first light was a private one, which LighthouseFriends says the Farmer family, early settlers of Bush Point, kept up.[^1] The Whidbey News-Times names John Farmer as the owner of the private lamp, a chicken and cattle farmer.[^6] Rowlett's directory says the 1933 tower replaced that private light.[^2]

From 1933 the light and fog signal were run semiautomatically, controlled from the residence of a caretaker.[^1] The sources do not name the caretaker.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 15 August 1908 the armored cruiser USS *Colorado* ran aground in fog on Puget Sound.[^1][^7] The Seattle Star reported three days later that the cruiser "ran aground during a fog on the Sound last Saturday" and was laid up for repairs.[^7] LighthouseFriends places the grounding on Liplip Point, the southeast tip of Marrowstone Island, "roughly opposite Bush Point."[^1] The Board's request for lights in the area followed.[^1]

In 1945 a fire damaged the tower and destroyed an adjacent structure, an inn built of driftwood and planking from wrecked ships.[^1][^6]

## After the keepers

The compressed-air horn was replaced by a diaphragm horn in 1945, and a modern horn followed in 1973.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the fog signal was discontinued in 1976.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard is listed as owner and manager.[^1][^2] The tower is closed to visitors.[^1][^2] Rowlett's directory says the site is open and the tower closed.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the grounds and tower are closed.[^1] The Whidbey News-Times says the light stands on private property, and that visitors may only photograph it from the road.[^6]

The sources disagree on whether the light is still in service. Rowlett's directory says it is active, and LighthouseFriends says the tower still exhibits a light.[^2][^1] The USLHS archive says the light is not an active aid.[^5] The Whidbey News-Times says the Coast Guard still maintains it.[^6]
