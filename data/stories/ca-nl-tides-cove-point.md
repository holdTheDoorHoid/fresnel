Tides Cove Point is a Canadian Coast Guard lightstation on the east side of the Burin Peninsula in Newfoundland and Labrador, about 2.5 km (1.5 mi) north-east of Fox Cove.[^3] A 2019 Fisheries and Oceans Canada list counted it among the staffed lightstations.[^5] It began in 1912 with a diaphone fog alarm, got its light in 1915, and has carried a square skeleton tower since 1992, according to Lighthouse Friends; the Lighthouse Directory gives 1993.[^1][^3]

## Building the light

The station was first known as Tide's Point. The Coast Guard's lists now call it Tides Cove Point.[^1][^2]

Its first aid to navigation was a sound.[^1] The diaphone fog alarm was worked by air compressed by an oil engine, and it sounded during thick or foggy weather. It gave two blasts of two seconds each, then a short silence, then the blasts again, and a long silence before the next pair, a cycle of about ninety seconds.[^1] In 1912 the station had a keeper's dwelling, an engine house and a store house, painted in red and white vertical stripes.[^1] A light was not established until 1915.[^1][^3] The Lighthouse Directory says the first lantern sat at one corner of a one-storey wooden fog-signal building, and that a lantern on a concrete fog-signal building replaced it later.[^3]

In 1957 S. J. Scott built a combination light and fog-signal building, with a tower standing out from one corner, and two dwellings, for $67,300.[^1]

The present tower is a square skeleton tower with an octagonal upper portion, 11.3 m (37 ft) high.[^2] Lighthouse Friends dates its erection to 1992. The Lighthouse Directory says 1993, and gives the height as 9.5 m (31 ft).[^1][^3] The Coast Guard list gives the light as a white flash every six seconds, from a focal height of 33.5 m (110 ft), with a range of 16 nautical miles. Its horn sounds a four-second blast every 60 seconds.[^2]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends names Michael Dicks as the first keeper, from 1912 to 1925. He was succeeded by his son John Dicks, who kept the station until 1949.[^1] In 1949 John Dicks was appointed a provincial superintendent of operations at St. John's, and his assistant Bob Power replaced him.[^1] Lighthouse Friends also lists John Kavanagh, without dates, and Gary Antle, until 1992.[^1]

The family lived on the station. John Dicks's daughter Irene grew up there with eight siblings. Lighthouse Friends prints an essay she wrote about it. The family kept horses, cows, goats and sheep. Everyone helped to care for the animals, cutting the hay was a team effort, and in spring, summer and autumn the whole family got the gardens ready for planting vegetables for the following winter.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The 3,872-ton British steamer *Larpool*, Master Charles Patton, was torpedoed on 2 November 1941 by the German submarine U-208. She had been in convoy ON-27, but after bad weather shortly after departure she lost contact with the convoy during the night of 28/29 October.[^4] Lighthouse Friends puts the attack about 250 miles east-south-east of Cape Race. uboat.net puts it about 150 miles.[^1][^4] uboat.net counts 43 people on board, of whom 26 died and 17 survived.[^4]

Lighthouse Friends says five men from one of the ship's lifeboats drifted for eleven days before reaching the shore at Tide's Cove, just north of the station, on 13 November 1941.[^1] In Irene's account, as Lighthouse Friends prints it, she was playing on the station grounds in the late afternoon when she noticed a small boat offshore near the edge of a fog bank. A bright light went up above the boat. She ran in to fetch her father, the keeper, who recognised the craft as a lifeboat and signalled it to make its way to Tide's Cove. After finding his assistant, he went there himself. As the keepers lifted five exhausted men from the lifeboat, they learned that the men had been aboard the Larpool.[^1]

uboat.net gives a different account. It says a boat of six men made landfall near Burin on 10 November. In a third boat, four men died of exposure before HMCS *Bittersweet* picked up ten crew and a gunner after thirteen days at sea and landed them at Halifax.[^4] Lighthouse Friends gives the same eleven survivors, picked up by a Canadian corvette and landed at Halifax.[^1] The two accounts agree on the sinking and on the eleven who reached Halifax. They disagree on the date, the place, and how many men came ashore from the lifeboat.[^1][^4]

Lighthouse Friends says the three weakest of the five men were taken to Burin Cottage Hospital and recovered after several weeks of care. It adds that in gratitude for the help, the captain, Charles Patton, gave Irene a first-aid kit from the ship.[^1]

## Today

The station was de-staffed and automated in 1992 when the new tower went up. It stood unwatched for several years and was re-staffed in 2003.[^1]

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the station.[^1][^3] A Fisheries and Oceans Canada page, last modified in September 2019, lists Tides Cove Point among the 23 staffed lightstations in Newfoundland and Labrador.[^5] On that list the station is a working home as well as a light. Lighthouse Friends and the Lighthouse Directory both say the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1][^3] Keep to the grounds the sources describe as open, and do not climb the tower.
