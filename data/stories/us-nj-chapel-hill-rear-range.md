Chapel Hill Rear Range Light was a wooden keepers' house with a light turret on its pitched roof, on a hill above Sandy Hook Bay in Monmouth County, New Jersey.[^1][^2] As the back light of a pair of range lights, it helped ships find Chapel Hill Channel from 1856 until 1957.[^1][^2] It is now a private house at the end of a long driveway.[^2]

## The place

Ships coming into New York harbor had a difficult corner to round.[^3] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1892 describes a sharp right-angle turn in the main ship channel. A heavy-draft vessel had to make a detour, first with a starboard helm past the northern part of Sandy Hook and then with a port helm to reach the entrance of Chapel Hill Channel.[^3]

At night the passage between the buoys was about 900 feet wide.[^3] The report calls it "an exceedingly delicate and dextrous piece of seamanship" for a vessel drawing over 20 feet.[^3] The two range lights were this one and Conover Beacon, about 1.5 miles to the north on the beach at Leonardo.[^2] Lined up in a right line, they marked the entrance.[^3] The report also notes that they were "at times more or less obscured by mist or smoke."[^3]

LighthouseFriends says the neighborhood was first called High Point.[^2] Its present name comes from a small Baptist church built on the hill in 1809.[^2]

## Building the light

The government bought three acres on the hill from Timothy Mount in 1853.[^2] Three years later the lighthouse was built.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1856 as the year the tower was established, and lists the tower as wood on a brick foundation.[^1]

LighthouseFriends credits a builder, Richard Carlow Jr., with several structures used to display range lights for vessels entering New York Harbor.[^2] It describes Chapel Hill as one of three similar buildings. The other two stood at Point Comfort in Keansburg and at New Dorp on Staten Island.[^2] The original optic was a range lens, installed in 1856.[^1]

The tower was 40 feet (12 m) from its base to the center of the lantern.[^1] The two sources give different focal heights.[^1][^2] The US Lighthouse Society's record puts the light 222 feet (67.7 m) above the water.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives a focal plane of 224 feet (68.3 m) above the bay.[^2] This story uses the first figure, and the sources do not explain the two-foot gap.

The house and tower were painted white.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the paint made the light hard to pick out in snowy winters.[^2] In 1874 two black screens, each about 25 feet (7.6 m) high and 40 feet (12 m) long, were set up on either side of the lighthouse to help with that problem.[^2]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1895 records that an oil house was built at the station.[^4] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1897 lists the Chapel Hill Range Cut channel buoys.[^5] A gas-lighted buoy went in on December 26, 1896.[^5] The same report says the buoys of that channel and of two others were lit by electricity through submarine cables from Sandy Hook.[^5] These were buoys, and the report does not link them to the house.[^5]

On January 20, 1907 the illuminant was changed from mineral oil to incandescent oil vapor, and LighthouseFriends says the light's intensity went up.[^2]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists nineteen people for the station.[^1] Eighteen were principal keepers, and one was a first assistant.[^1]

John Morrison was the first keeper, from 1855 to 1858.[^1] That start is a year before the tower's 1856 date, and the sources do not explain the gap.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says he received $400 a year.[^2] Charles Carhart took charge in 1869.[^2] LighthouseFriends says his thirty years at the station were longer than any other keeper's.[^1][^2]

Between 1899 and 1910 six people held the principal keeper's post.[^1] Two of them, John Dahlman and John W. Burke, are listed for a single year each.[^1] Jennie (Virginia) Porter kept the light from 1910 to 1911.[^1] She is the only woman the roster lists as principal keeper.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls her Jennie Porter.[^2]

Peter M. Peterson held the post for eighteen years, from 1916 to 1934.[^1] That is the longest tenure after Carhart's.[^1] The later keepers were Frederick W. Rich (1934 to 1938), Carl F.W. Anderson (1938 to at least 1941), Richard Allen Norton (1943 to 1952), Thomas E. Rasmussen (1952 to 1954) and John J. Chilly, listed from 1954.[^1] Domenic M. Turillo is listed as first assistant from at least 1950.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In a gale in 1871 the station's outbuildings were blown down.[^2] LighthouseFriends says they were rebuilt quickly.[^2]

During the Spanish-American War the light was put out as a safety measure from April 28 to August 1, 1898.[^2] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1899 lists the light as discontinued as a war measure on those dates.[^6]

## After the keepers

The house served until 1957.[^2] That year a beacon on a steel tower about 200 feet (61 m) to its north replaced it.[^2]

In 1959 the General Services Administration auctioned the obsolete lighthouse.[^2] A Manhattan businessman won it with a bid of $16,313.[^2] He bought it as a Christmas present for his son, an amateur astronomer, to use as an observatory.[^2]

The house has had several owners since then. An addition was placed on its eastern end, and decorative railings went up on the front porch.[^2] LighthouseFriends says it remains in excellent condition.[^2]

## Today

Chapel Hill Rear Range Light is a private residence, and it is not open to the public.[^1] Its grounds, dwelling and tower are closed.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends notes that the long driveway makes the house hard to make out.[^2] It asks visitors to respect the owner's privacy.[^2]

The lens formerly used here, a Barbier and Fenestre lens, can be seen at Navesink Lighthouse, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2]
