Manistique East Breakwater Light stands at the outer end of the east breakwater at the mouth of the Manistique River, where the river meets Lake Michigan, in Schoolcraft County, Michigan. Lights have been shown from the breakwater since a temporary red light was hung there in 1912, and the steel tower standing today dates from 1916. Its keepers left in 1969, and LighthouseFriends reports that the tower and the keepers' dwelling are privately owned.[^1][^2][^9]

## The place

The town of Manistique grew up at the river mouth around a lumber mill.[^2] In its 1892 request for $32,000, the Lighthouse Board argued for a light at Pointe aux Barques and wrote that the town "has a large lumber trade and many vessels call at that port." LighthouseFriends quotes the passage.[^2] The harbor itself was made by private and federal work. A breakwater built by a lumber company was taken over by the government, and between 1910 and 1915 "two breakwaters were built at the mouth of the river," enclosing a basin of 31 acres.[^2]

## Building the light

The Board's 1892 request was for a light on Pointe aux Barques, a headland on the Garden Peninsula farther along the shore. LighthouseFriends says that light was authorized but never funded.[^2] The Board's 1893 report, however, records a coast light and fog signal "here," at Manistique, authorized by the act of February 15, 1893 "at a cost not to exceed $32,000," with "no appropriation therefor has yet been made."[^6] The 1895 report repeats that wording.[^7] The 1899 report files the same unfunded recommendation under "Pointe aux Barques (Manistique)."[^8] The reports do not say whether the 1893 authorization and the Pointe aux Barques request were the same proposal, and this story does not assume that they were.

A temporary light came first. LighthouseFriends says that in 1912 a temporary fixed red light of forty-five candlepower was suspended from a pole to mark the west end of the newly completed east breakwater.[^2] That year the Bureau of Lighthouses asked for $20,000 for a permanent light, fog signal and keepers' dwelling. Congress provided the amount on October 22, 1913, and the Lighthouse Service's bulletin lists it as aids to navigation in Manistique Harbor.[^2][^3]

The bulletin's next entry, for 1914, lists "Manistique Harbor, Mich." among lights established, with two lens lanterns, one burning oil and one acetylene.[^4] LighthouseFriends says a 25-foot (7.6 m) steel skeletal tower with an acetylene light was established on the west breakwater on October 30, 1914.[^2] The east breakwater light moved into its new structure on August 17, 1916.[^2][^1]

The Lighthouse Service's own description of the tower, quoted by LighthouseFriends, calls it a square, pyramidal steel tower with the fog-signal machinery on its main floor and a diaphone on the third. The keepers' duplex was built of hollow tile with a stucco exterior.[^2] The first optic was a 300-millimeter lens lantern showing a fixed red light, and LighthouseFriends says a fourth-order lens replaced it in 1918. The Lighthouse Society's record gives a fourth-order lens as the original optic in 1916.[^2][^1] The sources disagree on the date.

## The keepers

The roster has thirty-nine lines, from 1914 to 1969, including Coast Guard crew under a separate heading from 1959.[^1]

Charles E. Corlett was the first head keeper, from 1915 to 1920. LighthouseFriends says he had been master of the North Manitou Shoal lightship before coming here.[^2] On January 11, 1918, the Secretary commended Corlett and his assistant, William Renier, for valuable assistance, as the Lighthouse Service Bulletin prints the notice. The notice describes no rescue.[^5] Corlett died in 1920, and Walter Ottosen came from Pilot Island in Wisconsin to take the post.[^2] Ottosen stayed until 1941. LighthouseFriends says he was awarded a star in 1937 for high efficiency in the previous year.[^2]

Later heads of the station included Walter Hanson, William A. Keller and Peter B. Scott, and Thomas J. Brennan, who served as second assistant and first assistant before heading the station for a year.[^1] The last head keeper was Gustav A. Pfister, from 1968 to 1969.[^1]

The sources name no woman who kept the light in her own right.

## After the keepers

A radiobeacon was established at the station at the opening of navigation in 1931.[^2] The railroad and its car ferry at Manistique stopped running in 1968. LighthouseFriends says the light was automated the following year and that the station was officially disestablished as a manned unit on August 30, 1969. The keepers' dwelling was sold, and the fourth-order lens was removed from the tower. It can currently be seen at the Wisconsin Maritime Museum in Manitowoc.[^2]

The National Register's asset record for the light is dated 2005 and places it "at offshore end of east breakwater, approx. 1,800 ft. from shore."[^9] In 2012 the federal government made the light available under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, and the Manistique city council voted to work with a conservancy to acquire it. Those plans fell apart. The General Services Administration opened an online auction in June 2013, and the only bid, $15,000, came from a private buyer in Ohio.[^2]

## Today

LighthouseFriends reports that the light and its keepers' dwelling are privately owned, that the grounds are open, and that the dwelling and tower are closed.[^2] It directs visitors to the boardwalk from Lakeview Park, which runs out to the east breakwater where the light stands.[^2] The keepers' dwelling stands on Range Street.[^2] The sources read here date the sale to 2013, so check the current owner and access before going. Keep to the walk, and do not try to enter the closed tower or dwelling.
