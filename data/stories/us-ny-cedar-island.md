Cedar Island Light stands at the end of a mile-and-a-half (2.4 km) peninsula in Cedar Point County Park, near East Hampton on the South Fork of Long Island.[^1] It guards the approach to Sag Harbor.[^1] The granite tower standing today dates from 1868, and the light stopped being a staffed station in 1934.[^1][^2] Suffolk County owns the lighthouse, which is closed to visitors while it is restored.[^1][^2][^10]

## The place

Sag Harbor was a whaling port in the early 1800s, and its fleet had begun placing stake lights around the harbor as early as 1810.[^1] The federal government bought Cedar Island from the town of East Hampton in 1838 for a new lighthouse. The island lay in the passage between the South Fork and Shelter Island, which made it hazardous for ships, and the light was meant to guide whalers and other vessels into Northwest Bay and Sag Harbor.[^1]

The island was about two acres in 1850.[^1] Erosion cut that by half in less than seventy years, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] Riprap went in when the first light was built, and between 1903 and 1906 another 6,600 tons of rock were added.[^1] A hurricane in 1938 filled the 200-yard (183 m) gap between the island and the shore, and the island became the peninsula known today as Cedar Point.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress provided $1,000 in 1837 and $2,500 in 1838, and the first light was built in 1839. It was a one-and-a-half-story keeper's dwelling with a small tower on its roof.[^1] Nine Winslow Lewis lamps with fourteen-inch reflectors shone 32 feet (9.8 m) above the sea, with a range of 12.5 nautical miles.[^1] A sixth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps in 1855, as the USLHS record and LighthouseFriends both give.[^1][^2]

By 1858 the wooden dwelling was struggling to hold up its cast-iron lantern. Congress approved $25,000 in 1867 to rebuild the station, and the new light went up in 1868 under W. & G. Beattie of Fall River.[^1][^2] It has a granite tower on a circular granite pier and an L-shaped dwelling.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes the dwelling in the Victorian Gothic style and links that style to the Roundout, Stuyvesant and Coxsackie lights, built about the same time; the National Register record lists the style as Italianate.[^1][^2][^3] The 1855 lens moved into the new tower, where it shone 44 feet (13.4 m) above the water. A machine-operated fog bell was added in 1882.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

The USLHS roster has 18 entries for 17 principal keepers, from Frederick King (1839–1841) to William Henry Follett (1917–1934).[^2] Hubbard L. Fordham appears twice, first from 1849 to 1853 and again from 1862 to 1869.[^2]

The roster lists Mary Edwards as principal keeper in 1862, after Nathaniel Edwards (1861–1862). The record does not say how the two were related, and no source used here confirms a family connection.[^2]

Charles I. Mulford was appointed keeper in 1897.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes him as a Civil War veteran who wore a peg leg after an injury in the war.[^1] The roster has him at the station until 1906.[^2]

William Henry Follett was the station's last keeper, from 1917 to 1934. LighthouseFriends says he had earlier been an assistant keeper at Montauk Point and at Hog Island Shoal in Rhode Island.[^1] His wife, Atta, lived at the station with him, and their grandchildren came to stay for long visits in the summer.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 13 July 1919 Follett went to the aid of three men who had been fatally burned in an explosion on the pleasure yacht *Flyer*. He carried them in his power boat to Sag Harbor and had them taken to a hospital.[^7] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin records the rescue, and LighthouseFriends says none of the three survived.[^7][^1]

The Light-House Board's reports for the 1890s record routine repairs. In the report for 1891–92 the boathouse was moved and overhauled, and a drive well was put down.[^4] In the report for 1893–94 a set of boatways was built.[^5] In the 1899 report a set of fifth-order lamps was furnished and fitted, and various repairs were made.[^6]

## After the keepers

The station was automated in 1934. An automated light on a skeletal tower replaced the lighthouse, and Follett was reassigned to Long Beach Bar Lighthouse, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1][^2]

In 1937 the government put the lighthouse up for sale, and Phelan Beale bought it for $2,002. Beale planned to turn it into a hunting lodge for his nearby game preserve, but the plan was never carried out.[^1] A later private owner used it as a vacation house. Suffolk County bought the property and added it to Cedar Point County Park.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives 1967 for that purchase; the Long Island Parks news gives 1974.[^1][^10]

A fire in 1974 badly damaged the empty building. The roof was replaced afterwards, the windows were bricked over, and the burned interior was left as it was.[^1][^8]

The National Register record for the lighthouse gives 18 April 2003 as its date published.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the listing came in 2002, after a joint effort by the county and the Long Island chapter of the US Lighthouse Society.[^1][^3] The 1902 oil house was restored in 2004.[^1]

On 31 October 2013 the four-and-a-half-ton lantern room was lifted off the tower and barged to the Sag Harbor Yacht Yard for restoration.[^1] The lantern was restored in 2014, and the restoration effort went on through appropriations of $300,000 for a new roof in 2016, $500,000 for the roof, exterior and foundation pier in 2017, and $4 million appropriated as of fiscal 2023.[^8][^1]

In March 2026 the Suffolk County committee approved $1 million for the next phase of restoration. That phase is to stabilize the main structure and install interior stairwells and observation platforms, which would allow visitors to reach the tower safely in the future.[^10]

## Today

The lighthouse belongs to Suffolk County and sits inside Cedar Point County Park. The grounds are open, but the dwelling and the tower are closed.[^1] By late July 2026 Dan's Papers reported that the exterior restoration was nearing completion, and that interior work had so far focused on stabilizing the structure.[^11] Friends of the Cedar Island Lighthouse, a newly formed nonprofit, aims to turn the restored building into a public destination for history and education.[^11] The Friends are a 501(c)(3) nonprofit that raises money for the restoration.[^9]

A public ceremony for the lantern's return was set for 1 August 2026, at the Sag Harbor Yacht Club. The lantern was then to be restored and reinstalled on the tower.[^11] The sources do not say whether that reinstallation has happened, so check before visiting. The building is closed.[^1] The work area should be left alone.
