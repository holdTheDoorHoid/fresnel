Stepping Stones Light is a square red-brick keeper's dwelling with a mansard roof, joined to a square tower, on a granite platform in western Long Island Sound.[^1] It stands about 1,600 yards offshore from Elm Point, Kings Point, on the reef that gives it its name.[^1] The light was first shown on 1 March 1877, and it still guards the approach to New York City's East River.[^1]

## The place

The reef is the hazard. The town historian's fact sheet says the light "Warns ships and boats of an extensive shoal and series of rocks which at times break the surface of the water and extend northward from Kings Point," and that "It takes its name from these rocks."[^1] The sheet calls it "a major hazard in western Long Island Sound" and says the light "sits on the northernmost reef."[^1]

The sheet also gives the older names. It repeats a Siwanoy legend in which a defeated Evil Spirit, pushed back toward City Island, "promptly “stepped” across these “stones” and fled to Long Island."[^1] It says that in the 1770s Long Island Sound was "known as “Devil’s Belt” due to the sudden storms and nor’easters," and that the name "Devil's Stepping Stones" "continued until 1877," when the new light shortened it.[^1] The sheet gives these as legend and naming history, not as dated record.

## Building the light

Money for the station was first set aside in June 1866.[^1] The sheet says "$6,000 was first allocated in June 1866," for a day beacon on the reef and a light near Hart Island.[^1] "When the property on Hart Island became difficult to obtain," the light was moved to Stepping Stones.[^1]

Construction began in late 1875 under A. D. Cook, with Irish bargemen and stonemasons from Throgs Neck.[^1] The foundation of the tower "was built directly over a large rock which just broke the surface at low tide," and "A total of 900 tons of boulders was barged to the site between 1875 and 1878."[^1] The dwelling was finished in January 1877, "although the year 1876 is carved on the granite panel of the tower."[^1]

The first lens was a fifth-order Fresnel "showing a fixed red light."[^1][^2] The Board's 1895 report records that "Some 300 tons of riprap stone were placed for the protection of the pier."[^3] In 1932 the light was changed to a fourth-order lens with a fixed green light.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive's roster names Finlay Fraser as the first principal keeper, "Finlay Fraser (1877-1879)."[^2] The town's sheet spells the name "Findlay Fraser."[^1]

After Fraser came James G. Scott, William McGloin, Cornelius Douglass, Elmer E. Gildersleeve and Charles Redfern, each serving between one and nine years, until Ernest Bloom arrived in 1910.[^2] Ernest Bloom started on 20 April 1910, the sheet says, and "was awarded the Lighthouse Service's efficiency pennant for the meticulous manner in which he maintained the lighthouse."[^1] The Bureau's 1912 list of pennants includes Stepping Stones with Bloom's name, for stations "showing highest general efficiency during the fiscal year 1912."[^4] The sheet's notes on Bloom, Holm and the 1934 winter are headed "From the United States Coast Guard Website"; the sheet gives no address for that page.[^1]

Edward M. Grant (1915 to 1917) and John E. Bergen (1917 to 1921) followed Bloom.[^2] Stephen Holm was keeper from 1921 to 1924, and his two rescues are told below.[^2] The roster lists Charles A. Rogers as keeper from 1924 to 1934, and it goes on through Edward T. Pastorini and Arthur M. Bettencourt in the 1930s and 1940s.[^2] The roster also has entries under a "USCG" heading for 1959 and 1960.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The 1921 Bulletin commended Stephen Holm, keeper of Stepping Stones, for rescuing from drowning two men whose canoe had capsized near the light station on 28 July.[^5] The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1922 says Holm "rescued two men whose canoe had capsized and landed the men and the canoe in City Island."[^8]

On 18 July 1923 Holm rescued two men from the sailboat *Mistral*, which had gone onto the rocks east of the light station and was sinking.[^6] The Bulletin says the keeper also got the boat off the rocks and towed it to a beach on Long Island.[^6] The town's sheet tells the same event this way: "two men ran the sailboat Mistral onto the rocks just east of the lighthouse."[^1]

The winter of 1934 is documented twice. The Bulletin's account is short. It reports "An exceptionally long period of cold," with ice that kept the keeper from reaching shore, and says he hoisted distress signals on 1 March.[^7] A small tanker bound for New York "was able to get close" to the station and hail the keeper. He asked that the superintendent of lighthouses at Staten Island be notified that he was "running short of food," although he "had sufficient for another day or two." The same day, the Bulletin says, the tender Hickory was "dispatched to deliver a food supply to" him.[^7]

The town's sheet gives more detail, and a different vessel. "On the morning of February 9, 1934, the mercury at Stepping Stones Lighthouse hit 14 degrees below zero," and "With the sound frozen, Keeper Charles A. Rogers, could not row ashore for supplies." On 20 February "the wind blew in a blizzard, which dumped 17 inches of snow overnight, the worst storm since 1888." Rogers was "Trapped and with only two days worth of food for his small family," and he "hung the flag upside down" on 1 March. The sheet names "Captain Sioss of the tug Muxpet" as the man who spotted the signal and broke through the ice.[^1] Rogers "refused" food, "stating that it was the Lighthouse Service's responsibility," and asked that the depot at St. George be told.[^1] The sheet says the depot sent the tender Hickory with supplies.[^1] The Bulletin says a tanker; the sheet says a tug. The two accounts also differ on the food. The sources do not settle which vessel reached him, or how much food he had.

## After the keepers

The station was modernized in 1944, and it was automated in October 1966.[^1] The automation put a modern optic with a flashing fixed green light into the lantern room.[^1]

The station was added to the National Register of Historic Places on 15 September 2005.[^1] In 2006 the Coast Guard "deemed as surplus" the station and offered it at no cost to eligible entities, among them government agencies and nonprofit corporations.[^1] "Eventually all suitors except the Town of North Hempstead withdrew their applications," the sheet says.[^1]

## Today

The town took ownership in 2008, and the Coast Guard still maintains the beacon.[^1] In 2012 "the Stepping Stones Lighthouse Preservation Society was established" with the town's help.[^1] On 1 July 2012 town staff and Society members "took an excursion to evaluate the conditions outside and inside the lighthouse."[^9]

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record lists the light as an active aid to navigation and says it is not open to the public.[^2] The town's photo captions say the light "can be seen from the Throgs Neck Bridge."[^9] It stands 1,600 yards offshore, so the only approach is by water, and the sources give no public access to the tower.
