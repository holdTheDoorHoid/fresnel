Ned's Point Light stands at the northeast end of Mattapoisett Harbor, on Buzzards Bay in Plymouth County, Massachusetts.[^1][^3] Its stone tower was built in 1837 and first showed its light in March 1838.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends calls it one of the shortest working lighthouses left on Buzzards Bay.[^2]

## The place

Mattapoisett was a shipbuilding and whaling town. LighthouseFriends says some 400 ships were built in its yards between 1740 and the 1870s.[^2] The 1981 National Register form says that Mattapoisett, a busy point in the mid-nineteenth century, "tended to be frozen over for long periods."[^1] The point takes its name from a former owner of the land, Edwin "Ned" Dexter.[^2]

## Building the light

In 1837, after John Quincy Adams helped convince Congress to approve $5,000 for the project, Ned's Point became home to a lighthouse, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The government paid $240 for the four-acre site, bought from H.H. Edwards through his guardian Barnabus Hiller. The National Register form dates the sale to 18 August 1837.[^2][^1]

Leonard Hammond, a local businessman, took the contract for the tower and keeper's dwelling. The register form says he built the tower and its lantern for $4,500. LighthouseFriends says Congress approved $5,000 for the project.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends also tells how the crew, with the work behind schedule, had balanced wooden planks over barrels for the tower's floor. When an inspector came to town, one board slid out from under his foot and sent him down into the foundation, though he was not hurt.[^2]

The 35-foot (11 m) tower was built of beach rubble, with a cast-iron lantern on top. Its granite stair was built without mortar, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The first light had eleven lamps. The 1981 form gives their reflectors as 13 1/3 inches, and LighthouseFriends, quoting the 1838 inspector, as thirteen and a half.[^1][^2] The Lighthouse Board's report for 1891-1892 records that a covered way was built from the dwelling to the tower.[^4]

The original fifth-order Fresnel lens went in 1856, according to LighthouseFriends. The US Lighthouse Society gives 1888 as the year the original lens was installed, so the two sources disagree.[^2][^3]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists eleven principal-keeper stints, held by ten people, from George Braley in 1837 to Russell B. Eastman, whose stint ends in 1923.[^3] George Braley kept the light from 1837 to 1846. The roster then lists Hannah Braley from 1846 to 1849.[^3]

Larnet Hall, Jr., whom LighthouseFriends calls a former stage coach driver, held the post in two stints. The roster gives 1849 to 1853 and 1859 to 1874, and LighthouseFriends says the first stint began in 1848. John Bumpus kept the light between Hall's two stints, from 1853 to 1859.[^3][^2]

George H. Kelly followed, from 1874 to 1892, then Francis E. Coffin, from 1892 to 1895, and William P. Howard, from 1895 to 1912.[^3]

Zimri T. Robinson, known as "Uncle Toby," came to Ned's Point in 1912 after seventeen years as keeper of Bird Island Light, LighthouseFriends says.[^2][^3] After he had a stroke, Jerry T. Robinson did most of the work until Toby died in 1914.[^2] Russell B. Eastman was appointed to replace him, and was the final keeper before the light was automated.[^2][^3]

LighthouseFriends describes the keepers' families. The wives often kept vegetable plots and animals to help feed the family, and when a keeper fell ill his wife often took over his work.[^2] In winter, keepers fetched whale oil from the oil house, sometimes in gales, warmed it by the stove on the ground floor, and carried it up thirty-two stone steps to the lantern.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1838 Lieutenant Edward Carpender inspected the light. As LighthouseFriends prints his report, the keeper told him "in the late storm, both buildings leaked in all directions." He removed the surface of the mortar in several places and found the stone "laid in what appeared to be little more than mere sand." He found the lantern only five feet eight inches (1.7 m) high, "which is too low, again, for the convenience of the keeper with his hat on."[^2]

An 1850 inspection found everything "in first-rate order and neat as a pin." The following year the lighthouse was described as badly built and leaky.[^2] The National Register chronology records that a sea wall was destroyed in 1869.[^1]

In 1888 a hurricane nearly destroyed the station. LighthouseFriends says that afterwards the original stone dwelling was pulled down, and a frame house was put up on its foundation.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1923 the light was fully automated. LighthouseFriends says the keeper's dwelling went across Buzzards Bay on the scow Eva and became quarters for the assistant at Wing's Neck.[^2] The register form gives 1920 for the move in its chronology, and 1930 in its description of the site.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1918 to 1923 lists a "change in illuminant" at Ned Point, and its 1923 issue has a Ned Point entry.[^5][^6]

LighthouseFriends, which lists two 1937 newspaper reports among its sources, says that in 1937 the lighthouse was put up for sale "to the lowest bidder." The original order had said "highest," and somewhere between Washington and Massachusetts the word was changed.[^2] James Stowell of Mattapoisett sent in a bid of one cent, and sixteen people bid in all. The highest offer was $3,500, and all the bids were rejected, because the government needed at least half of the property's assessed value of $19,050.[^2]

The Coast Guard decommissioned the light in 1952. In 1958 the grounds, except the tower and its right of way, went to the Town of Mattapoisett as a public park.[^2][^1] The light came back in 1961 with a new lens showing a six-second isophase: three seconds of white, then three of darkness.[^2]

In 1993 the local Coast Guard Auxiliary Flotilla adopted the light and renovated the tower in 1995 and 1996.[^2] In the summer of 2020, four auxiliary members who had been looking after the light formed the Friends of Ned's Point Lighthouse to raise money for its restoration. They began by removing lead paint from the inside. The tower had been shut since 2019 because of lead contamination and reopened in 2021.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and the grounds are a town park.[^2][^1] A 1 June 2026 notice in the Sippican newspaper gives the summer's tour days, from Saturday 20 June to 1 October. The dates may change with the weather or at the discretion of the light keeper.[^7] On tour days the tower is open to visitors.[^2]

The US Lighthouse Society lists the light as an active aid to navigation, with a 250 mm optic.[^3]
