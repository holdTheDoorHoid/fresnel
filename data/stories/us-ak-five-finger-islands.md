A white concrete lighthouse sits on a 2.9-acre island at the entrance to Stephens Passage, between Juneau and Petersburg, in the Inside Passage waterway of Southeast Alaska.[^1] It and Sentinel Island Light Station began work on the same day, 1 March 1902, and were the first lighthouses the United States built in Alaska.[^1] The concrete building is a replacement: fire destroyed the wooden original in December 1933.[^1][^4]

## The place

The Five Fingers are a group of small islands in Stephens Passage, which the National Register nomination places about 37 miles (60 km) northwest of Petersburg.[^1] The light stands at the north end of the southernmost island.[^1] The nomination ties the station to the gold rush that followed the discovery of gold on the upper Yukon River's tributaries, which sent steamers north through the Inside Passage. Large steamers overloaded with prospectors and freight found a route where, it says, "Fog, rain, strong tides, and a rocky shoreline" made the passage particularly difficult, and over three hundred accidents in those waters were reported in 1898.[^1]

## Building the light

In November 1900 the inspector and engineer of the Thirteenth Light-House District sent in a plan for spending the $100,000 Congress had voted for Alaska, and Southeast Five Finger Island headed the list, with an estimate of $32,900.[^2] The Light-House Board ordered plans for it and for Sentinel Island that December.[^2] Bids were opened on 6 June 1901, and the lowest for Five Finger, $22,500, was accepted.[^2] The National Register nomination presents the $22,500 as the sum Congress appropriated for the light; the Board's own report shows it was the winning bid, against a higher estimate.[^1][^2]

The first station was a square light tower on a two-storey wooden dwelling, with a Daboll trumpet fog signal in the same building.[^1] The tower showed a fourth-order fixed white light 68 feet (21 m) above mean high water.[^1] The station's light and fog signal were in service on 1 March 1902.[^1] George R. Putnam's 1917 history of the service likewise dates the first regular light stations in Alaska to that day, at Southeast Five Finger Island and Sentinel Island.[^7] Lighthouse Friends adds that the buildings were finished in late January 1902 and the light and fog signal installed the following month.[^13] The same year saw a boathouse and a carpenter and paint shop built, and both still stand.[^1]

The Light-House Service later added a radio beacon, in late 1931, the sixth in Alaska.[^1] By 1936 the beacon and an air diaphone fog signal were synchronized, so a pilot could time the gap between the two sounds and divide it by five to estimate his distance from the station in miles.[^1] The Bulletin of 1918 lists the light as changed that year from a fixed white light burning oil to a flashing white light burning acetylene.[^12]

## The keepers

The station was originally staffed by a head keeper and two assistants.[^13] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists Hermann Grossheim as both principal and first assistant in 1902, then a quick run of head keepers, and Lighthouse Friends notes that most keepers stayed only a season or two.[^3][^13]

The long exception was Benny M. Hall. The archive has him as second assistant in 1906-1907, first assistant in 1907-1909, and head keeper from 1909 to 1924.[^3] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index records an efficiency pennant for Hall's station in 1912-17 and an efficiency flag again in 1918-23; Lighthouse Friends says he twice won the pennant for the best-kept station in the district.[^9][^10][^13] Alexander Wiebe is listed by the archive as an assistant in several stints between 1909 and 1924, overlapping Hall's years as head keeper.[^3] Charles P. Mercer, head keeper 1924 to 1929 with a break in 1927, also received an efficiency flag.[^3][^11]

Nicholas Kashevaroff was head keeper in the late 1930s.[^3] The archive gives 1936 to 1938 and Lighthouse Friends 1936 to 1939.[^3][^13] Lighthouse Friends says he grew up on Kodiak Island and had served more than twenty years at remote Alaskan stations.[^13] After the Coast Guard took over all U.S. lighthouses in 1939, a four-man crew was stationed here.[^13] After Oscar Lindberg's stint ending in 1944, the archive's roster names only Sidney Kindler (1951-1952) until Coast Guard crews appear from 1959 to 1971, and then again in 1983-84.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the winter of 1902-03 the station ran short of food. The Skagway *Daily Morning Alaskan* of 8 February 1903 reported that Captain Goss of the steamer *Amur* was signalled as he came abreast of the island. The keeper rowed out with one assistant in a small boat with a single pair of oars. He said the station was out of provisions and that one of the three men had left more than three months before for supplies and had not been heard from since.[^18] The *Amur* gave them sugar, tobacco and fresh vegetables, and the captain held the steamer in the rough sea until the two were back in the lee of the island.[^18] Lighthouse Friends gives a fuller version, dated 6 January 1903, with the keepers living for a week on flour and fish; the newspaper page names neither date nor diet, and has the missing man gone more than three months, where Lighthouse Friends says one month.[^13][^18]

On the night of 19 May 1927 a fire destroyed the station's boathouse. The Bulletin credited Keeper S. Olsen, First Assistant W. S. Hamilton and Second Assistant Dan Kinnaley, together with a foreman and an employee from the Ketchikan depot, with prompt and efficient service in connection with the fire.[^5] The National Register nomination calls the surviving boathouse one of the two original 1902 buildings, so a boathouse was either rebuilt or the two sources describe different structures.[^1][^5]

In 1933 the Commissioner of Lighthouses commended Keeper Oscar Lindberg, First Assistant Oscar Bernhard and Second Assistant C. Eltman for rescuing two fishermen from an isolated part of the coast, giving them food and clothing; their boat had been found sinking 4 miles off the shore.[^6]

The worst night was 8 December 1933. The Bulletin says fire destroyed the station, a wooden combined dwelling and tower built in 1902, together with the radio beacon building.[^4] The tender *Cedar* was off the station landing supplies, and her crew helped the keepers save the boathouse and carpenter shop.[^1][^4] Lighthouse Friends says a cold spell had frozen the water pipes, and two keepers tried to thaw them with a blowtorch. The wall behind the pipe caught fire, and with the water supply frozen the lighthouse burned to the ground.[^13] The Bulletin reported a temporary unwatched light was set up, while the radio beacon and fog signal were to remain out of commission through the winter.[^4]

Lighthouse Friends reports that the 1982 winter crew, four Coast Guardsmen led by an officer in charge, went for a couple of weeks without their weekly helicopter delivery of food, mail and movies from Sitka. In that spell the station was battered by high swells and winds of 85 miles an hour, and ice encased the building, trapping the crew inside for days.[^13]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service rebuilt in reinforced concrete between 1934 and 1935, at a cost of $92,996.10 according to the nomination, with new quarters for four, a light and fog signal building and a new hoist house, ten feet east of the old site.[^1][^8] Lighthouse Friends gives the date it was put in commission as 16 December 1935.[^13]

A 1979 Notice to Mariners announced that the fog horn had been permanently discontinued.[^1] The Coast Guard removed its keepers in 1984, and the nomination calls Five Finger the last station in Alaska to be automated and unmanned.[^1] The last four-man crew left on 14 August 1984.[^13] The names of the last full-year crew are still painted on a wall in what was the root cellar.[^14]

The Juneau Lighthouse Association took a lease from the Coast Guard in 1997 and became the owner in 2004.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the General Services Administration deeded the property in January 2004; the station was listed on the National Register the same year.[^13] In 2011 the Alaska Whale Foundation spent five weeks here monitoring humpback whales.[^13] In 2020 the group running the station was reorganized as the Five Finger Lighthouse Society and moved from Juneau to Petersburg.[^13]

## Today

Lighthouse Friends gives the Society as the owner, and the Coast Guard still services the light, which flashes white every ten seconds from a solar-powered optic.[^1][^3][^13][^14] It also carries a NOAA weather station.[^14] In October 2026 NOAA technicians replaced the tower and instruments, and the Society's volunteers hauled the gear and technicians to and from the island.[^14][^15] The island is a volunteer-run site with no overnight moorage, and the weather has to be calm to reach it by boat or plane.[^14] The Society describes the trip from Petersburg as a four-hour boat ride and says the lighthouse is typically open from late May to early September; that page is undated, so anyone planning a visit should check with the Society first.[^16] The Society also notes that the red-roofed boathouse and carpenter shed survived the 1933 fire and still stand.[^17]
