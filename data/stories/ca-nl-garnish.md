Garnish Light stands on the quay at Garnish, a harbour village on the west side of the Burin Peninsula in Newfoundland.[^1][^5] It began as a beacon that local people put up in 1874.[^1][^3] The wooden tower that later carried it is still there, renovated by a heritage society; the working light today is a quick-flashing red light on a skeletal tower.[^1][^5][^6]

## The place

Garnish sits on the western shore of Little Garnish Barrasway, a small natural harbour sheltered from Fortune Bay by a sand bar.[^1] The 1836 census counted 76 residents, all Church of England. Twelve boats worked the harbour then; by 1857 there were thirty-eight.[^1]

The Garnish River runs into the harbour and was a salmon river. In 1872 George T. R. Snelgrove, the salmon warden, reported four men fishing it with twenty nets.[^1] The records do not say which dangers the 1874 beacon was set against.

## Building the light

The Board of Works paid $20.00 for labour on the Garnish Beacon in its 1874 accounts for Fortune Bay.[^3] The Board's account for Garnish itself, for the year ending 31 December 1874, lists freight, paint, an oil feeder, lamp wicks and the keeper's salary, and totals $118.35.[^2]

That keeper was George T. R. Snelgrove, named in the account.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he was the first keeper, at an annual salary of $48.[^1] The estimated expenditure on the general light-houses for 1879 allows $150 for Garnish.[^4]

LighthouseFriends reports that in 1875 the inspector thought the tower would have been more useful at the end of the breakwater. The tower was moved there in 1877, and the year after it received a new lantern, lamp and reflector.[^1]

In 1879 the inspector John T. Nevill left the harbour near dusk aboard the *Hercules*. The light looked good as he steamed away, but after he had turned from it for a few minutes it was barely visible. He concluded that only tampering could explain the change.[^1]

After the second keeper, John Grandy, died in 1884, a bedroom was added to the base of the tower for the keeper, in 1885. A new lamp and lens followed in 1886.[^1] In 1913 a new lantern went on the tower, and the light's signature changed to occulting white, five seconds on and five off.[^1]

The Lighthouse Directory dates the tower itself to 1885 and gives its height as 6 m (20 ft).[^5] LighthouseFriends dates the beacon to 1874 and treats the 1885 work as a bedroom added to the tower's base.[^1] The two accounts are not reconciled here.

## The keepers

George T. R. Snelgrove came first, paid $48 a year.[^1][^2] John Grandy followed from 1875 until his death in 1884.[^1] William Reeves, the local schoolteacher, succeeded him and kept the light until 1912.[^1]

Three generations of the Reeves family then kept the light: William, his son George, and a grandson who was also named George. They kept it until it was electrified in 1967.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The one dated disaster in the records is a fire. In 1880 it broke out in the lantern room on a hot summer day, destroyed the lamp and lens, and caught the tower. It was discovered and put out quickly, and LighthouseFriends says it was attributed to spontaneous combustion.[^1] No wreck or rescue is tied to the station in the surviving records.

## After the keepers

The light was electrified in 1967. Bertram Marsh served as caretaker of the automated light until 1991, when a modern skeletal tower took over its function.[^1] The Directory gives the historic tower as inactive since 1991.[^5]

The historic tower came off the breakwater in the late 1930s or early 1940s, LighthouseFriends says. It was sold to the town for $1, and the Garnish Heritage Society has since renovated and maintained it.[^1]

## Today

The historic tower stands on the quay at Garnish, and the Garnish Heritage Society manages the site. The site is open, and the tower is closed to visitors.[^5][^1]

The working light is a quick-flashing red light, shown 8.5 m (28 ft) above the water, with a nominal range of 6 nautical miles. The Coast Guard list gives it on a square skeletal tower, 4.3 m (14 ft) high, with a red and white daymark.[^6] The Directory describes a red flash every 3 seconds on a 3.5 m (12 ft) skeletal tower. The two disagree, and this story follows the Coast Guard list.[^5]

LighthouseFriends says the Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse. Its history says the historic tower went to the town, so the owner of the historic tower is not settled here.[^1]
