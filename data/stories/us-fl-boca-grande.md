Two lights mark the Boca Grande pass, where Charlotte Harbor opens onto the Gulf of Mexico on Florida's southwest coast.[^8] The older is a white frame lighthouse of 1890 at the southern tip of Gasparilla Island, in Lee County, built for the phosphate ships that loaded at Port Boca Grande.[^7][^8] The other is an iron skeleton tower first raised on the Delaware coast in 1881. It was taken down, shipped south and set up on Gasparilla Island in 1927, and it became the rear light of a range on the island, about 1.7 miles from its southern end.[^9][^10]

## The place

The pass mattered because of phosphate. The 1979 National Register nomination says new phosphate discoveries in west central Florida, and advanced mining techniques, "demanded a port capable of handling the increased volume of seagoing traffic."[^7] Phosphate mined in the Peace River area came down in lighters and barges to deep water, "where it was transferred to ocean-going vessels."[^7] LighthouseFriends says Congress appropriated $35,000 in 1888 for a lighthouse at the southern tip of the island to mark the entrance.[^8]

The railroad that carried phosphate to the port is dated two ways. LighthouseFriends says the line opened in 1907.[^8] The nomination places the change in transport in 1909, when the Charlotte Harbor and Northern Railroad was completed.[^7]

## Building the light

The lighthouse was completed in September 1890 and activated on December 31, 1890, the nomination says.[^7] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1891, describes a black lantern on the keeper's dwelling. The light was a fixed white light of third-and-a-half order, varied by a red flash every twenty seconds, with its focal plane 44 feet (13.4 m) above mean low water.[^1] A similar house, without the lantern, stood about 70 feet (21 m) away for the assistant keeper. The Board's entry ends: "This light was first displayed December 31, 1890."[^1]

The range needed a second light. The Board describes a square, pyramidal beacon about 735 feet (224 m) from the tower, "in range with Gasparilla Island light-house," with a black lantern 20 feet (6 m) above mean sea level.[^1] The beacon did not stay put. The Board reported that it "was washed out by the shifting of the channel" and was re-erected 175 feet (53 m) from high-water mark and 475 feet (145 m) from the main light.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates that move to 1892.[^8]

Work on the station went on for years. The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1894, records a contract for building an oil house.[^3] Its 1900 report lists repairs completed on September 30, 1899: slate roofs on the keepers' dwellings, and an addition to the wharf that took it "out to deep water."[^15] The same report records that the light's characteristic was changed on March 23, 1900, and that the original characteristic was reestablished on June 20.[^4]

## The keepers

Francis McNulty was the first keeper. LighthouseFriends says he activated the light on December 31, 1890, and was given an eighteen-foot (5.5 m) boat so he could tend the front light.[^8][^12] William Lester was head keeper from 1894 to 1923. LighthouseFriends calls him by far the station's longest-serving keeper.[^12][^8]

Lester's assistant from 1911 to 1914 was James T. Williams.[^12] The Digital Archive roster then lists Charles H. Williams as head keeper from 1923 to 1932, Osmund M. McKinney from 1932 to 1941, and Cody W. McKeithen from 1942 to 1951. Peter St. Juan is the last head keeper on the roster, for 1951 to 1952.[^12]

Charles F. Fine was an assistant keeper. LighthouseFriends says he began at the lighthouse in 1922 and died there on April 28, 1931.[^8] The roster dates his service from 1924 to 1931.[^12] LighthouseFriends gives the circumstances of his death in detail; this story does not repeat them.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On May 19, 1914, two power boats went ashore near the light. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin says Lester and Williams "rendered assistance in saving two power boats which had gone ashore near the light station."[^5] LighthouseFriends adds that the four men from the boats were fed and sheltered until their boats were refloated.[^8]

In May 1936 arrangements were made for the Weather Bureau office at Jacksonville to receive observations from lightships and from Florida reef light stations.[^6] During the hurricane disturbance of July 28, the senior meteorologist at Jacksonville advised the Key West superintendent that reports from several lights, Gasparilla among them, "were especially valuable."[^6]

Hurricane Charley struck the station on August 13, 2004, and LighthouseFriends says it destroyed the lighthouse catwalk and two cisterns.[^8] Hurricane Ian damaged the lighthouse in September 2022, and the property was closed until repairs could be made.[^8]

## After the keepers

The lighthouse was automated in 1956, according to LighthouseFriends.[^8] A Commissioner's report from the years 1915 to 1922 lists $7,607 for a sanitary plumbing system, a concrete wharf and general repairs at the range station.[^14] The 1979 nomination gives a different date for the move north, 1960, when the light "was moved two miles to the north."[^7] The Lighthouse Society's place record gives 1966 to 1986 as the years the 1890 lighthouse was discontinued.[^12] LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard abandoned it in 1966 and set up a 58-foot (18 m) steel skeleton tower nearby.[^8]

By 1970, LighthouseFriends says, erosion was threatening the old lighthouse and the sea was lapping at its foundation.[^8] The government built a 265-foot (81 m) granite jetty in 1971, and Florida Power and Light then pumped 100,000 cubic yards of fill into the area.[^8] The federal government transferred the lighthouse and about 13 acres to Lee County in 1972. The State of Florida received it in 1988, and it became Gasparilla Island State Park.[^8] LighthouseFriends says it was placed on the National Register in 1980, restored in 1985 and 1986, and re-commissioned as an active aid to navigation on November 21, 1986, with a drum lens; the Lighthouse Society's record gives the drum as 375 mm and LighthouseFriends as 377 mm.[^8][^12] The Barrier Island Parks Society, formed in 1989, opened its museum in the lighthouse a decade later, after a $165,000 project.[^8]

The rear tower's history began at Cape Henlopen, Delaware. The Barrier Island Parks Society says it was built in 1881 by the Phoenix Iron Company of Trenton, New Jersey, and "began service just north of Lewes, Delaware as the Delaware Breakwater Rear Range Light."[^10] LighthouseFriends says the rear range light there was discontinued in 1918. The tower stood inactive until it was dismantled and shipped by rail to Miami in 1921.[^9] The Digital Archive's place record gives 1932 as the year the range tower was established on Gasparilla Island.[^13] The tower was reassembled there in 1927, and the Society says a fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed and lit on January 1, 1932.[^10] The tower "remained dark until it was finally lit in 1932," LighthouseFriends says.[^9] The Society says the light "was not manned, but continuously maintained by the Light Keepers of Gasparilla Island Light Station, better known as Port Boca Grande Lighthouse."[^10]

The front range light was discontinued in 2003, and the rear light was renamed Gasparilla Island Lighthouse, the name the 1890 house had before it was deactivated.[^9] The Coast Guard awarded stewardship to the Barrier Island Parks Society in 2016.[^10] LighthouseFriends says the light was deactivated in early 2017.[^9] The Society raised $1.3 million for restoration, and the tower opened to its first public climb in April 2017.[^10] The light was relit on February 9, 2019, and LighthouseFriends says it "has been designated a private aid to navigation."[^11][^9]

## Today

Port Boca Grande Lighthouse stands in Gasparilla Island State Park.[^8] LighthouseFriends says the State of Florida owns the lighthouse and the Barrier Island Parks Society manages it.[^8] LighthouseFriends lists the lighthouse as open Wednesday to Monday, 11 a.m. to 4 p.m., closed in August and on major holidays. It closed after Hurricane Ian and reopened in February 2024, so check the hours before going.[^8]

The rear light, now called Gasparilla Island Lighthouse, stands about 1.7 miles from the island's southern end, beside Gulf Boulevard.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the tower has opened to climbers roughly one day a month since 2017, or in another passage "a few days per month," and not in August, when the temperature inside can top 100 degrees. Climbs happen only on scheduled tours.[^9]
