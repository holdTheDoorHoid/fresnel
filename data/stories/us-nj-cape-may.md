Cape May Lighthouse stands in Cape May Point State Park, near the southernmost point of New Jersey, on the northern side of the entrance to Delaware Bay. The brick tower of 1859 is the third lighthouse on this stretch of shore, and it is still lit and still climbed by visitors.[^1][^2]

## The place

The tower marks the New Jersey side of the entrance to Delaware Bay; on the Delaware side, opposite, a lighthouse had stood on Cape Henlopen since 1767.[^2][^3] The Light-House Board's 1891 report places the station on the north side of the entrance to Delaware Bay, on the New Jersey seacoast.[^4] The sea has taken the earlier towers and more. The National Register nomination says that the site of the first tower now lies some 100 yards out from the present shoreline, and LighthouseFriends adds that the neighbouring resort community of South Cape May was also taken by the sea.[^2][^3]

Government interest in the spot is older than any tower that stood on it. By a deed of 23 September 1785, Thomas Hand 2nd conveyed a tract on a high bluff to the Board of Wardens of the Port of Philadelphia, and a letter of 12 November 1785 confirmed the conveyance. The nomination says no record shows that a lighthouse was ever built on that land, which "has long since been covered by the Ocean."[^2]

## Building the light

The nomination reports that Congress approved an appropriation in 1821 and that the first lighthouse was finished in October 1823; it describes a tower 75 feet high with walls six feet thick at the base.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives the first tower as brick, 70 feet tall, lit by fifteen lamps and reflectors that revolved to give a flashing light, and says it was discontinued on 1 May 1847 once the sea had begun to surround it at high tide. The sources therefore disagree on the height of this first tower (75 feet in the nomination, 70 feet in LighthouseFriends), and neither figure rests here on a primary source.[^2][^3] They also tell the move of 1847 differently. The nomination says the 1823 lighthouse was rebuilt on a high bluff called Great Island, about a third of a mile from the present position; LighthouseFriends describes a new second tower on a parcel bought that year.[^2][^3]

A second tower followed in 1847, on land bought from Alexander Whilden on 9 April of that year for $1,150 and built by Samuel and Nathan Middleton. LighthouseFriends gives its height as 78 feet.[^3] An inspection report of 1851, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, found the tower damp and the keeper's dwelling leaking, and summed up the whole station as "rude in the extreme."[^3]

The present tower came after a further two acres were bought from Whilden by a deed of 8 April 1858.[^3] LighthouseFriends names Captain William F. Raynolds, then Captain W. B. Franklin and Major Hartman Bache, as the officers who directed the work, and gives the tower's height as 157 feet 6 inches (48 m), with a first-order Henry-Lepaute Fresnel lens of sixteen flash panels that showed a white flash every thirty seconds.[^3] The light was first lit in the autumn of 1859. LighthouseFriends says Halloween, 1859; Cape May Magazine says Halloween Eve.[^3][^12] Two keepers' dwellings were finished the next year, and the top of the old 1847 tower was taken down in 1862.[^3] The USLHS archive records the dwellings as wooden, in the Cape Cod style, and a brick oil house was added in 1893.[^1]

The National Register nomination of 1973 gives the lighthouse as 175 feet high, and elsewhere puts the light 165 feet above sea level. The USLHS archive lists 175 feet as the height of the light above mean high water and 157 feet from the base to the centre of the lantern, and LighthouseFriends also gives 157 feet 6 inches for the tower.[^1][^2][^3]

## The keepers

The USLHS archive, working from the National Archives' registers of keepers, lists eight principal keepers between 1823 and 1933: Ephraim Mills (1823-1829), Ezekiel Stevens (1829-1850), Downs E. Foster (1850-1853), William C. Gregory (1853-1861), Foster again (1861-1877), Samuel Stilwell (1877-1903), Caleb S. Woolson (1903-1924) and Harry H. Palmer (1924-1933).[^1] Assistants began to appear on the roster when the new tower opened.[^1]

Downs Foster is the keeper the sources say most about. LighthouseFriends reports that the 1851 inspector described him as a carpenter by trade who had taken charge in April 1850 with no training and no instructions. He was never supplied with paint, whitewash or brooms, and having asked and been refused, he stopped asking.[^3] He left after three years and came back in 1861 to run the new tower. In January 1876 the district inspector reprimanded him for giving away refuse oil and small quantities of alcohol, but his long service saved his place. The next year an inspecting officer judged him, at seventy, too old and feeble for the work, which was falling mostly to two assistants, and he was replaced a few months later by Samuel Stilwell.[^3] Stilwell had been first assistant from 1859 to 1861 and kept the light as head keeper for twenty-six years, retiring in 1903 at the age of eighty, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1][^3]

By the 1890s the station needed three keepers but had houses for only two families. The Light-House Board put it plainly in its reports from 1896 to 1900: the third keeper "has to be accommodated by makeshift arrangements which are thoroughly unsatisfactory and detrimental to the discipline and efficiency of the service." The Board asked for $4,000 for a third dwelling.[^5][^6] An addition to one of the dwellings was completed in 1903.[^3]

In 1905, LighthouseFriends reports, a reporter touring the tower at night with a keeper heard a thud in the lantern room, and the keeper came in from the gallery with a dead mud hen; he said they sometimes found five or six birds in a night.[^3] The same site says that in 1905 Assistant Keeper John Pusey upset his boat while hunting on the pond behind the lighthouse and was pulled out of the mud by the crew of the nearby life-saving station.[^3] The USLHS roster does list Pusey as second assistant from 1903 to 1906.[^1]

The last head keeper, Harry H. Palmer, was awarded the efficiency flag in 1927, according to LighthouseFriends, which says his station had been named the model station of the Fourth District for at least four years in a row; the Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1924-1929 index also lists the award, without giving the year.[^3][^7] The Bulletin records that the keeper of Cape May won second prize for lawn and garden from the Cape May County Chamber of Commerce in 1931 and first prize, with a blue ribbon, for the best-kept yard and garden in 1933, and names Harry H. Palmer as keeper and Floyd Schmierer as assistant.[^8][^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 12 June 1891 lightning struck the ball on top of the tower. The Light-House Board's report says it ran down the lantern framework, jumped from the lamp pedestal to the iron of the watch room, followed the gallery to the lightning rod, and sent part of its current along the electric call bells into the keepers' dwellings, "causing much damage to the interior." The damage was repaired, a new set of call bells and wires was fitted, and the chimneys of both dwellings were rebuilt.[^4] LighthouseFriends adds that the tower was struck again ten years later and that a keeper's daughter was stunned and burned; this account rests on that one source.[^3]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service dropped the second assistant's post in 1932 after a fixed lens with an electrically flashed light replaced the revolving oil-vapor lamp, and decided to automate the station in June 1933, retiring Palmer on a pension. LighthouseFriends says he chose to stay on at the station with his family and look after the property.[^3] A 1936 Bulletin describes the station as "formerly operated by keepers, now entirely automatic and unattended," with a 12-panel first-order flashing lens turned by a small electric motor, and a battery bank that would take over if commercial power failed.[^10] That is a different panel count from the sixteen panels of the 1859 lens; the sources do not explain the change.

The light went dark during the Second World War, and in 1946 the Fresnel lens was taken down and loaned to the Cape May County Historical Museum, replaced by a rotating aerobeacon.[^3] In 1964 the grounds passed to the State of New Jersey and became Cape May Point State Park. One of the dwellings, converted to a duplex in 1903, burned in an arson fire in 1968; the other still stood and housed park staff when LighthouseFriends wrote.[^3] The tower was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1973, when the Coast Guard still owned it.[^2] The Coast Guard leased the tower to the state in 1986 and transferred ownership in 1992. The Mid-Atlantic Center for the Arts restored the tower and opened it to the public in 1988, and in 1994 repainted it in its historic light beige with a red lantern after it had been painted white in the 1940s or 1950s. A solar-powered beacon replaced the aerobeacon in 2002, and the tower was repainted in 2017.[^3]

## Today

The lighthouse is an active aid to navigation, restored and maintained by the organization now called Cape May MAC (Museums+Arts+Culture), at 215 Lighthouse Avenue in Cape May Point State Park. LighthouseFriends lists the State of New Jersey as owner.[^3] Visitors with tickets climb the 199 steps of the original cast-iron spiral stair; the organization says that more than 2.5 million people have climbed since 1988, and that the tower closes in lightning or severe weather. The 1893 oil house serves as the orientation center and shop.[^11][^1] The first-order Fresnel lens is on display at the Cape May County Historical Museum in Cape May Court House, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] Check Cape May MAC for current hours and prices before visiting.[^11]
