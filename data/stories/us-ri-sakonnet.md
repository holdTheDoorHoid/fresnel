Sakonnet Light is a cast-iron tower that rises straight out of the sea on Little Cormorant Rock, about 800 yards (730 m) off Sakonnet Point in Little Compton, Rhode Island.[^2] Its keepers lived inside the tower itself.[^1]

## The place

Sakonnet Point lies at the southern tip of a peninsula shared by Tiverton and Little Compton, on the eastern side of the mouth of the Sakonnet River, which is really an inlet of the Atlantic.[^3] In March 1882 a Lighthouse Board inspector reported that a light here would make navigation safer. He described the lower river as a harbor of refuge for coasting vessels in storms, and the coast beyond it as a long stretch with no light. The National Register nomination says that the inspector's report may have followed petitions from local people or mariners, but that no document confirming this has turned up.[^1]

The inspector wanted the light on West Island. The members of a private club that occupied the island objected, and the Board settled on Little Cormorant Rock, about 900 feet (275 m) to the northwest.[^1] The Friends of Sakonnet Lighthouse add that the club was a fishing club, and that some of its members had become friendly with President Arthur, which apparently gave them political clout; that part of the story rests on their account alone.[^3]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $20,000 on 7 August 1882, and Rhode Island ceded the rock to the federal government on 15 March 1883.[^1][^2] The nomination calls the site inaccessible and exposed, which meant special arrangements for carrying materials and workers out to it. The cast-iron caisson, filled with concrete, went up in 1883 before winter weather stopped the work, and the tower was finished in 1884.[^1][^2]

The finished station is a prefabricated cylinder of cast-iron plates, four storeys high, about 66 feet (20 m) from the rock to the top of the lantern. The caisson is 30 feet 5 inches (9.3 m) across and holds a brick-walled basement with a rain-water cistern, a coal bin and a boiler. The first floor was the kitchen and living room, the second and third were bedrooms, and the fourth was divided between a bedroom and a tool room.[^1] The light was first shown on 1 November 1884.[^1] According to Lighthousefriends, the first lens was a fourth-order Fresnel lens by Sautter that showed a fixed white light followed by three red flashes, turned by clockwork whose weights had to be wound every three and a half hours.[^2]

## The keepers

The station had a principal keeper and an assistant, and later a second assistant.[^3] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists fourteen principal keepers between 1884 and 1950, beginning with "Clarence Otis Gray (1884-1891)".[^8] Turnover was high: the Friends count more than 50 men as keepers or assistants before the Coast Guard took over in the early 1940s.[^3]

Lighthousefriends tells of Lucius E. Chadwick, who struggled through steep swells to row 800 yards to his new post in bitter January cold. The cramped quarters did not suit him, and after climbing to the lantern room and looking out over the icy water he went back ashore and resigned, two days after accepting the appointment. The story is told only there, with no year given.[^2]

Winter was the hard season. Assistant keeper John Ganze, who served from 1928 to 1935 according to the archive, is quoted by the Friends, who take his words from Sarah C. Gleason's book *Kindly Lights*, describing the isolation: "You could drop dead in the winter and no one would find you till spring." He said the keepers were given a cord of wood for the winter, "and when it ran out-that was it!"[^3][^8] The only heat came from a stove in the kitchen.[^3]

William H. Durfee, principal keeper from 1920 until 1944 in the archive's roster, held the longest span of any principal keeper in the archive's roster. The Friends' history says he became principal keeper in 1921 and served about 20 years.[^8][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the early 1890s the Lighthouse Board reported repairs at Sakonnet: the regulating fan of the flash apparatus was repaired, and fourteen steps, seven on the east side and seven on the west, were cut into the rock to make boat landings safe.[^5]

On 24 July 1903 Nils Nelson, then assistant keeper, made a rescue that earned him a gold lifesaving medal. A man named George H. Child, working for the West Island Club, had been sent for the club's mail in a gasoline whaleboat. Returning, he was swamped near the lighthouse by an immense wave that dashed the boat to pieces on the rocks, and he swam to a rock and clung there. The Life-Saving Service's report said the boats sent from the clubhouse could not get near him, and that Nelson launched the lighthouse supply boat and rowed to the rock while the keeper directed him by signals. He told Child to throw himself toward the boat when the next wave passed. The report concluded that Child "would have perished but for the gallant action, at the peril of his life, of Nils Nelson".[^6]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that in the storm of 26 August 1924 one rowboat at Sakonnet was carried away and the other badly damaged. A quarter of the boat landing's deck planking was lost, and a third of the landing railing needed renewing. Repairs to the station, not counting boats, were estimated at about $200.[^7] The Bulletin does not name the keeper; Lighthousefriends says Durfee was on duty and saw waves breaking on a roof and spray rising above the top of the lantern room.[^2]

The hurricane of 21 September 1938 struck without warning, and it was worse. The Friends quote Durfee's own account, written afterwards: boats were smashed by four o'clock, the outside doors and the windows from the first floor to the third were carried away by five, and at half past five, as he lit the lamp, "There were seas that went by that completely buried the tower." The first of them broke seven plates out of the upper deck and knocked him off his feet. At sunrise the assistant, Joseph O. Bouley, put the light out and called him to look, and they saw that everything on the point had been washed away.[^3] Two-thirds of the 75 cottages and shanties at Sakonnet Point were destroyed, according to the Friends.[^3] A large crack in the caisson was repaired and the light stayed in service.[^3][^2]

## After the keepers

The Friends date Coast Guard control to 1940, and the archive's roster shows civilian keepers until 1944, then a keeper to 1950 and a Coast Guard line for 1952-53.[^4][^8] Hurricane Carol (1954) did further damage, and the Coast Guard, facing an estimated $100,000 to rebuild, chose to deactivate the station in 1955. The Coast Guard planned to blow up the tower, according to Lighthousefriends, but citizens of Little Compton objected, and ownership passed to the town on condition that it was kept and never relit.[^2] In April 1961 the town's taxpayers voted down maintaining it as a monument, and that August the government took bids. Carl and Carolyn Haffenreffer won with a bid of $1,111 (the Friends give $1,111.11, and say Carl Haffenreffer bought it at auction).[^2][^3][^4] By 1982 the nomination called the tower abandoned and in poor repair, with paint peeling, iron rusting and bird guano on the floors, but structurally sound.[^1]

Lighthousefriends says the Haffenreffers offered the tower in 1985 to any non-profit that could raise the estimated $75,000 for restoration, and that the Friends of Sakonnet Lighthouse, formed in response, raised $100,000 in a little over a year.[^2] The Friends' own chronology differs: it says the group was formed in 1984, that the lighthouse was transferred to it in 1985, and that it had raised approximately $100,000 by 1992.[^4] The nomination to the National Register of Historic Places was prepared in November 1982.[^1]

The Coast Guard installed a new optic, but the relighting was delayed by fears of liability if the light failed, and it took an act of Congress, with language in a 1996 Coast Guard appropriations bill, to clear the way. Lighthousefriends gives 19 March 1997 for the relighting; the Friends say the celebration was held on 22 March on Lloyd's Beach.[^2][^3]

## Today

The Friends of Sakonnet Lighthouse own the tower, and it is not open to the public.[^2] A grant of $844,323 through the Rhode Island Department of Transportation, together with nearly $200,000 the Friends had raised, funded a restoration that began in the summer of 2010: the cast-iron plates were blasted and painted, the corroded bolts replaced with stainless steel, and most of the original brick lining reused. Most of the work was finished in late 2011.[^3][^4][^2] The exterior was repaired and repainted in 2016, and the entrance door and windows were replaced in 2021.[^2]

The light was relit in 1997, and the Friends describe it as a red flash every six seconds, visible 7 nautical miles.[^3] The colour rests on the Friends' account alone. The Friends state that most of the old lens is in the Maine Lighthouse Museum in Rockland; a small part belongs to the Little Compton Historical Society.[^3] The tower stands on a rock offshore and is closed to visitors; Lighthousefriends says it can be seen from the village of Sakonnet.[^2]
