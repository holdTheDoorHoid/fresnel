Point Vicente Light is a white concrete tower on a cliff of the Palos Verdes Peninsula, in Rancho Palos Verdes, Los Angeles County, California.[^1] Its fog signal sounded in June 1925 and its light was first shown on 14 April 1926,[^2][^9] about eleven years after Congress first authorised the station and a decade after money was appropriated, with delays over the land and over rising costs in between.[^3][^4]

## The place

By the Bureau of Lighthouses' own description in 1915, Point Vicente (the reports then spelled it Vincente) was the most prominent point on the California shore between Point Loma and Point Conception, 220 nautical miles apart, and practically all steamer traffic south of San Francisco passed it.[^5] The nearest lights were at Point Hueneme and Point Fermin, which left what the Bureau called an unlighted gap of 32 nautical miles on the usual courses of coasting vessels.[^5] The Bureau also said Point Fermin's light was hidden from steamers coming down from the north until they were within 4 miles of Point Vincente.[^5]

The point offered no help to a navigator in fog. The Bureau called it "a bold, rocky point" where "practically no warning can be had by the lead," because the 100-fathom curve lay within half a mile of the shore.[^5] Masters and shipowners petitioned for a light and fog signal on 21 September 1907, 3 September 1909 and 28 October 1909.[^5] Years earlier, the Light-House Board had put a whistling buoy off the point on 22 May 1899.[^6]

## Building the light

A law of 3 March 1915 authorised a light and fog-signal station at Point Vincente but gave no money; the act of 1 July 1916 appropriated $80,000.[^5][^7] The Bureau's 1915 estimate for the work was a flashing light at 170 feet (52 m) and a first-class compressed-air fog signal, with three keepers' dwellings.[^5]

Then the work stalled on the land. By June 1917 the Bureau reported that "the site is under controversy" and that the United States attorney was preparing to sue to condemn it.[^4] A Lighthouse Service Bulletin item in 1918 noted the condemnation proceedings had been postponed and added that "another steamer recently went ashore there" in thick fog.[^8] In the report for the year ended June 1920 the suit had been put off again because of "more satisfactory arrangements with the land company"; drawings for the tower, fog-signal building and keepers' quarters were finished, and a standard cylindrical lens was then planned.[^7] LighthouseFriends, a modern history site, identifies the owner as Frank A. Vanderlip, who had bought the peninsula in 1913 and had other plans for the point.[^9]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported in 1922 that the site had been acquired, with bids for two keepers' dwellings and a fog-signal building to be opened on 22 August 1922.[^10] According to LighthouseFriends those bids were rejected as too high; a revised design in stuccoed frame with tile roofs was let for $36,990 in 1924, and the whole station cost $102,871.[^9] The Bulletin's own account, written just before the light was lit, says the land negotiations and threatened litigation held the work up for several years, that title was acquired in 1921, and that a great rise in construction costs then delayed the building further.[^3]

The fog signal went into commission on 20 June 1925. It was a 10-inch chime whistle sounding a group of two blasts every 60 seconds, worked by compressed air, and reports to the Bureau indicated it could be heard at least 3 miles in average conditions.[^3] The tower was reinforced concrete, with a 500-watt electric lamp in a modern third-order lens giving a double flash every 20 seconds, and, as "an extra precaution," a rotary air motor installed as a third method of driving the lens.[^3]

The sources do not agree on the numbers. The Bulletin gave the tower as 55 feet (17 m) high and the light as 185 feet (56 m) above the water, at 800,000 candlepower on a plot of about 12 acres.[^3] The National Register nomination, prepared by the Coast Guard, describes a 68-foot (21 m) tower, a 1.1-million-candlepower beam and 17.79 acres.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society archive gives 67 feet (20 m) from base to the centre of the lantern.[^11]

The lens has its own puzzle. The nomination says it was made in Paris in 1914, sent to Alaska that year and moved to Point Vicente in 1925.[^1] LighthouseFriends doubts the Alaska leg and points to a newspaper of early 1926 suggesting it came straight from France.[^9] The city of Rancho Palos Verdes says only that it was made in Paris in 1914 and first lit on 14 April 1926.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists George W. L'Hommedieu as head keeper from 1925 to 1930, with Harry Davis and Benjamin R. South as first and second assistants at the start.[^11] LighthouseFriends says L'Hommedieu came from Mile Rocks Lighthouse, Davis from Alcatraz and South from Piedras Blancas.[^9]

The station was not a peaceful one. LighthouseFriends, drawing on district files, reports that Davis wrote to the district superintendent in December 1925 complaining of profane language and of the keeper leaving the station without telling him, and that in August 1929 second assistant Raymond J. Deurloo brought a much longer list of charges, including threats against first assistant Frederick C. Zimmermann and his wife. An assistant superintendent who investigated reported friction between the keeper and his assistants, particularly between their wives, and also called the station one of the district's show stations, well kept under L'Hommedieu. All three men were reprimanded, and L'Hommedieu was moved to Piedras Blancas.[^9]

Anton Trittinger took over as head keeper in 1930 and stayed until 1945.[^11] LighthouseFriends says he raised two daughters with his wife Frieda at the station and won the district efficiency pennant for the best-kept station in California three years running, the third time in 1935.[^9] The archive lists Joseph May as first assistant from 1942 and head keeper from 1945 to 1955 (LighthouseFriends files him as second assistant); Charles H. Livesay was first assistant from 1939 to 1955.[^11] LighthouseFriends reports, citing a Los Angeles Times article of 27 May 1955, that May, then seventy, retired that year and told a reporter that he hated to retire.[^9] The roster also lists Coast Guard personnel at the station from 1948.[^11]

The nomination tells a ghost story. Keepers complained from the start, it says, of a woman in flowing gowns seen outside after nightfall; a younger assistant concluded that it was an effect of the rotating light.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the legend to after the war, when neighbours complained about the glare and the landward side of the lantern was painted white, and says a thicker coat in 1955 ended the apparition.[^9] The two accounts differ on when the story began.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

When the southern California earthquake struck in March 1933, the Bulletin reported that Point Vicente, "lying about 10 miles from Long Beach, reported a heavy shock but no damage to station or equipment."[^12]

On 1 August 1935 an El Centro newspaper, reporting the search for an Army pursuit plane lost over the sea, wrote that coast guard radiomen at Point Vicente had heard an airplane motor "gunning and cutting out in a fog bank" over the ocean, near where oil spots had been seen off the light.[^13]

During the Second World War the light was dimmed, as the city's account and LighthouseFriends both say.[^2][^9]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends gives 1971 as the year the light was automated; the dwellings housed Coast Guard personnel for many years.[^9] A Coast Guard radio station at the point, which monitored distress frequencies, was closed at a ceremony on 1 May 1980.[^1] The National Park Service's record for Point Vicente Light gives 31 October 1980 as the date the nomination was published.[^14]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light station, and its dwellings are closed; the tower itself is no longer open for visitors to climb.[^9] The Coast Guard Auxiliary opens it to visitors on the second Saturday of each month, from 10 a.m. to 3 p.m.; at other times the tower is seen through the fence of the Coast Guard compound.[^15][^9] In February 2019 the Coast Guard retired the third-order Fresnel lens and replaced it with an LED light; the lens was loaned to the city's Point Vicente Interpretive Center next door, where it is on display.[^2] A centennial celebration was rescheduled by the Friends of the lighthouse to 25 April 2026 after rain.[^15]
