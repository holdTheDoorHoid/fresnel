Partridge Island Lighthouse stands at the entrance to Saint John Harbour in New Brunswick. The light was first lit in 1791. The island was also a quarantine station for ships arriving with passengers, and in 1847 about 2,000 Irish immigrants were held there during a typhus epidemic. The present concrete tower dates from 1960, and the island is closed to visitors.[^4][^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The island sits at the entrance to the harbour.[^4] The Department of Marine and Fisheries describes it as protecting the harbour on the south side, with two and a half miles from the island to the head of the harbour.[^5] Its French name, Isle aux Perdrix, dates from the seventeenth century. The New Brunswick Archives repeats William Francis Ganong's identification of the Maliseet name as Quakm'kay'ganik.[^4]

LighthouseFriends says the island was set apart for a quarantine station in 1785 but was not used as one until 1830. The national historic site register gives 1830 as the year the station was established, to protect Canadians from contagious diseases carried by ships.[^1][^2] In 1871 the island had four hospitals, a lighthouse with a fog whistle and a population of 25.[^4]

LighthouseFriends says over 30,000 people were processed at the island in the 1840s, when the Irish potato famine brought the greatest influx of immigrants.[^1] The register says that during 1847 some 2,000 immigrants were quarantined there during a typhus epidemic, and that 601 of them are buried in a mass grave on the island.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes the sick being given a kerosene shower and then a hot-water shower, and says the island hospital was so overwhelmed that the infirm lay on the bare ground.[^1]

## Building the light

The light was paid for by a duty on shipping. LighthouseFriends says a bill for it was passed by the House of Assembly in 1788, and that the light began operating in 1791, with Samuel Duffy as its first keeper.[^1]

A fire in January 1832 destroyed the lighthouse.[^1] A large lantern hung from the signal post until a new one could be built, and the replacement, octagonal in form, cost £316 in 1833.[^1] In 1840 the eight sides were painted alternately red and white, a daymark that LighthouseFriends says persists to this day.[^1] The tower was 12.2 m (40 ft) from base to the top of the lantern, and its light stood at a focal plane of 36 m (119 ft).[^1] A new keeper's house went up in 1841.[^1]

Lighting changed often. In 1853 a gas plant made on the island was put into operation. LighthouseFriends says the gas gave a very bright light, cost about £100 more than oil in 1855 and 1856, and was abandoned some years after 1860.[^1]

The fog signal came in 1859. A steam whistle tested that November was heard by ships ten and six miles away, and it was in operation by the following spring.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the credit for the whistle belongs to the civil engineer Robert Foulis, as a House of Assembly committee found in 1864. Foulis died in poverty two years later.[^1]

The lamps and reflectors gave way to a dioptric lens in 1887 in a new lantern.[^1] The Department's 1905 report says the illuminant had been changed from coal oil to a three-mantle incandescent petroleum vapour light.[^6] A diaphone fog alarm, its air compressed by steam, was installed about 1906, and the engine house for it had been erected the year before.[^7][^8] In 1911 the tower was raised 2.7 m (9 ft) on a concrete foundation.[^1] A third-order lens and a 3 m (10 ft) lantern were placed on the tower in 1915.[^1]

The tower the island has now is newer. LighthouseFriends says the wooden octagonal tower, reportedly built in 1880, was torn down in 1959. The national register gives 1960 for the present concrete tower, "built in 1960 as the fifth light tower at this location."[^1][^3]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends' list of head keepers starts with Samuel Duffy at 1798 to 1811, but its own text makes Duffy the first keeper in 1791. The text says he was removed in 1810 because of his advanced years and replaced by Lewis Huestis. The list spells the name Huetis. These dates are LighthouseFriends' own.[^1]

James Reed, then his son Alexander, kept the light from 1830 until 1873 on LighthouseFriends' list. The list dates Alexander's service from 1843, but the text says he gave up the post after nearly forty years of service, which the list's dates do not match.[^1] Alexander had succeeded his father as keeper. In 1848 he petitioned for compensation for damage the landed emigrants had done to his property. In 1855 he asked for more pay because his children were kept out of school and the sick immigrants at the quarantine station exposed his family to disease.[^1] Ill health ended his service. James Wilson, who had been engineer in charge of the fog alarm since 1860, took over the light and the alarm. LighthouseFriends says Wilson earned $800 a year and paid an assistant out of it.[^1]

Later keepers followed the list. LighthouseFriends names D. L. Richards (1900 to 1906) and Hugh Andrews (1906 to 1913) next.[^1] The Department's report printed in 1908 says the new dwelling for the assistant keeper was also completed, and the city water extended to it.[^14] The Department's 1912 report says that "the light-keeper on Partridge island signals vessels bound for St. John."[^11] Its numbers show what that meant: in the year ended 31 March 1918 the island station signalled 104 steamers and 25 sailing vessels,[^12] and in the year ended 31 March 1919, 72 steamers and 30 sailing vessels.[^13]

LighthouseFriends' list runs on to 1986. This account does not name the keepers who served after 1960.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends says the Saxby Gale of 1869 swept away the landing stage and steps, blew over the bell tower and damaged all the buildings on the island. Repairs cost $438.[^1]

A bell boat, whose bell was rung by the motion of the boat on the waves, was anchored off the island's eastern end around 1860.[^1] Late in 1902 a storm took the top hamper off the bell boat.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the original bell boat sank at its moorings on 11 February 1903 and was replaced by a stronger one.[^1] In a later storm the Department's steamer Lansdowne went to Saint John "to recover the Partridge Island bell boat," and then tried to recover gas buoys that had drifted in the storm.[^10]

## After the keepers

A fire in 1953 destroyed the fog alarm building and its equipment, and a new installation was built.[^1] The register says the quarantine station was used until 1941, and that the island was occupied for the military defence of Saint John during both world wars and was also used as a light station.[^2]

The last resident keeper's post ended in 1986, the year LighthouseFriends says the light was de-staffed.[^1] The island's foghorn was silenced in 1999, despite strong public protest.[^1] A large Celtic cross was dedicated on the island in 1927 as a memorial to the Irish immigrants who died there, along with a local doctor who cared for them.[^1]

## Today

The present tower is a concrete octagonal light tower built to a standard plan of the Department of Transport. The national register notes its "continuing use as a coastal light," and its colour pattern, "with each side painted alternately in red or white."[^3] The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light.[^1]

The island is closed. LighthouseFriends says the grounds and tower are closed, and the breakwater out to the island is fenced at its end.[^1] The register says all buildings on the island were demolished in 1955 and in 1998 and 1999. The same entry describes remnants of buildings and structures from the quarantine years, including a doctor's residence built about 1872, buildings for second-class immigrants, marine officers' and smallpox hospitals from 1899 to 1901, a low-water wharf, and a cemetery with graves from the 1847 epidemic.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is "the only structure remaining on the island." The two accounts differ, and this story reports both.[^1][^2]
