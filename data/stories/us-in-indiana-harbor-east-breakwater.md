Indiana Harbor East Breakwater Light stands on the tip of an industrial breakwater in Indiana Harbor, near the southern tip of Lake Michigan.[^3] The light that bears the name today is a 1935 tower on the extended tip of the breakwater. It replaced a 1924 light on the breakwater's original pier, at its south end, which the Coast Guard later demolished.[^3][^2][^11]

## The place

Heavy industry crowded onto the southern shore of Lake Michigan in the early twentieth century.[^3] In 1901 Inland Steel accepted "fifty acres of free dune land near East Chicago" from the Lake Michigan Land Company, which also pledged a harbor and a railroad for the site. The harbor was dredged and a breakwater built in 1901 and 1902, and a canal was begun in 1903.[^3]

In 1907 a bill proposed a light and fog signal at the north end of the harbor's pier. The Lighthouse Board, asked to review it, said the harbor "did not merit a coastal light." Because the harbor was privately owned, the board said its owners should "build and maintain any needed pierhead lights and beacons."[^3]

## Building the light

The first lights to mark the harbor were a pair of skeletal metal towers on enclosed bases. One went up at the breakwater's outer end in 1911, and the other, 615 feet (187 m) shoreward, formed range lights "for guiding vessels into the harbor."[^3]

"In 1914, the federal government assumed responsibility for Indiana Harbor and the canal."[^3] A 1917 act appropriated $100,000 "for the establishment and improvement of federal aids to navigation at Indiana Harbor."[^3] The plan was for a "lighthouse tower and fog signal to be erected at the east end of the north arm of the breakwater," with a "skeleton steel tower" on the north end of the south arm.[^4]

By 1919 the plan had changed. A timber crib with concrete capping was to be placed by the United States engineers, "upon which will be erected the light and fog-signal station."[^5] The Commissioner's 1920 report planned the superstructure as "a hollow reinforced concrete superstructure (on crib), 44 by 65 by 20 feet high," and the 1921 report says the crib was "completed in August, 1920."[^6][^7]

Work on the building went on through 1922. "The reinforced-concrete basement was completed and work on the two-story brick and concrete light and fog-signal building was begun in May."[^8] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported, "Reinforcement in place ready for pouring concrete,"[^9] and a later Bulletin reported the station "85 per cent completed."[^10]

LighthouseFriends quotes the Bureau's own description of the finished building, which provided "ample deck room around the house."[^3] The light was a fourth-order lens "showing two white flashes of 4,000 candlepower every 20 seconds." The fog signal was a type F diaphone, "operated by compressed air."[^3]

Two smaller lights had already come into use on 31-foot (9.4 m) steel towers. The east pierhead light "went into operation on June 5, 1920," and the west breakwater light "commenced operation on July 14, 1923."[^3] The main light at the end of the east breakwater was "activated on May 2, 1924."[^3] Rowlett's directory describes the original station as "a square light tower with lantern and gallery centered on a 2-story keeper's house, all standing on a large rectangular concrete pier."[^11] Rowlett dates the station to 1923, while LighthouseFriends dates the main light to 1924.[^11][^3]

## The keepers

The Archive files the 1924 light under its own place record, and its roster begins with a principal keeper, "James C. Peitersen (1924-)."[^2] LighthouseFriends spells the name "James C. Peiterson."[^3]

Assistants came and went in the 1920s. Charles A. Linsmeier appears as first assistant in 1924, and George J. Meengs from 1924 to 1926.[^2] Arthur S. Almquist was second assistant from 1925 to 1926 and first assistant from 1926 to 1927.[^2]

Edward Kott began as first assistant in 1927 and served as principal keeper "at least 1929-1937," on the Archive's dating.[^2] William Renier was principal keeper from 1937 to 1941, and Louis Andrew Anderson was first assistant from 1936 to 1941.[^2]

Once the new tower stood, its light and fog signal were run from the old station by cable, and an elevated walkway joined the two.[^3] In 1952 "a radiobeacon was installed at the breakwater lighthouse as an additional aid for mariners."[^3] The Archive's roster also has a "USCG" heading for Coast Guard personnel, with entries running from "at least 1950" to 1968. It gives their names and dates only.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1929 the illuminant changed from oil vapor to electricity, which LighthouseFriends says improved the light's visibility.[^3]

The east breakwater was extended about 2,300 feet (700 m) north in 1934 and 1935, and as fill went into the lake, "the entrance to the harbor moved northward."[^3] On the new outermost point, "a steel tower set atop an arched concrete base" was placed to mark the new entrance. LighthouseFriends calls it "a twin" of the Port Washington, Wisconsin breakwater tower built the same year.[^3] The Archive records the tower's construction material as "Tower Construction Material: concrete."[^1] The two accounts disagree.

The new tower took the name of the breakwater light, and the old one became the inner light: "while the old lighthouse was called Indiana Harbor East Breakwater Inner Lighthouse."[^3] The original lens in the new tower was a fourth-order Fresnel lens, flashing white for one second every 7.5 seconds, with a focal plane of 78 feet (24 m).[^3]

The 1924 light did not last. The Archive's place record gives "Disposition: Demolished by Uscg" and "Existing: No."[^2] LighthouseFriends says the brick and concrete building "was demolished in the 1980s," and that "its concrete pier remains in place, marked by a cylindrical" tower.[^3] Rowlett's directory describes that tower as a "round cylindrical 'D9' tower," stating that "the present light, marked as Indiana Harbor Light 5, stands on the original pier at the south end of the east breakwater."[^11]

## Today

LighthouseFriends reports that "a modern green beacon sits atop the art deco tower," showing "three seconds on followed by three seconds off."[^3] The Archive's record, however, lists the station as not open to the public.[^1]

The Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and LighthouseFriends says simply, "Tower closed."[^3] Because the breakwater runs through a private industrial area, the same account advises that the light "is best seen from the water."[^3] Rowlett's directory gives the same closure for the cylindrical light on the old pier: "Site and tower closed."[^11]
