Hawkins Island Light, listed as Peninsula Harbour Light until 1962, stands on Hawkins Island at the mouth of Peninsula Harbour, on the north shore of Lake Superior, beside the town of Marathon, Ontario.[^1] Its first tower was a wooden one, lit in 1891.[^3] Fire later destroyed that tower.[^1] Its 1929 replacement was demolished in 1975 according to LighthouseFriends, but the Lighthouse Directory says it was destroyed after being deactivated in 1957.[^1][^8] The present light shows from a steel skeleton tower.[^8][^9]

## The place

Peninsula Harbour opens onto Lake Superior, with Hawkins Island at its mouth and the town of Marathon on its eastern shore.[^1] The harbour drew the Canadian Pacific Railway in the 1880s.[^2] The Marathon & District Historical Society says the railway chose it for a dock because of its great depth and its shelter from the wind. A warehouse was built beside the dock, a railway station went up, and a hotel was erected.[^2] The society, quoting a captain's recollection, says the railway's main camp on the bay came to be called Peninsula, and it dates a photograph of the village to about 1885.[^2]

The 1891 report places the new light at the south end of Hawkins Island, opposite the peninsula, at the entrance to the harbour.[^3] No source names a particular rock or shoal that the light was built to mark. The report's concern is visibility: the light should be seen from all points of approach by water.[^3]

## Building the light

In 1890 the Department of Marine gave the contract for the light to Charles Mickler of Collingwood.[^3] Mickler's price for the work was $3,165. The total cost of establishing the light, with its lantern and illuminating apparatus, was $5,340.[^3]

The tower was wood, square in plan, with a dwelling attached and an iron lantern on top.[^3] The woodwork was painted white and the lantern red.[^3] The 1891 report gives the height to the vane as 17 m (56 ft).[^3] The light was elevated 32 m (105 ft) above the lake, and its revolving white light flashed every 30 seconds. The report said it should be visible 16 miles from all points of approach by water.[^3] The light was first put in operation on 1 August 1891, according to the same report.[^3]

In August 1914 a new catoptric light was placed in the lantern room. It gave four flashes at three-second intervals, every 25 seconds, and its illuminant was petroleum vapour burned under an incandescent mantle.[^1] A float and pedestal, quadruple flash reflectors and a clockwork mechanism followed in 1925. They changed the pattern to a group of four flashes every 20 seconds.[^1]

## The keepers

David B. Hawkins kept the light from 1891 until 1910.[^1] The Department's keeper list for 1909 gives his appointment as 31 August 1891.[^5]

In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1903 the station received a hand fog horn. The Department's report for that fiscal year says it "will be used in answer to the fog signals of vessels whenever they are heard from the station."[^4]

Joseph Blondin succeeded Hawkins in 1910 and served until 1916. The Department's 1911 list names him at Peninsula Harbour, and LighthouseFriends dates his service to the same years.[^7][^1]

After Blondin the names come quickly. LighthouseFriends lists F. Nicholl from 1917 to 1918, then Mrs. M. J. McCoy from 1918 to 1920. Five more names are listed for 1921 alone: M. Sutherland, E. Lake, C. L. McCoy, C. Pigeon and M. Wissian. The list does not say why so many keepers fall into that one year.[^1]

David E. Coveney was appointed in 1921 and kept the light until 1945.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls him "David D. Coveney" in its text and "David E. Coveney" in its list. This account follows the list. During his time a fire destroyed the original lighthouse. No source dates the fire.[^1] The replacement, built in 1929, was a white, square, wooden dwelling with a white, square, wooden tower 12 m (39 ft) tall, showing a white flash every 30 seconds.[^1]

After Coveney the keepers changed often, most of them for a year or two.[^1] Harry O'Conner was keeper in 1949, and Edward Huard was keeper in 1953.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1908 the steamer Neepawah, chartered to carry supplies to the lights above Montreal, ran ashore in a fog near Peninsula Harbour on Lake Superior. The supplies had to be moved to another boat so the trip could reach the remaining lighthouses.[^6]

In 1949 boys playing on a beach at Marathon found a bottle with a penciled note inside, from Keeper O'Conner. The note said he had been sick for three weeks and could not get out. The boys took it to the chief constable, and a rescue party went out to Hawkins Island. O'Conner's body was found on a couch inside the lighthouse. He had died of heart disease.[^1]

In June 1953 Keeper Edward Huard and his helper, Joseph Archibald, who was twenty years old, were thrown into Lake Superior when their small boat capsized. A member of the crew of the Marathon Paper Mill's tug Allagash spotted the boat in rough water, and the crew pulled both men out. The two had been holding on to the overturned boat for more than half an hour.[^1]

## After the keepers

The fog signal changed more than once. In 1948 an electric fog trumpet was established at the station, giving a one-second blast every 15 seconds. In 1951 a fog typhon, a diaphone, replaced the trumpet and gave a two-second blast every 20 seconds.[^1]

In 1963 the names were changed. The island light was listed as Hawkins Island Light, and a new mast light at the southwest end of the peninsula, just west of Marathon, was listed as Peninsula Harbour Light. The diaphone moved to a white building beside the new mast. The island light's characteristic changed from a white flash every 30 seconds to one every four seconds.[^1]

The sources disagree about the end of the 1929 lighthouse. LighthouseFriends says it was demolished in 1975.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory says it was destroyed after being deactivated in 1957, and it dates the present light to 1975.[^8] This account cannot settle the difference. No source gives a date for the end of resident keepers. The last keeper named with a year, Edward Huard, is listed for at least 1953.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard's list of lights gives the Hawkins Island light as a white flash every four seconds, 27.1 m above the water, with a nominal range of 4 nautical miles. It is shown from a 12.8 m skeleton tower with a white slatwork daymark.[^9] The list's remarks read "Seasonal (in place year round)."[^9]

LighthouseFriends reports that in 2021 a skeletal tower with a slatwork daymark was displaying a flashing white light, and it advises that the modern light is best seen by boat.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory says the island is accessible only by boat, that the site is open and that the tower is closed.[^8]
