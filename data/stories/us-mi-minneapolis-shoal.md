Minneapolis Shoal Light Station is a steel tower on a concrete pier, standing alone in the water of Green Bay, Michigan, nearly seven miles south of Peninsula Point and about twelve miles south of Escanaba, by the Escanaba *Daily Press*'s reckoning; the U.S. Lighthouse Society archive says 10 miles south of the Point and Lighthouse Friends 9.6.[^4][^1][^2] It was built in the 1930s to take the place of a lightship, and it is still lit today.[^2][^4]

## The place

The shoal takes its name from a steamer. On 3 June 1898 the wooden steamship *Minneapolis*, built the year before in South Chicago, struck an unmarked shoal south of the Eleven Foot Shoal lightship in Green Bay. A month later the district inspector announced a striped spar buoy on the spot, marking the centre of a shoal lying 17½ feet (5.3 m) below the surface and asking mariners to give it a berth of at least a quarter mile.[^2]

The older lights on this approach to Escanaba were Point Peninsula Lighthouse, built in 1865, and a lightship that was placed on Eleven Foot Shoal, two and a half miles off the Stonington Peninsula, in 1893. Lighthouse Friends says that as larger vessels with deeper drafts were put on the lakes, more aids were needed to mark hidden offshore hazards, and that the lightship served with the shore light until a permanent structure was completed on Minneapolis Shoal.[^2] The *Daily Press* adds that lightships had been anchored at Eleven Foot Shoal since 1893 and that the new station replaced them.[^4]

## Building the light

The tower was designed under Guy B. Skinner, an architectural and structural engineer for the Bureau of Lighthouses.[^2] According to Lighthouse Friends, Congress paid for it from the Public Works Administration Act of 18 July 1933, appropriating $155,000; the finished station is said to have cost $170,600.30.[^2]

The foundation is a timber crib 64 feet (19.5 m) square, divided into 49 pockets, floated to the site and sunk in 21 feet (6.4 m) of water. The inner pockets were filled with stone and the outer ones with concrete, and a steel plate around the crib protects it from ice.[^2] A marine contractor from Escanaba, B. J. Gallagher, employed forty men on the crib, which was finished in June 1934; the crib was towed out and sunk that summer.[^2] The contract for the lighthouse itself went to Luedtke Engineering Company.[^2]

The steel station is 32 feet (9.8 m) square, with the keepers' quarters over the machinery room and a square tower above them carrying a fourth-order lens 82½ feet (25 m) above the water.[^2] The 1936 description says the light was a white flash of 20,000 candlepower, turned by electricity, with a diaphone fog signal driven by diesel-powered compressors and a radiobeacon whose signal was synchronised with the horn.[^2] The sources differ on when the light began work. The *Daily Press* says the station was built in 1934 and first lit in 1935,[^4] and the U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive gives 1935 as the year established,[^1] while Lighthouse Friends says the concrete deck and the steel lighthouse were finished in 1935.[^2] On the other side, the Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1936 volume says the station had been placed in operation "this season", alongside Peshtigo Reef, and was one of several new fixed stations replacing lightships.[^3] Lighthouse Friends quotes a published description that puts full commissioning at the opening of navigation in 1936, apart from the radiobeacon, and says Skinner, by then a district superintendent, announced the opening on 4 July 1936.[^2]

The Bulletin groups it with North Manitou Shoal and Grays Reef as stations of similar construction on massive piers over timber cribs of roughly 64 to 74 feet (20 to 23 m) square.[^3]

## The keepers

Walter Hanson was the first head keeper, from 1936 to 1941, with two assistants. William Strehl was first assistant in 1936-37 and William L. O'Hern is listed as second assistant until 1937 and first assistant from 1937 to 1952; Benjamin F. Aldrich was second assistant from 1937 to 1940.[^1] Anton M. Jessen, Jr. kept the light from 1942 to 1952, followed by Ole Morey (1952-54) and Harry C. Benjamin (from 1954 to at least 1957).[^1] Peter B. Scott was second assistant from 1940 and is listed as a first assistant before 1954.[^1]

By 1956 the roster changes character: the archive lists John Griffin as Coast Guard from 1956, and from 1960 a long run of Coast Guard men on one- or two-year stints, while the archive's principal-keeper lines for 1960 to 1970 include Walter N. Patterson, Charley F. Hurd, Jr., Daniel L. Ferguson and Eugene Nadeau.[^1] In 1979 Walt Lucas, of Wiscasset, Maine, was the officer in charge.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In October 1938, with Hanson ashore, forty-mile-per-hour winds raised twenty-foot (6 m) waves that swept over the pier and broke in the steel doors at the base of the station; the damage was put at $1,000.[^2]

Ice was the larger trouble. When Jessen and his two assistants came out to open the station in April 1948, ice three feet (0.9 m) thick still surrounded it. Jessen remembered that "When the ice first struck you could feel the lighthouse quiver on its foundation," and that the ice piled up as high as 45 feet (14 m), hiding the windows of the living quarters.[^2] Lighthouse Friends adds that Scott brought a puppy named Skipper to the station that season.[^2] A crew, quoted in a 2026 *Daily Press* article that dates the interview only to some time between 1955 and 1979, said that "The storm of November, 1955, was the worst in the experience of the present crew," with the structure shaking all night and sheathed in ice the next morning.[^4] When Lucas opened the station in April 1979, Green Bay was still frozen, and the moving ice rattled crockery in the cupboards.[^2]

## After the keepers

By the late 1970s the station was one of seven manned lights on Lake Michigan. A Coast Guard crew of three took turns standing watch and was relieved by boat, working two or three weeks on and a week off, and the Escanaba Coast Guard station serviced it weekly.[^2][^4] The station had a reputation as the "bug" capital of the Great Lakes, and a crew member quoted by the paper complained of May flies and then horse flies.[^4] On hot days a rope on the boom let crew members swing out over the water; a sailor who passed in 1973 saw one let go and drop twenty feet (6 m) into the lake.[^2][^4]

The sources disagree on when the crews left. Lighthouse Friends, writing from a 1979 visit, says the light would be automated the following year;[^2] the *Daily Press* says it was automated in 1979, and reports a Coast Guard chief telling the Escanaba paper in 1977 that automation had slipped from 1978 to 1979.[^4]

## Today

The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2006 and still shines through the navigation season.[^4] In May 2015 the Coast Guard declared it excess, and when no eligible organisation came forward the General Services Administration auctioned it. The first sale, begun on 18 August 2016, was ended on 27 October with a high bid of $38,001, after poor weather had prevented an inspection; a second auction in 2017 closed on 21 August with a high bid of $28,000.[^2] The lighthouse stands on state bottomlands.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives its owner as the Coast Guard, and the *Daily Press* in July 2026 says it remains under the Coast Guard's operational authority.[^2][^4]

It is not open to the public and the tower is closed. It is best seen from the water or the air, or, at a very great distance, from the southern tip of Peninsula Point.[^2][^4]
