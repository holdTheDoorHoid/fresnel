Fowey Rocks Light is an iron tower standing in about 10 feet (3 m) of water on a coral reef 6.3 miles south-southeast of Cape Florida, at the northern end of the Florida Reef lights. First lit on 15 June 1878, it is still a working aid to navigation and has long been nicknamed "The Eye of Miami".[^1]

## The place

Fowey Rocks is an offshore reef more than three nautical miles from land, outside Florida state waters, on the western side of the Straits of Florida. It takes its name from HMS *Fowey*, a British warship that was wrecked nearby in 1748.[^1] The National Register nomination describes the waters around it as a corridor that had been busy since colonial times, and as the scene of thousands of shipwrecks and groundings.[^1]

Until the 1870s the only federal light in the neighbourhood was Cape Florida Light, a masonry tower at the south end of Key Biscayne that began operating in 1825.[^1] The Light-House Board came to see it as inadequate for warning mariners of the reef.[^1] The National Park Service puts the problem plainly: ships no longer needed warning of the island, but of the "knife-edged reef" about 5 miles offshore.[^2]

Florida's title to the site passed to the federal government by deed. The National Register nomination dates the deed 1 May 1875; Lighthouse Friends gives 6 January 1875.[^1][^3] Lighthouse Friends adds that Congress allocated $100,000 for the lighthouse on 23 June 1874 and another $75,000 the following year.[^3] The Light-House Board later put the cost of the finished structure at about $163,015.[^4]

## Building the light

The tower was built from plans drawn up in the Light-House Board's own office, and it follows the iron-pile pattern of the earlier Florida Reef lights: nine iron piles, each driven about 10 feet into the live coral rock.[^5] The nomination says the design differed from its predecessors in having a shorter tower with a wider base, five horizontal tiers, and an octagonal two-story keepers' dwelling with a mansard roof in the Second Empire style.[^1]

The work was split among contractors. The nomination says Paulding and Kemble of Cold Spring, New York, made the foundation and first tier, and Pusey, Jones & Company of Wilmington, Delaware, the superstructure.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the superstructure was first let to Brown & Co. of Hellertown, Pennsylvania, which failed to finish it, and Pusey, Jones took over the contract.[^3] Arnold Burges Johnson, writing for the Light-House Board in 1889, says only that the parts "were made by three different contractors".[^5]

Johnson also describes the site work. Materials were "stored at Soldier Key, 4 miles distant" and towed out to the reef "when the weather would permit".[^5] A working platform about 80 feet square was set 12 feet above low water on iron-shod mangrove piles driven into the coral.[^5] Johnson says the main difficulty was the bad weather, and that it was solved by "pitching tents on the working platform", which left a force of workmen "high and dry above the running sea"; the derrick and shears had been put up "in the single day of good weather they had out of a month".[^5] The National Park Service also says the workers frequently stayed in tents on the staging platform.[^2] The nomination dates the start of foundation work to 1875 and the completed first tier to summer 1876, with work resuming in autumn 1876 and "frequent episodes of inclement weather" causing delays.[^1]

Twice during construction, ships ran onto the reef near the work. The nomination names them as the *Arakanapka* and the *Carondelet*.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the *Arakanapka* rammed the reef a few yards from the tower and that the *Carondelet* floated off after throwing most of its cargo overboard.[^3] The National Park Service names a different ship, *Arratoon Apcar*, as one of the two.[^2]

The superstructure went up fast once the iron arrived. Johnson's account has the derrick and shears sent up on 10 March and the dwelling finished on 30 April.[^5] The nomination agrees that the dwelling was complete in April 1878 and says the lantern and lens were installed in May.[^1] The lens was a first-order Fresnel made in Paris by Henry-Lepaute, which stood about 14 feet (4.3 m) tall with its pedestal and rotating machinery and had been shown at the 1876 Centennial Exposition in Philadelphia.[^1] The nomination gives 15 June 1878 as the date the light was first shown.[^1] The National Park Service describes the dwelling as painted white with green shutters.[^2]

The tower is about 130 feet (40 m) tall overall.[^1] The Light-House Board put the light's focal plane at 110 feet (34 m) above the water.[^4] The Board sent a model of it, built to a scale of three-fourths of an inch to the foot, to the 1893 Columbian Exposition in Chicago.[^1] Its report on the exhibit says the model "was made, not for exhibition, but to serve as a guide in planning future structures of like kind".[^4] A watercolour of the light shown with it depicted the tower, in the Board's words, "standing in lonely grandeur with the waves gently lapping its base".[^4]

The light changed over the years. It began as a fixed white light.[^3] The Board's 1891-92 report records that the positions of proposed red sectors were laid out, and Lighthouse Friends dates their addition to April 1893.[^6][^3] A lamp-oil store was hung beneath the dwelling in 1898, and in 1914 new oil tanks and an incandescent oil vapour lamp were installed.[^1] A Lighthouse Service Bulletin summary of changes lists Fowey Rocks as altered from fixed white to group flashing white in 1915.[^7] Lighthouse Friends dates the two-flash group, part of a numbering scheme for the Florida reefs, to around 1930.[^3] In October 1931 the Bulletin reported that the station had been electrified and fitted with a radiobeacon, raising the light from 22,000 to 83,000 candlepower.[^8] A 1933 note in the Bulletin found that all but one of the tension rods in the foundation were the originals, with an average life of 54 years in the tidal zone.[^9]

## The keepers

The first keeper was John W. Frow, who came over from the discontinued Cape Florida Light; his father, Simeon Frow, and Jefferson B. Browne were his assistants.[^1][^10] The USLHS roster also lists a Joseph Frow as second assistant for 1878.[^10] Browne later became a lawyer and in 1916 was elected a judge of the Florida Supreme Court.[^1] Lighthouse Friends calls him a recent high-school graduate and says that after fifteen months of lighthouse service and law study he enrolled at the University of Iowa Law School.[^3]

Lighthouse Friends says Frow's family was tied to the station in another way: one of Simeon's daughters married Robert H. Thompson, who served as second assistant in 1879 and 1880.[^3] The roster has a Robert H. Thompson back at the station as second assistant from 1894 to 1907 and again from 1908 to 1911.[^10]

In its report of 1894 the Board said the station was cared for by three keepers, who received $820, $510 and $490 a year.[^4] Head keepers tended to stay: John J. Larner served from 1880 to 1895, William D. Archer from 1895 to 1905, and Henry P. Weatherford from 1905 to 1922, after earlier years as first assistant.[^10] In 1916 the Bureau of Fisheries thanked Weatherford for the close attention he had given to collecting water samples at the station for it over nearly two years.[^11] Richard J. Palmer, second assistant in the 1910s, was head keeper from 1924 to 1932, and Hamilton S. Perry held the post from 1932 to 1943.[^10]

Keepers of the Lighthouse Service manned the station until 1939, when the service merged with the Coast Guard, and Coast Guard personnel then took over.[^1] Lighthouse Friends reports that a reporter who went out with a census taker in 1950 counted four coastguardsmen on the station.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Lighthouse Friends says the Frows had been aboard their new home for almost three months when a hurricane gave the new tower its first major test. According to that account, the storm raged for three days and loosened the lantern glass; Frow wrote that it was "allowing the rain into the lantern constantly".[^3] The page gives no date for the storm.

The station often helped vessels that struck the reef or the wrecks around it. In May 1884 seven men from the schooner *Marcia Reynolds*, which had sprung a leak and sunk off Sombrero light, had already "obtained a small supply of food from the keeper of the Fowey Rocks light house" before they reached the Fort Lauderdale House of Refuge, the Life-Saving Service reported.[^12]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin records many later cases:

- **February 1914.** Henry P. Weatherford, with a foreman and a working party, assisted the disabled schooner *Alice B. Phillips*.[^13] The nomination spells the name *Philips* and says the crew was rescued by workmen installing new equipment.[^1]
- **7 March 1915.** Weatherford and second assistant Richard Palmer took the five passengers and crew off the yacht *May Belle*, wrecked near the station; the nomination adds that it sank soon after.[^7][^1]
- **28 December 1919.** Weatherford, Palmer and James W. Marshall helped a yacht with two women and three men aboard that had struck part of an old wreck near the station. The women were taken to Miami in the station motor boat and the keepers helped repair the yacht.[^14]
- **28 March 1923.** Keeper Eley J. Whidden helped rescue five people from a launch that struck the wreckage of a schooner and sank.[^15]
- **6 November 1927.** Palmer, assistant keeper Robert J. Moore and additional keeper Hamilton S. Perry helped a yacht with four people aboard that had struck a submerged obstruction a few hundred feet from the station.[^17]
- **2 January 1929.** The yacht *Linda Lou* hit the same submerged wreck that had holed another yacht two months earlier, and came to the station for help. The Bulletin says Palmer could not repair it in the heavy seas, so crew and passengers were moved into the station with as much equipment as could be saved, and the yacht sank.[^18] Lighthouse Friends tells it differently: it says that the keepers were told to cut the yacht loose so a Coast Guard boat could tow it.[^3]
- **8 December (1933).** The small tanker *R. Ogario* was driven by a strong tide onto a piling about 100 feet north of the station while trying to deliver fuel. The assistant keeper took the master, his wife and two crew off the sinking vessel in three trips of the station boat.[^19][^3]

In the October 1926 hurricane the keepers estimated a wind of about 80 miles per hour at Fowey Rocks, and only minor damage was reported; Sombrero Key and Alligator Reef, farther down the chain, lost parts of their landings.[^16] The nomination says the 1935 hurricane stripped the wooden decking from the tower's first tier, which had been laid in 1927.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends reports one death: a coastguardsman named Riles, who served on the light during the Second World War, fell into the water while trying to climb aboard on 23 December 1942, was swept under the lighthouse and was killed when his head struck a brace.[^3] The same page tells how, in 1950, a Miami Daily News reporter was knocked into the sea by a wave as she stepped from a rowboat to the ladder, and a photographer's picture of her in midair won an Associated Press prize.[^3]

## After the keepers

The nomination says a radio beacon was installed in 1943 with its transmission tower on top of the lantern, and that the beacon moved to the mainland in 1972, though the tower stayed until 1996.[^1] The 1931 Bulletin item cited above reports a radiobeacon fitted at the station twelve years earlier, a difference the sources do not explain.[^8]

Fowey Rocks was the last of the great Florida Reef lights to be automated, in 1974, when the Coast Guard's resident crew left, according to the nomination and Lighthouse Friends.[^1][^3] The National Park Service says 1972.[^20] The nomination describes a rapid run of equipment changes after that: an automatic lamp changer in 1974, a windmill generator tried in 1975, a flash-tube array powered by solar panels in 1982, an acrylic optic in 1983, and the present automated VRB-25 beacon in 1997. The stairways and boat davits came off in the late 1970s.[^1] The first-order Fresnel lens was removed when the flash-tube array went in, in the nomination's account; Lighthouse Friends says about ten years after automation.[^1][^3] The nomination records it on display in the Aids to Navigation classroom building at the Coast Guard Training Center, Yorktown, Virginia.[^1]

The Coast Guard nominated the light for the National Register in 2010, and it was listed on 26 January 2011.[^20][^21] Lighthouse Friends says it was offered for transfer in 2011.[^3] Because the light lies inside the boundaries of Biscayne National Park, the National Park Service asked for a direct transfer, and on 2 October 2012 the park accepted ownership at no cost from the Coast Guard.[^20] The Florida Trust for Historic Preservation listed it among the state's eleven most endangered sites in 2015.[^3]

## Today

The tower belongs to the National Park Service and stands within Biscayne National Park; the Coast Guard still maintains the light as an aid to navigation.[^2][^20] The nomination describes a VRB-25 beacon showing a white flash every 10 seconds, visible for 15 miles in clear weather, with red sectors on the north and south arcs to mark hazardous water.[^1] That is the nomination's description from 2010, and the Coast Guard's current Light List is the place to check today's characteristic.

The lighthouse is reached only by boat and is not open to visitors. The nomination says so, and the Park Service said in 2012 that it was closed because of safety concerns while it considered repairs and "potentially provide for visitor access in the future".[^1][^20] Lighthouse Friends advises that the best views come by boat or plane, and that very distant views are possible from Cape Florida Lighthouse.[^3]
