Point Bonita Lighthouse sits at the end of a rocky point on the north side of the Golden Gate, in Marin County, California. To reach it a visitor passes through a tunnel cut through the ridge and crosses a suspension bridge; the National Register nomination calls it the only lighthouse in the United States approached by a suspension bridge.[^1] Inside is the second-order Fresnel lens first lit in 1855, now in a tower that is not on its original hill.[^1][^2]

## The place

Point Bonita is a sharp, rocky headland on the northern entrance to San Francisco Bay. The U.S. Coast Survey had recommended a lighthouse there as early as 1850, but the National Register nomination records that Congress did not fund it until 1853, the last of the four Bay-area lights the Survey had urged.[^1] Local mariners are said to have argued against the Lighthouse Board's first choice of Point Lobos on the south side, because Point Bonita could be approached within 150 yards in rough weather.[^2] Congress appropriated $25,000 on 3 March 1853.[^2][^1]

The nomination records that the sidewheel steamer *Tennessee* was wrecked near the point on 6 March 1853, three days after the appropriation, and that all of her roughly 1,000 passengers and crew reached shore at the cove that now bears her name.[^1] Construction was nonetheless delayed. In August 1853 the Superintendent of Lights wrote to the Secretary of the Treasury asking for speed, and on 29 September 1854 the Branch Pilots Association and a group of merchant ship masters petitioned against any more delay. Authority to solicit contracts reached the district's lighthouse inspector on 15 November 1854.[^1]

## Building the light

Local contractors built the first station.[^1] LighthouseFriends names them as Hofras and Cowing and describes a 56-foot (17 m) conical brick tower 260 feet (79 m) above the sea, with a brick-and-stone cottage nearby; the nomination also gives 260 feet for the tower's elevation.[^2][^1] The nomination adds that the Board departed from the usual early West Coast plan of a tower rising from the dwelling: the tower stood on the highest ground and the keepers' house about 440 yards inland and lower, probably because the summit was too small for both.[^1] The first head keeper, Edward Colston, lit the second-order lens for the first time on 2 May 1855.[^2][^8]

The height proved to be the flaw. The nomination says the light was too high to be seen through the low clouds that rolled in through the Golden Gate, and that the Point Bonita light was often hidden by fog at about the 300-foot level while lower ground stayed clear.[^1] The sources disagree on the elevation: Putnam's 1917 history of the service gives the first light as 306 feet (93 m) above the sea and the rebuilt one as 124 feet (38 m), where the nomination and LighthouseFriends have 260 feet (79 m) for the first.[^3][^1][^2]

The remedy took twenty years. According to LighthouseFriends, a steam fog siren went into service at the lower site in May 1872; a landslide undermined it two years later, so about 25 feet (7.6 m) was cut from the point, and the siren was back in operation on 1 January 1875.[^2] The nomination dates the first siren building to 1872, its partial loss to a winter storm two years on, and the levelling to 1875; the National Park Service gives 1874 for the steam siren.[^1][^9] Congress provided another $25,000 on 3 March 1875 to move the light itself.[^2] The nomination says construction was difficult because of the precarious terrain, and describes a landing, derrick and incline tramway installed in 1872, and a tunnel 118 feet (36 m) long, 6 feet 4 inches high and 6 feet wide at the base, cut in 1876 after landslides in 1872, 1874 and 1876 had damaged the path.[^1] LighthouseFriends adds that the railroad's Chinese tunnel workers were brought in to dig it.[^2]

The new light went into service on 1 February 1877. The lens was not new: the old lantern room and lens were lifted off the 1855 tower and set on a new one-story brick base, and the nomination says the combination is unusual among California lighthouses.[^1][^2] The old tower stayed on the hill as a daymark. LighthouseFriends says the War Department demanded its removal in 1907.[^2]

## The keepers

The roster compiled by the U.S. Lighthouse Society lists Edward A. Colston as principal keeper from 1854 to 1856.[^8] The first years were hard to staff. By March 1856, ten months after opening, the nomination counts two keepers and five assistants already come and gone.[^1] Putnam, quoting a keeper of 1855, gives the reasons: "There are no inhabitants within five miles of this point," and the only way from San Francisco was whatever sailboat could be hired, at $5; his first assistant, he wrote, would take the post only if their salaries were made equal, and then stay just four months.[^3] The nomination adds that supplies meant a steamboat from Sausalito after a three-hour trek over mountainous terrain, and that government pay bought little in Gold Rush San Francisco.[^1]

The station's first fog signal was a cannon, and it needed someone to fire it. Edward Maloney (Putnam spells it Malony), a retired army sergeant, was ordered on 6 August 1855 to fire a cannon every half hour during fog, day or night. Two months later he wrote: "I have been up three days and nights, had only two hours rest."[^3][^2] The sources disagree on the gun and on its end; LighthouseFriends also dates the firing orders to 8 August 1855, against Putnam's 6 August.[^2] Putnam calls it a 24-pounder and says it was dropped in 1857 as too expensive and ineffective, with powder alone costing nearly $2,000 a year; the Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1915 calls it a 10-pounder borrowed from the Benicia Arsenal in July 1855 and used until the fall of 1857; the nomination has a cannon installed on 6 August 1856 and replaced by a mechanically struck bell on 18 March 1858.[^3][^7][^1]

The longest-serving of the early heads was John B. Brown. The roster has him as second assistant in 1872, first assistant in 1873 and principal keeper from 1874 to 1901.[^8] LighthouseFriends credits him and his assistants with saving more than forty lives, and says ship owners and merchants presented him with a gold-headed cane in 1896.[^2] John F. Ingersoll followed for 1901 to 1929, then George F. Watters for 1929 to 1942.[^8]

Women appear in the roster as assistants in 1869. Mary A. Place is listed as first assistant in 1869, the year Henry M. Place was principal keeper, and Catharine Murphy as first assistant from 1869 to 1871, while Cornelius Murphy was principal keeper from 1869 to 1872.[^8] The roster does not say how any of them were related. Cornelius Murphy appears again as a second and third assistant between 1872 and 1878.[^8] The steam siren brought more staff: LighthouseFriends says a second and third assistant keeper were added in the early 1870s to handle the extra work.[^2]

By the 1900s families lived on the point. The 1906 earthquake struck the assistants' original dwelling at 5:13 a.m. LighthouseFriends quotes the station log: "Terrible earthquake occurred at 5:13 a.m. doing considerable damage to the assts. Quarters." Third Assistant Peter S. Admiral and the family of First Assistant Hermann Engel got out unhurt, according to that account, and some people lived in the old fog-signal building until a new double dwelling was finished in 1908.[^2][^8] LighthouseFriends also says that Alexander Martin, who lived in the later assistant's dwelling with his wife and young children, tethered the children in harnesses when they played outside, and that a daughter was once found dangling over the cliff in hers.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends reports that in October 1874 the 139-ton tug *Rescue* ran onto the rocks and Brown and his fellow keepers saved nine men.[^2]

On 26 October 1893 the Pacific Mail steamship *City of New York*, bound out of San Francisco for Japan and China, was wrecked on the point in dense fog. Her crew of 104 and 133 Chinese passengers were all saved.[^5][^4] The crews of the Fort Point and Golden Gate Park life-saving stations transferred the passengers to tugs and then moved $241,000 in mail and specie; the steamer's commander wrote that they landed the treasure shipments without the loss of a package.[^4] Her wreck was sold to a salvager, T. A. Whitelaw, who placed his wrecking schooner *Samson* near it; she was of 217 tons.[^5]

The wreck of the *Samson* is told at length in the Life-Saving Service's report for 1895. A gale rose from the southeast on 3 January 1895. The report says the schooner's crew flew an old ensign, a blanket and a white sheet, but the storm brought "heavy rain, thunder, and lightning." The report says the only people who appear to have seen the signals were the keeper and assistants at Point Bonita.[^5] The report quotes Brown saying that the schooner "was flying a bedspread all the afternoon, but no one seemed to take any notice of her."[^5] A boat from the *Samson* came to the lighthouse wharf, and Brown said he and the schooner's men agreed that if help were needed the schooner would blow a long and a short blast and he would sound the siren for the life-saving crews. He said the schooner blew the agreed signal about 4:20 p.m., and that ten minutes later he started the siren and sounded it at short intervals for half an hour.[^5] The report says the siren was "little more than barely perceptible" on the schooner, less than half a mile away, and could not carry to the stations more than three miles off against the gale, the rain and the roar of the surf.[^5] Keeper Hodgson of the Fort Point station took a lifeboat out behind a tug in the dark; the report gives the wind at times as seventy miles an hour on the Point Lobos signal station's record. It also says the keeper of the lighthouse reported that the lighthouse men who went outside were "frequently compelled to crawl upon their hands and knees to avoid being blown over the bluff."[^5] The *Samson* was lost on 4 January and three of her crew were drowned; the tug took eight men from one of her boats.[^5]

On 11 December 1932 the fog signals at the Golden Gate stations, Point Bonita among them, were sounded for about two and a half hours because of a heavy fall of snow. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin believed it to be the first time snow had required it, adding that no earlier such entry was found in the stations' records.[^6] LighthouseFriends gives the year as 1931; the Bulletin's own account says 1932.[^6][^2]

LighthouseFriends also recounts a wreck of 31 December 1979, when a tug lost two tows in swells of nearly forty feet: one barge, loaded with lumber, beer and paper, went on the rocks, while a second, carrying chlorine, drifted to a softer shore and its cargo was lifted off by air.[^2]

## After the keepers

The National Register nomination says the light was automated in 1980, the last in California, and that the Coast Guard removed a watch room from the roof in 1980 to restore the building's original look before turning the station over to the National Park Service in 1981 and opening it for tours in 1984.[^1] LighthouseFriends names Mark Van Buskirk, the last head keeper, as leaving in April 1981, living in Coast Guard housing built at the former life-saving station.[^2] The roster lists him for 1980 to 1981.[^8] The two sources differ by a year on when resident keepers ended.

Reaching the lighthouse has repeatedly been a problem. Erosion cut a gap in the trail in 1940, after which, LighthouseFriends says, a breeches buoy and then a wooden causeway served until the suspension bridge of 1954, built, according to the nomination, to span the unrepairable chasm.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends reports that a federal highway report found the rusting bridge a danger, that it was closed in 2010, and that a replacement of similar design (about $1 million) reopened access in April 2012.[^2]

## Today

The National Park Service says the Point Bonita Trail, which leads down to a viewing platform, is open only on the third Saturday of each month from 12:30 to 3:30 p.m., and the suspension bridge is closed until further notice.[^9] LighthouseFriends reports the bridge closed since August 2024 and says the Coast Guard continues to maintain the light and fog signal.[^2] The sources differ on ownership: the nomination says the station passed to the Park Service in 1981, while LighthouseFriends calls it Coast Guard property scheduled for transfer.[^1][^2] The lens is, according to the nomination, still the original second-order Fresnel, still operating.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the interior is typically accessible but the tower is not; check the Park Service calendar before you go, since the station lies within the Golden Gate National Recreation Area.[^9][^2]
