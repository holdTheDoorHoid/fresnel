Lower Néguac Rear Range Light is a white wooden tower on the beach at Lower Néguac, on the north-east side of Miramichi Bay in New Brunswick.[^1][^3] Paired with a light on the Lower Néguac wharf, it formed a range, two lights in line.[^1] The tower's light was put out in 2003.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Miramichi Bay is a shallow, funnel-shaped estuary, cut off from the Gulf of St. Lawrence by barrier islands.[^2] Along the entrance to the inner bay, a string of sandy islets is broken by a few narrow channels, or gullies, that vessels can pass through.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends says Jean Sovie settled Neguac in 1757, two years after the expulsion of the Acadians.[^1] In 1866 Lower Néguac was a farming, fishing and lumbering community of about 32 families, nine of them Savoy families, and in 1871 it had a population of 150.[^7] Lighthouse Friends and Rowlett spell the name without the accent, and the quotations here keep their spelling.[^1][^2]

Earlier lights had been set in two gullies, one at Neguac and one at Tabusintac. Lighthouse Friends says a light went up on the southern end of Neguac Sandbar in 1873 to guide ships through Neguac Gully.[^1] At Tabusintac Gully a main tower was built on Crab Island in 1873, and a beacon was added two years later to form a range. The gully shoaled, and the Tabusintac range was discontinued.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1891 the Department of Marine invited tenders to move the abandoned Tabusintac range lights to Lower Néguac.[^1] The lowest tender, $245, was considered excessive, so the Department sent W. H. Noble to move the lights. He did it in October 1891 for $205.07, and the range was lit when navigation opened in 1892.[^1][^2]

The front light was a lens lantern on a 5 m (16 ft) mast with a shed at its base. It stood on the east end of the cribwork that helped form the Lower Néguac wharf.[^1] The mast and shed were originally painted brown, and diamond-shaped slats on the mast made it conspicuous as a daymark.[^1] Its fixed white light stood 6 m (20 ft) above high water and could be seen from up to nine miles; the source does not say whether those are nautical miles.[^1]

The rear light was a wooden tower with an enclosed lantern, placed on the beach 320 m (1,050 ft) from the front light. Lighthouse Friends gives the tower as 10 m (33 ft) from base to the vane of the lantern.[^1] It gives its fixed white light as about 10 m (32 ft) above high water, seen for ten miles over a small arc on each side of the range line.[^1] Rowlett gives the tower as 8 m (28 ft), square and pyramidal, painted white with red trim, with a red lantern and a red vertical stripe on the range line.[^2] Lighthouse Digest describes an octagonal lantern and a red stripe on the side facing the alignment.[^3] The sources do not agree on the height.

The front light changed twice more in the 1890s.[^1] In 1895 it became a fixed red light and was raised 3 m (10 ft).[^1] In 1896 a new mast and shed went up beside the wharf roadway, about 14 m (45 ft) south-west of the old site, with the light 7.6 m (25 ft) above high water.[^1]

In 1929 the rear light was moved to a newly bought site 366 m (1,200 ft) from the wharf light.[^1] Lighthouse Digest says the structure was altered in 1929 and again in 1935, and gives no detail.[^3]

## The keepers

Charles McIntosh kept the lights from 1892 to 1916, the longest stint on the Lighthouse Friends list.[^1] The Department's keepers' statement for the year ended 30 June 1904 lists "Mcintosh, Chas" against "Lower Neguac Wharf Lights", with an appointment date of 19 December 1892. The statement for 1905 gives the same station name.[^4][^5] The statement for the year ended 30 June 1901 calls the station "Neguac Range Lights".[^6]

Edward McIntosh followed from 1916 to 1921.[^1] Mrs. Eliza Palmer kept the lights from 1921 to 1927, and she is the only woman on the Lighthouse Friends list.[^1] Peter Comeau is listed for 1927 alone.[^1] Peter D. Forbes kept the lights from 1927 to 1931, and Edmund Martin from 1931 to 1945.[^1]

Zeph J. Savoy was the last keeper, from 1945 until the range was automated in 1957.[^1]

## After the keepers

The range was automated in 1957.[^1] The rear light was decommissioned in 2003.[^1][^2][^3] Rowlett says the front light, a small skeletal tower on the wharf, stayed in service for several years after that and has since been deactivated too.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says an official light is still shown from the wharf.[^1] The two sources disagree about the front light.

## Today

Lighthouse Friends says the Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and that its grounds and tower are closed.[^1] Rowlett lists the Coast Guard as owner with a question mark, names a private site manager, and marks the site and tower closed as private property.[^2] Lighthouse Digest says the lighthouse is on private property and not open to the public, though it can easily be seen from the beach.[^3] The tower stays closed to visitors and stands on private property.[^1][^2][^3]
