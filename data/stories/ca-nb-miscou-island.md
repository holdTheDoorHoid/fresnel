A wooden tower of eight panels built independently of one another, white with red trim, stands on Birch Point at the north-east tip of Miscou Island, New Brunswick, where the Gulf of St. Lawrence meets the mouth of Chaleur Bay. It was first lit in the autumn of 1856, and it is still a working light.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Ships bound into Chaleur Bay, and coasters travelling between the Atlantic provinces and Quebec, passed this point on a flat, open island. The heritage statements stress the coastal traffic, and the second adds that the bay had considerable shipbuilding and a trade in lumber and fish.[^2][^3] The tower's setting on the flat island is part of its listed character.[^3] In 1853 the Commissioners of Lights for the Gulf of St. Lawrence argued for a light here because shipping accidents happened there every year. In 1854 merchants and residents of Bathurst petitioned the New Brunswick House of Assembly, and Lloyd's agent at Bathurst added a letter pointing to "the great number of casualties to shipping on the Island of Miscou, at the entrance of this Bay".[^1]

## Building the light

The colony's Lighthouse Committee at first said the fund could not stand the cost, then raised the light tax to pay for it. On 31 March 1855 £1,200 was appropriated for a lighthouse and keeper's dwelling, and on 25 August 1855 James Murray of Newcastle won a £1,220 contract for the tower, dwelling and woodshed, to be finished by 1 September 1856.[^1] The lantern came from England and arrived on 10 October 1856. William Hay, keeper at Point Escuminac, was sent to install the lamps and reflectors, and he lit the light on 4 November. The whole work cost £2,200.[^1]

The first light was fixed and red, from eight lamps with reflectors.[^1] The heritage statement notes that the octagonal tower was built as eight panels made independently of one another, which it calls an unusual technique.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives the new tower as 74 feet (about 22.5 m) from base to the top of the lantern vane; the Register's statement says it was raised from 22.5 to 24.3 m in 1903, and the national historic site plaque says 74 feet extended to 80.[^1][^2] In 1860 "smoke conductors" were added because the draught in calm weather did not clear smoke from the lantern room.[^1]

A steam fog whistle in its own building, 107 yards (98 m) east of the tower, began working in June 1875 and cost $6,055.[^1] It lay silent through 1888 awaiting a new boiler and resumed on 24 September 1889.[^1] In July 1903 it was stopped for a few days for repairs to its machinery.[^4] A revolving catoptric apparatus was installed for the spring of 1894.[^1] The Marine Branch report printed in 1910 covers the year to 31 March 1909. In it the department recorded work on the tower "to accommodate the new illuminating apparatus", costing $757.89, and a third-order dioptric light showing two flashes every 7½ seconds replacing the old revolving white light.[^5][^6] Lighthouse Friends dates the tower alteration to 1908 and says the lens was made in Paris in 1907.[^1] A diaphone replaced the steam plant in the fiscal year ended 31 March 1921.[^7]

## The keepers

George McConnell, "a practical engineer and mechanic" chosen from thirteen applicants, took over from Hay at £125 a year and stayed until his death in 1877.[^1] Robert Rivers succeeded him at $800 a year, paying David Bell from that to run the fog alarm.[^1] Rivers is listed at Miscou in the Department of Marine's keeper table for fiscal 1901, and Joseph L. Robichaud in the table for fiscal 1906.[^8][^9] Lighthouse Friends lists the later keepers as John A. Ward (1912 to 1940), John Lester Marks (1940 to 1957), Arthur Chiasson (1957 to 1983) and Robert Chiasson (1984 to 1988).[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

No individual wreck at the station is named in the sources used here; what they record is the sea eating away the point itself. In 1938 seven groins were built to hold the shore, but Lighthouse Friends reports they ended above the low-water line and made erosion worse; by 1942 the fix would have needed longer groins and a seawall, and since the land did not justify that cost, the plan became to move the tower.[^1] Work began on 1 August 1946 and the tower reached its new site 200 feet (61 m) inland on 13 September.[^1][^2]

## After the keepers

The light is now automated.[^2] Lighthouse Friends reports that the tower closed to visitors in 2001 over mercury contamination in the lantern room and lead paint in the soil. The mercury bath that floated the lens had been removed in 1995. The province put nearly $2 million into Miscou as an eco-tourism destination, including parking, washrooms and a lawn in 2009; the tower reopened in 2010, with $250,000 spent on siding and other work in 2017.[^1] It was designated a National Historic Site in 1974.[^3]

## Today

The tower and land belong to the Canadian Coast Guard; the Province of New Brunswick manages the site.[^10] Opening seasons differ by source: Lighthouse Friends says May to the October long weekend, and the Lighthouse Directory says the tower is open daily mid-June to mid-September, so check locally before travelling.[^1][^10] The road ends at the lighthouse (Route 113).[^1]
