Race Point Light stands in the dunes at the northwest tip of Cape Cod, in Provincetown, Massachusetts. The first tower here, built of rubblestone, was lit in 1816; the white cast-iron tower that replaced it in 1876 still shows a light. It stands about two miles out over the sands of Cape Cod National Seashore.[^1][^2][^12]

## The place

The 1981 survey for the National Register calls Race Point the main light guarding the entrance to Provincetown Harbor, and its chronology for the station notes more than 100 wrecks between 1816 and 1946.[^1] The point takes its name from the strong crosscurrent, a "race", that boats meet when they round the tip of the Cape.[^2] LighthouseFriends quotes the old *American Coast Pilot* on the stretch of shore leading to it: "Accordingly, the strand is everywhere covered with the fragments of vessels."[^2]

Merchants and mariners of Provincetown had been asking for a light on the point since 1808.[^2][^3]

## Building the light

Congress voted $8,000 for lights at Race Point, Point Gammon in Massachusetts and Petit Manan in Maine in April 1816. LighthouseFriends gives the date as 17 April; New England Lighthouses gives 27 April.[^2][^3] The first plan was a 20-foot (6 m) octagonal wooden tower; what was built instead was a 25-foot (7.6 m) tower of rubblestone, with a covered passage from the keeper's kitchen to the stone dwelling.[^2][^3] It was first lit on 5 November 1816, with Joshua Dyer as keeper.[^2] The notice to mariners promised "a revolving Light, to distinguish it from the one on the Highland of Cape Cod."[^3]

Lieutenant Carpender's 1838 survey saw the light with its ten lamps backed by 13-inch reflectors.[^2] The sources differ on when that arrangement began: the National Register survey says a revolving light with ten lamps and 13-inch reflectors was installed in 1838, while New England Lighthouses says the 1816 light was already a revolving one with ten whale-oil lamps, each with a 13-inch reflector.[^1][^3]

I. W. P. Lewis, inspecting in 1842, found the tower had no foundation and leaked, three panes of the lantern were broken and the reflectors were out of line. He judged the light so weak that, "when a fleet of fishermen are anchored in Herring cove, close by, a stranger would hardly be able to distinguish it from the lights set on board these vessels." The same visit found the dwelling "in very good repair, and most neatly kept", A separate inspection that year said the station boat would not last five minutes in a rough sea.[^2] A five-foot brick extension was added to the tower in 1845.[^2]

A fog bell was installed in 1852, at a cost of $2,500 according to the National Register survey.[^1] LighthouseFriends and New England Lighthouses put a fourth-order Fresnel lens in the tower in 1855, the year an assistant keeper was added to look after the bell; the archive's place record gives 1876 for the original lens.[^2][^3][^9] The Light-House Board's report for 1863 listed the work of the year before: the tower "partly taken down and rebuilt", a new lantern, and the bell moved near the dwelling.[^2] In 1873 a 12-inch steam whistle in a new wooden building replaced the bell, and a house for assistants followed in 1874.[^2][^3] The National Register survey instead says the whistle began operation in 1874.[^1]

The tower itself was failing. The Board's 1875 report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, said the lime in the mortar had disappeared and the tower had grown so leaky that it was covered with shingles.[^2] The replacement, finished in 1876, is of cast iron lined with brick and cost $2,800.[^1][^2][^3] LighthouseFriends and New England Lighthouses give its height as 45 feet (14 m); the National Register survey says 40 feet (12 m).[^1][^2][^3] The fourth-order lens moved into it.[^2][^3] A new frame dwelling was built for the head keeper and the old stone house came down.[^2] The National Register survey dates the brick oil house to 1876; LighthouseFriends says the present fog signal building dates from 1888 and the oil house from 1902.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

Joshua Dyer, a native of Truro, kept the light until he died in November 1822, aged 37. His brother Elijah took over and stayed until 1847.[^3][^9] Lieutenant Edward Carpender's 1838 survey praised the light's value, but when he called near the middle of the forenoon he found Elijah Dyer away from home and the lamps, reflectors and glass not yet prepared for the night.[^2] James Cushman, keeper from 1870 to 1885, had been wounded in Virginia during the Civil War, and one history says he was removed after inspections found neglect of duty.[^3][^9]

Promotion could be quick. The archive's roster lists Thomas V. Mullins as second assistant in 1881, first assistant in 1882 and principal keeper from 1885 to 1892, and Charles A. Havender as second assistant from 1888, then principal keeper from 1892 to 1904. If each is one man, both climbed the ladder at Race Point.[^9]

In the Light-House Board's reports of 1899, 1900 and 1901 the station is described as hard to staff. The assistants' dwelling had one outer door, and its hall and stairs were shared by two families. The Board wrote that the families were "deprived of privacy" and that this was "unfavorable to retaining assistants". The keeper's own house had two rooms on each floor. The Board asked for $2,800 to remodel both, and in 1901 the materials for turning the assistants' house from single to double arrived, a cellar was dug and the frame of the addition went up.[^4][^5][^6]

William H. Lowther was head keeper from 1915 to 1935. He had joined the Lighthouse Service on the tender *Mayflower* in 1906 and served at Thacher Island and the Narrows before Race Point.[^3][^9] His son walked more than two miles each way in soft sand to school, and Mrs. Lowther later said she watched several wrecks from the station, one of which, with two men drowning, kept her away from the light for a week.[^2][^3] James W. Hinckley, first assistant from 1920 and principal keeper from 1935, first carried groceries two miles over the sand, then rode a horse. He then rebuilt a Ford into a dune buggy, cutting the trip to town from 75 minutes to 30.[^2][^3][^9] The wind, he said, often reached a mile a minute, and gusts could throw a person several feet.[^2] He retired at Christmas 1937, aged 70, saying keepers' wives deserved pensions.[^2]

The roster shows Coast Guard crews from the 1940s.[^9] The wife of one assistant keeper of that time later recalled water drawn by a pump from a cistern, a refrigerator run by a kerosene lamp, and a Coast Guard jeep with sand tires for trips to town.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

For the steam whistle, the Light-House Board's reports give hours of running each year: about 830 hours and 36 tons of coal in the year ended June 1899, 694 hours and 30 tons in 1900, and 699 hours and 31 tons in 1901.[^4][^5][^6] A revolving machine was installed in 1899.[^4]

In November 1893 the *Myrtle*, sent to the station to carry lumber, anchored off the point, and a Mr. Wallace who went with her made observations on the audibility of the 10-inch and 12-inch whistles. He asked the keeper to get up steam, and in conversation learned that captains had often complained that the sound was never heard far to windward. Captain Samuel Fisher of the Race Point life-saving station told him that in a strong southeast wind seamen had the greatest difficulty in making the signal: "A sort of gap in the contour of the land runs from the whistle."[^7]

A bulletin of 1924 carried a keeper's report that on 26 June he saw the two Cape Ann towers with the naked eye, 37 miles away, for the first time in nine years.[^8]

## After the keepers

Electricity came in 1957. Three years later the Coast Guard pulled down the Gothic Revival duplex, and in 1961 modernized the other dwelling for $20,000.[^1][^10] In 1971 the Coast Guard proposed demolishing everything but the tower. The National Register survey records plans in 1986 to rehabilitate the station with the National Park Service instead.[^1] The archive and LighthouseFriends give 1972 for automation; the National Register survey and the American Lighthouse Foundation give 1978.[^1][^2][^9][^11]

Empty, the station became popular with vandals.[^2] In 1995 the Coast Guard leased the keeper's house and grounds to the New England Lighthouse Foundation, now the American Lighthouse Foundation, whose volunteers restored it. Overnight stays began in 1998. A solar system followed in 2003, and a wind turbine in 2007.[^2][^10][^11] The old Fresnel lens was reported in 1981 to be on display at the Provincetown Coast Guard Station.[^1]

## Today

The tower is an active aid to navigation owned by the Coast Guard, with a solar-powered VRB-25 optic, and licensed to the American Lighthouse Foundation.[^9][^11] The foundation's tours let visitors climb on Sundays from July to October, with a suggested donation of $5; they can be cancelled for nesting piping plovers. The station is reached on foot over the sand or by four-wheel-drive vehicle with a permit, and there is no public transport. Overnight stays in the keeper's house run May to October. Check the foundation's schedule before going.[^12]
