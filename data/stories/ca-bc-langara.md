The Langara Point light is a white hexagonal concrete tower on a cliff edge at the north-west tip of Langara Island, the northernmost island of Haida Gwaii, British Columbia.[^1][^8] It was built in 1912–13 to mark Dixon Entrance, the sea route to the new railway port of Prince Rupert, and it is still a staffed light station.[^1][^6][^9]

## The place

Haida Gwaii, off the northern coast of British Columbia, is separated from the mainland by Hecate Strait, with Alaska on the other side of Dixon Entrance to the north.[^1] The Grand Trunk Pacific Railway, begun in 1905, was to end at Prince Rupert, which had the advantage of being nearer to Asia than Vancouver. Lighthouse Friends' account says the Department of Marine saw that the coming railway needed a major landfall light on the north end of Haida Gwaii.[^1] Commander Frederick Learmonth sailed north aboard the *Egeria* to select sites for aids to navigation along Dixon Entrance and recommended the island's highest spot; the department's chief engineer, William P. Anderson, rejected the 159 m (523 ft) summit as too high given the fog "on all that coast", and chose a plateau on Langara Point instead.[^1] The Canadian Register of Historic Places likewise ties the tower to the growth of trans-oceanic traffic to Prince Rupert.[^6]

## Building the light

Work began in 1912 and, according to Lighthouse Friends, involved clearing five acres of timber and building a hexagonal tower, a double dwelling and a 670 m (2,200 ft) tramway down to a boathouse in a sheltered cove.[^1] The department's report for the year ended March 1912 already lists work under way at Langara, with a Class "E" fog alarm plant and "a first order single flashing light" among the equipment to be provided.[^2] That is earlier than Lighthouse Friends' start of 1912. The report for the year ended March 1913 lists the station's work as a 30-foot reinforced concrete tower, fog-alarm building, double dwelling, boathouse and outbuildings.[^3] The sources disagree on the tower's height: the heritage register gives 7.6 m (25 ft), the contemporary report 30 feet (9.1 m).[^3][^8]

Lighthouse Friends gives the lens from Chance Brothers as costing $14,225.27, the fog alarm $10,200.93 and the whole station, apart from those two, $64,585.44, with R. Chrystal and D. McLean supervising the day labourers.[^1] The same account describes a dispute over the cost of feeding the workers, and says horses were needed to haul supplies along a mile-long corduroy road from a more sheltered landing, because the cove below the station could be used only in perfect conditions.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates a derrick to 1915 and an aerial tramway to 1917.[^1] The department's report for the year ended March 1915 lists the installation of a derrick at Langara, and its report for 1916–17 says the government steamer's crew installed an aerial tramway there.[^4][^5]

The Chance Heritage Trust notes that the first-order Chance Brothers lens in use here is the only one of its size still in service in Canada.[^9]

## The keepers

James Forsyth, who had spent four years at Egg Island, arrived on 9 September 1913 with his wife Ellen and daughter Evelyn. The supply steamer *Estevan* came every six months, bringing a backlog of newspapers that the family stacked and read in order; Evelyn later remembered that they "came up for air" for the latest news.[^1] Forsyth asked for a transfer in 1918, wanting his teenage daughter nearer to other people, and the Race Rocks head keeper retired later that year.[^1]

In the Second World War the lighthouse was painted camouflage green and a radar station was built to monitor the North Pacific.[^6] William Norman Kinnear, head keeper from 1943 to 1945, went to Langara as his first posting after the family lost their house and chicken ranch for back taxes; his daughter Norma later said he looked after the light and the fog horn and sent weather messages in Morse code.[^10] Two radio operators lived in the other half of the duplex and, in her account, more or less took the place of an assistant keeper.[^10]

Ed Hartt came in 1957 after postings at Lawyer Island, Barrett Rock and Triple Island. Rats, introduced to the island somehow around the Second World War, were the first thing the Hartts met: traps set by the keepers caught about eight a night, and in one week Hartt shot fifty-nine with his revolver.[^1] The rats also drove burrow-nesting seabirds from the island, and a bait-station programme in 1995 removed them in under four weeks.[^1]

## After the keepers

Langara is still a staffed station. Lighthouse Friends lists its head keepers from Forsyth in 1913 to Olaf Wypior, in post from 2023 and recorded there in 2024.[^1] In July 2024 the Canadian Coast Guard said that "the twenty-five other staffed light stations" on the British Columbia coast would continue as before, after ending keeper duties at Carmanah Point and Pachena Point.[^12]

## Today

The tower is a tapered reinforced-concrete hexagon with a metal lantern and six buttresses, and its first-order Fresnel lens still sends out a white flash every five seconds.[^1][^7][^8] The Government of Canada recognised it as a Heritage Lighthouse on 12 February 2015.[^8] Seven buildings are listed with it: three keepers' dwellings dating from 1962 and 1965 (two single, one double), a 1938 boathouse, a 1975 engine and radio-beacon room, a 1965 workshop and a 2000 flammables building.[^8] The Canadian Coast Guard List of Lights places the light on the north-west point of the island.[^11]

This is a working, staffed station on a remote island. Lighthouse Friends says it is not easy to reach: its author flew to Masset and chartered a plane to fly over it, and records the lighthouse as owned by the Canadian Coast Guard, with the grounds open and the tower closed.[^1] It is a staffed station on a remote island, so it is not a place to turn up unannounced.
