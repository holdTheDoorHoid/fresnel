Patos Island is the northernmost of the San Juan Islands in Washington, and its light stands at Alden Point on the island's northwestern tip.[^1] Across the border, a little more than three miles to the west, the nearest neighbour was a Canadian light on Saturna Island.[^1] A red light on a post and a steam-driven fog trumpet went into service here in 1893; a wooden tower was added in 1908.[^2][^3]

## The place

The Spanish explorers Galiano and Valdés named the island in 1792, according to the National Register nomination, and *patos* is Spanish for ducks.[^2] HistoryLink dates the naming to 1791 and 1792 and offers two explanations for it: the island's ducks, or a rock on its eastern side that looks like a duck.[^1] The Washington State Parks history says the island was withdrawn from homesteading and made a federal lighthouse reservation in 1875.[^4]

The light was meant for busy water. The nomination says the station marks the division point between the eastern and western passages into the Strait of Juan de Fuca.[^2] In 1888, as LighthouseFriends reproduces it, the Lighthouse Board described the channel between Patos and East Point on the Canadian side as one used by the Alaska steamers, the Nanaimo coal fleet and vessels running in connection with the Canadian Pacific road. Of the Canadian point it said, "Vessel-masters dislike to approach it in foggy weather, as they are unable to locate themselves because of the swirling, irregular currents." The Board recommended a post light and a duplicate Daboll trumpet for the west end of Patos.[^3]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $12,000 for the station on 3 March 1891.[^5] The Board's plan was modest: a fixed red lens lantern on a tripod platform 10 or 12 feet (3 to 3.7 m) above the ground. It explained that the light was needed "to aid in locating the fog signal when vessels are close to the shore in foggy weather," because the white light on the British shore already marked the general place.[^5]

A contract was signed on 27 December 1892 for a double dwelling, a fog-signal building and water tanks. Work began in early March and was finished on 24 June 1893.[^6] The fog signal was set up in October 1893, and the signal and light went into service on 30 November 1893.[^6] HistoryLink describes the first light as a red light atop a post, with a third-class Daboll trumpet in a small one-story building.[^1]

The trumpet did not work well at first. The Board found it "deficient in strength," tried new reeds, and in the end fitted a longer trumpet, which it said "gives better satisfaction."[^6][^7] In the year covered by the 1895 report the trumpet ran about 268 hours.[^7] The hot-air engines that drove the compressors were also too weak, and in the year covered by the Board's 1900 report two oil engines were installed in their place, with cooling tanks and a 960-gallon redwood tank.[^8][^9] Washington State Parks and the lighthouse society's archive both give 1898 for the fog-signal building, while the 1893 report records a fog-signal building in the original contract; the reports read do not say what changed in 1898.[^4][^10][^6]

The tower came later. The nomination says "Originally, Patos was built without a tower, the present tower being a modification dating from 1908."[^2] HistoryLink says the red light stayed fifteen years despite complaints, until a 38-foot (11.6 m) tower was added in 1908 to carry a revolving fourth-order Fresnel lens flashing white every five seconds.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the new light to 21 November 1908, at a focal plane 52 feet (15.8 m) above the water.[^3] The lighthouse society's archive gives 35 feet from the base of the structure to the centre of the lantern, so the sources do not agree on the tower's height.[^10] The nomination describes the building as a wood-framed Greek cross with the square tower rising above one arm.[^2]

## The keepers

The first keeper, Harry Mahler, reported on 2 August 1893 at $700 a year; his assistant, Edward Durgan, was paid $500.[^1] The archive's roster lists Mahler as acting keeper in 1893-94 and head keeper from 1894 to 1903.[^10] In 1917, by then at Alki Point, Mahler told *The Seattle Times* that "It's not a lonely life from my viewpoint," as HistoryLink quotes him.[^1]

Durgan came back in December 1905 as keeper, with his wife and many of his children, and stayed until February 1911.[^1] His daughter Helene Durgan Glidden wrote *The Light on the Island* (1951) about the family's years there. HistoryLink calls it a blend of fact and fiction, and the National Register nomination itself points readers to it.[^1][^2] Durgan moved to Semiahmoo light near Blaine, where he died in March 1919; his wife Estelle, who was with him, took his place for a time.[^1]

In the early years of the light, HistoryLink says, a keeper who needed help could only fly the flag upside down, and telephone service did not arrive until 1919. A boat trip was the only way to fetch supplies or mail, and one of the closest American settlements was Eastsound on Orcas Island, about five miles off.[^1] George Lonholt followed Durgan as head keeper (1911-1922), then Hans Jensen (1922-1928).[^10]

The Coast Guard took over the Lighthouse Service in 1939.[^1] A 1958 *Seattle Times* article, as HistoryLink summarizes it, found the coastguardsmen and their families content with the solitude, and often skipping the 72-hour liberty granted every third week; by then patrol boats from Bellingham brought supplies and mail twice a week.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 23 December 1911 the station lost its assistant keeper, Noah Clark, Durgan's son-in-law. HistoryLink says he motored to Blaine to fetch his wife Mary and their young son; the boat's motor failed near Patos on a dark, stormy night, and he jumped overboard to swim for a rowboat and was never seen again.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he had boated to Semiahmoo, and that after the boat was disabled he tried to swim to the island for help and was carried off by the tide rip on the island's north side. Both accounts have Mary Clark and the boy rescued after the boat grounded on a shoal; LighthouseFriends puts that on the next day, and HistoryLink says the family drifted through the night and part of the next day first.[^1][^3]

In August 1912 a Canadian fishery protection tug, the *William Jolliffe*, answered a distress signal at the station. The assistant said that Lonholt had left in a boat, showing signs of insanity. The lighthouse inspector, Henry Beck, came and, in the words of a Victoria newspaper, "found a different state of affairs than was reported to Capt. Newcombe."[^3] The same newspaper account, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says the two men had been "constantly wrangling." The assistant is not named in that account; the archive's roster lists Guy C. Martin and William H. Stark for 1912.[^3][^10]

Four acts of assistance by Lonholt appear in the Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1922. When the launch *Verona* of Seattle was caught in a heavy storm off the station, ten passengers "were cared for at the station for two days, until the weather moderated." In a second case Lonholt looked after a disabled launch's captain, engineer, and the captain's wife and two children until a launch could tow the boat to Anacortes.[^11] In the Bulletin's 1925 volume, keeper Jensen and assistant Peter J. Schuergers are commended for helping three people in a gas boat who "had lost their bearings in a heavy fog off the light station."[^12]

## After the keepers

The light was automated on 24 June 1974.[^4] In August the State Parks Commission agreed with the Coast Guard to develop primitive campsites beside Active Cove, and the station was listed on the National Register in October 1977.[^4] The Coast Guard gave most of the island to the Bureau of Land Management in 1987 and the five acres of the light station in 2002.[^4]

Sources differ on who took the other buildings down. Washington State Parks says BLM staff contracted for the demolition of everything except the lighthouse building and the 1908 tower addition.[^4] A history on the Keepers of the Patos Light website describes a Coast Guard reserve work party in summer 1985 sent to raze the structures except the lighthouse, which they patched; the keepers' housing was too far gone to use, and bullet holes marred the tower's glass.[^13]

The fate of the original lens is not settled in the sources read. The 1977 nomination says it was on display at Admiralty Head Light.[^2] LighthouseFriends says it was saved by the author Jim Gibbs, but the Keepers' account says Gibbs's own description of his house does not specifically bear that out, and one of its authors recalls a fourth-order Fresnel lens in perfect condition in 1985.[^3][^13]

## Today

The light station is on federal land managed by the Bureau of Land Management, and the light is still an active aid to navigation with a modern solar-powered optic.[^3][^4][^10] The non-profit Keepers of the Patos Light, formed in 2007, worked with BLM to repair the building; LighthouseFriends reports a new roof, doors, windows and paint in May and June 2008.[^3] Patos Island Marine State Park offers campsites and moorage.[^4]

The island can only be reached by boat. From Memorial Day through Labor Day the lighthouse is open periodically when volunteer docents are available; at other times it is closed.[^14]
