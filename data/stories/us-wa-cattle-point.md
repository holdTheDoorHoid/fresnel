Cattle Point Light stands on the southeast tip of San Juan Island, in San Juan County, Washington, on a bluff above the Strait of Juan de Fuca, next to American Camp in the San Juan Island National Historical Park.[^1]

## The place

British Admiralty charts first showed the name in 1858.[^1] Before the boundary dispute, the Hudson's Bay Company, which set up Belle Vue Farm in 1853, brought cattle and other livestock ashore at a dock on the point.[^1] During the Pig War the point lay within American Camp, where the US Army was encamped.[^1] Speakers of the Lekwungen language, one of the Indigenous languages of the islands, still call the point Tl'ikweneng.[^1]

After the boundary dispute was settled, the Lighthouse Board, working from charts in 1875, identified 23 possible sites in the islands and chose four for major lights.[^1] Cattle Point was one of them.[^1]

## Building the light

The first light was "a simple brass lens lantern on a post," set up in 1888.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives its first showing as 1 October 1888.[^2] The National Park Service says it was placed near the site of the present lighthouse.[^3]

The present tower went up in 1935.[^1][^2] It is a 34-foot (10 m) octagonal concrete tower with a fog-signal building beside it.[^1][^2] The light sat 94 feet (29 m) up on its bluff.[^1] The 1935 lamp, an electric bulb of 1,500 candlepower behind a non-rotating drum lens, was visible for seven miles.[^1]

## The keepers

George Jakle looked after the light.[^1] HistoryLink calls him "a soldier who had been stationed at American Camp," who stayed on the island after his enlistment and raised sheep nearby.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls him the son of such a soldier, and the National Park Service says his family "were descended from an American Camp soldier" and homesteaded where American Camp now is.[^2][^3] The sources disagree on whether Jakle himself had been a soldier. A new rural mail route, reported in the San Juan Islander on 29 March 1912, was to run from the Friday Harbor post office "to Jakle's gate at Cattle point."[^4]

Jakle's supplies came by sea.[^1] A lighthouse tender called at Griffin Bay, near the point, at regular intervals and left supplies and kerosene on the beach.[^1][^2] The lens needed care every day, and the lantern's reservoir needed filling once a week.[^1][^2]

In 1921 the US Navy built a radio compass station beside the light, with a tower, a transmitter, a powerhouse and quarters for sailors.[^1] The sailors also looked after the light.[^1][^2][^3]

## After the keepers

When the Navy closed its compass station in 1935, the year the new tower went up, the Lighthouse Service ran the light without a full-time keeper.[^1] A keeper hired under contract looked after the tower and worked the beacon and fog signal.[^1]

The light was automated in the late 1950s.[^1] Its lantern was taken off, and a small 250-millimetre drum lens was put on a short mast, switched by photoelectric cells, flashing white every four seconds.[^1]

An Exxon television commercial used the tower, with a faux lantern room and a roof added for the filming.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives 1984 for the commercial in its text, but a photo caption on the same page says 1978.[^2]

Erosion threatened the foundation, and the Coast Guard made emergency repairs in the summer of 2010.[^2] A permanent fix, which needed more design and permitting, was carried out afterwards.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and its tower is closed.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists the site as not open to the public.[^5] HistoryLink says the lighthouse can be seen by a short walk up a trail from the parking area near the end of Cattle Point Road.[^1]

The National Park Service describes a steep, primitive trail down to a rocky beach below the lighthouse.[^3] It says Cattle Point is part of San Juan Islands National Monument, which in turn belongs to the Bureau of Land Management.[^3]
