Ship Shoal Light was an iron screwpile tower, 105 feet (32 m) to its lantern, that stood on a shoal in the Gulf of Mexico about ten miles south of Grand Isle, Louisiana, from 1860 until it was discontinued in 1965.[^1][^2]

## The place

Before the tower there was a ship.[^2] In 1848 the Louisiana Legislature petitioned Congress for a lighthouse on the island then called Isle Dernier, the westernmost of a string of barrier islands. Congress paid instead for a lightship on Ship Shoal, several miles southeast of the island.[^2] The Revenue Cutter McLane was converted for the job, and on 29 December 1849 she took station on the shoal under the name Pleasonton.[^2]

In August 1856 a hurricane struck Last Island, and more than 200 people died. The hurricane and the storms that followed split the island into five smaller islands, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] In 1852 the new Lighthouse Board decided to replace the lightship with a permanent iron tower, modeled on the iron towers already built on the reefs of the Florida Keys.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress allocated money for the work in 1852, 1853, 1855 and 1856: $3,000 for a survey and plan, then $20,000, $30,000 and $38,019.70.[^2]

In January 1858 the iron tower was assembled at the I. P. Morris Company's Port Richmond Iron Works in Philadelphia, then taken apart and shipped to Louisiana.[^2] Eight thirty-foot (9 m) screwpiles were driven into the shoal, and the tower rose on them. The two-story cylindrical dwelling for the keepers stood twenty feet (6 m) above the water.[^2] The archive gives the light's height as 105 feet (32 m), and records a second-order Fresnel lens installed in 1859.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the tower a height of 125 feet (38 m).[^2]

The first light was shown at sunset on 29 February 1860, with William Croft as head keeper, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The archive dates the station's establishment and its original lens to 1859.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive's roster has 265 entries for the station, 264 of them dated between 1859 and 1946.[^1] Charles S. Simmons is the first head keeper on the roster, for 1859 to 1860. The archive gives William Croft for 1860 to 1861, while LighthouseFriends names Croft as the keeper at the first lighting.[^1][^2] Louis Johnson was head keeper for fifteen years, from 1883 to 1898.[^1]

By 1866 several people at the station had been paralyzed. The cause was red lead paint on the tower: rain ran down it into the cistern that supplied the drinking water. The paint was stripped off with a solution of caustic potash and the tower was coated with coal tar. The Lighthouse Board reported, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, that the health of the keeper and his assistants improved at once.[^2]

When the Civil War began, Confederates took the lens and rotating apparatus to Berwick for storage. Union forces later took the tower and returned it to service on 10 November 1864 with a new lens.[^2] On 8 June 1865 the New Orleans Tribune printed a Lighthouse Board notice about the wreck of a schooner, which lay "eighteen miles E. N. E. from Ship Shoal Light," and was an obstacle to navigators.[^6]

In 1876 keepers James Williams and Frederick Hamilton caught a shark nine feet eight inches (2.9 m) long. Inside it they found a watch, two metal buttons and a buckle, LighthouseFriends reports.[^2]

LighthouseFriends also tells of an attack in 1882. Third Assistant Fred Leach rowed out to a becalmed boat, towed it to the station and found on it a stranger, James Woods, who was fed and given full access to the station. On his fourth night there, Woods attacked Leach with a hatchet. Leach and head keeper Edward M. Dunn were both struck in the exchange of gunfire that followed, and Woods was shot in the knee. The keepers barricaded him in the upper part of the tower until he gave up. Woods was sentenced to fourteen years, LighthouseFriends says. The page refers to newspaper accounts without naming one.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In October 1868 the center of a severe cyclone passed over the station. LighthouseFriends says the storm's seas made the tower vibrate, so that oil was thrown out of the reservoirs, and the lamps could not be relit for six hours.[^2]

The Service's 1915 bulletin, reporting the September hurricanes on the Gulf coast, names John McNamara, keeper at Ship Shoal, and his three assistants, William H. Oliver, Frank J. LeBouf and Eugene F. Burke, among the employees the Secretary of Commerce commended for work under hazardous and trying conditions during the hurricane.[^7]

On 5 January the Service's 1923 bulletin reports, the first assistant, F. Z. Cuvillier, and the second assistant, Thomas Broxson, gave assistance to a motor boat that had become disabled and was drifting out toward Atchafalaya Bay.[^8]

On 13 March the keepers heard a roaring noise from the southwest, and a tidal wave came in. The Service's 1924 bulletin says it reached a 12-foot (3.7 m) mark at the station.[^9]

In August 1926 a hurricane struck the Gulf coast, with its center a short distance west of Timbalier Lighthouse. The Service's 1926 bulletin reports property damage of about $32,000 from the storm, and says the wharves, boathouses, tie-rods and riprap at Ship Shoal needed extensive repairs.[^10]

## After the keepers

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1896, records a contract for transporting granite blocks from Fort Pickens, Florida, and placing them around the tower, and says the work was completed.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the granite was brought in to protect the foundation after the seabed had begun to wash away.[^2]

In 1900 the Board prepared plans and specifications for metal repairs to the station and asked for bids.[^4] The metal work was delivered in March 1901, and the tower was scaled and painted.[^5]

By 1870 the seabed beneath the tower was being washed away, and the tower leaned northeast.[^2] The station was automated in 1929, and by then the tower had developed a noticeable lean.[^2]

The station was discontinued and abandoned in 1965.[^1][^2]

In the 1990s the Town of Berwick publicized plans to bring the tower ashore, to a park on the Atchafalaya River beside the relocated Southwest Reef light, but the plan was dropped.[^2] The plan was revived in 2019.[^2] The Louisiana Trust for Historic Preservation put the lighthouse on its list of most endangered places in 2020.[^2]

## Today

The archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and that its tower is closed.[^2] The foundation has weathered numerous storms since the keepers left. A plan to take the tower ashore before a Coast Guard deadline of March 2021 did not happen, and LighthouseFriends judges that saving the lighthouse is now less likely.[^2]

LighthouseFriends' notes describe an aerial look at the site, on a visit the page does not date. From the air, only the upper portion of the dwelling was visible, and the lower part appears to have vanished in the late 1990s.[^2] The page says the structure is best seen by boat or plane. The tower is closed, so visitors should not climb it or approach it.[^2]
