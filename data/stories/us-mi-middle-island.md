Middle Island sits in Lake Huron about ten miles north of Alpena, Michigan, roughly halfway between the lights at Thunder Bay Island and Presque Isle. Its brick tower, finished in 1905, marked both a turning point for lake traffic and the door to the only deep-water harbour of refuge on that stretch of coast.[^1]

## The place

The island takes its name from its position midway between Thunder Bay Island and Presque Isle Harbor, and is nearly a mile (1.6 km) long and five-eighths of a mile wide.[^1] Its south side gave shelter in any wind, but the Lighthouse Board's description of the hazard is the plainest. In 1896 it wrote that a light and fog signal were needed "to make available the harbor of refuge behind Middle Island, the only one in the vicinity having sufficient depth of water for the modern deep-draft lake vessels, and to mark a turning point in the regular course of vessels bound up or down the coast."[^3] Terry Pepper adds that shoals with less than six feet (1.8 m) of water surround all of the island except its northeast side, so the refuge could be hard to find in darkness or thick weather.[^2] A Life-Saving Service station was built on the island in 1881, according to Pepper; the US Lighthouse Society's archive records its ruins among the structures on the site.[^2][^4]

## Building the light

The Board put the island in its estimates in 1896 at $25,000 and repeated the request in the years that followed.[^3][^1] Sources differ on when Congress acted: Lighthouse Friends gives an act of 3 March 1903 and Pepper gives 3 March 1902.[^1][^2] The contractors, Gearing Bros. & Co. of Detroit, landed on 11 June 1904, and Pepper says the lighthouse tender *Amaranth* brought the working party and materials.[^1][^2] On 27 September 1904 a forest fire crossed much of the island. The unfinished buildings survived, but the contractor's camp and a quantity of building material were destroyed.[^1]

The work stopped for winter and resumed with the opening of navigation in 1905. The double keepers' dwelling was finished on 26 April, and the light and fog signal went into operation on 1 June 1905.[^1][^2] The buff-brick tower was fitted with a fourth-order Fresnel lens showing a fixed red light.[^1][^2] Its height depends on the measure: Lighthouse Friends calls it seventy-six feet (23 m) tall, while Pepper gives 70 feet (21 m) to the centre of the lantern and 80 feet (24 m) to the top of the ventilator ball.[^1][^2] The red-brick fog-signal building held steam whistles, which sounded a three-second blast every twenty seconds.[^1][^2] In the first season, from that autumn into the next spring, the signal ran for 157 hours and burned about 11 tons of coal.[^2] Pepper has a brick oil house built the following year, 1906, while Lighthouse Friends says one was finished in the summer of 1905.[^2][^1]

## The keepers

Patrick H. Garrity lit the station and stayed until 1925, the longest tenure on the archive's roster.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says he had spent fifteen years as his father's first assistant at Presque Isle before taking the new post.[^1] Three keepers served at once, a head keeper and two assistants, and the roster shows the junior posts turning over often: George J. Hassett was second assistant in 1905 and first assistant from 1909 to 1919.[^4]

In the Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year 1916, Garraty, Hassett and Alexander Brock appear by name in the table of assistance given to vessels, which also lists the yacht *Irvington*.[^5] Lighthouse Friends connects the two: the three keepers went to the aid of the *Irvington*, which had run aground, and removed its female passengers.[^1] In another episode, the Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1918 reported that Oscar J. Louks, first assistant keeper at Middle Island, had given help to a yacht that had stranded on Crooked Island, Michigan.[^6][^7] Louks does not appear on the archive's roster, which names Hassett as first assistant until 1919.[^4]

By the 1930s and 1940s the roster shows a succession of keepers each serving a few years, among them Clement E. Richardson (1934 to 1937), Eli A. Martin (1937 to 1939), Harold C. Fraser (1942 to 1946) and Ellsworth L. Kniffin, first an assistant and then head keeper until 1950.[^4] Coast Guard crews are listed for 1960 and 1961.[^4]

## Changes to the light

The light was made brighter on 4 November 1914 by changing to an incandescent oil vapour lamp.[^1] Sources disagree on the next steps. Pepper says that by 1928 the apparatus was an electric, green third-order lens with alternating five-second periods of light and dark, and that a compressed-air diaphone had replaced the steam whistles. Lighthouse Friends dates the diaphone to 1931 and the electrification, with the same green five-second characteristic, to 1938.[^1][^2] The daymark also has two dates: Pepper says the tower was painted white with a black band after the Coast Guard took over aids to navigation in 1939, while Lighthouse Friends, citing Light Lists, says the change from natural buff came in 1953.[^2][^1] Pepper also passes on a retired Coast Guardsman's account of an inspector's visit in 1958, after which the station crew did the painting themselves, working from a rope hung from the gallery.[^2] Lighthouse Friends dates the change of the band to reddish-orange to 1970; Pepper says only that the black band was painted over at a time he could not determine.[^1][^2]

## After the keepers

The station lost its resident crew in October 1961. The fog signal was discontinued, and the light became a white flash every ten seconds.[^1] Pepper reports that in the 1960s boaters vandalised the empty station and stripped the dwelling of almost everything removable.[^2] In 1972 the government transferred eight acres to Alpena County for a park, and a local group that began work in the early 1980s returned the land when it saw the scale of the job.[^1]

In July 1989 Marvin Theut bought the duplex and fog-signal building at a sealed-bid auction.[^1] During the first boarding-up trip that August, a calm morning turned to four-foot (1.2 m) waves, the men's rowboats swamped, and a Coast Guard helicopter took them off.[^1] Theut founded the Middle Island Lighthouse Keepers Association in 1992, and the fog-signal building opened to overnight guests in 2001.[^1] At the 2002 Great Lakes Lighthouse Festival a man returned a panel from the Fresnel lens that he had taken in 1975; where the rest of the lens is remains unknown.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard declared the tower surplus in 2010, and in October 2011 it was announced that the Middle Island Lighthouse Keepers Association would receive it.[^1] The lodge and duplex were offered for sale in 2021, and a December 2024 listing offered the whole 227-acre island, with the working lighthouse maintained by the Coast Guard, for $3.9 million.[^1][^8] The island is reachable only by boat. Because the sources differ on who holds the island and its buildings, and the tower is closed, visitors should go only on an association trip or with the owner's permission. The light has a National Register of Historic Places record (reference 06000133) published on 15 March 2006, and a 2024 news item gives 2006 as the year it was listed.[^9][^8] Lighthouse Friends says the lighthouse is owned by the Middle Island Lighthouse Keepers Association, that volunteer trips to the island run on summer weekends, that the tower is closed, and that overnight stays were suspended in 2017.[^1]
