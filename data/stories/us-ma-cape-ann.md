Two granite towers stand about a quarter-mile apart on Thacher Island, a rocky island roughly a mile off Rockport, Massachusetts, on the east side of Cape Ann.[^1] Cape Anners are said to have called the first pair of lights here "Ann's Eyes"; the pair that replaced them in 1861 is still there, and the south tower still shows a light.[^3][^12][^2]

## The place

Thacher Island lies about 30 miles (48 km) north of Boston and about two miles (3 km) from Gloucester Harbor, and covers some fifty acres of rock with little soil.[^1] It takes its name from Anthony Thacher, who survived a shipwreck here on 14 August 1635 that drowned his four children and seventeen other people. The General Court later granted him the island.[^3] The nomination for the station says a child-sized pile of stones near the southeast end may mark the grave of Elizabeth Avery, a cousin's daughter whom Thacher wrote he had found dead on the shore.[^4]

The towers were placed to warn of a hazard rather than to mark a harbor. The National Historic Landmark nomination describes the pair as the first American lights built to mark a "dangerous spot" along the coast, and it names the danger as the Londoner, a partly submerged reef half a mile south-southeast of the island.[^5] In the 1880s, a count cited in the nomination found about 70,000 vessels a year passing Thacher, and in the preceding eight years 98 total losses and 378 partial losses in the area.[^6]

## Building the light

In April 1771 the Massachusetts colonial government authorised twin lights and bought the island; a committee of six, including John Hancock, was named to build them.[^3] The nomination gives the price as 500 pounds.[^7] It reports both towers first lit on 21 December 1771, Forefathers' Day, and the lights are described as 45-foot (14 m) towers of wood and stone.[^3][^7] A Lighthouse Service article of 1932 instead says the station was first established by the Massachusetts Bay Colony in 1773, and the nomination, too, says the colony officially established it in 1773; the sources do not say what changed between the lighting and the establishment.[^8][^7]

The towers did not last the Revolution as lights. In 1775 the nomination says they were put out, and it quotes, from a 1975 history of the war around Gloucester, a report of 6 July 1775 that militia from Cape Ann had gone to the island, smashed the lantern glass and lamps, and "brought away the oyl [sic] together with Captain Kirkwood's family".[^7][^3] The island had no keeper after 1780 until the General Court paid for repairs in 1784, and the federal government took over the lights, which the nomination dates to 1789 on one page and to June 1790 on another; the Lighthouse Service's 1932 article says 1789.[^3][^9][^7][^8]

By 1857 the survey committee reported that the towers were "built of inferior materials, badly constructed" and recommended two first-order lights.[^10] Congress appropriated $81,417.60 in 1859, and the new granite towers were first lit on 1 October 1861.[^10] The drawings are signed by an Army engineer, Captain W. B. Franklin; the nomination says nothing is known of an architect and offers only a theory that Gridley J. F. Bryant may have designed them.[^10] The granite came from New Hampshire, because engineers judged Rockport's own stone too soft and too iron-rich.[^11] Each tower stands 124 feet (38 m) tall and holds a 156-step cast-iron staircase; they were placed on an exact north-south line, so that fishermen could line up one light behind the other to find true north.[^12]

The fog signals came in stages. The nomination dates the first to 1861 and records that in June 1869 the Light-House Board noted the transfer of a fog signal from Sandy Hook to Cape Ann, with a second steam signal completed in 1871.[^13] A new brick whistle house was built in 1886 and 1887, and the nomination says it replaced the earlier signals when it was completed in 1888.[^13] In the Light-House Board's report for 1894, the 10-inch steam whistle ran about 554 hours and burned about 37 tons of coal; in the report for 1900 it ran about 617 hours and burned about 38 tons.[^14][^15] By 1900 clockwork replaced the engines that opened the valves, and the nomination gives the signal's pattern as an 8-second blast, 4 seconds of silence, a 4-second blast and 44 seconds of silence.[^13] The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported that the steam whistle was changed to a compressed-air diaphone in fiscal 1916.[^16]

## The keepers

The first keeper, appointed on the day of the first lighting by the two captains overseeing the work, was Captain Kirkwood, whom the nomination calls a Tory.[^3] Joseph Sayward kept the lights from 1792 to 1814.[^17] When Benjamin Lincoln, the superintendent of lighthouses, sent the keeper seventeen questions about the towers, the answers recorded Sayward's age as 60 in September 1792 and a household that included his wife and three young women.[^7]

Three keepers named Wheeler followed one another: Aaron from 1811 to 1834, Austin from 1834 to 1836 and Charles from 1836 to 1849.[^17] Winslow Lewis installed Argand lamps in 1815 and built the keeper's house in 1816 at a cost of $1,415; its attic held a schoolroom for the keepers' children.[^18] A larger principal keeper's dwelling followed in 1876, and by the nomination's count the three dwellings of that era could house five families.[^19]

Addison F. Tarr was principal keeper from 1881 to 1912, thirty-one years by the archive's roster.[^17] Assistants in those years included Edward P. Lowe (1882 to 1891) and Elliott C. Hadley (1891 to 1896).[^17] Several keepers and assistants appear in the Lighthouse Service's own bulletins. John E. H. Cook, keeper, gave food on 26 July 1917 to two fishermen from the schooner *Commonwealth* who had been lost in the fog; the report says they had been without food or water for four days.[^20] On 27 July 1923 Elno C. Mott and second assistant Llewellyn D. Rogers towed a disabled speedboat to safety.[^21] On 21 January 1928 Cecil R. Kelley, Simeon A. Orne, George W. Seavey and an additional keeper, Mahlon Dunn, helped put out a fire that threatened the dwelling at the radio compass station on the island.[^22]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The nomination preserves a bleak line from the correspondence about the death of "Keeper John Parley" on 20 October 1891, who "was washed off the ways by an ugly wave, the ways being only 51/2 feet wide."[^23] The USLHS roster has no Parley; it lists a John Farley as first assistant in 1891.[^17] The boat slip, 170 feet (52 m) long in 1889, was rebuilt four times between 1853 and 1907 according to the Light-House Board reports, and was lost in the hurricane of 1939.[^23]

During the Portland Gale of 26 and 27 November 1898, the nomination says the steamer *Portland* was last seen about midnight some 12 miles northeast of Thacher Island.[^6] A story the nomination repeats from a 1996 guidebook holds that the twin lights' fog signal alerted the liner *America*, carrying President Wilson home in 1919, in time to avoid the island; the nomination presents it as a possibility.[^6]

At 9 p.m. on 31 December 1920, according to the keeper's report of 3 January 1921, a flock of geese struck the north tower, killing five and breaking two panes of the lantern; three came through the glass and chipped the prisms of the lens.[^24]

## After the keepers

In 1932 the Lighthouse Service reported that the north tower's light was discontinued on 1 February and that four 250-watt electric lamps were installed in the south tower's lens, flashing five times every 20 seconds.[^25] A 6,000-foot (1,800 m) submarine cable from the mainland, with current from Gloucester, powered the station.[^25] The article called Cape Ann the last of the twin lights in the United States, noting that the Atlantic coast had once held seven twin lights and one triple light.[^25] The Bulletin gave the new light's strength as 160,000 candlepower; the nomination gives 70,000 for 1932 and 160,000 for the 1979 automated light.[^25][^12]

The south tower was automated in 1979 and the Coast Guard crew left in 1980; the archive gives 1980 as the year of automation.[^12][^2] The south tower's first-order Fresnel lens was removed in 1975 and is on display at the Coast Guard Academy museum in Groton, Connecticut; the north tower's lens was destroyed, with only pieces surviving locally.[^12] Weather has been hard on the buildings: the nomination records the boathouse destroyed in October 1991 and the ramp wrecked in December 1992 and again in December 1995.[^23] A solar-powered optic was installed in 1998.[^12]

## Today

The north tower has been renovated and shows a steady amber light, which the nomination says imitates the original 1771 candle light and is a licensed private aid; the south tower shows the red flashing light.[^12][^26] Ownership is divided: as of the nomination's writing around 2000, the Coast Guard maintained about 28 acres around the south tower with a transfer to Rockport planned, while about 22 acres at the north end belonged to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and were leased by the town.[^26] The Thacher & Straitsmouth Islands Association, a volunteer nonprofit, works with the town's island committee to preserve the structures.[^27]

You reach the island only by boat. The association's launch leaves from Rockport's Granite Pier with reservations required, runs from June, and takes six passengers per trip; the island is a wildlife refuge, so pets are not allowed. At the time of writing the island was closed for the season, with trips set to resume in June 2027.[^28]
