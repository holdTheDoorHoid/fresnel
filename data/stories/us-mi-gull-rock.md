Gull Rock Light stands on a small rock in Lake Superior, in Keweenaw County, Michigan, half a mile (0.8 km) off the western end of Manitou Island, where it marks the narrow channel between Manitou and the tip of the Keweenaw Peninsula.[^1][^2] Its brick tower went up in 1867, joined to a keeper's dwelling.[^1][^2] The light was automated in 1913, and the station has had no resident keeper since.[^1][^8][^10]

## The place

Manitou Island had carried a light since 1849.[^1] Terry Pepper's history says that light stood at the island's northeast end, where it served the regular line of navigation. It did little to serve vessels making the emergency passage into Bete Grise Bay, on the south shore of the point.[^8] Down-bound captains were forced to navigate what Terry Pepper calls "the hazardous two and a half mile wide passage" between the point and Manitou Island.[^8] The Light-House Board's extract on Gull Rock describes that passage as dangerous and unmarked. It recommends a light to mark the channel and says the small island called Gull Island had been selected for the site.[^3]

Gull Rock is a low islet.[^8] Terry Pepper describes it as about 250 feet (76 m) long and 100 feet (30 m) wide, with its highest point less than twelve feet (3.7 m) above the water under the calmest conditions.[^8] On stormy days, when vessels were most likely to be threading the passage, the rock became "virtually invisible in the gray darkness," he writes.[^8] Michigan's representative in Congress, John Fletcher Driggs, presented a petition for a light on the rock on 17 January 1865, Terry Pepper says.[^8]

## Building the light

The Board's extract says the appropriation became available on 28 July 1866, and that the site was secured by reserving the rock for light-house purposes.[^3] Terry Pepper gives 29 July 1866 as the date Congress approved the money.[^8] Work started at the opening of the 1867 season.[^8] The extract says the work's completion "was somewhat delayed" by the death of William Tunbridge, the foreman in charge. Terry Pepper says Tunbridge drowned while working on the rock, and LighthouseFriends also reports his drowning.[^3][^8][^1]

Terry Pepper says construction crews were already scheduled to be in the vicinity for the identical lights on Huron Island and Granite Island.[^8] The district engineer in Detroit, he writes, judged that the cost of the new light could be significantly reduced if the same design and crew were used.[^8]

The keepers' dwelling was a seven-room brick house of a story and a half, with the square brick tower centered in its gable end.[^1][^8] The sources do not agree on the tower's height. The USLHS record gives 39 feet (12 m) from base to lantern, LighthouseFriends gives 45 feet (14 m), and Terry Pepper puts the top of the ventilator ball 46 feet (14 m) above the foundation.[^2][^1][^8]

The lantern held a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^1][^3] The Board's extract describes it as "a lens of the fourth order, fixed, red," and says the light was first exhibited on the night of 1 November 1867.[^3] Terry Pepper gives the focal plane as 50 feet (15 m), and the range expected in clear weather as 13 and a half miles.[^8]

## The keepers

Thomas Jackson was the first keeper.[^8] LighthouseFriends says he was removed before the 1868 navigation season opened, and Stephen Cocking, a Civil War veteran, replaced him. Cocking served until 1877.[^1][^2]

LighthouseFriends reports two episodes from Cocking's years.[^1] When he arrived to open the light on 13 May 1873, he found ice stacked six feet (1.8 m) high on the island.[^1] On 26 October 1875 he reported huge waves striking the lighthouse, and the pounding was smashing the boathouse doors.[^1]

Terry Pepper says the district inspectors approved a number of keepers' wives as assistants.[^8] Mary Corgan, wife of Keeper James Corgan, served as his assistant for all six years he kept the light, from 1877 through 1883.[^8] The roster also lists Mary Cocking as first assistant from 1872 to 1877, and Alice Nolen from 1892 to 1903.[^2]

The roster has Norman H. Guilbault as head keeper from 1885.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he died in March 1888, and that his assistant, John Nolen, was then promoted.[^1] Nolen served as head keeper for another fifteen years, far longer than any other head keeper on the rock.[^1][^2] The sources differ on Nolen's dates. Terry Pepper, citing Nolen's logbook, gives his keepership as April 1897 to May 1903.[^8] Terry Pepper says the light was put out for the winter when the passage began to ice over, and that Nolen wintered at Copper Harbor. LighthouseFriends says the family rented a house in Calumet so their seven-year-old son Jamie could go to school.[^8][^1]

In January 1900 Jamie fell ill with what a doctor from Houghton diagnosed as scarlet fever, part of an outbreak among the children of Calumet.[^1] He died on 9 February, and on 13 March the Nolens' three-year-old daughter Violet died too.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the family went back to the rock the following month.[^1]

Henry Corgan's service is set out in the Bulletin's notice of his retirement in September 1924.[^6] It says he first entered the service in 1866 at the Manitou Island light, and reentered it in 1882, serving nine months at Gull Rock.[^6] It says he was appointed to the same station again in 1885.[^6] The roster lists him as first assistant at Gull Rock from 1885 to 1887.[^2] The Bulletin's index gives his Gull Rock service as 1882 to 1885, which reads differently from the notice's continuous service after 1885.[^7] The notice says he then served continuously at Gull Rock, Point Peninsula, Copper Harbor and the St. Clair Flats Canal Upper light, with more than fifty years' service in all.[^6]

When the light was automated in 1913, Arthur De Champlain, who had been in charge of the rock for about a year, was transferred to the Detroit River light as second assistant.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 7 November 1892 the Canadian schooner-barge G. M. Neelon broke from her tow and struck the rock in heavy seas.[^8] Terry Pepper says the vessel was abandoned and considered a total loss, then recovered by a wrecker a year later and put back into service.[^8]

Nolen's log for 12 October 1898 records that the men could not work at the boat ways, as the sea was coming up to the boathouse door and spray was flying across the rock.[^8] In 1901 the Board reported a retaining wall of rubble masonry, 40 feet (12 m) long, built at the northeast corner of the dwelling, with a coping of Portland cement mortar.[^4][^8]

On 16 September 1912 the steel freighter Spokane drove onto the rock while trying to escape a storm, and broke in two.[^8] Terry Pepper says she was declared a total loss, but she was recovered, rebuilt and sailed for another 23 years.[^8]

In November 1913 the steamer L. C. Waldo went aground on the rock and broke in two.[^1][^9] The Calumet News of 11 November printed the headline "STEAMER L. C. WALDO BREAKS IN TWO AT GULL ROCK, OFF MANITOU; CREW OF 28 SAVED," and its text puts the wreck on the Saturday morning.[^9] The same paper says the tug Hebard brought the captain and crew into Houghton.[^9]

Accounts of the rescue differ. Terry Pepper says the Copper Harbor lifesavers struggled for four days to save the crew.[^8] LighthouseFriends, quoting the Coast Guard's 1915 report, describes the Eagle Harbor and Portage crews, a power lifeboat, and 24 persons rescued, 22 men and two women, landed at Houghton.[^1] The newspaper's figure of 28 saved and LighthouseFriends' figure of 24 do not agree.[^1][^9]

## After the keepers

In June 1913 an acetylene light replaced the oil lamp in the fourth-order lens, and the Bulletin's list of changes for 1913 shows Gull Rock moving from oil to acetylene.[^5][^8] Terry Pepper says the lamp was switched on at dusk and off at dawn by a sun valve, and that the characteristic changed from fixed red to a red flash every three seconds. Maintenance passed to the keepers of Manitou Island, and the rock was "secured and largely abandoned."[^8][^1]

Gull Rock was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in July 1984.[^11] Under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000, the Coast Guard offered the light for free in 2002. In July 2005 it was awarded jointly to the Michigan Lighthouse Conservancy and Gull Rock Lightkeepers, a group formed in 2004.[^1] In 2008 a new cedar shingle roof went onto the tower, and volunteers cleaned debris from collapsed floors, ceilings and walls. Landing craft carried the rubble to a regional landfill.[^1] Four grants from the Michigan Lighthouse Assistance Program helped pay for work on the privy foundation, temporary windows and subfloors.[^1]

The lantern today carries a solar-powered 250 mm acrylic optic, Terry Pepper says.[^8] The 2025 Light List gives the characteristic as two white flashes every five seconds, from a white square tower on the dwelling.[^12] The original fourth-order lens is no longer in the lantern.[^8] LighthouseFriends says it is on display at the Great Lakes Shipwreck Museum at Whitefish Point. Terry Pepper says the lens is reportedly there, but that recent evidence suggests the lens in that museum came from an old iron tower on Gull Island in Lake Michigan.[^1][^8]

## Today

The light is owned by Gull Rock Lightkeepers and the Michigan Lighthouse Conservancy.[^1] Gull Rock Lightkeepers, a nonprofit, says it operates and maintains the light through public donations and in conjunction with the Coast Guard.[^10] The grounds, dwelling and tower are closed, and LighthouseFriends says the light is best seen from a boat.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record lists the station as not open to the public.[^2] After a complete restoration, the Lightkeepers hope to open the light to the public through a fellowship program and, later, tours.[^1]
