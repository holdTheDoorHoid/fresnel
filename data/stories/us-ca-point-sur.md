Point Sur is a 361-foot (110 m) volcanic rock that sits at the edge of the Big Sur coast, joined to the mainland by a low neck of sand. On top of it, in Monterey County, California, stand the sandstone tower and keepers' houses of a light station first lit on 1 August 1889.[^1][^2]

## The place

Spanish explorers noted the rock before any lighthouse was planned. LighthouseFriends reports that Cabrillo called it a "moro" rock in 1542 and that Vizcaíno's 1603 map labelled it a point that looks like an island; the low ground joining it to land is a tombolo, and in the past water has swept right round the rock.[^3] The National Register nomination describes it as a steep, island-like landform with the lighthouse on a ledge 240 feet (73 m) above the ocean.[^1]

The need was a gap in the coast. Until the light was built there was no lighthouse between Piedras Blancas and Monterey, a distance of 60 miles, and Point Sur was an important departure point for ships between San Francisco and the south.[^1] The nomination names two wrecks that argued the case: the SS Los Angeles broke up in the surf at Point Sur in 1873, and two years later the Ventura was lost at the same place with a cargo of silks, linens, farming tools and wagon parts.[^1] The park history at pointsur.org says it took mariners eleven years of petitioning before money was granted in 1886.[^4]

## Building the light

The work was hard from the start. The nomination says all equipment and supplies had to be brought in through the surf and hauled to the top of the rock, building sites had to be blasted out, a quarry was opened for stone, and an incline railroad carried material up from the landing.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the landing of materials to late May 1887 and says work stopped when funds ran out on 1 November 1888, resuming after Congress added $10,000 in April 1889.[^3] A newspaper report in January 1889 lists "Completing the lighthouse at Point Sur, $10,000" among deficiency estimates put to the House.[^5] The nomination says the cistern was built in 1888 to supply water for the year-long construction effort and gives its capacity as 69,800 gallons, where LighthouseFriends gives 53,000.[^1][^3]

Three of the buildings are Richardsonian Romanesque in sandstone quarried on the site: the tower with its attached fog-signal room, the head keeper's building and the assistant keepers' triplex.[^1] The head keeper's house began as a one-storey "hoist house" for the funicular railway; after a wagon road was built in 1902, the nomination says, the railway was abandoned and the building became a dwelling.[^1][^3] The date of the road is not settled: the Light-House Board's 1900 report already has it nearly complete, and its report for the year ended June 1901 says the roadway from the landing to the top of the rock was completed.[^11][^22] The triplex was built for the keepers' families, and the nomination notes that the station kept four keepers.[^1]

The lens was a first-order Fresnel; the park's history says the optic alone weighs 4,330 pounds.[^6] LighthouseFriends dates its first lighting to 1 August 1889, as does California State Parks.[^3][^2]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society archive, working from the registers of lighthouse keepers, lists James Nightwine as the first principal keeper (1889 to 1890), followed by John F. Ingersoll (1890 to 1901), Ora O. Newhall (1901 to 1908) and John W. Astrom (1908 to 1927).[^7] Astrom's nineteen years are the longest in that list. Marcus H. Ovaitt was first assistant from 1889 to 1894.[^7]

Isolation shaped the work. The state park's history says the four keeper families lived at the end of a long, often dangerous trail to Monterey, got their mail and supplies by horse and wagon from Pfeiffer's resort, and had a garden plot each; bulk goods such as coal, firewood and animal feed came by lighthouse tender about every four months, landed by whaler and skiff and hoisted in cargo nets.[^8] The nomination adds that until 1919 there were no connecting roads to Carmel, that animals and vegetable gardens supplied a large part of the keepers' diet, and that cows were kept long after commercial milk became available.[^1]

Children were a part of station life. According to the park's history, they could not attend school daily until 1927, when head keeper William Mollering asked the school district for a teacher; the district agreed on condition of at least six pupils, and Mollering's son began a year early to make up the number.[^8] LighthouseFriends tells a small story from the 1930s: Keeper Thomas Henderson sold milk from his cow to the other keepers, fell out with an assistant, Bill Owens, and refused to sell to him; Owens had milk delivered to the gate on Highway 1 and soon asked for a transfer to Point Arena. The archive's roster lists a William Owens as third assistant from 1935 to 1937, but no record in the sources connects the two.[^3][^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The weather and the rock together damaged the buildings. The Light-House Board's report for 1891 says that "Great trouble was experienced in keeping the rain from driving through the porous stone of which the dwelling walls are built." A coating of alum and soap was only partly successful, and the staff beads round exposed windows were reset in white lead.[^9] In 1896 the Board reported that the water, drawn from a well on the sand spit, sometimes turned brackish, and that one keeper was living in the temporary structure put up for the construction superintendent because the quarters were too few.[^10] By 1900 the Board was renewing a recommendation, made in its last four reports, for $6,000 to build a new cottage, because the quarters for the four keepers were insufficient.[^11]

The fog signal had a long working life. The station's twelve-inch steam whistles, run in duplicate on wood, were in operation some 855 hours in the Board's report for 1893, 1,048 in 1894 and 1,466 in 1895.[^12][^13][^14] A newspaper notice in October 1889 said the signal's blasts would be changed to thirty-five seconds from about 1 November.[^15] In August 1890 the whistling buoy off the point went adrift; the Morning Call reported it missing on 20 August and the Board recorded that it was recovered at Piedras Blancas a week later.[^16][^17]

The Board's 1900 report explains why a wagon road was built. The original tramway had run from the sandpit near the landing beach to the top of the rock, 285 feet (87 m) up, over the crest and down the seaward side, partly on a 30-degree incline; because it was costly to keep up and "somewhat dangerous", the Board decided to build a roadway for teams round the rock. Work was stopped at the end of January 1900 because the cost had exceeded the estimate, owing to the hard rock and very stormy weather, and a contract to finish the roadway was let in June 1900.[^11]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1919 recorded that keeper John W. Astrom and assistant keepers Herbert C. Trotter and Charles H. Livesay had rescued two men who had been adrift in a launch for four days.[^18]

LighthouseFriends dates the loss of the Navy airship USS Macon off the point to 12 February 1935 and says Keeper Henderson watched her tail fin fail and testified at the Navy's inquiry.[^3] A wire report of 13 February said survivors had reached San Francisco, and a Washington item that day counted two lives lost in the disaster.[^19]

## After the keepers

A radiobeacon was added to the station in the 1920s: the Bulletin's 1924 to 1929 index has an entry "Point Sur radio beacon aids CG," and LighthouseFriends dates the beacon to 1 December 1925.[^20][^3] The state park's history says Highway 1 was completed in 1937 and that the Coast Guard took over aids to navigation in 1939.[^8]

The sources disagree on when the keepers left. The archive's roster of keepers ends in 1972, and LighthouseFriends dates automation to 1972; California State Parks and the Point Sur history page say 1974.[^7][^3][^2][^8] Afterwards the support buildings were boarded up.[^3] The first-order lens was taken out of the tower in 1978 according to LighthouseFriends and the park's history, and in 1979 according to the National Register nomination.[^3][^8][^1] The two accounts of where it went differ too: the park's history says it was transported in 1978 to the Allen Knight Maritime Museum of Monterey, while LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard removed it for fear of vandalism, that a revolving beacon replaced it, and that it was loaned to the Maritime Museum of Monterey in 1992.[^8][^3] The National Park Service's NPGallery gives 3 September 1991 as the date published for the station's National Register record.[^21]

## Today

Point Sur State Historic Park describes the station as the only complete turn-of-the-century light station open to the public in California, and says the light has been in continuous operation since 1889.[^2] The buildings are being restored by State Parks staff and volunteers with the nonprofit Central Coast Lighthouse Keepers.[^2] The station is closed except by docent-led walking tours, which the park lists as three hours long with a 360-foot (110 m) climb and two stairways, the longer of 61 steps; there are no reservations, and tours may be cancelled for weather. Check the current schedule with the park before you go.[^2]
