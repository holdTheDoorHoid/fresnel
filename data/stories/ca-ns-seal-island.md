Seal Island Light is a white tower with two red bands on the southern point of Seal Island, about fifty kilometres south of Yarmouth, Nova Scotia. It was first lit on the night of 28 November 1831, and families of keepers tended it for more than a century before the light was left unwatched in October 1990.[^1][^2]

## The place

Seal Island lies off the southwest corner of Nova Scotia, on the route to and from the Bay of Fundy. Lighthouse Friends calls the island "a critical point" for vessels on that route.[^1] Blonde Rock, about six kilometres to the south, "snared many a vessel," the same history says.[^1] The Canadian Register of Historic Places describes the tower as "a response to the many shipwrecks which occurred in and past the Bay of Fundy."[^3]

Before there was a light there were people on the island whose work was rescue and burial. Richard Hichens and Edmund Crowell had settled there by 1823, and they helped the men who came ashore alive.[^1][^2] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society (NSLPS) says that shipwrecked men who reached the island often died of cold and hunger in winter, and that preachers and residents from Yarmouth and Barrington came to find and bury the dead. "On one occasion 21 people were buried in shallow graves in a single day," the society writes.[^2]

Hichens had been shipwrecked on Cape Sable himself in 1817. He later married Mary Crowell, whose father was a Barrington preacher and who had heard many accounts of the deaths on Seal Island.[^2] The Register credits her lobbying with the decision to build the light.[^3] Hichens and Crowell petitioned the province for a wharf and for boats. The wharf was finished in 1827, and that year, at their urging, Governor Sir James Kempt surveyed a site for a lighthouse about 500 metres from shore.[^2]

## Building the light

Work on the tower began in 1830.[^2] The Lighthouse Friends history says that in 1826 a Nova Scotia committee had noted that New Brunswick supported just one lighthouse and Prince Edward Island none. New Brunswick agreed in 1830 to pay toward the cost of a light here and half the yearly running expense.[^1]

The tower is octagonal and made of wood, standing on a stone and mortar foundation, with timbers 14 m (47 ft) long and 41 cm (16 in) square at each corner.[^1] The sources do not agree on its height. Lighthouse Friends gives 18 m (sixty feet) from base to vane.[^1] The NSLPS calls it "the 67 foot timber lighthouse," or about 20 m.[^2]

The light was first shown on the night of 28 November 1831.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives the year of completion as 1831 and the cost as 1,009 pounds.[^1] A daughter was born to Richard and Mary that same evening, and the NSLPS says that began a family lightkeeping tradition that lasted more than a century on the island.[^2]

In 1857 William Condon, Superintendent of the Board of Works, visited. He had heard "several complaints" that the light was "very poor," and he thought "the principal fault is with the lamps in use." New lamps were supplied the following year.[^1] A bell was put up for fog in 1832, and a new lantern room was fitted in 1862.[^1]

Water was a constant trouble. A well went dry, and water had to be hauled in after a nearby pond dried up. A reservoir built in 1871 had its walls fall in "just a few months after it was finished." After that, water and the coal for the fog alarm had to be brought in again.[^1]

The fog signal changed the station. In August 1870 a steam whistle went into service. The history says that in snowstorms and fog it sounded "two five-second blasts each minute."[^1] The same year a second-order Fresnel lens from France, which had been in storage, went up in the tower.[^1] The Department's report for 1901 said the fog alarm building "will be moved to the south point of the island."[^9] In February 1903 the whistle went into a new building on the beach near the south tip, 178 m (585 ft) from the lighthouse. The Department's report for 1903 says the whistle "has been removed to a building erected on the extreme south point of the island, 585 feet S 26 W. from the lighthouse" (about 178 m).[^1][^10]

A new second-order lens went in during 1907, and Lighthouse Friends says the light then became a group of three flashes every fifteen seconds.[^1] The Department's report for 1907 describes the new light as "a second order double flashing light" being installed.[^12] The report for 1908 calls it "the new second order triple flashing light, which was being installed last year," and says it had been "put in operation."[^13] The two reports disagree on whether the light flashed twice or three times in each group.

## The keepers

The first keepers were Richard Hichens and Edmund Crowell. The pair had been helping shipwrecked mariners since they moved to the island, and in 1830 the government gave them money to employ "two able-bodied men to reside with them on the Seal Islands, for the purpose of assisting shipwrecked mariners."[^1] From 1836 Hichens had sole charge of the light, and Crowell stayed on the island and was paid to help the shipwrecked.[^1]

The early keeper dates are in dispute. Lighthouse Friends has Hichens still in charge in 1857, when Condon visited.[^1] The NSLPS list gives different dates and a different sequence. It ends both Hichens and Edmund Crowell in 1855, and it lists "Crowell, Corning" from 1855 to 1870 and "Crowell Jr., Corning" from 1870 to 1891.[^2]

Thomas Crowell was keeper until 1880. His son Corning, who had been the fog alarm engineer since 1873, took over the light that year.[^1] The Department's keeper list for 1907 names "Crowell, John" at the Seal Island light and fog alarm.[^12] Lighthouse Friends gives John Crowell's years as 1899 to 1926, and the NSLPS gives 1891 to 1927.[^1][^2] The NSLPS repeats a family account that between 1902 and 1917 John Crowell "did not take one day of holidays," and that he missed no watch.[^2]

Ellsworth A. Hamilton kept the light from 1926 to 1941 in the Lighthouse Friends list.[^1] The roster table gives the names of the keepers after him, down to the keepers of 1990.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The earliest wreck in these sources is HMS *Blonde*, which struck the rock that bears her name in January 1782. Lighthouse Friends says her crew and prisoners "launched boats and made it safely to Seal Island," and that they "might have perished" had two American privateers not arrived days later.[^1]

In early July 1843 the Royal Mail steamer *Columbia*, carrying ninety passengers, was running in fog when she struck Black Ledge, near Seal Island, Nova Scotia. The sources disagree on the day. The Boston Transcript, reprinted in the Providence *Republican Herald*, says it was the Monday afternoon.[^5] A mail officer's letter, dated Seal Island, 4 July, and printed in the same paper, says she grounded on the Sunday at a quarter past one in the afternoon.[^5] That letter says that "a boat, with the master of Seal Island Light, came alongside" to report where she lay, and it names the keeper as Mr. Hitchins.[^5] The Missouri *Radical* spells the name Hitchings, and calls him "keeper of the Light House on Seal Island." The paper reports that the passengers voted a subscription "for the inhabitants of Seal Island, for their kind attentions and hospitality to the passengers," and that they planned to write to the light commissioners in praise of the keeper and of a Mr. Crowell.[^6] The passengers also passed resolutions "acquitting Copt. Shannon from all blame."[^6] The newspapers give only the surname. Lighthouse Friends names Richard Hichens as keeper in these years, but no source links the two names directly.[^1]

In late April 1869 the British steamship *St. George*, bound from Portland, Maine, to Glasgow with a load of wheat, went ashore on Blonde Rock. The New-York Tribune printed a Portland dispatch of 30 April. It says she went ashore on Blonde Rock, placed at Seal Island, off Nova Scotia.[^7] Some papers had placed the rock off Maine. A correction in the *Portland Daily Press* said the rock "was wrongly located in the newspapers" and lay off Nova Scotia, "and not off the coast of Maine."[^8] Lighthouse Friends says the crew were "taken off and landed at Barrington," and the hull and cargo were sold at auction.[^1]

The Department of Marine's annual report dates the Seal Island life-saving station to 1880: "Seal Island station. — Established 1880."[^11] Its crew then was a coxswain and six boatmen.[^11] In the fiscal year 1902–03 the station's small lifeboat was "found to be unseaworthy and faulty in construction," and it was "condemned and replaced" by a new self-bailing boat.[^10] In the report for 1913 to 1914 the station's "subsidized volunteer crew" was called out on 9 June 1913 to the wreck of the schooner *Maia*.[^14]

The report for 1916 to 1917 records a collision off Seal Island on 12 August that cost four men their lives, and it says the inquiry at Halifax found the master and mate of the steamer *Borghild* "in default."[^15] The report gives the date as "August 12" without a year; the year follows from its fiscal year. On 13 October 1923 the government steamer *Aberdeen* stranded on the Limb Ledge off Seal Island and became a total wreck. The report's movements section names that ledge, but a later note in the same report says she was ashore on "Black ledge."[^16]

## After the keepers

The NSLPS says that by the early 1970s the Coast Guard's automation programme reached Seal Island, where "the light and horn were semi-automated."[^2] Lighthouse Friends says that by 1986 the keepers' families had moved off the island, and two teams of two keepers worked alternate 28-day shifts.[^1] Work on a new engine room and remote-monitoring equipment began in 1989.[^1] On 17 October 1990 the light became officially unwatched, "bringing an end to 159 years of staffed history."[^2]

A double dwelling for the keeper and his assistant was built in 1954, and an oil plant replaced the steam fog alarm in 1918.[^1] In 1978 the circular iron lantern and the Fresnel lens were removed, and a rotating beacon and a new aluminum lantern room took their place.[^1] The old lantern and lens were taken to Barrington and set on a replica of the tower's top half.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light.[^1] Its list of lights gives the characteristic as flashing white every ten seconds, from a focal height of 33.4 m (110 ft) above the sea, with a range of 19 nautical miles.[^4] The NSLPS says all operating lights in Nova Scotia are automated. Its page is inconsistent about this light: one panel reads "Operating: This light is operational," and a summary table reads "Still Operating: No."[^2]

The Seal Island Light Museum in Barrington, Nova Scotia, is "a 35 foot high replica of the top half of the Seal Island Lighthouse." It is crowned by the cast-iron lantern, which was removed from the light in 1978, and it houses the Fresnel lens, which was also removed then.[^2]

Seal Island is reached only by boat.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1] The NSLPS says it has been over a decade since the society ran a trip to the island.[^2] The way to see the light is from the water. The tower is closed, and visitors should not try to climb it.
