Addenbroke Island Light stands on the western point of Addenbroke Island, in Fitzhugh Sound on the central coast of British Columbia.[^1][^2] Its first light was shown in 1914 from a two-storey dwelling with a lantern room on its roof.[^1] Today a round concrete tower, built in 1998, carries a white light, and Lighthouse Friends lists a keeper at the station until at least 2024.[^1][^2]

## The place

Ships going north on the Inside Passage pass a corner of Queen Charlotte Sound and then enter Fitzhugh Sound, where a group of islands on the eastern shore narrows the sound to about 3 km at this point.[^1] The westernmost of those islands is Addenbroke Island, named by Captain George Vancouver in 1792.[^1] The station is reachable only by boat, but the light is "easily seen from passing ships."[^2]

## Building the light

The Department of Marine's annual report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1914 lists work at Addenbrooke among the changes in British Columbia, and its entry opens with the erection of a combined light-house and dwelling.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says the work began in 1913, by day labour under the direction of D. McLean. The Department had decided on the site to mark the narrowest point in Fitzhugh Sound.[^1]

Mariners were told that a fixed white light of the fourth order would be shown from 15 April 1914, with the fog bell sounding a single stroke every five seconds in thick weather.[^1] The first dwelling was a square, two-storey house with an octagonal lantern room on its hipped roof.[^1]

The lantern room did not last. A new single dwelling was built in 1961 by McGinnis Construction.[^1] In 1968 a light on a steel skeletal tower replaced the lantern room on the old house.[^1][^2] The round concrete tower that stands today was built in 1998.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

William A. Guthro was the first keeper. He came up from Nanaimo with his wife, Edith, and served from 1914 to 1921.[^1] He was on sick leave for about four and a half months in 1918, when Hugh Anderson was put in charge.[^1]

When the Guthros left in 1921, Robert Smith and his wife, Edith, took over.[^1] Smith fell ill in March 1922 and died in a Vancouver hospital in May.[^1] Edith Smith then took the post of keeper herself.[^1] Lighthouse Friends quotes her on the job: "Time did drag sometimes." She recalled that fishing boats came in now and then for a visit.[^1]

She married Walter Brydon in October 1924.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says his name appears in Department of Marine records from 1924 until 1928.[^1]

Ernest Maynard came to Addenbroke in the summer of 1928 from Lucy Island, where Lighthouse Friends says he had served for two years.[^1] The Daily Colonist of Victoria reported that he had been "transferred to Addenbrooke" early that summer, and that he had been keeper at Lucy Island "for a long time." The paper called him "a returned man" who had lived in Prince Rupert for years and was survived by a wife and family.[^1][^6]

Lighthouse Friends lists more than twenty head keepers from 1914 to 2024, the most recent being Tim Rogers, from 2023.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Lighthouse Friends says that on the morning of 16 August 1928 Maynard was found dead on the station, near the boathouse.[^1] The Daily Colonist, quoting a dispatch from Prince Rupert dated 17 August, says a fisherman had reported the body. The paper describes a shotgun wound to the head, says there were no other details, and says there was "nothing to suggest if the tragedy had been accidental or suicide."[^6]

Lighthouse Friends gives a fuller account. It says that although suicide had been considered, the authorities who examined the scene concluded that Maynard had been woken by a commotion at the station's dock and was shot when he went to investigate.[^1] A provincial police corporal then questioned residents all along Fitzhugh Sound. The sources do not say how that inquiry ended.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] A Coast Guard table in a 2011 Senate committee report, drawn from April 2010 data, lists Addenbroke with two permanent residents. It also marks the light as automated, which the report explains as meaning that "the navigation aids can function in the absence of a lightkeeper."[^4]

In July 2024 the Coast Guard said that the twenty-five other staffed light stations on the British Columbia coast would continue with normal operations.[^5] The release does not name Addenbroke.[^5] Lighthouse Friends lists a keeper there until at least 2024.[^1] Rowlett's directory describes a staffed light station with two keepers' houses, a fog signal building and other structures.[^2]

Both Rowlett and Lighthouse Friends say the site is open and the tower is closed.[^2][^1] This account does not suggest landing on the island or climbing the tower, which is still an active aid to navigation.[^2] The light shows a white flash every five seconds, from a focal height of 24 m (79 ft).[^2]
