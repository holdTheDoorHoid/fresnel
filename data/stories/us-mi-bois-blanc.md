Bois Blanc Light is a yellow-brick tower attached to its dwelling, on a point on the north side of Bois Blanc Island in Lake Huron, at the eastern approach to the Straits of Mackinac.[^1][^2] A stone tower on the site fell in a storm in December 1837. The yellow-brick building of 1867, which replaced the second tower, still stands, and the station has been privately owned since 1925.[^1][^2]

## The place

Bois Blanc Island is about twelve miles (19 km) long and six miles (10 km) wide, and lies along the eastern approach to the Straits of Mackinac.[^2] The Lighthouse Board's report for 1891-1892 describes a "dangerous reef" running out from the island's southeastern point, and says that many vessels have met disaster there.[^5]

Congress appropriated $5,000 for a lighthouse on the island on 23 May 1828.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the contract went in March 1829 to Philo Scovill of Cleveland, for a "light-house and dwelling-house."[^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says a circular stone tower of sixty-five feet (20 m) and a detached dwelling were completed in 1829, and that the light went into service in the spring of 1830.[^2] The USLHS record gives 1829 as the year the station was established.[^1]

The first tower stood close to the lake. LighthouseFriends quotes Stephen Pleasonton's 1842 report on the station: the high water of 1836 and 1837 "brought the water to its base," and "a storm arose, in December, 1837, and, forcing the water upon the base of the light-house, it was undermined and fell."[^2]

Emily Ward, the keeper's daughter, was on the island that night. Her account is printed in *Grandmother's Stories* (1889), a book of family stories by Frances B. Hurlbut that is dedicated to Emily Ward on her eightieth birthday.[^3] The book says that every great wave "would dash itself to foam against its brawny sides." Emily went out into the storm, she recalled, because "I thought it was my duty to save the government property."[^3] She went back into the tower for the lamps and reflectors: "About five o'clock I saw that if I was to save the lamps and the great reflectors I must begin at once."[^3] She made several trips up and down the stairs: "I think I climbed those stairs five times before I got everything movable down."[^3] Then, after "the tower tottered," she and Bolivar, who had been left at the window to watch, ran for the woods, and "with one mighty crash, down went the huge pile of masonry."[^3]

The second tower, built farther from the lake, was a thirty-five-foot (11 m) stone tower. LighthouseFriends says Congress funded its rebuilding in 1838.[^2] It had a fourth-order Fresnel lens from 1857.[^2] The USLHS record gives 1867 as the year the original lens was installed, so the two sources disagree on the lens.[^1] An 1866 report called the station's condition "very dilapidated." The Lighthouse Board had asked for $14,000 for a new light, and Congress granted it on 2 March 1867.[^2]

The third tower was a yellow-brick dwelling with a square tower attached to its lakeward end, finished later in 1867.[^2][^1] The Board recorded a brick cistern built in the cellar in 1891-1892.[^4]

## The keepers

The USLHS roster gives the principal keepers from 1829 to 1924. The first, Eber Brock Ward, is listed from 1829 to 1843.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls the keeper Eber Ward. It says that his son, Eber Brock (E.B.) Ward, left his father on the island in 1833 to work for an uncle, and later became a wealthy man in Detroit.[^2] The roster gives the keeper the name LighthouseFriends gives his son, so the two sources disagree on which Ward is meant.[^1][^2]

LighthouseFriends says the keeper was away from the island on the night of the 1837 storm, detained on Mackinac Island by bad weather.[^2] Emily Ward was at home on the island, and she went back into the tower that night.[^3]

Two women appear on the roster as principal keepers: Mrs. Charles M. O'Malley, from 1854 to 1855, and Mary Grainger, in 1857.[^1] Henry Metivier was keeper from 1894 to 1924.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that in April 1922 he was returning to the island on the first trip of the season aboard the passenger steamer *Colonial*.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Life-Saving Service also had a station on the island. Its annual report for 1891 says that stations at "Marquette and Bois Blanc Island, Michigan" had been completed and put in operation.[^6] The Service's report for 1892 gives an example from the island under its heading "Warned by night signals": "A steamer heading for the island (Bois Blanc) changed her course and went clear on seeing the surf man's night signal."[^7]

On 20 April 1922, according to LighthouseFriends, the wooden passenger steamer *Colonial* was caught in ice floes on her first trip of the season and drifted down Lake Huron leaking. LighthouseFriends says a Coast Guard crew rescued everyone aboard and brought them to the island, and the steamer was left to be claimed by the lake.[^2]

## After the keepers

The station was discontinued in 1924.[^1][^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1924 to 1929 lists "Bois Blanc, light changed to flashing" among its entries.[^8] LighthouseFriends says a light was then shown from a forty-six-foot (14 m) black skeletal tower near the shore.[^2]

The station was sold in 1925 to a private owner, and later owners restored the dwelling to a livable condition.[^2] In 1942 the light was moved to a white skeletal tower, which served as a better daymark.[^2] The current cylindrical tower near the lighthouse was built in 1986.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is privately owned, and LighthouseFriends says it is best viewed from the water.[^2] Shepler's eastbound lighthouse cruises pass it, and seasonal ferry service runs to the island.[^2] The grounds, dwelling and tower are closed.[^2]
