White River Light stands on the White Lake channel where it meets Lake Michigan, in Fruitland Township near Whitehall, Michigan.[^1][^13] Its first keeper, William Robinson, tended the light for 47 years, from 1872 until 1919, and the light station has been a museum since 1970.[^2]

## The place

The Michigan legislature asked Congress for a lighthouse "at the entrance to White Lake" in 1853. In 1866 Congress appropriated $67,000 for a new channel and $10,000 for a lighthouse, according to the Lakeshore Keepers history. The channel was not finished until 1871.[^2]

The first light was a pier-head beacon at the end of the short south pier. It was "a square white wooden pyramid-shaped 27-foot beacon with a fifth-order Fresnel lens," burning lard oil. William Robinson, "a local man and former seaman," became its first keeper in 1872.[^2]

The Life-Saving Service also came to the river mouth. Its report for the fiscal year ending 30 June 1884 records a thirty-year lease, obtained from the estate of William M. Ferry of Grand Haven, "for the station at White River, Lake Michigan."[^3]

## Building the light

Construction began in August 1875 "with the addition of an elevated wooden catwalk to the pierhead light," the Lakeshore Keepers history says. Grading of the "sandy hilltop overlooking Lake Michigan" was finished on 28 September, and the cast-iron lantern came by lighthouse tender from the Milwaukee depot.[^2] The archive gives the tower as limestone and octagonal, 38 feet (12 m) from its base to the lantern.[^1] Lakeshore Keepers says the foundation is local limestone and the walls are yellow Michigan brick, so the two sources disagree on the building material.[^2] The history says "the fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed in April 1876," while the archive gives 1875 as the year the lens was installed.[^1][^2]

In December 1892 the Light-House Board recorded that the light's character changed from "a fixed white light, varied by a red flash every minute," to one with "a red flash every forty seconds."[^4] A list of lights printed in the Board's 1899 report still gave the White River light as a red flash every minute.[^14] The history says the lens was changed again in 1912, to "a ten-second white flash followed by ten seconds of darkness."[^2]

Electric light came in 1918. The history says a cable was run under the channel that year, and the Commissioner's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1919 lists White River among the lights changed to electric incandescent during that fiscal year.[^2][^5]

## The keepers

The archive's roster begins with William Robinson III as principal keeper from 1872 to 1919.[^1] The history gives Captain Robinson's service as "from 1872 to 1919," and says that he and his wife, Sarah, "raised 13 children at the light station."[^2] The same history says a son, Thomas, "served as assistant keeper from 1877 to 1919." The archive lists a first assistant named Thomas Robinson, but only for 1880 to 1882. The sources do not say whether these are the same man.[^1][^2]

After being told that he had to retire, Robinson died on 12 April 1919, aged 87, the history says.[^2] The archive lists William E. Bush as a laborer from 1911 to 1919 and principal keeper from 1919 to 1943. The history calls Bush "Robinson's grandson, William Bush."[^1][^2]

Between 1942 and 1959 the archive lists Leo Wuori, Guy L. Gordon, Frances Johnson and Andrew Newald as principal keepers. It lists Frances Johnson as first assistant from 1944 to 1948, and she was principal keeper from 1949 to 1954.[^1] The history says she was "assistant keeper to her husband, Leo Wouri" (the archive spells him Wuori) and was head keeper "after their divorce."[^2]

The history calls Frances Johnson Marshall "the last female lighthouse keeper in Michigan," and says that in 1953 she "stumped the panel" on the CBS quiz show *What's My Line?*[^2]

General context: the Light-House Board's printed rules, in a copy the Internet Archive labels 1864, say that women and servants were not to be employed in managing lights "except by the special authority" of the Department. That is general context for the period, not a record of this station; the sources here do not show how the rule applied at White River.[^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Life-Saving Service reported a salvage at White River on 25 October 1887. Its crew saved "a large quantity of pine timber and shingles" that had drifted ashore "during the heavy weather that had prevailed for several days."[^6] The Service's report covers the fiscal year to 30 June 1888, so the date falls in October 1887.[^6]

On 7 April 1888 the lookout saw a rowboat with two men "caught in the floating ice" near the harbor entrance, in "a fresh northwest wind, with a heavy sea." The keeper and a surfman ran to the pier head with a heaving line and hauled the boat "through the ice into the harbor."[^7]

On 4 April 1889 the steamer M. T. Greene, of Chicago, was steaming out between the piers when she "grounded about a hundred and thirty yards west of the White River Station." The station crew ran lines to her, and she soon backed off.[^8]

In its 1901 report the Light-House Board said that, because the War Department was extending the pier, the beacon would be moved "100 feet outward," and 214 feet (65 m) of metal elevated walk would be built.[^9]

The Lakeshore Keepers history, drawing on Tag's 1996 history of the station, says a bark's jib-boom broke off forty-eight feet (15 m) of the elevated catwalk in 1879, and reports "at least 15 incidents recorded between 1873 and 1879" in the area of the light station.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1925 the pier light changed from oil to acetylene and from a fixed light to a flashing one. The Bulletin lists the White River pierhead among the lights changed from oil to acetylene, and from fixed to flashing.[^11]

The history says the main tower light was "automated in 1945." The archive, however, lists principal keepers until 1959, so the sources do not settle when keeping ended.[^2][^1]

The station was decommissioned in 1960, and the Fresnel lens was removed and shipped to the Detroit Coast Guard Depot.[^2] The archive gives the year discontinued as 1960 and the disposition as "sold to Fruitland Township."[^1] The history says that "In 1966, Fruitland Township purchased the station and opened a museum in the lighthouse in 1970," and that the Coast Guard returned the lens in 1972.[^2] In 1975 a rifle shot through the lantern-room windows chipped the lens. The history says it "was then moved to the first floor of the museum, where it remains."[^2]

## Today

The township's own page gives the light's address as 6199 Murray Road, Whitehall.[^13] Lakeshore Keepers operates five Lake Michigan lights, White River among them, and says they are "open to the public from May to October." Visitors can walk the grounds and, in season, climb the tower. The tower climb costs "$8 for adults."[^12]

The operator's site says the lights are "closed for the season and will reopen in Spring 2027."[^12] In 2024 Lakeshore Keepers converted White River into a resident volunteer facility.[^2]
