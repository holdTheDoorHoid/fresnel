The Cape San Juan Light, in Spanish the Faro de las Cabezas de San Juan, stands on a hill at the northeastern corner of Puerto Rico, near Fajardo.[^1][^2][^10] It is a cylindrical brick and stone tower beside a one-story keepers' dwelling, built by the Spanish colonial government around 1880 and still lit.[^5][^11][^10] The sources do not agree on the year it first showed its light.[^1][^5][^10] Its lantern was badly damaged by a hurricane in 1932.[^3]

## The place

The light stands on the highest point of Cape San Juan, at the island's northeastern tip.[^1] The National Register nomination says it marked the most north-eastern angle of the lights that guided ships into the Vieques and Culebra passages toward the Caribbean and the Atlantic.[^4] Near the foot of the hill, swamps and marshes separate the lighthouse from the mainland, and a phosphorescent lagoon lies to the south.[^2]

The nomination quotes the eighteenth-century historian Fray Iñigo Abbad y Lasierra, who in 1782 wrote that sailors recognized Cabezas de San Juan through the mountain peaks behind it, which he named Loquillo and Laivonito.[^1] East of the light, the nomination says, the keys have names that indicate treacherous waters.[^2] From seaward, it says, the lighthouse is set against the distant mountains of Fajardo.[^2]

The site was carefully surveyed in October 1856, and the spot chosen then is the one the light still occupies.[^1]

## Building the light

The nomination says the first plans were commissioned in 1876 and construction began in early 1877, but that earliest project was dropped, possibly for budgetary reasons. A second plan, of about 1880, served as the basis for the structure built in 1881.[^1] The builder delivered the work to the Colonial Governor in March 1882, and the light was officially lit on 2 May 1882.[^1] LighthouseFriends says construction did not begin until 1881.[^11]

The Lighthouse Board's annual report for 1901 says the light "was begun in 1878 and was finished in 1880 by the Spanish, at a cost of 16,300 pesos."[^5] The Board's dates, the nomination's and LighthouseFriends' do not agree.[^5][^1][^11]

The keepers' dwelling was built for one keeper of the first and third class, and the main doorway has a hood moulding inscribed "1880."[^2] The tower is about 15 feet (4.5 m) across and rises 44 feet (13.35 m) to the top of its brick and stone lantern.[^3] A 1902 description gives the original optic as a third-order lens by Sautter, Lemonnier & Cie. of 1881, which showed a fixed white light with a red flash every three minutes.[^3] The nomination's evidence indicates that this lens was removed in 1916 and replaced by a fourth-order Barbier & Bernard lens, 20 inches (50 cm) across.[^3] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record gives a third-order Fresnel lens installed in 1885, a different date.[^10]

## The keepers

None of the sources cited here names the Spanish-era keepers who served before 1901. The U.S. Lighthouse Society's keeper register begins in 1901 with Severo del Olmo, principal keeper from at least 1901 to at least 1907. Manuel del Olmo followed as principal keeper from at least 1908 to at least 1930.[^10] LighthouseFriends says Manuel del Olmo was head keeper for over twenty years and received the lighthouse efficiency pennant, for the district's model station, in 1913, 1915 and 1916.[^11]

The register's assistant keepers include Julio L. Rengel, Ramon Romero, Rufino Flores Graniel, Agustin S. Cruz, Genaro Alejandro and Pedro P. Roldan. Andres Rivera was an assistant from 1920 to 1935.[^10] The register also lists Juan Ramos Ferran as principal keeper from 1933 to 1935, Angel Rodriguez from at least 1959 to 1960, and Robert Konitz from 1960 to at least 1961.[^10] LighthouseFriends adds Jose Perez Castillo as a head keeper from 1935 to at least 1941; the USLHS register does not list him.[^11]

On 12 September 1913 Manuel del Olmo, keeper, and Augustin S. Cruz, assistant keeper, rescued a man from drowning, from an overturned fishing boat. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin printed the rescue; the USLHS roster spells the assistant's name Agustin.[^6][^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends describes an episode in the Spanish-American War. On 6 August 1898 about thirty-five sailors and marines from the U.S. monitor Amphitrite, under Lieutenant Charles Nelson Atwater, took the light. A revolver slipped from a sailor's holster and went off, wounding Naval Cadet Boardman in the thigh; he was taken back to the Amphitrite and died of the wound.[^11] After reports of a Spanish force on 8 August, gunfire broke out at the lighthouse around midnight, and the Americans withdrew the next day.[^11] This account comes from LighthouseFriends alone and is not corroborated in the sources cited here.

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's account of the November 1918 earthquakes reports minor cracks at the Cape San Juan station, among other Puerto Rican lights.[^9]

A hurricane in 1932 severely damaged the original third-order lantern, according to the nomination.[^3] LighthouseFriends dates the storm to the night of 26 and 27 September 1932 and says the lens and lantern room were severely damaged or destroyed.[^11] The nomination describes the replacement as a U.S. Westinghouse four-way revolving beacon with four flashing panels.[^3]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1914 records that the light's oil lamp was changed to incandescent oil vapour.[^7] The Bulletin of 1917 records a change in the light's characteristic, from a fixed white light with alternating flashes to a group-flashing white light.[^8]

The nomination says the floor plan was redone, possibly in the 1940s or early 1950s.[^3] LighthouseFriends says modern bathrooms and kitchens were installed around 1950.[^11]

In 1975 the Conservation Trust of Puerto Rico acquired 321 acres on the San Juan headlands, rescuing the site from a proposed resort development. The lighthouse lies within that reserve.[^11] The National Register listed the lighthouse on 22 October 1981.[^12]

## Today

The lighthouse stands inside the Las Cabezas de San Juan Nature Reserve. LighthouseFriends says the Conservation Trust of Puerto Rico owns it, that the grounds, dwelling and tower are open during tours, and that visits need a reservation.[^11] The reserve offers trolley tours, and a nighttime tour lets visitors see the light operate.[^11] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record lists the light as an active aid to navigation.[^10]
