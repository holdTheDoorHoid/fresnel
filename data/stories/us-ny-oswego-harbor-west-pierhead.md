Oswego Harbor West Pierhead Light stands at the end of a breakwater where the Oswego River meets Lake Ontario, in Oswego, New York. Its light, 57 feet (17 m) above the water, went into a metal tower in 1934. LighthouseFriends calls it a flashing red light, and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1935 an occulting red.[^1][^2][^7] Before it, an iron pierhead beacon stood on a crib at the breakwater's end from 1881 until 1931.[^2]

## The place

The lights were there for the harbor entrance. A plan approved in 1870 called for an outer breakwater roughly a mile long, to make a 100-acre safe harbor on the west side of the river mouth.[^2] An earlier octagonal stone tower, built in 1822, stood on the river's east bank below Fort Ontario.[^2] Congress paid for a stone tower on the west pier in the 1830s, and LighthouseFriends says its third-order light was discontinued on 15 April 1917.[^2] The Lighthouse Board asked for $20,000 in 1879 for a light and a fog bell on a crib just inside the breakwater's end.[^2] While the money was pending, the pierhead beacon's red light was screened off on 13 September 1880, so that only a vessel east of the breakwater, able to enter the harbor safely, could see it.[^2]

## Building the light

An octagonal iron tower replaced the wooden pierhead beacon in 1877.[^2] On 17 October 1881 that iron beacon was moved onto a crib 35 feet (11 m) square at the breakwater's end and fitted with a fourth-order lens that could be seen from all directions.[^2] A fog bell hung from a bracket on the tower's north face, a boathouse went onto the crib a year later, and by 1887 the light had two keepers of its own.[^2] In September 1900 the iron tower was painted white instead of brown.[^2] A Lighthouse Board list of works from the 1891-92 report includes an "Oswego Breakwater fog signal" with money set against it.[^5]

In May 1931 the Great Lakes Dredge and Dock Company began work on a new arrowhead entrance, and on 10 August 1931 the iron tower was discontinued. That day a temporary light went onto the roof of a New York state barge canal terminal elevator.[^2] Afterward the west breakwater was extended, and a fifty-foot (15 m) crib was built at its outer end, filled with stone and capped with reinforced concrete.[^2] A Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1935 says the new station "is located on a specially constructed pierhead foundation on the west side of the" harbor entrance.[^7]

The 1934 metal lighthouse was fabricated in Buffalo and set over a basement built inside the crib. It was a thirty-foot (9 m) square building with a thirteen-foot (4 m) square tower in one corner.[^2] Its lantern held a fourth-order Fresnel lens made in France by L. Sautter, Lemonnier & Co., and with the aid of red-tinted panels it produced "a flashing red light, fifty-seven feet above the water."[^2] The electric tyfon fog signal gave a three-second blast every thirty seconds when needed.[^2] The Bulletin of 1935 describes the light and the fog signal differently: an occulting red of 5,100 candlepower from a 500-watt lamp, and an air horn.[^7] The sources disagree, and this story does not settle which description is right. A duplex house for two keepers and their families went up at 31 West Fifth Street in 1934, and a radiobeacon, synchronized with the fog signal for distance finding, was activated on 25 April 1936.[^2]

## The keepers

The breakwater light had keepers of its own from the 1880s, and LighthouseFriends prints a separate list for it.[^2] The archive's roster does not begin until 1934, so these earlier names rest on that one list. William Box is the first head keeper it names, in 1887. John Halloran followed him in 1888, and Thomas F. Cotter kept the light from 1888 to 1903. Daniel J. Sullivan, who had been an assistant there, was head keeper from 1903 to 1917.[^2] Among the assistants were George Greenfield, from 1891 to 1896, and Arthur Shaw, from 1912 to 1917.[^2] LighthouseFriends also lists a woman, Florence G. Garland, in 1903 among the heads of the stone tower, not of the breakwater light.[^2]

Charles H. Tucker is harder to place. LighthouseFriends lists him as an assistant at the breakwater light from 1896 to 1897, and as head keeper of the stone tower on the pier from 1904 to 1929.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' table of life-saving and property-saving assists for fiscal 1918 has his entry as "C. H. Tucker, keeper, Oswego Light Station, N. Y."[^8] The Bulletin of 1914 also calls him keeper of "Oswego Light Station", before the rename, so the name may have meant either tower then. The sources do not settle which tower Tucker kept before 1917.[^6]

After 1934 the archive's roster gives the keepers by name. Bert E. Egelston was principal keeper from 1934 to 1943, and Olin W. Stevens followed him from 1943 to 1946. Karl H. Jackson was first assistant from 1934 to 1942. Osgar K. Elmer and Carl A. Sprague were second assistants in the 1930s and 1940s.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates Egelston's appointment as head keeper at Oswego to 1929, and says he had begun his career as a fourth assistant at Buffalo Harbor Lighthouse.[^2] The archive starts his term in 1934.

LighthouseFriends describes a 1938 visit by representatives of the Oswego *Palladium-Times*. They found the fog-signal equipment and a backup generator on the bottom floor, a steel stairway up to a living room, radio room, kitchen and bedroom, and the tower clean from the basement to the top. The keepers on duty that day were Egelston, Jackson and Elmer. Their work also covered the gas buoys in the harbor, an acetylene light on the east breakwater and a light on a grain elevator.[^2]

From 1960 the roster also lists Coast Guard crew under a separate "USCG" heading, most of them for a single year, until 1964.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In its report for fiscal 1893 the Lighthouse Board recorded that vessel men complained they failed to hear the breakwater's bell ring "when the evidence of the keeper proves that it was rung." Captains reported that at times they were "within half a mile of the bell before they hear it." The Board renewed its recommendation that a steam fog signal replace the bell.[^3]

The Board's report for fiscal 1894 said the breakwater station was in good condition but needed some change for the protection and comfort of its keepers, who "often have to remain several days at the beacon in storms."[^4]

On 3 July 1914 Keeper Tucker brought two women to the light station. The Bulletin says they had been "rescued from drowning by his son and two other young men."[^6]

On Friday, 4 December 1942, a Coast Guard picket boat set out from the Oswego station to relieve Karl Jackson, who had been stranded at the light for three days by bad weather.[^11][^2] The station's commanding officer, Lieutenant Alston Wilson, went in the boat.[^2][^11] LighthouseFriends says it went out "along with seven coastguardsmen from the station," with the relief keepers Bert Egelston and Carl Sprague aboard, and calls the storm the worst Wilson had seen in his thirty-five years of service.[^2] The county's release gives eight Coast Guardsmen besides Wilson and the two keepers.[^11]

The accounts differ on what happened next. The county says the crew "was able to relieve Jackson of his post" and that the boat's engine failed before the vessel capsized.[^11] LighthouseFriends says the waves smashed the boat against the crib, staving in planks and killing the engine, and that Jackson was taken aboard when this happened. It says the sea then carried the boat across the harbor entrance into the east breakwater.[^2]

The two accounts also differ on the survivors. The county says four men reached the east arrowhead breakwater. LighthouseFriends says two did, and that they were rescued an hour or so later.[^11][^2] Both give six dead. The county names Wilson and "Boatswain's Mate First Class Karl Jackson" among them.[^11] LighthouseFriends says two other bodies, including Jackson's, were found over thirty miles away near Henderson Harbor in April.[^2]

## After the keepers

The breakwater light was renamed Oswego Light Station in April 1917, and LighthouseFriends says it was electrified in 1921.[^2] The Commissioner's list of improvements for fiscal 1921 includes "Oswego Light Station, installed electricity as illuminant."[^9]

Coast Guard personnel were withdrawn in 1968, when the light was automated.[^2] The Fresnel lens came out of the lantern in 1995, when the light was converted to solar power, and went on display at the H. Lee White Maritime Museum.[^2] The National Park Service's asset record for the light gives 1 December 2000 as its date published and 1934 as its significant year.[^10]

In 2006 the lighthouse was offered to federal, state and local agencies and to nonprofit groups under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000.[^2] In May 2009 the City of Oswego received the deed, after negotiating a lease agreement with the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, which owns the harbor breakwall the light stands on.[^2] A $73,750 contract was awarded in 2012 to clean the inside of the lighthouse of hazardous materials, and by 2015 the interior restoration was nearly complete.[^2] The H. Lee White Maritime Museum leased the lighthouse from the city in 2014, a move made to expedite the restoration.[^2]

## Today

The archive lists the light as an active aid to navigation. Its current optic is listed as VRB-25, and the original optic as a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^1] The archive records the light as not open to the public.[^1]

LighthouseFriends describes boat tours run by the H. Lee White Maritime Museum, available on Fridays and Saturdays from mid-June to early September, and says the dwelling and tower are closed.[^2] A 2022 county release describes the museum's "educational lighthouse tours and preservation efforts."[^11] The LighthouseFriends page does not date its tour schedule, and no source gives a current one, so check with the museum before a visit. An acceptable view of the light can be had from the end of the pier where the museum is.[^2]

A memorial stone for the six men who died in 1942 was dedicated at Veterans Memorial Park in Oswego in 1997.[^2]
