The Hyannis Rear Range Light is a brick tower of about 19 feet (5.8 m) at the end of Harbor Road, on the shore of Hyannis Harbor in the village of Hyannis, Barnstable, on Cape Cod.[^1][^2][^3] It was the back light of a range that guided boats into Hyannis Harbor, and it has been dark since 1929.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

By the 1840s Hyannis was a busy port. LighthouseFriends says that "over 200 shipmasters were living in Hyannis and Hyannisport" by then.[^2] When Congress paid for a light in 1848, the purpose it gave was to "warn vessels away from Southwest Shoal."[^2]

The harbour had a private light first. Daniel Snow Hallett, a local man, "recognized the need for a lighthouse and decided to privately establish one."[^2] He moved a one-room shack onto the site, installed a light in its loft that shone through a four-paned window, and printed directions for sailing into the harbour, which he sold to coasting captains to help pay for the light. He also started a petition to Congress.[^2] His only child, Daniel Bunker Hallet, helped keep the lamp going: "the boy walked two miles with his dog, Pilot," across the fields in the evenings, slept in the loft, kept the whale-oil lantern trimmed, and went home for breakfast before school.[^2] The USLHS roster begins with Daniel S. Hallett in 1847, two years before the federal light was lit.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $2,000 on August 14, 1848.[^2] Stephen Pleasonton, the Fifth Auditor of the Treasury, who oversaw the lights, described what the money bought, as LighthouseFriends quotes him: "a small tower erected and fitted up with lamps and reflectors, for the sum appropriated."[^2] In Pleasonton's account, the man hired to attend the light was paid fifteen dollars a month.[^2]

The first federal keeper was appointed in February 1849.[^2] LighthouseFriends says $800 was approved for a keeper's house in September 1850, to be joined to the tower by a covered walk.[^2] The USLHS record instead dates the keeper's quarters to 1849.[^1] The sources disagree, and this story does not settle it. The tower is conical and brick. The USLHS record gives its height as 19 feet to the centre of the lantern and its first optic as an oil lamp with a reflector.[^1] Deyo's 1890 county history gives the tower as "twenty-one feet high."[^4] The two figures differ, and the sources do not explain why.

The lighting changed in steps. In 1856 a fifth-order Fresnel lens was installed, replacing five lamps in fourteen-inch reflectors. By 1863, LighthouseFriends says, "a single lamp set in a twenty-one-inch reflector was being used as the light source," and a new cast-iron lantern went on the tower that year.[^2] In 1878 the characteristic changed from fixed white to fixed red.[^2] Deyo, writing in 1890, describes the light as "a fixed red, visible nearly twelve miles."[^4] A summer kitchen and fuel-house were added to the dwelling in 1889, a brick oil house went up in 1902, and an iron deck replaced the wooden one on the tower in 1904.[^1][^2]

The rear light became half of a range only in 1885. On July 7, 1884, $350 was earmarked for a small beacon to range with the main light. The beacon went up on the Old Colony Railroad wharf in 1885, and when aligned with the rear light it guided boats into the harbour.[^2] Rowlett calls the rear light "the rear light of a range beginning in 1885."[^3] Storms often put the beacon out, and it had to be almost entirely rebuilt in 1886.[^2] Deyo also lists a "Hyannis Beacon light," described as a framed building with a red light that "is used in connection with surrounding lights in giving courses for safe navigation."[^4] His history does not say where it stood. It is probably the wharf beacon, but that is our inference, not his.

General context, not a Hyannis record: the 1881 *General Instructions to All Light-Keepers* told a keeper with no assistant to visit the light at least twice in the night between 8 p.m. and sunrise, and on stormy nights to keep it constantly looked after.[^6] The Hyannis sources do not say how those rules were kept there.

## The keepers

The USLHS list of principal keepers runs without a gap from Daniel S. Hallett in 1847 to Waldo Leighton in 1929.[^1] LighthouseFriends supplies the stories behind the names.[^2]

Daniel Snow Hallett's stint on the roster ends in 1849, the year James Bears was appointed. The USLHS archive gives his birth year as 1801 and his death year as 1852.[^5] James Bears was appointed on February 12, 1849, at a salary of $350 a year.[^2] Four years later Almoran Hallett, a brother of Daniel Snow Hallett, replaced him.[^2] Franklin Baker kept the light from 1861 to 1871, according to the archive. No other source here says more about him.[^1]

John H. Lothrop came from the pilot's trade. LighthouseFriends calls him "Pilot John," after a long career as a branch pilot helping boats from Maine to Virginia.[^2] He became keeper in 1871. His son Alonzo Freeman Lothrop helped him at the station and "took over following his father's death in 1878."[^2] Alonzo's records for 1882 show that "6,000 tons of coal, 1,250,000 feet of lumber and 4,000 barrels of fish" passed through the harbour that year.[^2] He resigned in 1899, and the source says it was "due to poor health."[^2]

John A. Peak kept the light from 1899 to 1915. He "frequently found himself arguing with railroad personnel" until the railway cars that blocked the front light were moved.[^2] Local children came to the lighthouse for sailing lessons in his time.[^2] LighthouseFriends also reports a rumour that Peak "positioned a bedroom mirror" so he could watch the light from his bed.[^2]

Waldo Leighton arrived in 1915 from Race Point, where he had served eight years.[^2] He supplemented his pay by caretaking nearby properties and by selling clams and scallops.[^2] When the light was decommissioned in 1929 he was transferred to Nobska Point, at Woods Hole.[^2]

## After the keepers

The light was decommissioned in 1929, when the main wharf moved to Lewis Bay.[^2] The lantern room was removed. The front range stayed on as an automatic acetylene gas beacon.[^2] A. W. Fuller bought the defunct station for about $7,000, and the property changed hands several times after that. LighthouseFriends says the old keeper's house "has been markedly altered and enlarged."[^2] Rowlett says it has been expanded at least twice and is now a private residence.[^3]

LighthouseFriends dates a new lantern room on the tower to 1986 and calls it "not quite traditional."[^2] Rowlett describes the top differently: the lantern, removed in 1929, "has been replaced by an octagonal sunroom (1989)."[^3] The two sources disagree on what went up and when. Rowlett records red bands added when the tower was repainted in 2012.[^3]

## Today

The tower is privately owned and is not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends lists its grounds, dwelling and tower as closed.[^2] Rowlett says the site and tower are closed, but that there are views from the street and from nearby Keyes Beach.[^3] The keeper's house is a private home, and this story does not name its owners.
