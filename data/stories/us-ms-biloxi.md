Biloxi Lighthouse is a cast-iron tower standing in the median of U.S. Highway 90 on the Mississippi Gulf Coast.[^8] It was completed in 1848 from iron sections bolted together and lined with brick.[^9][^6] It is the only one of the coast's thirteen historic lighthouses still standing, by the engineers' count, and the Younghans family tended it for over six decades.[^6][^1] LighthouseFriends counts "more than ten" lighthouses on the coast.[^8]

## The place

The Mississippi Sound lights were authorized in 1847 by legislation sponsored by Mississippi Representative Jefferson Davis.[^9] Stephen Pleasonton, the Fifth Auditor of the Treasury, wrote that he intended "to put a Cast Iron Light House at Biloxi."[^8]

Congress appropriated $12,000 "for the erection of a lighthouse at Biloxi, Mississippi" in March 1847.[^1] The United States then bought a one-acre tract "on the shores of the Mississippi Sound from one John Fayard."[^1] The Board's lists place the light at the "western entrance to Biloxi Bay, Mississippi."[^3] The site was "exposed to wind and water," and the nomination notes that "The site of the third capital of the French province of Louisiana" lay nearby.[^1]

## Building the light

The tower was cast by iron founders. A Baltimore firm won the contract "with a bid of $6,347.50," and the sum "included both construction costs and shipment from the East coast to Biloxi."[^1] The illuminating apparatus, "consisting of nine cast brass lamps and reflectors," was bought separately from Winslow Lewis.[^1]

The tower reached Biloxi "in March, 1848," aboard the brig *General North*, and Henry Scoles of New Orleans supervised its erection.[^1] The city's own account says the tower "was completed in the spring of 1848."[^9] The National Register nomination says the cast-iron tower is "believed to be the first lighthouse in the South" built of that material. Its inside wall is brick, "laid in a conical fashion to conform to the cast iron sections."[^1]

The sources give different heights. The nomination says the light "stands fifty-three feet above ground level" and "sixty-one feet" above the sea.[^1] The engineers' project profile also gives "53 feet in height above grade."[^6] The city's page refers to "the 64-foot-tall lighthouse."[^9] LighthouseFriends gives a "forty-five-foot-tall tower."[^8] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive gives a tower height of 48 feet.[^2]

The nomination says "The lens were fifth order power."[^1] The city's page says "In 1856, a fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the array of lamps and reflectors," and LighthouseFriends describes a fifth-order lens after the Civil War.[^9][^8] The sources disagree, and the order is given here as the nomination gives it.

## The keepers

Marcellus J. Howard was the first keeper, "the first lighthouse keeper at Biloxi," and he "served in that capacity until 1854."[^1][^2]

In 1854 Mary Reynolds took the post. The archive spells the name Maria J. Reynolds.[^2] LighthouseFriends calls her "the first female keeper, serving from 1854 to 1866."[^8] She "received the appointment at Biloxi with the aid of Mississippi's newly-elected senator, Albert Gallatin Brown."[^1] In June 1861, "a group of Biloxi citizens ordered the light extinguished and seized the key to the tower from Mrs. Reynolds."[^1] The group called itself the "Home Guards," and the nomination names its leader as Biloxi's mayor.[^1] "Mrs. Reynolds is officially listed as the lighthouse keeper until 1866."[^1]

"Perry Younghans replaced Mary Reynolds" in 1866, and "death terminated his position as lightkeeper in less than one year."[^1][^2] His wife Maria took over the duties "immediately upon her husband's death."[^1]

Maria Younghans kept the light for more than fifty years.[^1][^8] The Lighthouse Service's bulletin of 1925 records her death on 20 August 1925, "at the age of 83," and that she "was placed on the retired list at the close of December 31, 1918, having had over 51 years of active service."[^4] The nomination says she "did not officially retire from the position until 1920."[^1] The archive lists her from 1867 to 1918.[^2] The sources do not agree on her retirement year.

Her daughter Miranda became the "official assistant" and then the keeper.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives her "retirement on April 23, 1929."[^8] The archive lists her from 1919 to 1929.[^2]

After 1929 the sources differ. The nomination says Miranda "was replaced in that year by Biloxi's last civilian lightkeeper, W. D. Thompson," and that "Thompson's tenure ended in 1939, when the United States Coast Guard assumed jurisdiction over the structure."[^1] The archive lists William B. Thompson from 1929 to 1935, then Frederick A. Schrieber (1935 to 1937) and Joseph J. Olivier (1937 to 1940).[^2] LighthouseFriends says Olivier retired in 1940 "and the light was automated."[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Mrs. Reynolds wrote to the governor in 1861 about her service, saying: "I ascended the Tower at and after the last destructive storm" of 1860.[^1]

The Board recorded damage from the storm of 1 October 1893. Its report says that "This station was seriously damaged by the storm of October 1, 1893, when the breakwater was destroyed."[^3] The New Orleans *Daily Picayune* reported, as LighthouseFriends prints it, that "At Biloxi Mrs. Younghans, the plucky woman who was in charge of the light, kept a light going all through the storm."[^10][^8] LighthouseFriends says the breakwater was "repaired the following year using timber and concrete."[^8]

The Commissioner's report for 1920 lists repairs at the station: "Repaired lantern, galleries of dwelling and the outbuildings," and the "scaled and painted tower."[^5]

Hurricane Camille came in 1969. LighthouseFriends says it "destroyed the keeper's dwelling in 1969," and the Biloxi Lighthouse still carries water marks from it.[^8][^7] The tower's inside bears marks of the water levels: Camille "rose to 17.5 feet," and Katrina, in 2005, "reached 21.5 feet above mean sea level."[^8]

Hurricane Katrina struck in August 2005. The engineers' project profile says the lighthouse "withstood the storm surge that occurred during Hurricane Katrina in August 2005 but experienced a partial collapse of its interior brick lining."[^6] The city restored it in 2010 after a "14-month, $400,000 restoration."[^9] LighthouseFriends dates the rededication to 19 February 2010: "On February 19, 2010, a rededication ceremony was held."[^8]

## After the keepers

The city's own page says the Coast Guard "assumed responsibility for the light's operation" in 1939.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the light was automated in 1940, when Olivier retired.[^8] The nomination says the lamp was converted "from oil to electricity" in 1926.[^1][^8]

The Coast Guard deeded the tower to the city in 1968. The nomination says it was "deeded to the city of Biloxi by the United States Coast Guard in August, 1968."[^1] The city had acquired the keeper's dwelling in 1941 for a Chamber of Commerce office, at a cost of $1,200, according to LighthouseFriends.[^8] The dwelling was destroyed in 1969.[^8] The National Register listing followed: the nomination says the Keeper of the National Register listed it "on 10/03/1973."[^1]

## Today

The tower belongs to the City of Biloxi.[^9][^8] In late October 2026 WLOX reported that the lighthouse "has officially reopened after its restoration project, the first since Hurricane Katrina," after a $52,000 restoration.[^7] WLOX says the tower is "open daily for tours from 9 a.m. to 10 a.m." Admission is "$5 for ages 12 and up, $2 for ages 6 to 11, and free for children 5 and under," and tickets are sold at the Biloxi Visitors Center across the street.[^7] LighthouseFriends says both "Grounds open, tower closed" and that tours are offered daily from 9 to 10 a.m.; the newer reports describe tours.[^8][^7]
