Head Harbour Light stands on a small rocky islet off the northern tip of Campobello Island, New Brunswick, where it marks the way into Passamaquoddy Bay. Its wooden octagonal tower was built in 1829. The heritage statement calls it one of the oldest lighthouses still standing in Canada.[^2][^3]

## The place

The light is on an islet off the northernmost point of Campobello. Until 1830 people reached it mostly by boat. The islet can be reached on foot only at or near low tide.[^1] The Friends of the Head Harbour Lightstation say that David Owen, who owned Campobello, conveyed "a plot of land" on the northeast tip to the Crown two months before he died, "being one acre of land."[^3] The Friends also describe the light as a guide to ships entering Passamaquoddy Bay through Head Harbour Passage.[^3] LighthouseFriends records that £80 was spent in 1830 on a road from the north end of the island to Wilson's Beach, the nearest settlement.[^1]

## Building the light

In February 1829 the House of Assembly of New Brunswick appropriated £400 "for defraying the expense of building a Light House on the northern head of Campo Bello Island."[^1] The whole cost came to £656. The tower stood 15.5 m (51 ft) high, and the light was in operation that same year, with John Snell as its first keeper at £120 a year.[^1][^2]

A keeper's dwelling was built near the tower in 1840, and in 1842 a larger lantern room was placed on top.[^1][^2] That year a large red cross was painted on the eastern side of the white tower, as a daymark for ships in the Bay of Fundy.[^1] The heritage statement describes the cross as painted "on one side of the lighthouse."[^2]

## The keepers

John Snell lived in the lower part of the tower until the dwelling was built in 1840, and his family grew up there.[^1] LighthouseFriends lists John B. Snell as keeper from 1831 to 1861, and John R. Snell from 1861 to 1872. The Friends' history puts John Snell's time at about 1830 to the mid-1840s, so the two accounts do not agree.[^1][^3] In 1835 Keeper Snell was granted £25 to help pay the medical bills and passage to England of Henry Mably, who had been maimed in an "accidental explosion of a charge of gunpowder" while working at the light.[^1]

Mary Snell, the keeper's daughter, lost her sight at seven. In her later years she wrote down her memories of growing up at the light. They describe the sounds she came to know: "the screaming of the sea gulls," the splash of a passing steamboat's wheels, and the sailors singing as they weighed anchor to leave the harbour. LighthouseFriends quotes her account.[^1]

Later keepers included Henry McLaughlin, who held the light from 1872 to 1879 and was then moved to Grand Harbour. Samuel Craig, a fog-trumpet engineer, came in 1880 to keep the light and run the fog signal. Neil A. Seely served from 1882 to 1903, and the Department's keeper table for 1901 lists him at the "Head Harbour Lighthouse and Fog Whistle."[^1][^10] Then came a run of keepers and fog-alarm assistants, and LighthouseFriends' list ends with Douglas Daggett, who held the post from 1960 to 1971.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In a gale in 1830 the sea broke nearly halfway up the tower. The Saint John *Courier*, as LighthouseFriends reports it, recorded that the storm carried away the keeper's boat, firewood, spring water, a barn and every other loose item, and that a cow reached the shore of a neighbouring island.[^1]

Most of the station's later history in the sources is about its fog signal. In 1875 E. B. Winchester, captain of the International Steamship Company's *New York*, wrote to the Marine agent in Saint John. LighthouseFriends prints the letter, which said a steam fog whistle was needed at the light, because the fog-trumpet was not enough in thick weather when the wind came from the east.[^1] The steam trumpet tried in 1877, which cost $2,500, did not function satisfactorily and was discontinued the following year. An improved signal, which cost $6,199, went into operation on 30 January 1880. It sounded eight-second blasts with intervals of thirty-five seconds, and it was hailed as "the loudest and best sounding fog-alarm in the Bay of Fundy."[^1] On 8 December 1904 the fog bell, which had been rung by hand, began to be worked by machinery that gave two strokes every thirty seconds. It was set 24 m (80 ft) north of the lighthouse.[^5]

## After the keepers

The Department's reports show upkeep while the keepers were still there. In the fiscal year to 30 June 1906 the tower, dwelling and reservoir were being repaired.[^6] In the fiscal year to 30 June 1907 a new cistern was made inside the lighthouse.[^7] In the fiscal year to 30 June 1909 the tower was repaired, a new tramway was built, and the bridge from the fog-alarm building to the mainland was completed.[^8] A new fog-alarm building went up in 1914 and 1915. The heritage statement dates it, and a work shed, to 1914–1915, while LighthouseFriends gives 1915 and says it was fitted with a class-C diaphone.[^2][^1] In the fiscal year 1923–24 the old wooden sea wall at the base of the lighthouse was replaced by a concrete wall.[^9] The station was manned by Coast Guard staff until 1986.[^1] In 1988 the lighthouse was classified as a heritage site.[^1][^3] The Friends' website dates their taking over its care to 2000, when they became a non-profit, but the heritage statement records a transfer to the Friends in 2006.[^1][^3][^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard list still shows the light, as a private aid. It is a fixed red light, 17.6 m above the water and visible 13 nautical miles, from an octagonal tower with red and white horizontal bands, with a fog horn giving a four-second blast. It runs year round, 24 hours a day.[^4]

The station is cared for by volunteers. The Friends say it can be reached on foot only at low tide, that there is a small fee for crossing at that time, and that members get half-price tower tours.[^3] The Friends' website gives 11 October 2026 as the close of the 2026 season. Anyone planning a visit should check the tide and the Friends' schedule first.[^3]
