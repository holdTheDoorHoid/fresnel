Charlotte-Genesee Lighthouse stands on a hill above the west bank of the Genesee River, where the river meets Lake Ontario, near Rochester, New York.[^1][^2] Its sandstone tower was first lit in 1822, and it stopped being the port's light in 1881.[^1][^2] Today a historical society runs it as a museum, and the tower and keeper's house are open to visitors in season.[^9]

## The place

In 1804 President Thomas Jefferson made a seventy-mile stretch of Lake Ontario's south shore an official port district, centered on the river mouth.[^1] Ships were first guided in by a torch on a large pilot tree or by a lamp on one of the region's early hotels.[^1] LighthouseFriends puts the City of Rochester six miles south of the port, but the Society's record lists the station's city as Rochester.[^1][^2]

The river mouth was marshy, and its sandbars proved troublesome to ship traffic.[^1] A pair of 2,600-foot (790 m) piers, about 360 feet (110 m) apart, was built there in 1829.[^1] As land filled in around the piers, the entrance shifted north, away from the lighthouse.[^1]

In 1821 the recently widowed Mehitabel Hincher was paid $400 for a four-acre plot for lighthouse purposes.[^1]

## Building the light

Ashbel Symons was awarded the contract.[^1] The tower was forty feet (12 m) of native sandstone, topped by an eight-sided iron lantern of 144 panes, and the work came with a two-room keeper's cottage and a well.[^1] It took seven months in 1822 and cost $3,301.[^1] A separate contract fitted the tower with lamps and reflectors.[^1] In 1855 a fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced them.[^1]

In 1838 Lieutenant Charles T. Platt reported to the Secretary of the Treasury that the station was not in ideal condition: the ten lamps were worn out, and fourteen lantern panes were broken.[^1] He recommended a new stone lighthouse on the west pier.[^1] The government kept the 1822 tower in service and built a frame beacon on the pier instead.[^1]

Towers followed in order. A cast-iron tower went up on the west pier on 5 November 1880.[^1] The 1822 light was discontinued on 21 February 1881.[^1][^2] In 1884 its lantern room and fourth-order lens were moved to a wooden beacon on the west pier, which began showing on 1 April 1884.[^1]

Congress, by the act of 3 March 1891, appropriated $4,300 for a steam fog signal in place of the bell.[^3] The Light-House Board reported that the signal, set on the old light-house crib on the west side of the west pier, was completed and ready for operation on 30 September 1893.[^4]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster has forty-eight lines for the station, from 1822 to 1947.[^2] When the station opened, Giles H. Holden, who had strong backing from many influential citizens, was passed over in favor of David Denman, an elderly veteran.[^1][^2] When Denman died shortly into his term, Holden got the post.[^1] He raised some of his ten children at the lighthouse, was dismissed in late 1835, and refused to vacate until the spring navigation season.[^1][^2]

In 1853 Luther Jeffords was head keeper and minded the main light.[^1][^2] His assistant, Cuyler Cook, had served until that year; LighthouseFriends says Cook's father, Michael Cook, had helped build the light.[^1] Cook's replacement was his cousin Samuel Phillips, whose duty was to tend the pier light.[^1][^2]

John Stephenson was head keeper from 1861 to 1868 on the roster.[^2] LighthouseFriends says his body was found in the river more than a decade after Cook drowned, and that there were "suspicions of foul play."[^1] It gives no more detail.[^1]

George V. Codding kept the pier lights from 1913 until he retired in 1940.[^1][^2] Wilbur L. Folwell Sr., the last keeper, transferred to another station when the pier light was automated in 1947.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the evening of 18 August 1853, Samuel Phillips twice tried to reach the pier light by boat, and twice the heavy seas stopped him.[^1] On a further attempt he went with Cuyler Cook.[^1] After they reached the pierhead, Phillips lit the beacon, but a large wave knocked him down as he was leaving.[^1] He caught a bolt set in the pier and climbed back to the tower.[^1] Cook's boat had capsized, and he was drowned while trying to reach the pier.[^1] Phillips spent the night at the beacon until a lifeboat reached him the next day.[^1]

The annual reports measure the fog signal's running hours.[^4][^5] For the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894, the Light-House Board gives "some 116 hours" for the 6-inch steam whistle, which used about 5 tons of coal.[^4] For the year ended 30 June 1895 it gives "some 220 hours" and about 10 tons of coal.[^5]

The Light-House Board's report for 1896 records that the pierhead beacon was moved nearly 500 feet (150 m) to the outer end of the new extension of the west pier, and that the fog signal was moved about 1,000 feet (300 m) to a position near the end of that pier.[^6]

## After the keepers

In 1902 a beacon went up at the outer end of the east pier, and the rear light on the west pier was discontinued.[^1] In 1917 generators and storage batteries were installed to power the pier lights, and the west pier light changed from fixed red to occulting white.[^1]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year 1918 (July 1917 to June 1918) lists "Charlotte Harbor, N. Y." among the lights whose illumination was improved, with two electric incandescent lights.[^7] An undated detailed estimate in the same bound volume describes the station as a low frame tower with a fog-signal house on the outer end of the west pier.[^8] It proposes a steel structure "for this station with a higher tower."[^8]

In 1931 a skeletal steel tower with a red box replaced the light and fog-signal structure on the west pier.[^1] After the pier light was automated in 1947, the old lighthouse was home to the commander of the local Coast Guard station.[^1]

Coast Guard personnel occupied the dwelling until 1982, when the Coast Guard offered to lease the lighthouse property to the Charlotte Community Association.[^1] The Charlotte Genesee Lighthouse Historical Society was formed the next year to preserve the site.[^1] The society set out to restore the tower and cap it with a replica lantern in time for Rochester's sesquicentennial in 1984.[^1] The restored tower was relit on 18 June 1984, with a fourth-order Fresnel lens from the Ninth Coast Guard District.[^1]

In 1994 Monroe County took title and leased the property to the society.[^1] A cylindrical tower, the D9, was installed on the west pier in 1995, and it is the working pier light.[^1]

On 25 September 2014 a crane hoisted the restored lantern room back onto the tower.[^1] A replica fourth-order lens was placed in it and activated on 1 November, the anniversary of the first lighting in 1822.[^1] The stone was whitewashed in 2016.[^1]

## Today

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record marks the old tower as not an active aid to navigation.[^2] The working pier light is the D9 tower on the west pier, a different structure.[^1]

Monroe County, which took title in 1994, leases the property to the historical society, which runs tours of the tower and keeper's house.[^1][^9] For the 2026 season the tours run every Friday, Saturday and Sunday, from 1 May to 1 November, from 1:00 to 5:00 p.m. The last tour starts at 4:15 p.m.[^9]
