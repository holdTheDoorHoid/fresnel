Marblehead Light stands at the northern tip of Marblehead Neck, where it marks the eastern entrance to Marblehead Harbor in Essex County, Massachusetts.[^1][^2] A light has shown there since October 1835.[^2] The tower that stands now is a 105-foot (32 m) skeletal iron frame, first lit in April 1896, and the 1981 National Register form describes it as the only light in New England with that cast-iron form.[^1][^2][^3][^8]

## The place

Marblehead began as a fishing village and later built up a foreign trade, behind a long neck of land that shelters a deep harbor.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that in 1831 Eleazer Graves and other residents petitioned for a light at the harbor entrance, to protect the local economy.[^2] The Lighthouse Board's 1894 report described the harbor as "nearly landlocked" and said that "a light has been maintained to guide vessels in and out of this harbor."[^5]

The sources disagree about when the land was bought. The 1981 nomination dates the purchase of about three acres from Jos. W. Green and Ephraim Brown, for $375, to 1833, the same year it says Congress appropriated $4,500 for the buildings.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives about four acres and puts Congress's approval on 30 June 1834, with the purchase after it.[^2]

## Building the light

The first light was a brick tower about 23 feet (7 m) high, joined to the keeper's house by a covered walkway. Its octagonal lantern housed ten lamps burning whale oil, giving a fixed white light 50 feet (15 m) above mean high water.[^2] LighthouseFriends says it was first exhibited on 10 October 1835.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1835 as the year the station was established.[^3]

In 1857 a sixth-order Fresnel lens replaced the oil lamps and reflectors.[^2][^3] Houses built near the light later blocked its view. The 1981 form notes cottages blocking the tower in 1880, and LighthouseFriends dates the big summer houses to the 1870s.[^1][^2]

On 10 July 1883 an auxiliary light was first shown from a 100-foot (30 m) mast set up near the tower.[^1][^2] The keeper's house was replaced during James S. Bailey's keepership, at an estimated cost of $5,000. LighthouseFriends says the family moved into the larger two-story wood-frame house in 1878.[^2]

In 1893 the Lighthouse Board listed $45,000 for a "new tower" at Marblehead.[^4] Its report that year proposed "to build a brick tower about 100 feet high, on the site of the present tower," and its 1894 report renewed the recommendation.[^16][^5] The tower approved for Marblehead in 1895 was an iron skeleton design made by Chamblin, Delaney, and Scott of Richmond, Virginia. LighthouseFriends says it was likely chosen for its cost of $8,786, against the $45,000 estimate for a brick tower.[^2]

The Board's 1895 report says the light moved to a temporary tower on 22 June 1895, "to make place for the erection of a new tower," and that the old tower was demolished.[^6][^7] The light moved from the temporary tower to the new iron tower, built on the old site, on 17 April 1896.[^8][^2] The 1981 form gives the construction date as 1895.[^1]

A covered way was built from the dwelling to the new tower.[^9] LighthouseFriends dates a brick oil house to 1907.[^2] The Board's 1899 report records that the keeper's dwelling was connected to the city water supply.[^10]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster names nine principal keepers from 1835 to 1941.[^3]

Ezekiel Darling, a Duxbury man and former gunner on the USS Constitution, kept the light for twenty-five years, from 1835 to 1860.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends says he was wounded in the War of 1812 and was refused a war pension because he was "disfigured, but not disabled."[^2] In 1842 the engineer I. W. P. Lewis inspected the station. Darling had already complained to the inspector about a leaking tower, a sweating lantern and a damp dwelling. Lewis wrote, "Perfect order, cleanliness, and apparent comfort reign throughout the whole establishment, much to the credit of the keeper."[^15][^2]

When Darling was forced to retire in 1860, LighthouseFriends says he was nearly blind and almost seventy.[^2]

Jane C. Martin was appointed keeper in 1860. LighthouseFriends says she had gained experience assisting her father, Ambrose Martin, at Baker's Island Light.[^2] The roster gives her service as 1860 to 1863.[^3] LighthouseFriends has John Goodwin follow her in 1862 and serve ten years, where the roster has him from 1863 to 1872.[^2][^3]

James S. Bailey kept the light from 1872 to 1892 and replaced the dwelling (above). The rescues credited to him are told below.[^2][^3] Albert M. Horte's roster line covers only 1892 to 1893.[^3]

The roster has Henry T. Drayton from 1893 to 1925. LighthouseFriends gives 1892 as the year he took over in one place and says he and his wife spent thirty-five years at the station.[^3][^2] They raised eight children there, and five were born in the lighthouse.[^2] For most of the Drayton years the road to Marblehead was passable only at low tide. LighthouseFriends quotes a daughter, Mary, who remembered that "there were only two neighbors in the winter."[^2]

Russell B. Eastman followed, from 1925 to 1930.[^3] Edwin C. Rogers kept the light from 1930 to 1938. LighthouseFriends tells of the day his young son went missing and was found high on the tower, perched on the ventilator ball, and his father had to climb up to bring him down.[^2] Harry S. Marden took the post in May 1938, and the roster ends his service in 1941.[^3][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1843 Darling took a dory with four Marblehead men into what the 1857 account calls "a tremendous sea" and brought ashore the officers and crew of the brig John Hancock, which had been "dashed to pieces on Tinker's Island in a violent easterly storm." An 1857 newspaper account, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says he received a medal from the Humane Society for the rescue.[^14][^2]

In 1889 a Boston Globe article credited Keeper Bailey with rescuing seventeen people near the light.[^2] In one case he saved six men who had clung to the rigging of their schooner in a winter gale, and he cared for them for several days. When he asked the schooner's owners to repay the cost of feeding the men, he was told that "nobody had ordered him to feed them."[^13][^2] On another occasion he spied a boat in trouble in the harbor and went after a man from it; the Massachusetts Humane Society gave him a medal for the rescue.[^2]

On 21 September 1938 a hurricane that the National Weather Service counts among the most destructive in recorded history struck Long Island and southern New England.[^12] LighthouseFriends says that four months after Marden took the post, a storm it calls the worst in New England's history reached the coast, and the light lost its power. Marden drove his car up to the tower, connected its battery to the wiring, and stayed in the tower all night. LighthouseFriends says he was proud that the light kept shining through the storm.[^2]

During the First World War the station's boathouse was fitted with a telephone and turned into living quarters for military personnel watching the nearby waters.[^2] The light went dark during the Second World War and remained off-limits to the public from 1941 to 1946.[^2]

## After the keepers

Electricity reached the lighthouse in 1922, but not the keeper's dwelling. In the same year the light was changed to fixed red to set it apart from the backdrop of the town's lights.[^2] It became fixed green in 1938.[^2]

In 1947 Chandler Hovey gave the land around the light to the town for a park, which is named after him.[^2] The last family to live in the keeper's house was that of Joseph Barry, who ran the town's parks, and the house was pulled down in 1959.[^2] The light was automated in 1960, and its Fresnel lens was replaced by a modern optic.[^2][^3]

The paint history is also disputed. The 1981 form says the tower was painted white when it was built in 1895, and records it painted red in 1933, later green, and sandblasted and painted brown in 1980 at a cost of $100,000.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the tower "remains its original" metallic brown, and that periodic requests are made to paint it a "prettier" white.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the tower and licenses it to the Town of Marblehead, which looks after it, while the Coast Guard monitors the light.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society lists it as an active aid to navigation.[^3]

The grounds, in Chandler Hovey Park at the north end of the Neck, are open all year. The lighthouse itself is open by special arrangement only, and the tower is closed.[^11][^2]
