Duxbury Pier Light, known locally as Bug Light, is a squat cast-iron tower standing in the water at the mouth of Duxbury Bay, about five miles northeast of the town of Plymouth, Massachusetts.[^1] Lit in 1871, it is credited by the National Register of Historic Places nomination as the first lighthouse in the United States built on a submerged site with a cylindrical cast-iron caisson, a method later used for more than forty "sparkplug" lights.[^1]

## The place

Plymouth, Kingston and Duxbury share one bay, and the main channel into it runs past a shoal called Duxbury Pier, roughly midway between Saquish Head to the north and Pier Head to the south. The shoal was not prominently marked until the late 1850s, when an unlighted masonry beacon was built on it.[^1] Shipping grew through the 1860s, and Congress appropriated $17,931 on 15 July 1870 for a new light in the Plymouth area.[^1] LighthouseFriends adds that an earlier appropriation of $5,000, made in 1860, had been returned to the surplus fund two years later.[^2]

## Building the light

After test borings on the shoal, the Lighthouse Board decided to put the light directly on it. Ice was the worry: the Board wrote that "the run of ice is very severe from Plymouth Harbor," and that the tower would therefore be built "in one solid mass to a height of 15 feet above the water."[^1] The nomination traces the caisson idea to the English physician Lawrence Potts and notes that caissons had been used for bridge piers but not for lighthouses.[^1]

The iron caisson was assembled on shore and towed out. William Burgess Jr., who was there, remembered that it "was put together in North Dock and a cofferdam built inside of it, and one Sunday we towed it down with the government schooner and our sloop Rose Wood, placed it in position then broke the cofferdam and sunk it."[^1] He recalled a diameter of 28 feet at the bottom, while the Board's plan, as quoted in the nomination, called for 25 feet; the sources do not reconcile the two.[^1][^2] The caisson was filled with concrete to 15 feet above the water, iron courses were bolted on above it, and by June 1871 the tower stood 36 feet high.[^1]

The light was first shown on 15 September 1871 from a fifth-order lantern holding a fourth-order Fresnel lens made by Henri LePaute of Paris, according to the nomination and the Lighthouse Society archive; LighthouseFriends calls it a fifth-order lens.[^1][^2][^11] The nomination says the lens showed red through a red glass shade from the start, while LighthouseFriends says the light was changed from white to red on 30 April 1894.[^1][^2] A gallery was added to the tower in the fiscal year ended June 30, 1897,[^3] and a 1,000-pound fog bell, struck by clockwork, was installed in 1897 and replaced in 1902.[^1] A 700-gallon cistern was built into the concrete pier in the fiscal year 1900.[^4]

## The keepers

William Atwood lit the first lamp and stayed until 1878, with an assistant, John A. Richmond, from 1872.[^2][^11] The station was staffed with a keeper and one assistant.[^1]

LighthouseFriends, drawing on contemporary accounts, tells of the winter of 1875: in early February the captain of the revenue steamer *Gallatin* saw the station's distress flag. Ice kept boats away, so the steamer called across the water. The keeper and his wife had been cut off since 22 December, had run out of wood, and had been living on about a pint of water a day.[^2] A Connecticut weekly, reprinting a telegram that March, said the keeper told the vessel's captain he had had "no communication with any one" outside the light since 22 December; once the ice was cut through, the keeper and his wife were supplied with wood and water.[^14] The same source reports that the tower's landing ladder had to be repaired and stone piled around the base after that winter's ice.[^2]

Not every keeper was praised. In February 1892 a former keeper, James H. Bagnall, wrote to the Lighthouse Board that the light was often neglected because of the drunkenness and absences of Michael J. Curran, who had taken over on 21 April 1891; Bagnall wrote that "the light was never so badly run by any of the keepers since it was built." Curran was moved to another station in December 1892.[^1]

Milton Reamy began as assistant keeper here in December 1878. He later kept Plymouth and Minots Ledge lights, retiring on 1 January 1919; the Lighthouse Service Bulletin said he spent 28 years at Minots Ledge.[^7] In 1920 Tolman Spencer was rowing back to the station in winter cold when wind and tide held him off; LighthouseFriends, which lists a Boston Post article of 2 January 1921 among its references, says his wife raised the alarm and went out in a motorboat, and that he was found far out in the harbour, ice-covered and exhausted.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Ice kept coming. After the winter of 1917-18 the Lighthouse Service allotted $11,000 for ice repairs here and at Scituate Breakwater. At Duxbury Pier it deposited 600 tons of riprap in a mound on the northwest side of the tower, about 25 feet away, to guard against heavy field ice and to give the keepers a more sheltered landing.[^5]

Keepers answered boats in trouble. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1917 volume reports that keeper Frank A. Davis helped a disabled power boat that was in danger of going ashore; the boat's name is too garbled in the scanned text to read with confidence.[^8] In 1935 the Bulletin commended keeper Frederick C. "Bohn" (the roster spells him Bohm) and assistant George S. Bell Jr. for rescuing two men in a small boat that was filling with water in a strong wind on 27 May.[^9][^11] An item in the 1936 volume says assistant Ernest H. Stacey towed in a man drifting past the station with broken oarlocks and repaired his boat.[^10] The nomination relays regional historian Edward Rowe Snow's claim that Bohm rescued 90 people, among them 36 Girl Scouts, in a single year.[^1]

A small earthquake on the night of 28 February 1925 was felt in the tower. The keeper reported that it swayed for about a minute and that a teakettle two-thirds full slid along the stove.[^6]

After the Coast Guard took over in 1939, a three-man detachment worked two weeks on and one off, with two always on station. One of them, Harry Salter, left an account of the 1944 hurricane: waves of about 30 feet struck, a wave opened a trapdoor in the gallery floor, and he fell through. Another wave pushed him against the ladder and he climbed back. The storm destroyed the station boat, the outdoor privy and the fog signal equipment room.[^1]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1964, ending resident keepers.[^1] The tower deteriorated and was vandalised. In 1983 the Coast Guard planned to replace it with a fibreglass tower; local residents formed Project Bug Light and won a five-year lease to restore it.[^1] The Coast Guard also worked on it from 1983 to 1985, and again from 1996 to 2001, when a contractor scraped over 1,200 pounds of rust and applied three coats of paint.[^1] The tower was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2014.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that the group's volunteers spent 420 hours cleaning and painting it in 1994-95.[^2]

## Today

Bug Light is still an active aid to navigation, showing two red flashes every five seconds, and is visible from shore.[^1] The Lighthouse Digest reported that in September 2019 the General Services Administration transferred ownership from the Coast Guard to the nonprofit Project Gurnet and Bug Lights.[^13] The group describes Bug Light as lying on the channel to Plymouth, Kingston and Duxbury harbours.[^12] The tower is offshore and not open to the public; it is best seen from the water or from shore.[^2][^11]
