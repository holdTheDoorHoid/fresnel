Long Eddy Point stands on the northern tip of Grand Manan, New Brunswick. For most of its history it was a fog signal rather than a light. Islanders still call it "The Whistle." A steam whistle sounded there from 1874, and a combined concrete tower and fog alarm was built in 1966.[^1][^2]

## The place

Grand Manan sits at the mouth of the Bay of Fundy. The heritage statement says the station was set up to serve the island's fishing and shipping, and that the thick fog around Grand Manan had caused "a significant number of shipwrecks."[^2] The first signal stood on the north-west head of the island, on a terrace halfway up the cliff.[^1][^4] Supplies came by steamer to a wharf that James Tatton built, with a wagon road up to the station.[^1]

## Building the light

The notice to mariners of 1 May 1874 announced that "a steam fog-whistle has been erected on the north-west head of Grand Manan."[^1] It cost $8,318.43. Its start was held up when the Allan Brothers foundry in Carleton burned, and the signal began operation on 1 July 1874. LighthouseFriends says it "could frequently be heard in Eastport," across the water in Maine.[^1]

The station's first engineer was James Tatton, who owned the land and was paid $700 a year.[^1] Tatton also built the wharf and the road to it.[^1] The keeper's dwelling was too small for two families. LighthouseFriends quotes the Department's annual report of 1875 as asking for an addition to house the assistant engineer's family, who was a son of Mr. Tatton.[^1]

The first fog alarm stood on the terrace, where, the Department's report of 1905 says, "the best acoustic results could not be obtained."[^4] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1905 the Department rebuilt the station on the shingle beach at the foot of the cliff, where "the sound should throw out better to seaward." The new diaphone, run by compressed air, was put in operation on 15 January 1905.[^4] In August 1906 the signal was changed to two blasts of 2½ seconds each minute.[^5]

A new keeper's dwelling was built in 1948.[^1] In 1966, according to LighthouseFriends, a combined lighthouse and fog alarm building went up on the bluff at the northern end of the island, with a red flashing light on a square tower.[^1] The Register describes it as "a square, reinforced concrete tower combined with a fog alarm building," and counts it as the first lighthouse built on the site.[^2]

## The keepers

The first name on LighthouseFriends' keeper list is "Tatton," from 1874 to 1886, and its text identifies him as James Tatton, the first engineer.[^1] George Thomas Tatton kept the station from 1886 to 1922. The Department's keeper table in its report for the year to 30 June 1906 lists "Tatton, Geo. T" with the "Long Eddy Point Fog Whistle, Grand Manan."[^1][^6] A C. Tatton is listed from 1922 to at least 1939.[^1] So the Tatton name runs through the station's first sixty-five years.

The keepers from 1965 onward are named only as the LighthouseFriends list prints them: Percy Alger Harvey, 1965 to 1977, Willard W. Parker, 1977 to 1982, and Barry Bagley, 1983 to 1989.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In January 1857 the British ship *Lord Ashburton*, from Toulon for Saint John, was lost on Grand Manan. An Eastport dispatch in the *New-York Tribune* reported that all the officers were lost and that eight men were saved, "badly frozen."[^7] The same dispatch reports the ship *Manlius* lost on the island too, and says her crew were rescued after being "over a week in the boat and in the woods."[^7]

LighthouseFriends tells how James Tatton, led by his dog, found the body of James Lawson in his barn at Long Eddy. Lawson had survived the wreck of the *Lord Ashburton*, scaled a cliff face and stumbled a mile to Long Eddy before collapsing in the barn. Tatton then began the search that saved seven other crewmen.[^1]

The heritage statement says the station was set up to provide only an audible signal, "necessitated at this location by the thick fog."[^2] In 1893 an abutment 18 m (60 ft) long was built to protect the fog alarm from debris falling from the cliff, and a new abutment followed in 1897.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard list gives Long Eddy Point a red light that flashes, 38.3 m above the water, with a nominal range of 14 nautical miles. It shows from a white rectangular tower with a red top, 9.3 m high. The fog horn sounds a four-second blast, then is silent for 56 seconds, and the station runs year round.[^3]

The Register says the station is "still known locally as 'The Whistle'," and that it is "highly valued by community members and tourists alike for its scenic views." Whale and bird watchers come to the point too.[^2] The Coast Guard owns the lighthouse. The grounds are open and the tower is closed. The keeper's dwelling still stands near the lighthouse.[^1] To get there, take the car ferry from Blacks Harbour to North Head on Grand Manan, then follow Whistle Road for about 5 km.[^1]
