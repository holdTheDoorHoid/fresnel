Buffalo Main Light stands at the mouth of the Buffalo River, on the grounds of a Coast Guard station. Its octagonal limestone tower was finished in 1833. The light was discontinued in 1914, and the US Lighthouse Society record lists it as no longer an active aid to navigation.[^3] The Coast Guard still owns the tower, a local association looks after it, and it opens to visitors on scheduled tour days.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5]

## The place

Buffalo sits where Lake Erie meets the Niagara River, and Congress made it a port of entry in 1805. The New York legislature proposed buying land for a lighthouse six years later, but the War of 1812 put the plan off, and the British burned the village.[^1] The site chosen lay at the mouth of Buffalo Creek, a creek later renamed the Buffalo River.[^2]

The town grew once the Erie Canal opened. The canal's western end emptied into the harbor at Buffalo in 1825, and the population quadrupled between 1825 and 1832.[^1] In the same years the government decided that the old light could scarcely be seen through "the smoke of the village."[^1]

## Building the light

In 1817 Oliver Forward, the collector of the port, was commissioned to find a site. He used $351.50 of his own money to buy one.[^2] Congress allocated $17,000 that March for two lighthouses, at Buffalo and at Erie, Pennsylvania, and both began operating the following year.[^1] LighthouseFriends notes a dispute over which of the two was the first lighthouse on the Great Lakes.[^1] George Putnam's 1917 history of the lights records that "the first lights appear to have been established in 1818 at Buffalo."[^6] Penberthy describes the first tower as a 30-foot (9.1 m) stone tower, while LighthouseFriends calls it a sloping conical tower that went up in 221 days.[^2][^1]

In 1826 the Treasury Department appropriated $2,500 "to erect and build a pier, and lighthouse and ice-breaker." Penberthy says the contract was to be finished by 15 November 1829. LighthouseFriends says 1828.[^2][^1]

The octagonal tower of cut gray limestone was completed in 1833, a year after Buffalo became a city, and its year is cut into the lintel above the door.[^2][^1] Penberthy gives the height to the top of its iron lantern as 44 feet (13.4 m). The light came from fifteen oil lamps set in parabolic reflectors.[^1] A greenish lens placed in front of them was taken out after the harbor superintendent and the collector of the port found that the reflectors alone gave a better light.[^2][^1]

A Navy lieutenant, G. J. Pendergast, described the light in 1837. LighthouseFriends quotes his description: "At Buffalo, there is already a good stationary light, on a stone tower at the end of the harbor pier."[^1]

In 1852 the Lighthouse Board recommended the tower as one of twenty principal lake lights to receive a third-order Fresnel lens, and $2,500 was set aside for a fog bell.[^1][^2] The bell's installation was delayed until 1856. Both sources tie the delay to poor performance by similar bells on the Maine coast, and Penberthy says the wait was to "perfect the fog-bell machinery."[^1][^2] The lens arrived in 1856.[^1] The old lantern was too small for it, so the tower was reworked.[^2] An extra story of stone casement windows and a new two-story lantern raised the tower to 68 feet (21 m), by Penberthy's count, and the focal plane to 76 feet (23 m), by LighthouseFriends' count.[^2][^1] The USLHS place record gives 1857 as the year of the original lens, and gives the height from base to lantern centre as 61.5 feet (18.7 m).[^3] The sources do not agree on these dates and heights, and this story gives each figure with its source.

## The keepers

The first keeper LighthouseFriends names is John E. Skaats, head keeper from 1818 to 1827. The USLHS roster does not start until 1833, so Skaats appears only in LighthouseFriends.[^1][^3]

The USLHS roster has 20 entries for 19 different people. Apart from one undated entry under a "Contractor" heading, they fall between 1833 and 1914.[^3] Its first principal keeper is William Jones (1833–1834), followed by Sylvanus Russell (1834–1837) and Alexander Ramsdell (1837–1855).[^3] LighthouseFriends gives Jones's term as 1827 to 1834 and Russell's as 1834 to 1838, and it calls Ramsdell "Captain Alexander Ramsdell" for 1838 to 1855. This story uses the archive's dates. LighthouseFriends also lists a James Anderson as head keeper from 1855 to 1859, a name the roster does not carry.[^1][^3]

Ramsdell's eighteen years, by the roster's dates, make him the longest-serving principal keeper on the list.[^3] Edward Lee kept the light from 1861 to 1871, and Wallace C. Hill from 1871 to 1876.[^3] George J. Safe was principal keeper from 1893 to 1905, and his successor, John Safe, held the post until 1914, the year the light was discontinued.[^3] George Safe also served as first assistant in 1909 and 1910.[^3] The other assistants on the roster are Fernando Jones (1833–1834), Edgar C. Ames and Alonzo C. Brady (both 1873), Daniel D. Hill (1905–1907), John A. Smith (1907–1908), Edward C. Wendell (1908–1909) and George M. Fetes (1910–1914).[^3] The archive also lists a Samuel Wilkinson under a "Contractor" heading, with no dates.[^3]

The work was physical. From 1905 the new lens turned on clockwork driven by suspended weights, and the keepers had to wind the weight up every seven hours.[^1] The weight descended through a drop tube, and holes had to be cut through two of the tower's stone steps to fit it.[^1] In 1906 a two-ton coal bin was built into the tower's lower level, because a keeper complained that the stove did not heat the watchroom properly.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the night of 31 October 1821 the steamboat *Walk-in-the-Water* was caught in a storm and struck the beach near the light. LighthouseFriends calls her the only steamboat on the Great Lakes at that time, and says the lighthouse served as a rescue station that night.[^1] A survivor, Mrs. Alanson W. Welton, recalled the fireside the survivors gathered around at the lighthouse, which "was comforting to our chilled limbs."[^1]

The Great Storm of 1844 did serious damage to the works around the light.[^2] A northeast wind began driving water up the lake on 15 October. On the evening of 18 October the wind shifted to the southwest and pushed the water into Buffalo, where many areas were under up to eight feet (2.4 m) of water.[^2] The storm breached the breakwater that ran out toward the light, and some stones weighing as much as two tons were moved more than twenty feet (6.1 m) from their places.[^2] The government began rebuilding the breakwall the following year, with stones of one to three tons set in hydraulic cement. The work stopped in 1846 and resumed in 1853.[^2]

Two smaller entries in the Board's reports show the routine upkeep and its trouble. In its report for the fiscal year 1891–92 the Light-House Board noted that "some 236 lineal feet of plank walk, 4 feet in width, around the keeper's dwelling, were relaid with new material."[^7] In the fiscal year 1893–94 a vessel's anchor pulled up the pipe that supplied the station with water from the river, and the Board reported: "the pipe which supplied the station with water for domestic uses was torn up from the bed of the river by a vessel's anchor."[^8]

## After the keepers

The port changed around the light. Construction of the breakwater began in 1868.[^2][^1] Penberthy gives its length as 4,000 feet (1,219 m), and LighthouseFriends gives 2,500 feet (762 m).[^2][^1] On 15 July 1870 an appropriation of $30,000 was made for two lighthouses.[^2] The north-end breakwater light was completed in 1872 and received the fog bell from the old tower.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the new breakwater light pushed the old tower down to the rank of a pierhead light.[^1]

In 1902 the Lighthouse Board voted to change the tower's characteristic from a fixed glow to white flashes, so that it would stand out against the electric lights of the city.[^1] A four-panel third-order lens made by Chance Brothers & Co. of England was installed on 8 April 1905. Its cost was $3,386.74, which LighthouseFriends notes was more than six times a keeper's yearly salary.[^1] Its flash came once every five seconds.[^1]

During Prohibition the tower served as a watchtower against rumrunners.[^1] The station was discontinued in 1914. The four-panel lens went to a new breakwater lighthouse, and the old tower stayed dark for most of the twentieth century.[^1][^3] A red occulting light on a steel tower was set up on the south pier, just east of the old light, when it was discontinued.[^1]

In the 1950s the Army Corps of Engineers planned to demolish the tower for a river-widening project. Public outcry saved it, and it was restored in 1962.[^1] Floodlights lit it briefly in 1976 for the bicentennial and in 1982 for Buffalo's sesquicentennial.[^1]

The National Park Service's National Register record for the tower gives 19 July 1984 as its date published.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the tower was added to the Register in 1979.[^1] The record's PDF, at 22 KB, appears to be a placeholder rather than the nomination text.[^9] In 1984 the tower was leased to the Buffalo Lighthouse Association. The association's own website says it was formed in 1985.[^1][^4] From 1987 to 2013 a fourth-order lens from the South Buffalo light was on display in the lantern.[^1]

In 2010 the Coast Guard announced it would give up a few of its thirty-one acres on the point, and Congressman Brian Higgins obtained over $6 million to reconfigure the station for public access. The lighthouse was opened to the public in October 2011.[^1] After 9/11, tours had been cancelled and public access had been limited.[^1] In August 2013 the fourth-order lens was taken down for repairs. An acrylic replica of a third-order lens was installed in the lantern on 28 September 2015, and the light was relit on 25 June 2016. It is not an official aid to navigation.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the tower, and the Buffalo Lighthouse Association manages it.[^1] The grounds are open during daylight hours in season. The tower opens on scheduled tour days or by appointment, and a group of 30 or more needs a reservation.[^5] LighthouseFriends describes the grounds as open seasonally and the tower as closed,[^1] so check the association's current schedule before a visit. The tower stands on Coast Guard property, at the station on the south side of the river entrance.[^1] The four-panel third-order lens once used in the tower is on display at the Buffalo and Erie County Historical Society.[^1] The lantern now holds a replica, and the light is not an official aid to navigation.[^1]
