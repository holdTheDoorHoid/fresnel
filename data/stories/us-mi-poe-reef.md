Poe Reef Light stands on a concrete pier in Lake Huron, about six miles east of Cheboygan, Michigan,[^8] marking a shoal at the eastern end of the South Channel that runs between Bois Blanc Island and the Lower Peninsula. It replaced a succession of lightships, and unlike a lightship, a lighthouse could stay on station through the winter.[^2]

## The place

Poe Reef lies off the southeast end of Bois Blanc Island. The Light-House Board described it in 1893 as "at the entrance of the Straits of Mackinac" and noted that the combined tonnage from Green Bay and the rest of Lake Michigan passed it on the way to the lower lakes.[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives the reef a least depth of twelve feet (3.7 m) and describes a spit running a mile out from the island, with the reef lying beyond it.[^2] The reef takes its name from Orlando M. Poe, chief engineer of the eleventh lighthouse district.[^2]

The first aid here was a ship. An appropriation act of August 1892 let the Board turn money meant for another station into light-vessels, and it chose to mark the reef with one of them, No. 62. The Board's 1893 report described her as a wooden vessel under contract at Toledo, Ohio, to be 80 feet 9 inches (24.6 m) long, with a red hull lettered in white and a 6-inch steam whistle as fog signal; Lighthouse Friends gives her length as just over 87 feet.[^1][^2] The same Board report footnotes that she was placed on her station on 29 September 1893, while its next report lists her light and whistle as shown from 27 September.[^1][^3] Lightships followed: No. 62 served until 1910 and changed places with No. 59, then the steel No. 96 took the station in April 1915, and No. 99 arrived in 1921.[^2][^4] The Commissioner of Lighthouses described No. 96 as 101 feet long with an electric light on her mast and a 6-inch air siren.[^4]

## Building the light

Shipowners' pressure for something permanent began early; Lighthouse Friends reports that the Lake Carriers' Association asked for a lighthouse and fog signal as early as 1913, and that Congress made an initial appropriation of $78,000 in early 1927.[^2] A base was leased on the Cheboygan River, and the district's work force built a timber crib, which was towed out and sunk in 24 feet (7.3 m) of water on the southeast end of the reef. A bunkhouse was lifted onto the crib on 13 September 1928 so the men could live on site.[^2]

The station was placed in commission on 15 August 1929.[^2][^5] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that it stood a short distance from the position of the lightship it replaced and was the first step in a larger plan for the Straits of Mackinac.[^5] As quoted by Lighthouse Friends, the Commissioner's report described a 60-foot-square (18 m) pier of concrete over a timber crib, a steel tower with a third-order lens at a focal plane 71 feet (21.6 m) above the water, an air diaphone fog signal and a radiobeacon, with living space for three keepers.[^2] Poe Reef and Cape Henry were, by Lighthouse Friends' account, the first US stations to have synchronised radiobeacons and fog signals.[^2]

In September 1931 a diver found the crib undermined at all four corners, worst at the northwest and southeast, to a depth of 8 to 18 inches.[^6] The northwest corner was repaired that autumn, and the southeast corner was cleared of gravel and filled with concrete by the tremie method. Lateness of the season put off the other two corners to the 1932 working season, after which 1,952 tons of riprap were placed around the crib.[^6][^2]

## The keepers

The first head keeper was Fred Kling, who came from Cheboygan Light when the older station was discontinued and who had entered the service in 1901. The archive lists him as Frederick L. Kling, 1929 to 1931, while Lighthouse Friends says he ended his thirty-year career in 1930.[^2][^7] Later heads included Clarence P. Tupper (1932 to 1936), Clarence R. Land (1936 to 1944), Robert E. Westveld (1947 to 1954) and Earl F. Duffy (1954 to 1964).[^7]

Tupper's 1936 log entries, his last season before a transfer to Forty Mile Point, are quoted on Lighthouse Friends. On 1 May he recorded sounding for fog all day and night, and on 7 June, after another foggy morning, he wrote: "Too long hours and too much work." On 12 July he wrote of "A long, lonesome, hot, dismal, dreary day. Just like prison," adding that everything was covered with herring flies.[^2]

Westveld, a service-station owner from Fenville, Michigan, took the keeper's examination in 1934 when business was slow and began as third assistant at Poe Reef.[^2][^7] Lighthouse Friends says he felt safe on the offshore station even in spring, when the current pushed ice against the pier hard enough to make hanging lamps swing in the upper floors; in severe storms, it adds, spray from waves breaking on the deck struck the fourth-storey windows.[^2] The Coast Guard took over lighthouses in 1939; Westveld stayed on as a civilian, and Coast Guard crews are listed here from 1952.[^2][^7]

## After the keepers

The light was painted all white at first; in 1957 the top and bottom thirds were painted black.[^2] It was automated in 1974 and the last Coast Guard crew left.[^2]

## Today

The General Services Administration declared the lighthouse excess in 2013. A 2017 auction closed with a high bid of $112,111 that was not completed, and a second auction in 2019 closed on 23 September with a high bid of $97,000.[^2][^8] The tower stands on bottomlands owned by the State of Michigan, so a buyer must sign a private-use agreement, and the Coast Guard keeps the light and an access easement.[^8] Who bought the lighthouse after the 2019 auction is not recorded in the sources cited here. A regional tourism page still describes the tower as Coast Guard owned and closed.[^9] It is best seen from the water, on lighthouse cruises from Cheboygan, or from Lighthouse Point in Cheboygan State Park.[^9][^2]
