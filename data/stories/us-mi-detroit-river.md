The Detroit River Light is a cast-iron tower standing on a stone pier in the open water of western Lake Erie, off the mouth of the Detroit River in Monroe County, Michigan. It was built in 1884 and 1885 on a timber crib that had been framed at Amherstburg, Ontario, and it took the place of a Canadian lightship that had marked the Bar Point shoal since 1875 and was removed once the new light was done.[^1] Its keepers lived inside the tower.[^1]

## The place

Bar Point is a shoal that runs out from the Canadian shore into the northwest corner of Lake Erie. LighthouseFriends reports that in 1873 the Lighthouse Board noted a run of groundings there, including several the previous autumn, and explained them this way: ships leaving or entering the Detroit River had no clear signal for when to turn, and in heavy weather they turned too soon.[^2]

A lightship was already there. Heap's 1889 book on lighthouses says the Canadian government had kept one on Bar Point since 1875, but judged it "not adequate to the needs of commerce": it could be seen only a short distance, and its lights were hard to tell from the lights of vessels near by.[^1] The Lighthouse Board agreed that a fixed structure was still needed, because the lightship was hard to pick out among passing vessels.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated money in stages: $20,000 on 7 August 1882, $40,000 on 3 March 1883 and $8,000 on 3 March 1885.[^2] Heap gives the total as $68,000 for a lighthouse and steam fog signal at the mouth of the river.[^1] The site was chosen so that ships coming from the east could steer straight for the light and clear the shoal, and so that ships from the southwest could use it as a range with the Canadian Bois Blanc light.[^1] The engineer in charge was Captain C. E. L. B. Davis of the Corps of Engineers, who also drew the plans.[^1]

The foundation was a timber crib 90 feet (27 m) long, 45 feet (13.7 m) wide and 18 feet (5.5 m) high. It was built at a wharf in Amherstburg with the permission of Canada. Framing began on 19 March 1884, and the crib was sunk on 3 July.[^1] One source adds that a carpenter was killed there when he fell and struck the bottom of the crib.[^2] By the end of the season the pier had settled unevenly, by nearly 16 inches. The builders loaded it with 550 tons of rubble stone and left it for the winter. When the men guarding the warning lights left, the settlement had stopped at 18 inches and the pier was level.[^1]

Work resumed in May 1885. The station was finished and lit by 20 August of that year.[^1] The iron tower came from the Russell Wheel and Foundry Company of Detroit, which won the contract with the lowest bid, $7,450. Its three lower storeys were lined with brick to make rooms for the keepers, and a fourth-order Fresnel lens sat in the lantern.[^2] A frame fog-signal building stood at the other end of the pier, with two steam whistles.[^2]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the keeper registers, lists keepers from 1885 to 1969. For the civilian years it shows Charles D. Northrup as the first head keeper (1885-1886), followed by Richard Oldrey (1886-1890). Northrup came back as head keeper in 1890 and stayed until 1898. In between he served as second assistant and then first assistant.[^3] Enoch W. Scribner moved up the same ladder: second assistant in 1891, first assistant in 1893 and head keeper from 1902 to 1912.[^3]

The Lighthouse Board described the isolation plainly in 1899. The light stood on a stone pier six miles from the nearest provision market, Amherstburg, and nine from South Rockwood, Michigan. The keepers could land only in good weather and were sometimes kept from returning for several days. They had to leave their boats on the shore, "unprotected from the weather and from thieves."[^4] Congress approved $1,000 for a boathouse in June 1900. A lot of about an acre was bought on the Huron River near its mouth, and the boathouse went up with room for three boats and a part of the upper floor fitted out for the keepers to sleep and cook in when needed.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Ships hit the pier more than once. LighthouseFriends reports that on the night of 21 July 1886 the schooner *E. Blake*, in tow of the Canadian steam barge *Tilley*, struck the pier and damaged a railing and a boat crane, a bill of $377.60 that was not collected until 1890.[^2] In May 1892 the schooner *Constitution*, in tow of the steamer *Oscoda*, struck it again. The Board's report says the damage to the railing was repaired and the owners paid.[^6]

Weather found its way indoors too. The report of 1894 says the 15 circular windows in the iron tower "did not exclude wind and water in storms," so they were taken out and replaced with new ones in brass frames.[^7] The fog whistle ran about 110 hours in that report year and about 122 hours in the year ended June 1899.[^7][^4]

On 22 August 1921, by the Bulletin's account, a yacht, the *Lucille* of the Toledo Yacht Club, with three men and three women aboard, caught fire near the light. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin says Keeper Harry Keondway, first assistant R. E. Dissett and second assistant L. C. DeRusha went to the blazing yacht and rescued all six, who had been forced to jump overboard. One of the station's own rowboats caught fire from the yacht in the rescue.[^8] The Bulletin gives the date as 22 August; LighthouseFriends says 21 August and adds that the keepers took the six to the station until a passing steamer was hailed.[^8][^2]

On the night of 23 August 1932 Head Keeper John Sweet and second assistant William J. MacDermaid answered a call from two men who had to abandon a sinking motorboat. They pulled the exhausted men from the water and gave them dry clothes. MacDermaid was commended.[^2] By 1931 the station had a radiotelephone, which the keepers used to talk to the Detroit depot twice a day. It was also used when a young woman visiting a keeper was hurt landing at the pier. A radio call asked anyone who heard it to contact the Coast Guard at Trenton; amateur radio operators picked it up, and a Coast Guard vessel reached the pier in a little over an hour.[^2]

In December 1997 the freighter *Buffalo*, bound from Detroit to Cleveland with 27 crew, struck the lighthouse at about 5:20 a.m. on a Friday, according to an Associated Press report dated 13 December. The Coast Guard reported a 25-foot gash in the ship's bow, no injuries, and only minimal damage to the lighthouse's stone foundation; the light stayed in service. The AP gives the ship's length as 635 feet, LighthouseFriends as 625.[^9][^2]

## After the keepers

The station was automated in 1979, ending its resident crew.[^2][^10] A radio fog signal came in 1925 and a new fog-signal building with a heating plant and an air horn in 1936. A gauge board on the pier, added in 1924, showed passing ships the depth of water in the river, and an amber low-water warning light was added in 1927.[^2] The second fourth-order Fresnel lens used in the lighthouse is now on display at the Michigan Maritime Museum in South Haven.[^2]

The Coast Guard declared the light excess in May 2015. It was offered to eligible organizations under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act; after no suitable steward was found, it was sold at auction in 2017.[^2][^10] LighthouseFriends reports a winning bid of $188,500; Lake Erie Living gives about $188,000.[^2][^10] The buyer, Daryl Jamieson of Gibraltar, Michigan, planned a restoration and a bed-and-breakfast, formed the Detroit Lighthouse Society to raise money, and was working with a historic preservation firm.[^10] He died in 2023 before restoration work began.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is an active aid to navigation, and the US Lighthouse Society's archive records it as not open to the public.[^3] The National Register's record for the Detroit River Light Station is dated 4 August 1983.[^11] The tower stands in open water, and in rough weather it is nearly impossible to get onto the crib.[^10] LighthouseFriends's writer reached it on a chartered boat from the south end of Grosse Ile, a trip of about six miles.[^2] The Coast Guard keeps the right to reach the light and horn for maintenance.[^10] Who holds the property after 2023 is not stated in the accounts used for this history.
