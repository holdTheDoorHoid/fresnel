The Cape North light at Money Point, on the northern tip of Cape Breton Island, Nova Scotia, used a red-and-white iron tower from 1911 to 1980. The tower had first been built at Cape Race, Newfoundland, in 1856, and it now stands on the grounds of the Canada Science and Technology Museum in Ottawa.[^3][^5][^2][^1]

## The place

The Cape North station stands on Money Point, about one nautical mile southeast of the cape.[^4] NSLPS describes Cape North as a bold, steep-sided, rocky headland, and notes that "Violent local squalls are common off this headland."[^4]

The Cape Race light had its origins in shipwrecks.[^3] LighthouseFriends says several wrecks off the southeastern corner of Newfoundland prompted the 1856 tower.[^3] The light was to be paid for with tolls on shipping, and the colonies were slow to agree to them.[^1] Zachary Tingley's article for Ingenium notes that, except for Prince Edward Island, the colonial governments were in no hurry to fund the Cape Race light.[^1] In 1886 the Newfoundland House of Assembly passed an act that handed management of the light to the Dominion of Canada.[^1]

## Building the light

The Cape Race tower was built from thirty-two cast-iron plates, cast in Britain and bolted together at Cape Race.[^3] Its first light used eight lamps with polished reflectors.[^3] LighthouseFriends dates a rotating mechanism to 1866 and a steam whistle to 1873.[^3]

Cape North had a light before the iron tower came. NSLPS dates a square wooden tower at Money Point to 1875, first lit in 1876, and a revolving red and white light from 1883.[^4]

The Department of Marine and Fisheries' annual report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 records under Cape North that "Extensive repairs and additions were made to the dwelling at this station, cost $251.40."[^6] Its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1906 lists "Cape North, a new fog alarm" among works under way.[^7] For the twelve months ended 31 March 1908, the report's Nova Scotia section has an entry for "North cape", which reads: "A 3-inch diaphone was installed in the new fog-alarm building described in last year's annual report."[^9] The next report, for the twelve months ended 31 March 1909, records that a double dwelling for the fog-alarm engineer and lightkeeper was completed at a cost of $2,321.60.[^10]

At Cape Race a new, taller concrete tower was built to carry a much larger lens, which NSLPS dates to 1907.[^4][^1] The sources do not agree on when the old tower was moved to Money Point. Rowlett gives 1907, Ingenium 1908 and LighthouseFriends 1909.[^5][^1][^3] NSLPS puts the concrete tower and the move in 1907.[^4] The Department's report for the twelve months ended 31 March 1910 describes the work: "The old Cape Race tower was taken down in sections, and shipped to the site, where it is now being erected."[^11] The iron tower was first lit at Money Point in 1911. LighthouseFriends says it began service at the opening of navigation that year, and NSLPS gives 1911 as the start.[^3][^4]

The Department's report for the twelve months ended 31 March 1911 says: "A 3rd order single flashing light, showing one bright flash every 5 seconds, has been placed at this point." The same entry gives petroleum vapour as the illuminant.[^12] NSLPS's history gives a third-order dioptric lens, flashing white every five seconds, with a diaphone fog signal, from 1922 to 1941.[^4] LighthouseFriends says a third-order Fresnel lens was later mounted in the lantern room of the Ottawa tower.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1905 also records a collision at sea off Cape North on 12 September 1905.[^8] A preliminary inquiry was held at Sydney, Nova Scotia. A formal investigation was ordered, but the vessels had left before the order could be carried out.[^8]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard planned to demolish the Cape North tower in 1978, but work on a replacement at Money Point did not begin until July 1980.[^3] David Baird, director of the National Museum of Science and Technology in Ottawa, was looking for a lighthouse that could be taken apart and rebuilt, and he chose this one.[^3]

The tower went dark in early October 1980, after a temporary light was set up.[^3] Workers removed the Fresnel lens first and packed its four panels in foam.[^3] A truck pulled by bulldozers hauled the sections, four at a time, up the mountain behind the station.[^3] The three-week job ended just in time: the next day the rough access road was snowed in for the winter.[^3]

In Ottawa the museum poured a concrete foundation and placed limestone boulders around the tower, to suggest a rocky shore.[^3] The third-order lens went back into the lantern room.[^3] In June 2010 an earthquake spilled part of the mercury that floated the lens. Staff cleaned up the spill, and a contractor decontaminated the tower.[^3]

From 1980 the Money Point light stood on a square wooden tower, which NSLPS says was automated by 1987.[^4] In 2009 a skeleton tower replaced it.[^4]

## Today

The tower stands on the museum grounds on St. Laurent Boulevard in Ottawa.[^3][^5] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower is open in season.[^3] Rowlett's directory says the site is open but the tower is closed.[^5] The two sources disagree, so check the museum's current hours before a visit.

The light at Money Point is a different structure.[^4] NSLPS says its 2009 skeleton tower, called "Lighthouse IV", stands at the site.[^4]
