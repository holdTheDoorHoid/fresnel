Ivory Island Lightstation stands on Robb Point, where Seaforth Channel meets Milbanke Sound in British Columbia, and it has shown a light since 1898.[^1] The Inside Passage gives mariners a protected route, but in a few places vessels are exposed to the Pacific Ocean. Milbanke Sound is one of them.[^1]

## The place

In February 1894 the British Columbia Board of Trade asked for a light on Ivory Island, to mark the junction of Milbanke Sound and Seaforth Channel.[^1] The point chosen was Surf Point, the island's southwest tip, which is now more often called Robb Point.[^1]

## Building the light

The steamer Quadra landed a work crew on Ivory Island in the summer of 1898.[^1] The building went up by day labour under Alexander Bruce of Vancouver, at a cost of $2,009.35, and the light was put in operation on 1 October 1898.[^1]

The Department's 1898 description, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, gives the first building as a square wooden house with vertical walls and a square lantern on its roof. It stood 9 m (30 ft) high to the vane, and its fixed white light, from a seventh-order dioptric lens, was 20 m (66 ft) above high water.[^1] The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1900 records a new boathouse, at a cost of $89, and a new boat, for $45.[^2]

The station had a hand-powered fog horn from 1901.[^1] The Department's report for the year ended 31 March 1907 says a new fog alarm was being built.[^3] The next report says the building was finished and the diaphone installed.[^4] The plant came from the Canadian Fog Signal Company of Toronto for $1,900, and the building and machinery cost $3,441.65.[^4] The following year's report gives $2,222.39 as the total expenditure on the diaphone installation.[^5] The reports give both figures without explaining the difference.[^4][^5]

In 1957 the light on the square dwelling was replaced by one shown from a red steel tower.[^1][^7] Lighthouse Digest's 2006 database gives 1983 as the date the present tower was built, a date Lighthouse Friends does not mention.[^7][^1]

## The keepers

Peter Wylie was the station's first keeper.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the isolation proved too much for him.[^1]

When Captain James Gaudin, the Marine Agent, visited on 24 March 1899, he described Wylie as "an intelligent painstaking and helpful officer". Gaudin found that Wylie had gone more than three months without speaking to anyone.[^1]

J. C. Thompson took charge in June 1899. He noted that Wylie had been "the most overjoyed man in Canada" on his release.[^1] Thompson resigned after just over a year. His letter said his wife would not leave unless he went with her, and that "We are so far from medical assistance."[^1]

James Forsythe came next.[^1] In November 1904 his wife and baby caught dysentery from the station's contaminated water, and he resigned.[^1] The government steamer Kestrel took the mother and child to hospital at Bella Bella.[^1]

F. Reuter, a boatman whom Lighthouse Friends describes as having "few equals on the Pacific Coast," was appointed on 2 May 1905.[^6][^1] Because of his isolation he could not complete his naturalisation papers. In the war he contributed to the Patriotic Fund, but that apparently was not enough.[^1] In June 1916 he was dismissed, after more than a decade on the island, as "an alien enemy subject".[^1]

The Fisheries and Oceans Canada list gives an Ivory Island series for 1937 to 1955, marked archived. It describes the daily observations from this "random site" as part of a short time series.[^9] The same open-data page says eleven stations were added to the sea-surface sampling programme in the mid-1930s, and that the programme is now called the British Columbia Shore Station Oceanographic Program.[^10]

In 1942 a reporter, Jean Ness Findlay, visited the station. Lighthouse Friends, which cites her article in *The Vancouver Sun*, says the new keeper, Charlie Hoy, had just arrived with his wife, a canary, four cats and a dog named Rusty.[^1] Later, Sylvia Hacking is listed as head keeper from 2004 to 2007, one of the women who kept the light in their own right.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the first November, storms destroyed the boathouse.[^1] Keeper Wylie waded into the surf to salvage what debris he could with a boat hook, which Lighthouse Friends calls an unwise thing to do.[^1]

In December 1908 the new fog alarm was damaged by the sea, and the Kestrel's captain reported it out of commission.[^1] It was later determined that an earthquake centred near the Queen Charlotte Islands had triggered a tidal wave that broke in part of the fog-alarm building.[^1] In the summer of 1909 a breakwater was built to protect the station's buildings, and the lighthouse tower was raised so the fog alarm would not hide the light.[^1]

The 1942 article, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, says a tidal wave "the previous November" had damaged the bridge that linked the lighthouse point with the rest of the island.[^1]

In 1938 a landing boat capsized in the surf, and John Desmuelles, a deckhand on the tender Elberni, drowned.[^1] Two men who clung to the overturned boat were rescued.[^1]

On Christmas Eve 1982 a winter storm had been buffeting the station for about a week.[^1] Early on Christmas morning a wave came over the sea wall and flooded the station.[^1] Afterwards only the dwelling and the radio antenna were left standing. Lighthouse Friends says there is no way of knowing whether the 9.8 m (32 ft) metal tower was knocked down by that wave or one that followed it.[^1] Keeper Gordon Schweers, head keeper from 1981, was living in the square dwelling that night, and Lighthouse Friends says it was "groaning in every rafter like an old barque".[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard's list of lights gives the light as flashing white every five seconds, 20.5 m above the water and visible for 16 nautical miles, from a skeleton tower.[^8] Lighthouse Digest's 2006 entry gave 15 nautical miles.[^7]

Lighthouse Friends says the station remains staffed, and its keeper list runs to at least 2024.[^1] Lighthouse Digest's 2006 entry also calls it a staffed light station.[^7]

Lighthouse Digest's 2006 entry gives the wooden keeper's house as "destroyed by storm in December 1982". Lighthouse Friends says the original square dwelling was still at the station in 2010.[^7][^1]

Access is restricted. Lighthouse Friends says the station is best seen from the water, and that the light can be seen from ferries that serve the Inside Passage out of Port Hardy. The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1] It is a staffed Coast Guard lightstation and the keepers' home, so do not land on the island uninvited.[^1]
