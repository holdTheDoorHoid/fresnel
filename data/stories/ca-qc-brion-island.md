Brion Island lighthouse stands near the western end of Île Brion, one of the Magdalen Islands group in the Gulf of St. Lawrence, in Quebec.[^1][^11] Its wooden tower is 14.9 m (49 ft) high.[^2][^13] It was first lit in 1905 and has been automated since 1967.[^2][^1]

Government reports mostly spell the island "Bryon", though a 1906 heading says "Bryan". The register uses "Brion".[^6][^11][^13]

## The place

Jacques Cartier came upon the island in June 1534, LighthouseFriends says, and named it "Ille de Bryon" after his patron, Philippe de Chabot, Seigneur de Brion and Admiral of France.[^1] The hydrographic surveyor Admiral Henry Wolsey Bayfield wrote of the island, and LighthouseFriends prints his warning that "very great caution is requisite in approaching the reefs".[^1] The light was meant, LighthouseFriends says, to help guide vessels between the Gulf of St. Lawrence, the Miramichi River and Chaleur Bay.[^1]

LighthouseFriends calls Spencer and William Turner the first "permanent" residents. They were buried on the island after their boat was wrecked on 4 December 1847.[^1] A Dingwell family built the island's first permanent house in 1851, and LighthouseFriends gives the population at its peak as fifty, in 1878.[^1] In 1903 the Department bought 0.8 hectares at the western end of the island from William Dingwell.[^1]

## Building the light

The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1904 says the light "will be put in operation on the opening of navigation in 1905".[^2] The lighthouse was built under contract by E. F. Munro of Westville, Nova Scotia, whose contract price was $5,497.[^3]

The report says the lighthouse stands 755 feet (230 m) from the extreme west point of the island. It is an octagonal wooden building with sloping sides, painted white, and its height from base to lantern ventilator is 49 feet (14.9 m). Its lantern is iron and was painted red.[^2]

The light is elevated 126 feet (38.4 m) above high water mark, and the report says it should be visible 19 miles from all points of approach. The report does not say whether those are nautical miles. Its apparatus is catoptric.[^3]

The sources do not agree on the first signal. The 1904 report planned "a group-revolving white light, showing 3 flashes".[^3] The 1905 report says the light was changed to "a revolving red and white light, instead of a group flashing white light as heretofore".[^4] It describes a temporary apparatus giving one red flash and two white flashes every two minutes.[^5] The 1906 report describes the temporary light installed in 1905 as "a group revolving catoptric light showing three bright flashes".[^6] The sources do not settle which came first.

In September 1906 the temporary apparatus was replaced by "a third order dioptric light and lantern". It gave four bright flashes, then an eclipse of fifteen seconds, and was supplied by Barbier, Benard & Turenne of Paris.[^6]

## The keepers

The keepers' lists show "Chevrier, P" at Byron Island in the 1906 list, and "Procule Chevrier" against Bryon Island in the lists for 1908 and 1909.[^7][^8][^9] LighthouseFriends calls him Procule Chervier, the first keeper, and says he was in charge "from 1905 to 1908". Its list of head keepers gives 1905 to 1918.[^1] The 1909 list still shows him at Bryon Island, so the narrative's end date does not fit the reports.[^9] The 1918 end date comes from LighthouseFriends alone.[^1]

After him, LighthouseFriends names Edward Richard (1918 to 1927), Edward F. Keating (1927 to 1943), James Reid Turnbull (1943 to 1948) and Norman H. McKay (1948 to 1957) as head keepers.[^1] Its list runs into the 1960s, but the Marine reports used for this research do not reach that far.[^1]

The Department's annual report printed in 1911 describes the icebreaker Montcalm on "her first trip". She left Quebec for Belle Isle on the Newfoundland coast, Bird Rock, Brion Island and the Magdalen Islands, and carried workmen and construction materials.[^14]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A letter written at Quebec on 10 September 1902 reported on the steamer Indiana. The report it appeared in counts her as one of eleven important casualties in the St. Lawrence that season.[^10] The letter says that, instead of shaping her course to pass about the same distance from Bird Rocks, her captain "steered a course which brought Bryon Island ahead".[^10]

On 5 August 1918 the vessel Maskinonge stranded on Bryon Island, one of the Magdalen group.[^11] The formal investigation, held at Halifax on 11 September, found that the master, David Thomas Evans, was "severely reprimanded and censured for injudicious navigation".[^11]

Between 8 and 29 August 1918 the Quebec Salvage and Wrecking Company's assistance included towing the steamship "Frankmere" off the southeast point of Bryon Island and convoying her to Halifax.[^12]

LighthouseFriends says "at least fifty-six shipwrecks have occurred there".[^1] The register gives a wider count for the island region: "Plus de 700 naufrages sont répertoriés au fil des ans dans cette région insulaire" (more than 700 shipwrecks are on record over the years in this island region; translation mine).[^13]

## After the keepers

When the lighthouse was automated in 1967, LighthouseFriends says, "the keepers left for good".[^1] The register says the original cast-iron lantern was replaced in 1969: "Sa lanterne d'origine en fonte est remplacée en 1969 par l'actuelle lanterne polygonale en aluminium" (translation mine: its original cast-iron lantern was replaced in 1969 by the present polygonal aluminium lantern).[^13]

In 1970 a Texaco subsidiary, SAREP, bought the island for oil exploration.[^1] After no oil was found, the Government of Canada bought the island and established an ecological reserve of 650 hectares in 1984.[^1] In 1972 the station's buildings other than the tower were demolished.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse was declared a historic monument in 2006.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light, and LighthouseFriends records the grounds and tower as closed.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes boat access: five-hour zodiac excursions to the island, offered by Excursions en mer. The trips "only go when the sea is very calm", and may run only once or twice a week.[^1]

The light still shows a white flash every three seconds.[^1]
