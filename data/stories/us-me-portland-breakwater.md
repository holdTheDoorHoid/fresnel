Portland Breakwater Light, known locally as Bug Light, stands at the end of a breakwater on the south side of Portland Harbor, in South Portland, Maine.[^1][^2][^3] Its cast-iron tower went up in 1875 to replace an 1855 wooden light, and its design is based on the Choragic Monument of Lysicrates in Athens.[^1][^2] It was darkened in 1942, and it has shone again since a relighting in 2002.[^1][^8]

## The place

A storm in November 1831 ravaged Portland Harbor and destroyed wharves and buildings.[^8] A 2,500-foot (760 m) breakwater was planned to protect the harbor's entrance, running out over Staniford Ledge.[^1][^8] Construction began in 1836, according to LighthouseFriends, or in 1837, according to New England Lighthouses.[^1][^8] By the end of 1838 the breakwater was 1,800 feet (550 m) long. It never reached its planned length, and it was uncapped for much of its length.[^1][^8] Vessels had to pass between its end and Hog Island Ledge, so the unlit end became more of a hazard than a help.[^1][^8]

In September 1853 the Lighthouse Board's secretary recommended a sixth-order light at the end of the breakwater.[^8] He wrote that the breakwater was "nearly under water at high tide, and therefore on dark nights difficult to be seen."[^8] LighthouseFriends quotes the Board's petition to Congress, which said the breakwater "is very much in the way of vessels entering the harbor, and a small light is needed on the end of it."[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $3,500 for the light on 3 August 1854.[^8][^1] A small octagonal wooden tower went into service on 1 August 1855. Its sixth-order Fresnel lens showed a fixed red light 25 feet (7.6 m) above high water.[^8][^1]

The breakwater was extended between 1872 and 1873, and the wooden tower was placed at its new end until a permanent light could be paid for.[^1] In 1874 Congress appropriated $6,000 to replace the wooden tower, which LighthouseFriends says had become "decayed and unfit for further service."[^1] The new iron tower was first lit in June 1875 by Keeper Stephen Hubbard.[^8] The old wooden tower was moved to Little Diamond Island, where it became a lookout at a buoy depot.[^1][^8]

The National Register nomination describes a circular tower about 11 feet 8 inches (3.6 m) across, made of cast-iron plates fashioned as fluted columns with Corinthian capitals covering the joints.[^2] The walls are iron plates with 4 inches (10 cm) of brick filler behind them.[^2] The nomination says the structure was first built in 1855 and rebuilt in 1875, and its physical description gives the 1855 date for the cast-iron tower.[^2]

The nomination says Thomas Ustick Walter, who designed and erected the National Capitol's cast-iron dome, "might have had something to do with the structure," as a possibility only.[^2] New England Lighthouses repeats the suggestion.[^8]

LighthouseFriends describes a keeper's residence onshore, torn down in 1871 and replaced on the same foundation.[^1] The Board's 1889 report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says the station "contained no provision for a dwelling for the keeper" and could be reached from shore only by the breakwater, "at much discomfort, and even peril in severe storms."[^1] An annex with two dwelling rooms was built at the tower that year.[^1][^8]

LighthouseFriends says the light's characteristic changed in 1878 from fixed red to a red flash every fifteen seconds, and later to a fixed red light with a red flash every forty seconds.[^1]

## The keepers

The USLHS Digital Archive lists twelve head keepers, from 1855 to 1934.[^3] The first was W. A. Dyer, who was paid $400 a year and resigned after two years.[^1] Keeper William L. Willard died of a tumor in 1860, at the age of twenty-six, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] His older brother Benjamin F. Willard, who took over next, was removed after four months.[^1]

Keepers reached the light on foot along the breakwater.[^8] New England Lighthouses says this often became a battle against waves, wind and ice, and that keepers sometimes had to crawl the 1,800 feet (550 m) on their hands and knees.[^8]

Stephen Hubbard, who lit the iron tower, kept the light until 1887.[^3][^8] Albus R. Angell served from 1887 to 1900, and Parker O. Haley from 1900 to 1908.[^3] William T. Holbrook kept the light from 1909 to 1919.[^3] New England Lighthouses dates his service from 1910 and says his family lived in the dwelling.[^8]

The last head keeper was Preston L. Marr, from 1919 to 1934.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he was born in 1871 at Hendricks Head Light, where his father was keeper, and that he was head keeper at the Cuckolds for twelve years before he was transferred to the breakwater.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

No dated wreck or rescue at the light itself is recorded in these sources. LighthouseFriends says that in storms waves and spray swept the breakwater, battering the lighthouse and often soaking the keeper.[^1]

A fog bell was moved between a buoy on Staniford Ledge and the breakwater light.[^1][^5] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1895 says a bell buoy placed on Staniford Ledge on 24 June 1895 was operated electrically from the breakwater light station.[^4] In January 1896 the bell was taken to the light for the winter, and it went back on the buoy in May.[^5]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1896 says an electric motor and machinery for striking the bell were installed.[^6] By 1899 a Stevens striking machine had replaced the electric one.[^7]

New England Lighthouses reproduces a February 1914 letter from the district inspector.[^8] The letter said a complaint had come from the captain of the steamer Bay State that the station's bell was not sounding at 3:44 a.m. during a recent thick fog.[^8] Keeper William Holbrook replied that he had sounded the bell from 7:30 to 10:30 p.m. The weather was clear at 11:00 p.m., and he went to bed.[^8] He was awakened by a whistle blast from the Bay State and had the bell sounding again by 3:45 a.m.[^8] The inspector wrote back that "such reports are received, and you are cautioned to make every effort in the future to have the fog signal at your station in operation during foggy weather."[^8]

## After the keepers

The light was electrified in 1934, and the keeper at Spring Point Ledge Light took over its care.[^1][^8] The keeper's house on the breakwater was removed in 1935.[^1][^8]

In 1940 the Todd-Bath Iron Shipbuilding Company built a facility on the shore beside the breakwater, and fill was placed along both sides of the breakwater to enlarge the company's property.[^1] LighthouseFriends says "only 100 feet of the breakwater remained in open water."[^1]

The light was darkened in 1942.[^8] LighthouseFriends says it was discontinued that June.[^1] In May 1943 it was decided that the light and fog signal were no longer needed for local navigation.[^8]

The light was declared surplus property and sold into private hands.[^8] Spring Point Associates donated the property to the City of South Portland in 1985.[^8] The South Portland–Cape Elizabeth Rotary Club spearheaded a 1989 renovation.[^8] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1973.[^2][^1] A Liberty Ship memorial was dedicated in the park in November 2001.[^8]

Through the 1990s the tower's condition deteriorated, and its ventilator ball was stolen.[^8] The South Portland–Cape Elizabeth Rotary Club and the Spring Point Ledge Light Trust restored it, and it was relit as a private aid to navigation on 14 August 2002.[^1][^8] New England Lighthouses says the lamp is now a 250 mm optic that shows a white flash every four seconds.[^8]

## Today

The City of South Portland owns the lighthouse, according to the USLHS record and LighthouseFriends.[^3][^1] The grounds are open, and the tower is closed.[^1] From Bug Light Park, visitors can walk the short breakwater to the light.[^1] New England Lighthouses gives directions to free parking along the water near the light, and says the light can be seen from many excursion boats out of Portland Harbor.[^8]

The light is a private aid to navigation, relit in 2002.[^1] The station's sixth-order lens is at the South Portland Coast Guard Station, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1]
