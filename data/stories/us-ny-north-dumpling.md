North Dumpling Light stands on North Dumpling Island in Fishers Island Sound, off the north side of Fishers Island, New York.[^1][^2] It was established in 1849 as a light on a brick keeper's house with an attached tower, rebuilt in 1871, and given a fifth-order lens in 1892.[^1][^2][^3][^4] Its beacon was moved to a steel tower nearby and automated in 1959.[^1][^2] The island is private and closed today, and the light is seen from the water.[^1][^2]

## The place

The two largest of the small islands north of Fishers Island are called the Dumplings.[^2] In 1838 Lieutenant George M. Bache considered the site for a new light.[^2] He preferred South Dumpling, because a line from there to the Stonington light ran through a clear channel, while a line from North Dumpling ran close to shoals to the north.[^2] Congress did not take his advice.[^2] Nine years later it provided $5,000, and the single light went on North Dumpling.[^2] The Winthrop family, which had held the island, sold it to the federal government in 1847 for $600.[^2]

A trial of the Little Gull Island fog signal, described in Arnold Burges Johnson's *The Modern Light-House Service* (1889), shows how far its sound carried in these waters.[^12] In a thick fog the observers' steamer ran out of New London harbor and was compelled to anchor, but later worked round the neighboring lights.[^12] From the North Dumpling light station, seven miles from the Little Gull siren, the party heard it "at a force of 6" on a scale of 10.[^12] The passage does not give the year of the trial.[^12]

## Building the light

The first optic in the lantern was seven lamps set in fourteen-inch reflectors, with red shades that gave the light its distinctive character.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the first upgrade, to a sixth-order Fresnel lens, to 1855.[^2]

In its 1867 report the Lighthouse Board described the lighthouse as "very much decayed," according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] Congress appropriated $15,000 in 1870 to rebuild the station.[^2] The work was finished in 1871, with a new mansard roof on the keeper's house and a new 29-foot (8.8 m) tower and lantern on top of the dwelling.[^1][^2] The archive's record gives the tower's establishment as 1871 and the keeper's house as French Second Empire in style.[^1]

The light was changed in October 1891, from fixed red to fixed white with a fixed red sector.[^3][^7] The Board's report gives the change as 16 October 1891. The Tribune's notice to mariners, published in September, said "on or about October 15, 1891," and LighthouseFriends gives 15 October.[^2][^3][^7] In July 1892 a fifth-order lens, new lamps and a new pedestal replaced the sixth-order apparatus.[^2][^4] The archive's record instead gives a fifth-order lens as the original optic of 1871.[^1] The Board's report and LighthouseFriends both place a sixth-order lens in the lantern until 1892,[^2][^4] so the archive's date and order for the first lens are in conflict with them.[^1]

In the year ended 30 June 1892 a new bell tower was built and fitted with new apparatus,[^3] and in the year ended 30 June 1894 a boathouse was built on the southwest side of the island.[^5]

## The keepers

The first keeper assigned, Riley Clark, could not be found when the light was to start, and a local sea captain, Joseph Dayton, held temporary charge.[^2] In December 1848 Alfred Clark was named the official keeper.[^2] The archive lists him from 1848 to 1854.[^1] A steamboat company had placed a fog bell on the island.[^2] When Alfred Clark learned that the keeper at Little Gull Island was paid $43 a year for the same work, while the company paid him $32, he stopped ringing the bell for the company.[^2] Congress provided $800 for a government fog bell in 1854.[^2]

The archive lists John Baker (1854 to 1862), John Spencer (1862 to 1869), Henry N. Spencer (1869 to 1876) and John Gunn (1876 to 1886) as head keepers.[^1] The archive lists Catherine Gunn as first assistant keeper from 1876 to 1886, the same years John Gunn was head keeper. The archive does not say how the two were related, and this story does not assume it.[^1]

On a foggy evening in 1899, the machinery that tolled the fog bell failed while Keeper Joseph T. Fowler's wife was caring for the light alone.[^2] LighthouseFriends says she climbed the bell tower and rang the bell by hand, and that the Board wrote to praise her courage.[^2] The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1900 records that the old fog-bell machine was replaced by a new one, and gives no reason.[^6]

After Fowler (1895 to 1911) and James J. Barnes (1911 to 1916), the archive lists Henry Burkhardt from 1916 to 1930.[^1] A Commissioner of Lighthouses table of life and property saved in the fiscal year 1915 names J. J. Barnes, keeper of North Dumpling Light Station, among the employees credited.[^8] In 1923 an accuser claimed that Burkhardt ran extra lights around the lighthouse, which were used as signals between the mainland, boats and Fishers Island.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that the Coast Guard saw some strange lights and flares around the station, but found no evidence connecting the keeper to illegal activities.[^2]

Richard A. Fricke was head keeper from 1931 to 1934 according to the archive.[^1] Laureat Leclerc, listed from at least 1935 to 1943, was keeper when he made the 1936 rescue described below.[^1][^10] The archive also lists Andrew A. McLintock (1943), Ralph A. Gadbois (1947 to 1949) and William I. Clark (1949 to 1956).[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 18 August 1934 Keeper R. A. Fricks, with his son, rescued two men and a boy whose sailboat had capsized about a mile from the station.[^9] The Bulletin spells the keeper's name "Fricks"; the archive and LighthouseFriends have "Fricke."[^1][^2][^9] On 21 May 1936 Laureat Leclerc went to three men adrift in a boat near the station and towed them to Fishers Island.[^10]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1938 account of the recent hurricane on the New England coast lists North Dumpling among the New York stations where the principal damage occurred.[^11] The others it names are Latimer Reef, Race Rock, Little Gull Island and Orient Point.[^11]

## After the keepers

A telephone plant was installed on the island in 1902.[^2] It let the keeper report a lightship broken adrift in the ice, but when the Service would not provide a shelter for the equipment, the phone was removed.[^2]

In 1959 the beacon was moved from the lighthouse to a steel skeleton tower and automated.[^1][^2] The lighthouse and grounds were sold to George Washburn, a New York investment manager, for $18,000.[^1][^2] In 1977 David Levitt, president of the Doughnut Corporation of America, bought the property for $95,000, remodelled and enlarged the keeper's house, and persuaded the Coast Guard to return the optic to the tower and remove the skeleton tower.[^2] In 1986 the inventor Dean Kamen bought the island for $2.5 million.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that after a dispute with Suffolk County over a windmill, he declared the island a "North Dumpling Republic."[^2]

## Today

The island is privately owned, and the grounds, dwelling and tower are closed.[^1][^2] The archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^1][^2] Do not land on the island, which is private. LighthouseFriends says the light is best seen from a boat, and that excursion boats pass it.[^2] The archive's record lists the light as not an active aid to navigation.[^1]
