Carquinez Strait Light stood at the end of a long pier on wooden piles, off Vallejo, California, near the junction of the strait and the Napa River, and it showed a red light from January 1910 until 1951.[^2][^1] Its keepers' dwelling, which had an attached tower, was later moved to a marina on Glen Cove, where it is now an office and a rental.[^1]

## The place

Vessels bound for the rivers first crossed the wide San Francisco and San Pablo bays. From there a ship could turn north up the Napa River to Mare Island, or carry on east toward the Sacramento and San Joaquin rivers.[^1] LighthouseFriends ties the growth of that traffic in the mid-1800s to the discovery of gold at Sutter's Mill and to the Navy's new base at Mare Island.[^1] A light at the south end of Mare Island, built in 1873, marked the entrance to the strait and the Napa River.[^1] The Lighthouse Board later concluded that a beacon offshore, near the junction of the strait and the river, would serve navigation better.[^1] On 4 March 1907, after repeated requests from the Lighthouse Board, Congress appropriated $50,000 for a light and fog signal at Carquinez Strait.[^1]

## Building the light

Starting in August 1908, a steam pile driver sank hundreds of wooden piles into the muddy bottom of the strait to carry a pier roughly 1,500 feet (457 m) long. A dock at its far end let the lighthouse tenders tie up, and a causeway led to a rectangular platform for the light.[^1] The dwelling, with its attached tower and fog-signal building, was finished near the end of 1909. The 28-room dwelling was "designed to house three keepers and their families."[^1]

A notice to mariners from the Twelfth Lighthouse District, printed in the *San Francisco Call* on 19 January 1910, said the light station "was established January 15, 1910," in a structure recently erected on the north side of the strait's entrance.[^2] The archive's place record gives 1908 as the year the station was established. The notice gives 15 January 1910, and LighthouseFriends dates the start of pile driving to August 1908.[^7][^2][^1]

The light was fixed red, of the fourth order, 56 feet (17 m) above the water, and was expected to be visible 13 miles in clear weather.[^2] The tower was a cream square frame structure topped by a black lantern. The tower, dwelling and fog-signal building all stood on piles in 8 feet (2.4 m) of water.[^2] A 4,600-foot (1,400 m) pipe carried water from the Vallejo city system to a 5,840-gallon tank on wooden trestles near the light.[^1]

The fog signal was also tested and changed in the following years.[^3][^4] In 1913 tests were made at the station to find the right height for the "mushroom top" above the mouth of a vertical trumpet. The bulletin reported that in that locality the height was 25 inches.[^3] A 1915 installation described in the bulletin used a duplicate compressed-air plant to drive a pair of 6-inch automatic sirens in a small siren house about 900 feet (270 m) from the powerhouse. The bulletin said the installation had been in successful operation for more than a year.[^4] In 1921 an inspection found "very extensive damage by teredo" to the piling under the station.[^5] The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1922 lists $6,749 for "concrete protection for foundation piles" at the station.[^6]

## The keepers

Andrew Czarnecke was the first head keeper, with Arthur E. Berry as first assistant.[^1][^7] LighthouseFriends says Czarnecke had been an assistant at East Brother Lighthouse before he came here.[^1] In 1911 he swapped stations with Charles S. Kaneen, head keeper at Piedras Blancas.[^1][^7] Kaneen stayed more than twenty-five years, the longest of any keeper here.[^1][^7]

The archive names later head keepers Frederick C. Saunders (1936 to 1942) and Thomas G. Lewis (1942 to 1957).[^7] John E. Gonzales was first assistant from 1951 to 1955.[^7] Stanley V. Adler is listed from 1954, and Robert A. Miller from 1961 to 1963.[^7] The archive lists Lewis and Adler together in 1954 to 1957, and no source explains the overlap.[^7] LighthouseFriends calls Miller the station's last officer in charge, who left in the summer of 1963.[^1] The Coast Guard men the archive names for 1950 to 1963 are Davis O. Garsh, Gary A. Fountain, Hawley D. Upmeyer and Raymond L. Stemler.[^7]

## After the keepers

In 1951 the manned light was replaced by a smaller beacon and fog signal at the end of the now-extended pier.[^1] The Coast Guard built a flat-roofed structure with four apartments atop the bluff overlooking the pier.[^1] The 28-room residence was offered for sale in 1955.[^1] Robert Hubert, a San Francisco building contractor, bought it that year to make the center of a small marina. The price included the Fresnel lens.[^1] After a futile search for a lighthouse mover, Hubert oversaw the move himself, renting timbers and hiring barges and cranes.[^1] While preparing the building he fell from the dwelling and severely injured his chest. The move was put on hold until he recovered, and in the meantime vandals broke in and smashed the Fresnel lens.[^1] Three businessmen then joined the project, which they called Lighthouse Harbor. After much delay the building made the short move to the cove now known as Glen Cove.[^1]

## Today

The former residence, minus its light tower and fog-signal building, is now the office of Glen Cove Marina in Vallejo.[^1] In 2014 a large part of the lower floor became an event space, and the upper two stories became a three-bedroom rental known as the Lighthouse Inn.[^1] LighthouseFriends says visitors are welcome in the reception area, where there is nothing lighthouse-related to see, and that the upstairs can be rented for overnight stays.[^1] The building is owned by Western Water Ways, Inc.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record lists the station as open to the public.[^7] The sources do not say what remains of the pier or of the 1951 beacon.
