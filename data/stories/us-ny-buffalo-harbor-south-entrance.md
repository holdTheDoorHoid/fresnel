The South Entrance South Side Light stands at the southern entrance to Buffalo Harbor, at Stony Point on Lake Erie. It is a conical iron tower with a fog-signal house beside it, and it was first lit on 1 September 1903.[^1][^2] Its gallery optic stayed in use until 1993, and the light is now inactive.[^1][^2] The Lighthouse Society's archive lists the station as the Buffalo South Breakwater light.[^2]

## The place

The harbor's lights came in stages. LighthouseFriends says the city quickly outgrew its first lighthouse, built in 1818, and that the 1833 stone tower was superseded by a breakwater station in the 1870s.[^1] As traffic grew, a lengthy breakwater was built south of the main harbor.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1900 records the start of the south entrance works: "By the act approved June 6, 1900, $45,000 was appropriated for establishing suitable light and fog-signal stations to mark the main southern entrance of the new breakwater at Buffalo."[^3]

The southern entrance lay near the recently completed Lackawanna Steel plant, LighthouseFriends says.[^1]

## Building the light

The plans were changed after storms. The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1901 says that the plans for the towers and fog-signal house were complete, but that "conditions developed during two severe storms which made it necessary to materially modify the completed plans to provide greater strength."[^4] LighthouseFriends says the storms "badly damaged" a section of the breakwater near where the towers were to stand.[^1]

The design settled on two lights: a beacon with a fog signal on the south side of the entrance, and a smaller light on the north side.[^1] Work on the south beacon and fog-signal house was held up by difficulty in getting iron. The contractors could not finish by the end of 1902, and the unfinished lighthouse was sealed up for the winter.[^1] LighthouseFriends says both the north and south lights were first exhibited on 1 September 1903.[^1]

The Lighthouse Society's archive describes the tower as conical and of iron, with the light 43.5 feet (13.3 m) above mean high water and an original fourth-order Fresnel optic.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes that lens as giving a repeated sequence of two white flashes and then a red one, each spaced ten seconds apart.[^1]

The keepers' house was a brick duplex. LighthouseFriends says Frank Petit built it for $8,764 on land bought from the Lackawanna Steel Company.[^1] The fog signal took longer. A compressed-air fog siren was ready on 12 November 1904.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive's roster begins with Delos Hayden, principal keeper in 1903.[^2] Edward Van Natta is listed for 1903 to 1904, John H. Burns for 1904, and George V. Codding for 1904 to 1913.[^2] William L. Gordon followed, from 1914 to 1917.[^2]

Two men named Safe appear on the roster, from 1915 to at least 1940. The archive lists "John Safe (at least 1919-1930)" as principal keeper, and "Frederick G. Safe (1930-at least 1940)" after him.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives the first name as "John E. Safe."[^1] The 1915 Lighthouse Service Bulletin names John Safe as the station's assistant keeper.[^5] The archive lists him as first assistant "at least 1915-at least 1917," before the principal keeper entry.[^2]

Assistant keepers on the roster include Hector V. de Grasse (1904 to 1908), Louis C. Froelich (1908 to at least 1913), and Waldo B. McCullor, who served as second assistant from 1928 to 1931 and as first assistant from 1931 to 1945.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The 1915 Lighthouse Service Bulletin records one rescue by this station's assistant keeper. On 13 September John Safe, assistant keeper of the South Buffalo South Side Light Station, "towed to safety an airship" with two men in it. The airship had become disabled and "dropped into the water about a mile from the light station."[^5]

Two severe storms during the work damaged the breakwater and forced the change of plans described above.[^1][^4]

## After the keepers

The fog signal changed in 1934. An air diaphone replaced the original siren, and the station was connected to commercial power by a submarine cable, LighthouseFriends says. The diaphone sounded a two-tone blast of three seconds, followed by about twenty-seven seconds of silence.[^1] A radiobeacon was set up next to the light on 4 April 1939. It sent a dash followed by three dots, the Morse signal for "B."[^1]

The light was automated in 1962. LighthouseFriends says it is now inactive and was replaced by a modern post light that is easy to maintain.[^1] The fourth-order lens was removed in 1967 and went on display at the Coast Guard's Buffalo station. In 1987 it was moved into the 1833 tower, and in 2013 it was removed, restored and placed on display at the Heritage Discovery Center.[^1] The gallery optic stayed in use until 1993, when a light on a nearby pole took over.[^1]

Ownership changed in 2011, when the deed was transferred to the Buffalo Lighthouse Association.[^1] Fundraising for a $185,000 stabilization and safety phase began in 2012, and LighthouseFriends says roughly $380,000 had been spent on the lighthouse by 2020.[^1]

## Today

The tower belongs to the Buffalo Lighthouse Association, which was formed in 1985 "to preserve the historic lighthouses of Buffalo Harbor."[^1][^6] The station is listed on the National Register of Historic Places (NRIS 07001191) as part of the Light Stations of the United States group.[^7]

LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed. It also says the lighthouse cannot be reached by land because of the industrial property around it.[^1] The Lighthouse Society's archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^2] Do not go onto the breakwater to reach it. Contact the Buffalo Lighthouse Association about access.
