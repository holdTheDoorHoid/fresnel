Channel Head Lighthouse stands on a small island on the west side of the harbour entrance at Channel-Port aux Basques.[^1][^3] The town sits at the southwest tip of Newfoundland.[^2] The station was established in 1874, but LighthouseFriends says the light did not start until the spring of 1875.[^1][^2] The register describes the prefabricated, cast-iron tower as a good example of its type.[^3]

## The place

Basque whalers used the harbour in the 1500s for shelter and fresh water, which is where the port's name comes from.[^2] In 1856 a telegraph cable was laid between Cape Ray, near Port aux Basques, and Cape Breton.[^2]

On 12 October 1864 Captain Orlebar, who had been in charge of the Royal Navy's Newfoundland survey, recommended a light on Channel Head. He wanted a fixed white light of second-rate power, kept low, because the land stands 29 m (95 ft) above high water.[^2]

In 1865 two officials looked at the site. Robert Oke, Newfoundland's inspector of lighthouses, preferred Channel Head, and G. F. Baillairge, a Canadian government engineer, preferred Cape Ray. Canada built a lighthouse at Cape Ray in 1871, and Newfoundland was left to pay for its own light on Channel Head.[^2] In 1873 a petition from M.H. Genge of Channel and Rev. T.A. Goode of Port aux Basques went to the Newfoundland House of Assembly, asking for the light.[^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says work on the lighthouse was carried out in 1874. Austin Oke arrived on 5 November to fit the lantern, but the season was too late, and the light did not begin operating until the spring of 1875.[^2]

The first tower was a square wooden tower on a slightly larger wooden base, nine metres tall by LighthouseFriends' account. It showed a fixed red light from a focal plane of 27.5 m (90 ft).[^2]

The heritage statement (FHBRO report 88-037) says the lightstation was built in 1874-75, and it describes a circular tower about eight metres high. It appears the tower was made in Britain and sent to Newfoundland to be put together.[^4]

LighthouseFriends dates a circular iron tower, 17 m high, to 1895. It says the tower was put up beside the wooden one and fitted with a fourth-order lens.[^2] The Lighthouse Directory also dates the iron tower to 1895, and gives it as 11 m (36 ft) high.[^1] The sources do not agree on the date or the height of the iron tower.

The inspector, John Nevill, judged the site hard to reach and recommended a small dwelling beside the tower. It was built in 1877, the year after the 1876 incident described below.[^2] A cistern was recommended in 1879 and built in 1890.[^2]

The Newfoundland Railway reached Port aux Basques in 1898, and a ferry service to North Sydney started with it. A fog signal building went up that year, where a four-ounce gun-cotton charge was set off every eight minutes in fog. In 1906 a compressed-air diaphone replaced the explosives.[^2]

## The keepers

Timothy Pike was hired as the first keeper, and LighthouseFriends lists his service as 1874 to 1906.[^2] In 1876 the inspector, John Nevill, noted that Pike could not reach the light on at least one occasion, so the light was not lit that night.[^2]

After Pike, LighthouseFriends names E. Pike from 1907 to at least 1921, and John T. Gillam from at least 1935 to at least 1938.[^2]

## After the keepers

Commercial electricity reached the station in 1963, and the light was converted from kerosene to electric in 1966.[^2] A two-storey keeper's duplex was built in 1965, and a radio beacon was installed in the fog building later that decade. The duplex was removed in 1990.[^2]

The Canadian Coast Guard is the custodial agency for the station.[^4] The tower was designated a Recognized Federal Heritage Building in 1988. The 1988 statement says the station "has survived over a century of severe weather conditions without any major repairs or alterations".[^2][^4]

The Lighthouse Directory reports a major restoration in 2015. In April 2016 the Coast Guard said the station's wharf had become unsafe and would be removed, which would have left helicopters as the only way in. Protests by the Channel Head Heritage Lighthouse Society led the government to change course, and a new wharf and walkway were built in spring 2017.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard's list gives the light as "Fl W 10s" from the Channel Head Light Station.[^5] The Lighthouse Directory gives its focal plane as 29 m (95 ft), with a white flash every 10 seconds.[^1]

The Lighthouse Directory says the station is reachable only by boat but visible from the mainland, that the site is open and the tower closed, and that a tour operator runs picnic trips to the lighthouse.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower closed, and that thousands of people see the light each year from the decks of the Marine Atlantic ferries.[^2] The Coast Guard owns the station, and the tower is closed to visitors.[^2]
