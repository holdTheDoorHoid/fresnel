Eagle Island Light stands on the northeast tip of Eagle Island, in East Penobscot Bay off Deer Isle, Hancock County, Maine.[^1][^2] Congress allocated $5,000 for it in March 1837, and its keepers first lit it in late September 1838.[^2][^3] How much of that first tower still stands is a question the sources answer in different ways, and the story below says where they disagree.[^3]

## The place

The light sits on a six-acre point at the island's northeast corner.[^2] A local landowner, John C. Gray, deeded the point to the government in 1837, LighthouseFriends reports.[^2] The caretakers' history gives the reason for the light as the increase in shipping coming and going from Bangor, up the Penobscot River, much of it logging schooners.[^3]

## Building the light

The contractor, Mr. Hildreth, built a conical tower of rubblestone, a 25-foot (7.6 m) structure with a wrought-iron lantern, and a one-and-a-half-story rubblestone dwelling for the keeper.[^2]

The sources do not agree on what the tower's walls were made of. LighthouseFriends says rubblestone. The caretakers describe granite block on the outside, brick inside and rubblestone between. The archive lists granite rubblestone.[^1][^2][^3]

John Spear, the first keeper, was paid $350 a year and lit the lamp in late September 1838. LighthouseFriends gives 28 September.[^2][^3]

The first keeper's tower did not please his successor. Nathan Philbrook, keeper from 1841, reported in 1842 that the tower was "a rough and defective piece of work," that its stairs were dangerous to climb, and that in winter the whole inside was covered with ice. LighthouseFriends quotes the report.[^2]

Whether the tower was rebuilt is the open question. The caretakers say that in 1857 "the original light was largely reconstructed," that a fourth-order Fresnel lens went in, and that the old stone stairway gave way to cast-iron steps.[^3] LighthouseFriends mentions the 1857 lens but not a rebuilt tower.[^2] The archive dates the tower to 1838 and the lens to 1858.[^1] The keeper's house changed in 1857 too. A wooden house with four bedrooms and a workshop that linked it to the tower replaced the stone building.[^2][^3]

The lamps burned whale oil or lard at first. By 1877 they had been converted to kerosene.[^2][^3] Keeper Ambrose Sweetland, in the station logbook in May 1879, complained that the light kept him up nearly all night, and he wished the Board would go back to lard oil.[^2]

Supplies came by rowing about two miles to Deer Isle, and then had to be hauled up a steep trail to the bluff.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates a 76-step stairway to 1894 and says it made that trail much easier to use.[^2] The Board's 1895 report records a flight of stairs built up the bluff on the southeast side of the reservation.[^4] The caretakers describe a 76-step wooden stairway on the eastern side of the cliff, without a date.[^3] The sources do not say whether these were the same stairway.

Smaller works followed. The Board's 1895 report records an oil house, and its 1896 report records a boundary fence built around the reservation.[^4][^5]

## The keepers

The archive lists 25 principal keeper stints, held by 23 people, from John Spear in 1838 to Wayne McGraw in 1959.[^1] Two of the people on the list were women. Sarah Spear is listed for 1848 to 1849, between two stints by John Spear, and the published sources say nothing more about her.[^1] Lucy Ball tended the light after her husband's death in 1913.[^2][^3]

The Ball family kept the light from 1883 to 1913. John Ball was keeper until 1898, and his son Howard T. Ball followed him.[^1][^3] John stayed on at the station after he retired and lived there until his death at 82.[^2][^3]

Howard Ball had caught pneumonia while helping guide a fishing vessel to safety, and he died early in 1913. LighthouseFriends gives 31 January as the date.[^2][^3]

His widow kept the light until Edward S. Farren arrived on 23 March 1913, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] Farren kept the light until 1918.[^1]

A more everyday grievance shows up in the station's papers. In July 1908 Howard Ball wrote to the district inspector that Mr. John M. Quinn's sheep were getting into the reservation pasture and his garden.[^2] The station log for 1 to 4 June 1909 records sheep driven off the reservation on four days running.[^2]

Frank E. Bracey was keeper from 1931 to 1945, according to the archive.[^1] The caretakers say the last civilian keeper was Frank E. Bracey Jr.[^3] LighthouseFriends describes a Frank E. Bracey, Jr. as the keeper's son.[^2] The sources do not settle which name belongs to the keeper.

Ralph K. Banks, keeper from 1949 to 1952, later gave an author an account of his time on the island, and in it he rated the island above any other place he had lived.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 4 December 1917 Keeper E. S. Farren "rendered assistance to the captain of a sloop in the vicinity of the light station, who had been stricken with heart disease," the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported.[^6] The Commissioner of Lighthouses listed Farren again among the lighthouse staff who saved life or property in the fiscal years 1918 and 1919.[^7][^8] The fiscal 1918 entry gives the act as "Rendered assistance to sick captain," the same act the Bulletin describes. The table prints his initials as E. L., and the fiscal 1919 table prints E. S.[^7][^8] No source given here names a shipwreck at this station.

## After the keepers

A fog bell was installed in 1932. It hung outside the wooden pyramidal tower, and a keeper wound its weights up the tower "much like a grandfather clock."[^1][^3]

The Coast Guard took over the station in 1945.[^2][^3] The caretakers say the Coast Guard took over the operation of the light that year.[^3] LighthouseFriends says Coast Guard personnel took over keeper duties.[^2] The archive still lists a principal keeper for each stint until 1959.[^1]

The light was automated in 1959. A 300 mm lens replaced the Fresnel lens, a battery bank charged by a diesel generator ran it, and the light was changed to flashing every four seconds. The fog bell was turned off.[^1][^2][^3]

In 1963 the Coast Guard became alarmed at vandalism at other abandoned light stations in the area. It decided to burn the keeper's house, despite stiff opposition from islanders. The other buildings went too, except the lighthouse and the bell tower.[^2][^3]

While crews tried to take down the fog bell, it broke loose and tumbled down the cliff into the water. A local fisherman later retrieved it, and it now stands on Great Spruce Head Island, on a mount in a meadow.[^3]

The Coast Guard tried to discontinue the light in 2000, but it remained an active aid to navigation.[^2]

Under the Maine Lights Program the light went to the Eagle Light Caretakers, a non-profit organization. The caretakers date the transfer to 1996.[^2][^3]

In 2013 the caretakers' board developed a multi-phase restoration plan with the J. B. Leslie Company. A few private donors helped the caretakers complete the first two phases. The final phases, which they estimated at $50,000, were on hold when their history was printed.[^3] LighthouseFriends dates the phases to 2017, 2018 and 2023.[^2]

## Today

The Eagle Light Caretakers own the light.[^1][^2] It is solar-powered, with an LED optic installed in 2017.[^1] The archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower closed, so visitors should not climb it.[^2] The island is reached by boat, and the Eagle Island mailboat, which operates out of Sunset on Deer Isle, passes by on its way to deliver mail to several islands.[^2] The caretakers sponsor an annual open house each August.[^2]
