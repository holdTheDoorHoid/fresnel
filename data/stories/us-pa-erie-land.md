Erie Land Light stands on a bluff above the eastern entrance to Erie Harbor, on Pennsylvania's Lake Erie shore. Its first tower was lit in 1818, one of the first lights built on the Great Lakes. The tower that stands today dates from 1867. The light was last put out in 1899, and since 1934 the site has been a city park.[^2]

## The place

In 1792 Pennsylvania acquired a triangular tract with forty-five miles of Lake Erie frontage, for $151,640.[^2] Presque Isle, a slender peninsula seven miles long, reaches out from that shore and forms a harbor nearly five miles long.[^2] Congress passed an act in 1810 that authorized a light for the harbor.[^2] Soon afterward the government obtained two acres of mainland bluff above the harbor's eastern entrance from John Kelso.[^2] Congress provided money in 1810 and 1811, but the War of 1812 held up the work.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress made a new allocation of $17,000 on 3 March 1817 for two lake lights.[^2] The Presque Isle light and the Buffalo light were completed and began operation in 1818. LighthouseFriends says they are considered the first American lighthouses built on the Great Lakes.[^2] The Port Authority calls the Erie Land Lighthouse "the first commissioned lighthouse on the Great Lakes."[^4]

The contract specified a twenty-foot (6 m) stone tower.[^2] An iron lantern nine feet (3 m) tall held ten lamps and reflectors. Because the bluff was high, the light's focal plane was 93 feet (28 m).[^2] Nearby, a one-story frame house with three rooms was provided for the keeper.[^2] The Port Authority describes the 1818 tower as built "out of wood."[^4] The two accounts disagree on the material: LighthouseFriends describes a stone tower.[^2]

An inspection in 1851 found that the stone tower was starting to settle. Metal bands were placed around it to steady it.[^2] By 1857 it was clear that the tower had to be replaced. Thomas Mehaffey oversaw a new tower of brick brought from Milwaukee.[^2] Local people objected, because they thought comparable brick could have been bought nearby for less.[^2] The brick tower was 56 feet (17 m) tall. A third-order Fresnel lens was installed in its lantern, which LighthouseFriends says the local paper called "a splendid affair."[^2] The Port Authority says the tower was rebuilt in 1857 "due to structural complications."[^4] LighthouseFriends places the new brick dwelling in 1858.[^2]

The brick tower did not last. Its foundation settled further, and frost cracked its walls.[^2] It was dismantled in 1866, and test borings found quicksand beneath the site.[^2] The third tower went up on eight layers of oak timbers, with limestone and cement above them.[^2] It is 49 feet (15 m) tall, built of Berea sandstone. Its completion year, 1867, is inscribed above the door.[^2][^4] It carried a third-order Fresnel lens.[^2] In 1871 Congress appropriated money to raise the roof of the keeper's dwelling to give it a full second floor.[^2]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster begins with John Bone: "John Bone (1819-1833)."[^3] LighthouseFriends says Captain John Bone took the post in 1818, in his forties, and served until 1832.[^2] The two sources disagree by a year at each end. The 1830 census, as LighthouseFriends reports it, counted four girls and two boys in Bone's household, besides his wife, all living in the keeper's small house.[^2] In 1838 an inspector found the light "one of the most useful on the south shores of the lake." He also noticed that its lamp chimneys were too short, so that soot collected on the reflectors.[^2]

The roster lists fifteen principal keepers and one first assistant between 1819 and 1899, plus two contractors with no dates.[^3] Most of the principal keepers served only a few years.[^3] Two of the roster's names are women's names: Isabella Miles, principal keeper for 1853 to 1854, and Alice J. Fargo, first assistant from 1873 to 1881.[^3] Alanson J. Fargo was principal keeper from 1871 to 1881. The roster does not say whether Alice J. Fargo was related to him.[^3]

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 lists, under a heading for Erie outside the harbor, work on the keeper's dwelling: a 24-foot (7 m) stoop across the west front, and some 143 feet (44 m) of walk.[^1] The heading names no light. The same report lists the Presque Isle pier light under separate headings, so the link to Erie Land is an inference.

George W. Miller, the last keeper, came to Erie from Conneaut Lighthouse after sixteen years there. He was the first keeper when the light was re-established in 1885.[^2][^3] In 1894 natural gas from a nearby well was piped to the keeper's dwelling for fuel.[^2] Miller served until 1899, when the light lost its keeper "for good."[^2]

## After the keepers

A new lighthouse on the lake side of the peninsula was completed in 1873 and took the name Presque Isle Lighthouse. The old bluff light was renamed Erie Lighthouse, though locals referred to it as the Erie Land Lighthouse.[^2][^4]

In 1880 the Lighthouse Board decided the bluff light could be discontinued.[^2] Its lantern, lighting apparatus and iron stairs were taken out and stored at the lighthouse depot in Buffalo.[^2] On 1 March 1881 the tower and dwelling were sold at public auction for $1,800 to Myron Sanford, the owner of the surrounding land.[^2] Local citizens and mariners protested the decision.[^2] On 7 July 1884 Congress provided $7,000 to re-establish the light. The property was promptly repurchased, and the light was relit on 1 July 1885 with a revolving third-order Fresnel.[^2]

After the last keeper left, the lantern room came down from the tower and went to Marblehead Lighthouse in Ohio.[^2] In 1934 the federal government turned the property over to the City of Erie "for public-park purposes."[^2] The city rents the keeper's dwelling to caretakers, who look after the lighthouse property, now known as Lighthouse Park.[^2]

Restoration came in stages. A new wooden lantern room was unveiled on 17 December 1990.[^2] On 26 December 1999, a century after the light was last put out, a ceremonial relighting was held.[^2] In 2000 archaeologists found the foundation of the original lighthouse about 200 yards (183 m) west of the present tower.[^2] The Port Authority says it applied for and received two grants totaling $390,000 for the restoration, and that the tower was restored in 2004.[^4] The new utilities are to support a light "for decorative purposes." The Port Authority says the lighthouse will not become a private aid to navigation.[^4]

## Today

The grounds are open daily from dawn to dusk.[^5] The tower can be climbed on set days.[^5] For September and October the hours page lists Friday through Monday, 10 a.m. to 5 p.m., with the last climb at 4:40 p.m.[^5] A separate October schedule gives Saturday and Sunday, 10 a.m. to 3 p.m., with the last climb at 2:40 p.m.[^5] The tower has 69 steps to the top.[^4] The Lake Erie Lights page says the lighthouses are closed in the off-season, so check the hours before going.[^5]

LighthouseFriends says the City of Erie owns the lighthouse. It also reports that in 2021 the Presque Isle Light Station Board was given control of Erie Land Lighthouse, under a two-year agreement with the Port Authority.[^2]
