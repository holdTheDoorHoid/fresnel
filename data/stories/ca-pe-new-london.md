New London Lighthouse is a small white wooden tower with a house built onto its back, standing at the end of Cape Road near the entrance to New London Bay on the north shore of Prince Edward Island. Built in 1876, it served for most of its life as the rear light of a pair of range lights, and for fourteen or so of those years it was kept by a mother of three, Maisie Adams, who is described by the Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society as the Island's only woman lightkeeper.[^1][^3]

## The place

New London Bay lies on the north coast of Prince Edward Island, and a bar lies across its entrance. A departmental report of 1903 and another of 1904 show how unsettled the channel over it could be (see the storms section below).[^6][^7] The first lights here were two small lanterns raised on day beacons, set up after George B. McKay and other New London inhabitants petitioned the Prince Edward Island House of Assembly for a grant toward the cost in 1868. After the Island joined Confederation in 1873, the federal General Superintendent of Lighthouses visited and recommended replacing them with a tower.[^1] The Prince Edward Island heritage record adds that the new light was meant to support the young fishing industry of the area.[^4]

## Building the light

George McKenzie of French River won the contract, at $1,300, and finished the tower in the summer of 1876. Lighthouse Friends gives its height as 13.3 m (43.6 ft), 4.5 m square at the base and tapering to 2.3 m at the lantern, with a narrow two-storey dwelling attached on the landward side; the Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society gives 13.3 m for the tower in its data table but 14.1 m from ground to weathervane in its description.[^2] The light was first lit on 1 October 1876: a fixed white light in the lantern, and below it a red "streak" light shown from a window 4.9 m (16 ft) below the lantern, visible only to vessels lined up with the channel.[^1]

In 1879 the streak was replaced by a separate front range light built nearer the harbour entrance by John McKay for $130, and the tower became the rear light of a range. Lighthouse Friends, whose wording the Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society repeats, says a heavy gale soon afterwards carried "the tower" 183 m westward and that its lantern was destroyed by fire; the sentence is not clear about which tower is meant.[^1][^2] In 1891 both lights were moved 61 m southwest, nearer the channel, in a job done under contract by James Burclay for $110.[^1][^4]

## The keepers

McKenzie, who is styled Captain in the sources, stayed on as keeper for about twenty years.[^1][^2] In 1894 a chimney broke in the lantern while he slept in the watchroom just below it, and the lamps caught fire. The lighting apparatus was destroyed, but Lighthouse Friends, reporting the departmental account, says McKenzie went into the burning lantern and put out the fire alone, saving the tower.[^1] James H. McLeod took over in January 1896; a later departmental staff list shows him appointed on 29 January 1896.[^1][^8]

Lighthouse Friends lists the later keepers as James A. Pidgeon, John Ross McKenzie, Hugh John MacRae (from 1931) and, briefly in 1940, his brother Charles Earl MacRae. Five of Hugh John MacRae's children later wrote a book about their childhood at the light, *Living Under the Light*.[^1] The Pidgeon dates differ: Lighthouse Friends' list gives 1912 to 1923 and again 1931, its narrative says he served from 1918 to 1931, and the Lighthouse Society gives 1912 to 1931, with no John Ross McKenzie.[^1][^2] The roster below shows them as the sources give them.

When Hugh John MacRae went on military leave in October 1940, Claude Adams became temporary keeper on 1 December. He was already ailing, and his wife Maisie, from French River, took on most of the duties with two small children; a third was born at the lighthouse in October 1941, during a wartime blackout order.[^1][^3] Claude died of cancer on 23 January 1943; neighbouring men asked the Department of Transport to give her the post, as she had no other means of support and had been doing the work all along.[^3]

Maisie later moved a few kilometres away so her son would be nearer school, walking to the light each afternoon to light the lamp and each morning to put it out. She hauled lamp oil in a five-gallon can and carried it up the steep stairs, with no rails, to the lantern; two neighbours helped her lift the oil barrels onto a stand.[^3] In 1944 a Department of Transport man named J.K. Lacey jacked the building onto oil barrels to replace its wooden foundation; a summer storm raised the tide over the barrels and into the kitchen, and the family spent the night there. The Society's account says her daughter Mary recalled that she was earning $47 a month by the time she retired.[^3] The sources disagree on when she left: 1955, 1956, 1957 and 1959 all appear.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^9] William Roland Paynter followed as temporary keeper from 1 May 1957 until the light was electrified in 1958, by Lighthouse Friends' account; the Lighthouse Society lists him as 1957 to 1960 and says staffing ended in the mid-1960s.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the winter of 1902-03 heavy ice grounded off the harbour entrance, and the current running out scoured a new channel through the outer bar. The Department of Marine and Fisheries reported the new channel in its annual report.[^6] A temporary back range light was shown from a lantern hoisted on a pole, while the tower itself showed a fixed red light as a coastal light.[^1][^7] The following year's report calls the channel "very unstable" and says it had shifted back so that the best water lay again in line with the old range lights.[^7] The heritage record also notes that the clipper *Marco Polo* was wrecked on sandbars in July 1883, about five miles east of the lighthouse.[^4]

## After the keepers

Once electrified and no longer staffed, the lighthouse was rented out as a private summer home from the mid-1960s until 2000, when the Canadian Coast Guard ended private leases of Island lighthouses.[^1][^2] The front range light was removed in 2001, and the red day-mark stripe was taken off the tower in 2008, so it again read as a lighthouse rather than a range light.[^2] The grid power was taken out in 2009 and a solar-powered light now does the work.[^2] The province designated it a heritage place on 3 October 2012.[^1][^2] The Canadian Register of Historic Places also lists its rear range tower under the Federal Heritage Buildings Review Office.[^5]

## Today

The light is still active; the Lighthouse Society describes it as an isophase white light, two seconds on and two off, with a nominal range of 7 nautical miles.[^1][^2] It is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard, and the grounds and tower are closed.[^1] The Lighthouse Society says a petition for ownership had gone in through Parks Canada's Heritage Lighthouse Program, but gives no date and no outcome.[^2] It is reached by Cape Road, which ends in a parking spot and a walk down a dirt road to the site.[^1] The heritage record counts it one of four surviving Island lighthouses with an attached keeper's dwelling.[^4]
