Cap-au-Saumon is a lightstation on a rocky bluff above the north shore of the St. Lawrence River in Charlevoix, Quebec, first lit in 1894 on a cliff that Lighthouse Friends calls La Pointe de Roches.[^2][^3] Parks Canada says only two families kept it between its building and its automation, and its keepers also watched the river traffic for the government.[^1][^2]

## The place

Hare Island (Île aux Lièvres) lies roughly midstream in the river and forces mariners to choose between a north and a south channel. In 1890 the Marine Department's chief engineer, William P. Anderson, toured the river's lights. He noted that pilots disliked the north channel for its stronger current and poor anchorages, though masters of seagoing ships favoured it. He recommended lights near the north end of Hare Island and at Cap au Saumon (Cape Salmon) to mark the two ends of that channel.[^2] Quebec's heritage register describes the station as a secondary light that guided navigation along a poorly marked stretch of the north shore.[^3]

## Building the light

According to Lighthouse Friends, Jean Morray's bid of $3,700 to build the station's buildings was the lowest. Work began in the spring of 1894 with a fog alarm building close to the cliff edge and a combined lighthouse and dwelling behind it. The square tower rose 14 m (46 ft) from the dwelling, with an iron lantern, and the light was first shown on 15 September 1894.[^2] The fog alarm used compressed air made by a steam engine and gave an eight-second blast in each thirty seconds. The machinery was installed in duplicate so that a spare horn or boiler could be started if one failed.[^2]

A Marine Department report of the early 1900s said the fog alarm machinery had been overhauled and "a stronger blast secui'ed" (as the OCR reads).[^4] A type "F" diaphone, sounding three blasts a minute, replaced the steam horn in 1917, according to Lighthouse Friends. A Marine Department report of 1917-18 lists a diaphone among the new fog signals placed at Cape Salmon.[^2][^8]

The wooden lighthouse was replaced in 1955 by the present octagonal, tapered tower of reinforced concrete, built to a standard Department of Transport plan.[^1][^3] Parks Canada's statement of significance lists six related buildings that contribute to its character: the 1955 keeper's and assistant keeper's dwellings, a 1955 storage shed, the 1894 fog alarm building, and two 1955 pumphouses.[^1]

## The keepers

Parks Canada says the station had only two keeper families between its building and its automation: the Bouchards (1894 to 1940) and the Carrés (1940 to 1982).[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives a more detailed list: Arthur Simard (1894 to 1895); Louis Bouchard (1896 to 1916); J. D. Bouchard (1916 to 1926); J. U. Bouchard (1926 to at least 1937); Louis-Phillipe Carré (1941 to 1966); Edmour Carré (1966 to 1972); and Neil Carré (1973 to 1985).[^2] The two lists do not agree on every date, and Simard is not mentioned by Parks Canada. The Department of Marine's list of lightkeepers for 1908 names Louis Bouchard as keeper at Cap au Saumon.[^9]

Lighthouse Friends says the keepers lived a fairly isolated life: the land approach was a very steep road, and rough weather often stopped boats.[^2] A semaphore and telegraph at the station let the keeper report river traffic to Quebec. In its 1910 report the department said a signal station would open at the lighthouse, adding "This station should prove of great value, being situated half way between Father point and Quebec."[^6] After the Second World War the equipment gave way to telephone and radiotelephone. Neil Carré, who grew up at the station, remembered that "everywhere you went you had to take a pencil, paper, and binoculars" to note each passing ship for the report to Quebec. The keeper was paid $200 a year for this extra work, and the signal service ended in 1965.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Two accidents near the station appear in Marine Department reports. In a Marine Department report of the early 1900s, a steamer under Captain Dundas passed Cape Salmon at 3.25 a.m. in hazy weather and ran onto the north side of White Island reef at 4.45 a.m. The inquiry blamed the captain's "imprudent confidence in his pilot" and found that "if Captain Dundas had been on the bridge after passing Cape Salmon, the casualty would not have occurred."[^5]

A Marine Department report from the First World War years records that on 1 August the steamers Batiscan and Bengore Head collided in fog off Cape Salmon. The Bengore Head had to be beached on Hare Island reef, and the Wreck Commissioner's court held the Batiscan at fault.[^7]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the light was automated in 1980; Parks Canada and the Route des phares page say 1982, and Lighthouse Friends lists Neil Carré's tenure to 1985.[^1][^2][^10] The station was later abandoned and fell into disrepair. In July 2003 an Air Canada captain, Peter Noreau, who had loved the light since boyhood, formed the Corporation du Phare de Cap-au-Saumon and arranged a lease with the government. Lighthouse Friends says he spent nine years restoring the property.[^2] The town of La Malbaie declared the lighthouse area a heritage site in 2014.[^3]

## Today

Parks Canada's statement of significance calls the concrete tower an excellent example of its type, standing high on its bluff and easy for passing ships to see.[^1] Lighthouse Friends lists the Canadian Coast Guard as owner, with the grounds open by permission only and the tower closed. It says access is a steep walk of about 3.2 km on a road with a gate that forbids entry without authorisation.[^2] Quebec's heritage register says the shore is reachable only by river or air and that the site is run by the nautical tourism company Duvetnor, which differs from Lighthouse Friends' account of a road hike and a lease held by the Corporation du Phare.[^2][^3] Anyone wanting to visit should arrange it in advance and not enter without permission.
