The Graves are a scatter of bare ledges at the north-east corner of the approaches to Boston Harbor, and the granite tower that stands on them, finished in 1905, has marked the outer entrance to the harbor's main ship channel for well over a century. Reaching it takes a boat, and since 2013 it has had a private owner.[^1][^2]

## The place

The name is older than any lighthouse. The ledges are said to have been named not for the dead but for Rear Admiral Thomas Graves, who noticed the danger they posed in 1634.[^1] They lie about 11 miles from Boston, and the National Park Service describes them as only about 15 feet (4.6 m) above sea level at high tide.[^3] Before the tower, buoys were the only warning.[^3] A spindle was proposed for the ledge in 1851, but an Army engineer judged the formation could not carry one, and a bell buoy was moored there on 22 June 1855.[^1][^2]

What changed in the early 1900s was the traffic. According to Kraig Anderson's history, after the Broad Sound channel was dredged to 30 feet to admit larger vessels, the Lighthouse Board proposed a tall granite light with a fog signal on the Northeast Grave to mark the sea end of that channel.[^1]

## Building the light

On 28 June 1902 Congress paid for the range lights on Lovells and Spectacle islands and put up $75,000 of the roughly $188,000 the tower was expected to cost.[^1] A contract for granite cut at Rockport was signed on 28 April 1903, and work on the ledge began on 1 June that year.[^1] Surveyors found the northern end of the ledge shelving away, so the tower was moved about two-thirds of the ledge's length to the south-west.[^1] Royal Luther of Malden was in charge of construction, under Army engineer William S. Stanton.[^1]

In summer the workmen stayed on the ledge itself, in a shanty with quarters, a messroom and a kitchen for thirty men, which spared them the trip to and from the site each day.[^1] The plans called for 882 stones in 44 courses, rising 88 feet (27 m).[^1] Putnam's 1917 survey also counts 882 granite blocks and notes that the rock stood only three feet above low water.[^2] Congress voted the remaining $113,000 on 28 April 1904. The lantern and watchroom arrived on 6 March 1905 and were set on the tower between 3 May and 9 June; the first-order Fresnel lens and its revolving machinery went in by 1 July.[^1] The year 1903 is carved over the door.[^1]

The light was first lit by head keeper Elliott C. Hadley on 1 September 1905.[^1][^3] It showed two white flashes every six seconds, at 98 feet (30 m) above high water, and the lens was rated at 380,000 candlepower.[^1][^2] A Daboll trumpet fog signal, run on compressed air, went into service on 23 November 1905; in the twelve months ending 30 June 1907 it sounded for 876 hours.[^1]

Inside, the National Register form describes a cistern about 35 feet deep, an engine room with two semi-diesel engines for the horn, a kitchen on the third level, a bunk room on the fourth and a library and watch room on the fifth.[^4] That description dates from the later years of the station, not 1905.

## The keepers

Four men were assigned at the start: Hadley as head keeper, John E. H. Cook, Octavius H. Reamy and Robert M. McAfee as assistants.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's archive, drawn from the keeper registers, lists about 136 stints between 1905 and 1976.[^5] Reamy's is the long thread. He began as second assistant in 1905, became first assistant in 1906, and was head keeper from 1924 to 1939.[^5] Kraig Anderson's list gives Pearl S. King as head keeper in 1923 to 1924, whereas the archive lists him as a first assistant for those years.[^1][^5]

Turnover among the assistants was high, with many of them listed for a year or less.[^5] One of the harder stints was Harry Whin's. In December 1916, according to Anderson, he was alone with supplies and relief weeks late when a gale hit with winds over 100 mph; for five days and nights he kept the light and horn going and ate only fish.[^1] On Christmas Eve he launched the station boat for Nahant Rock and the provisions, and wrote later that it "Took me 5 hours to get back!"[^1] The archive spells his name Harry Whine.[^5] When the head keeper came back the next day, Whin resigned.[^1]

Not all the trouble came from the weather. On the night of 7 December 1924, by Anderson's account, William A. Day, who had come back to the station as an assistant, telephoned his son to say that first assistant Olsen was crazy; the district superintendent later found they had quarrelled but saw no evidence that Olsen was insane, and recommended that Day be dismissed.[^1] The archive spells the first assistant's name Anton Olson and lists Day as second assistant in 1924.[^5] The archive also lists dozens of Coast Guard men at the station from the mid-1940s to 1971.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In July 1914 head keeper Henry C. Towle towed a disabled motor dory with a 25-foot motor boat, and on 22 August 1915 he rescued two men in a disabled motor boat near the rocks and took them into the light for the night, as the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported.[^6][^7]

Hadley reported in 1910, according to Anderson, that the pounding of the sea never shook the tower but that firing at the harbor forts did, and that the first sign of it was broken dishes.[^1] In the November 1935 storm, the Bulletin recorded, the keeper reported that the sea moved rocks of around three tons up a sloping ledge and onto the riprap, something that had not happened since the tower was built.[^8]

In April 1938 the 419-foot British freighter City of Salisbury, carrying a cargo that included zoo animals, ran onto an uncharted rock about half a mile from the Graves. Nobody was lost, though some of the animals were.[^1][^3]

## After the keepers

The station was automated in 1976; the first-order lens was removed and the light replaced by a modern lamp.[^1][^3] An earlier mercury spill, in the early 1970s, had forced a temporary shutdown for decontamination.[^1] The light went solar in summer 2001.[^1]

In 2012 the Graves was declared federal surplus and offered free to towns and nonprofits under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act. With no taker, the General Services Administration auctioned it, and the sale closed on 31 August 2013 at $933,888, far above the $381,000 previous high for an auctioned lighthouse.[^1][^9] The buyer was Boston businessman Dave Waller and his wife Lynn.[^9][^3] The Coast Guard remains responsible for the light as a working aid to navigation.[^9]

## Today

The Graves Light is privately owned and closed to the public; the grounds and tower are closed, and the ledge is awash at high tide.[^1][^3][^9] It is best seen from a boat, and the Boston Harbor Islands cruises pass it.[^1] The light remains an active aid to navigation.[^1] The Wallers have restored the interior and, according to the National Park Service, installed a first-order Fresnel lens assembled from pieces of Australian lenses in August 2023.[^3] Sources disagree on where the ledge lies: the 1980 National Register form puts the station in Hull, Plymouth County, while the state's inventory sheet says Boston, Suffolk County, and in 2023 the Massachusetts Land Court ruled it is not in Hull.[^4][^1] The Park Service gives the tower's height as 113 feet (34 m); Anderson's figure for the masonry is 88 feet.[^3][^1]
