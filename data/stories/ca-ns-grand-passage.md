Grand Passage light stands on the northern tip of Brier Island, Nova Scotia, where it marks the north entrance to Grand Passage, the narrow water between Brier Island and Long Island in the Bay of Fundy. The light was put in operation in 1901, and its present concrete tower dates from the 1960s. Locals call it the "Northern light."[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

LighthouseFriends names Passage Shoal and Cow Ledge Shoal as the hazards in the passage, and says tide streams that can run up to five or six knots make them especially treacherous.[^2] The 2021 announcement describes the passage as running between Brier Island and "the western shore of St. Mary's Bay" instead of Long Island.[^3] Parks Canada places the light within a wider expansion of navigational aids in the Bay of Fundy, as traffic grew, in a region long known as the "Graveyard of the Fundy."[^1]

## Building the light

The Department of Marine's report for the year ended 30 June 1901 says the light was put in operation on the north point of Brier Island "to indicate the north entrance to Grand passage."[^4] It was a fixed red light, elevated 62 feet (19 m) above high water, which the report says should be visible for 8 miles from all points of approach by water. Its seventh-order dioptric apparatus was housed in a square, wooden building with sloping sides, surmounted by an octagonal iron lantern painted red. The lighthouse stood 34 feet (10 m) from its base to the vane on the lantern.[^4] The contractor was Frank H. Piper of Westport, whose price was $897.50.[^4]

A fog bell followed. LighthouseFriends dates it to 1903, on a framework near the northern side of the light tower.[^2] The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1904 says a fog bell "has been established at Grand Passage lighthouse". In thick or foggy weather the bell was to give single strokes at intervals of five seconds.[^5]

In the fiscal year to 30 June 1905 the Department's report records a contract of $3,150 for keepers' dwellings at the two light stations on the island, carried out by a Westport contractor.[^6] LighthouseFriends names him as E. C. Bowers.[^2]

The sources disagree on the date of the present tower. Parks Canada says the first lighthouse was rebuilt in 1964 with a fog alarm building attached, and the 2021 announcement says the 1964 tower replaced the original 1901 lighthouse.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends says the original was demolished in 1968 and replaced by the current one.[^2] The present tower is a square, reinforced-concrete tower, 8.5 m (28 ft) tall.[^3]

## The keepers

Charles Buckman was the first keeper, from 1901 until his death in 1940.[^2] LighthouseFriends then names no head keeper until Wickerson Lent in 1977, so the sources read here give no keeper for the years between.[^2]

Wickerson Lent was an assistant at the station in 1960 and 1961. LighthouseFriends says the assistant's post was dropped soon after he arrived, because the light was being automated. He returned as head keeper from 1977 to 1984.[^2] Lawrence F. (Pete) Welch was head keeper from 1984, and LighthouseFriends says he was keeper when the station was de-staffed in 1988.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 17 March 1911 a Digby dispatch in the Evening Star, under the heading "Schooner Lost in Storm", reported that a northwest gale which swept the Atlantic seaboard had cost the three-masted schooner Allen Greene of Deer Isle, Maine, in Grand Passage.[^7] The Daily Kennebec Journal of the following day reported that she struck rocks near Flowers Cove, that her cook, Ambrose N. Ames of Rockland, was washed into the sea and drowned, and that the captain and five crew reached the shore of Brier Island. "There is no house near Flowers cove," the paper said, and the captain and his men went into the woods to find shelter.[^8] Neither paper says that the light or its keeper took part.

In November 1914 Buckman found a bottle that held a leaf from a memorandum book. On the back of the leaf someone had written "August 8th, 1914 - German war ship off Coast of Maine," according to LighthouseFriends.[^2]

## After the keepers

The station was staffed into the 1980s. LighthouseFriends says it was de-staffed in 1988, and Parks Canada says it was automated that year.[^1][^2]

In December 2021 the federal government designated Grand Passage Lighthouse a heritage lighthouse under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act.[^2][^3]

## Today

The station includes a former lightkeeper's dwelling, which the Canadian Coast Guard now uses as an office and bunk house. Parks Canada says the station is a base for the Coast Guard's search and rescue team.[^1] The station's fog bell can be seen at the post office in Digby, where it is part of a memorial to those lost at sea.[^2]

The owner is disputed. LighthouseFriends says the Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and that the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^2] The 2021 announcement lists Grand Passage among the lights "managed by new, non-federal owners" without naming the owner.[^3]

To reach it, LighthouseFriends gives this route: leave the Westport ferry dock, turn right onto Water Street and drive about 1.6 km. The road turns to gravel after about 1 km, and a parking area lies beside the light.[^2]
