Perry's Victory and International Peace Memorial is a 352-foot (107 m) Doric column of pink granite on a narrow isthmus at Put-in-Bay, on South Bass Island in western Lake Erie.[^1] It honors the battle of 10 September 1813, and the Coast Guard added navigation lights to the monument in 1940.[^3][^5]

## The place

The column stands at the eastern edge of the town of Put-in-Bay, four miles from the Ohio mainland.[^1] It commemorates the Battle of Lake Erie, in which Commodore Oliver Hazard Perry defeated a British squadron commanded by Captain Robert H. Barclay. The 1966 nomination says the victory "gave the Americans control of Lake Erie."[^1] Put-in-Bay was Perry's base before the battle, and he returned to the harbor afterward to bury the dead.[^1]

The monument was not first planned here. A monument on Gibraltar Island, just north of South Bass Island, was to honor the victory. The 1966 nomination dates the cornerstone laid there to 1859. The 2015 nomination places the laying of the cornerstone at the celebration of 10 September 1859, but elsewhere refers to "the cornerstone set there in 1858." The sources do not settle whether the stone was set in 1858 or 1859.[^1][^2]

The 1966 nomination says the Gibraltar monument was never completed and is "marked only with a bronze plaque."[^1] The 2015 nomination adds that in 1864 Jay Cooke bought Gibraltar Island and erected a small monument with a bronze plaque there, and that this monument still stands.[^2]

The column was built on swampland. The area around it is fill brought in to make lawns, and the ground has settled to several feet below the present lake level.[^1]

The column's top has long served sailors as a landmark. A Coast Guard bulletin of July 1940, as quoted by LighthouseFriends, said the monument "has long been used as a landmark by mariners" and that its decorative lighting had not fully served vessel traffic.[^3]

## Building the light

The design came out of a competition. The 1966 nomination calls it the largest architectural competition ever conducted in this country or in Europe up to that time.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the judging to 25 January 1912.[^3] The 1966 nomination names the architects as Joseph H. Freedlander and A. D. Seymour, both of New York.[^1] The 2015 nomination gives Seymour's full name as Alexander Duncan Seymour, and its chronology lists the selection of the architects, with the start of construction, in 1912.[^2] The builder was J. C. Robinson & Son.[^2] The granite was set course by course. The 2015 nomination says all seventy-eight courses were in place by early July 1914.[^2]

The cornerstone was laid on 4 July 1913.[^1][^2] The remains of three American and three British officers killed in the battle were placed in a crypt beneath the floor, with services on 11 September 1913.[^1] The column was finished in 1915, and the memorial opened to the public on 13 June 1915.[^1]

According to the 2015 nomination, which quotes a 1961 construction history, William Marks installed the electric light and power equipment and was the monument's first maintenance man.[^2] Electric floodlights were installed in 1928 to light the column at night.[^2] The memorial was dedicated on 31 July 1931.[^1][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In July 1920 lightning damaged the column. The 2015 nomination says no lightning protection had been designed for it, and none was installed.[^2]

The 1966 nomination, as revised in 1976, records that severe storms in 1972 and 1973 destroyed most of the south seawall, which was 1,000 feet (300 m) long, and flooded the grounds.[^1] None of the sources for this story ties a wreck or rescue to the monument.

## After the keepers

The monument has no keepers. Its later history is one of changing owners and lights. President Roosevelt declared the memorial a national monument on 2 June 1936, and a presidential proclamation on 6 July 1936 included it as part of the National Park Service.[^3] The National Park Service owns the monument today.[^3]

In July 1940 the Coast Guard added two marine-type lights to the monument's illuminating equipment, as the bulletin quoted by LighthouseFriends describes.[^3][^5] In 1953 the Coast Guard was permitted to install two navigational lights on the penthouse. The 2015 nomination says those tall lights "formerly provided navigational assistance," and that the Coast Guard lights at the corners now are much smaller and less intrusive than the earlier ones.[^2]

## Today

The National Park Service runs the memorial. The grounds, the memorial plaza and the rotunda are free to enter.[^7] The observation deck is not accessible in the 2026 and 2027 seasons because of the Upper Plaza Rehabilitation. The visitor center keeps seasonal hours.[^6]

The 2025 Light List still shows the light on the roof of the monument. It is a white isophase light with a six-second period, 352 feet (107 m) above the lake, with a range of nine nautical miles.[^4] The National Park Service's register record for the memorial gives 15 October 1966 as its date published, and its boundary-increase record gives 28 April 2015.[^8][^9]
