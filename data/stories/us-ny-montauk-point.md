Montauk Point Light stands on Turtle Hill, at the far eastern end of Long Island, and is described by the sources as the oldest lighthouse in New York State.[^2][^5] It was authorised by Congress in President Washington's time,[^2][^3] and in 2023 its Fresnel lens was lit again.[^5]

## The place

President Washington signed the bill for a lighthouse at Montauk on 12 April 1792; a 2012 account in *Lighthouse Digest* names Ezra L'Hommedieu, the New York Chamber of Commerce's representative, as the man who chose the site.[^3] George R. Putnam, writing for the Lighthouse Service in 1917, called it the first lighthouse for which Congress appropriated money, apart from those taken over from the colonies.[^4]

The sources disagree on when the tower was finished. Putnam says it was built in 1797; the U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive gives 1796 for the station and 1797 for the tower, and a 2021 Society piece says construction began in 1796 and was completed the following year; a 2023 New York Almanack summary says construction was completed in November 1796, and LighthouseFriends has the tower and dwelling done less than five months after work began in June 1796.[^4][^1][^16][^2][^5]

## Building the light

According to LighthouseFriends, the contract went to the New York builder John McComb, whose low bid was $22,300. Work began in June 1796, and the sandstone tower and a keeper's dwelling were finished in under five months. The same account describes an octagonal tower of about 78 feet (24 m), with walls seven feet thick at the base.[^5]

The station grew in stages. LighthouseFriends dates a brick keeper's dwelling to 1837, the U.S. Lighthouse Society to 1838.[^5][^1] In 1860, according to LighthouseFriends, the tower was raised by 14 feet (4 m), given a new lantern and an iron staircase, and a large two-storey dwelling was added.[^5] The Society's record lists the original lens as installed in 1860, while LighthouseFriends says a first-order Fresnel lens went in three years before the tower was raised, in 1857.[^1][^5] The first-order lens was later replaced by a revolving lens of three and a half order, which LighthouseFriends dates to 1903.[^5] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1921 the Lighthouse Service spent $1,394 installing a lens pedestal and clock at the station.[^6]

The fog signal came next. The Lighthouse Board's reports of the 1890s describe a first-class Daboll trumpet "worked by caloric engines, in duplicate", which ran about 575 hours in the year reported in 1894 and about 754 hours the next year.[^7][^8] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1897 new fog-signal houses were finished (on 14 April 1897), and on 19 June the engines, tanks, compressors and sirens were delivered to the station.[^9] LighthouseFriends dates the pair of oil engines in the new fog-signal building to 1898.[^5] By the 1899 report a first-class siren, worked by oil engines and compressed air, had run about 595 hours.[^10] In its 1900 report the Board recorded that a deflector had been built to send the sound eastward, "to provide against a lack of sound in that direction".[^11] The tower's colour was changed on 15 May 1899, the date the Board records; LighthouseFriends describes the change as the reddish-brown band that now crosses the white tower.[^10][^5]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the keepers' registers, lists Jacob Hand as the first principal keeper, from 1796 to 1812.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he was 64, and that he was appointed on the condition that his grandson live with him and serve as assistant without pay.[^5] When Jared Hand was proposed to succeed his father, letters of 30 May and 1 June 1808 recommended him, and President Jefferson wrote on the original: "I have constantly refused to give in to this method of making offices hereditary."[^12][^4] Jared Hand did get the post, and the archive lists him from 1812 to 1814.[^1]

The archive's list holds 32 principal-keeper entries in all. Henry Baker served 1814 to 1832 and Patrick T. Gould 1832 to 1849.[^1] The longest was James G. Scott, from 1885 to 1910, who according to LighthouseFriends came from Stratford Shoal Light.[^1][^5] In September 1898, with an army camp at Montauk Point, the New York *Sun* reported that a stray mule from the camp had settled in at Montauk light and made friends with the horse of the keeper, Capt. Scott.[^13] In May 1910 the *New-York Tribune* reported that "Captain Scott, the keeper of the light" gave a hearty welcome to a party of pathfinding motorists who had driven out from Montauk Inn.[^14]

LighthouseFriends records two stories from Scott's years: that the cocoa cargo of a schooner drifted ashore and was made into a chocolate bread pudding, which Keeper Scott kept serving his guests, and that in 1898 his fourteen-year-old son Walter drowned in Money Pond near the lighthouse.[^5] The same site says a wood duck that crashed into the lantern room one night in 1906 was found next day by Scott's eight-year-old grandson, had it stuffed, and that it was later given to the museum.[^5]

The archive's later rosters show a family at work. John E. Miller was principal keeper from 1912 to 1929, and John A. Miller served as first assistant from 1917 to 1943.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that in 1925 a father-and-son team named Miller were suspected of helping rum-runners at the point, but that there was not enough evidence to accuse them.[^5] The archive lists Thomas A. Buckridge as principal keeper from 1930 to 1943 and George W. Warrington as second assistant from 1928 to at least 1941.[^1]

By 1939 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin could report that, among Atlantic coast lighthouses, Montauk Point drew the most visitors: about 16,000 in a season.[^15]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the Army Signal Corps occupied the station during the Second World War and built the concrete fire-control tower beside the lighthouse.[^5] The archive lists Archie W. Jones as principal keeper from 1946, in the Coast Guard period, and keepers continued to be named until the 1980s.[^1]

Erosion threatened the site. According to the U.S. Lighthouse Society, the tower stood 297 feet from the bluff's edge when built and only about 90 feet in 2021.[^16] LighthouseFriends says that by the late 1960s the Coast Guard was preparing to build a replacement light inland, until a trench-and-reed terracing method promoted by Giorgina Reid was tried on the bluff.[^5] The station was automated in 1987; the Montauk Historical Society leased it on 1 April 1987 and opened a museum on 23 May.[^1][^5] Ownership of the buildings passed to the Society in 1996.[^16][^17] The tower was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1969 and made a National Historic Landmark in 2012.[^5][^3]

## Today

The Montauk Historical Society owns the light station and runs it as a museum.[^17] The Army Corps of Engineers began work in March 2021 to rebuild about 1,000 linear feet of the stone revetment below the point; The East Hampton Star put the project at $30.7 million, while the New York Almanack summary of the 2023 reopening announcement puts the overall effort, including restoration, at about $44 million.[^17][^2] On 6 November 2023 the three-and-a-half-order Fresnel lens removed in 1987 went back into service in the lantern, as a two-year Coast Guard pilot program.[^5] LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds are open and the tower and dwelling open during visiting hours; hours vary by season, so check with the Montauk Historical Society before travelling.[^5]
