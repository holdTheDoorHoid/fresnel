Cabot Head Lighthouse stands on a cliff at the northern end of the Bruce Peninsula, east of Wingfield Basin on Georgian Bay, in Bruce County, Ontario.[^1][^10] Its light was first shown on 18 May 1896. The light still shows, from a skeleton tower beside the old wooden lighthouse, but the lighthouse has been closed to visitors since 2016 or 2017, and the road to it has been closed to vehicles since the storms of 2019 and 2020.[^6][^8][^9]

## The place

Cabot Head takes its name from the explorer John Cabot. Ships running the eastern shore of the Bruce Peninsula for Owen Sound, Collingwood or Midland had a long stretch with no light. LighthouseFriends notes that a light on Griffith Island had stood since 1858 and one at Tobermory since 1885, but the roughly sixty-mile sail between them was unmarked.[^1]

Just west of the lighthouse lies Wingfield Basin, a natural harbour whose entrance was held back by a shallow bar. The harbour was dredged in 1914, and a pair of range lights was set up on its shores to guide ships into the anchorage.[^1]

## Building the light

The Department of Marine and Fisheries called for bids in July 1895. By the next spring John George of Port Elgin and Richard Webb of Southampton had the station ready.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes a two-storey wooden dwelling with a tower rising from one corner, and a square wooden fog alarm building on an outcrop about 60 m (200 ft) east of the light. It gives the tower as 15 m (49 ft). The Lighthouse Directory describes the wooden tower as about 11 m (35 ft) high. The difference is not resolved here.[^1][^9]

The first keeper, William Campbell, lit the light for the first time on the evening of 18 May 1896. Its apparatus was a set of parabolic reflectors and kerosene lanterns, turned once every eighty seconds to show three flashes. LighthouseFriends puts the total cost of the station at $7,473.59.[^1]

Fire struck the fog alarm building in August 1907. The Department's report for 1909 says that "the fog alarm building and plant were destroyed by fire, in August, 1907." A new wooden fog alarm building was built by contract, by J. C. Kennedy of Owen Sound, and a duplicate diaphone plant was bought from the Canadian Fog Signal Company of Toronto for $8,100 and installed.[^4] LighthouseFriends dates the new building to 1908.[^1]

Storm warnings came later. The Department's report for the fiscal year 1917-18 says that an 18 m (60 ft) steel skeleton tower was erected at Cabot Head for the Meteorological Service, for storm signal purposes.[^5] LighthouseFriends dates an 18 m (60 ft) tower beside the lighthouse to 1915, and says the keeper had to fly storm warnings that came by a telephone line from Toronto. The two accounts disagree on the year, and the story does not choose between them.[^1]

## The keepers

Keepers were political appointments in the early years. LighthouseFriends says that when the Liberals won the federal election of 1896, Campbell, a Conservative, lost his post to Samuel J. Parke.[^1] Parke's service ran until 1898, when Charles Webster took over.[^1]

The Department's keepers' statement for 1904 lists "Webster, Chas." at Cabot Head, Light and Fog Alarm, appointed 10 May 1898.[^2] Webster was followed by Leslie Martindale, from 1906 to 1925, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] The Department's statement for the fiscal year 1913-14 lists "L. Martindale" against "Cabot Head, light and fog alarm," with an appointment date of 15 June 1912.[^3] That date does not match LighthouseFriends' 1906 start. The sources do not say whether 1912 marks a transfer or a different appointment.[^1][^3]

Howard Boyle, who had enlisted in the Canadian Overseas Expeditionary Force in 1916, was keeper from 1926 to 1951. Veterans were given preference for new posts after the First World War.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that the Boyles kept a garden, stone walls and lilac bushes around the station. Mrs. Boyle recalled that when she first arrived she had a wood cook stove and drew water from the bay by sliding a pail down a wire from the cliff-top house, then hauling it back up with a rope.[^1] In 1929 the group flash was replaced by a single white flash every 25 seconds.[^1]

Harry Hopkins kept the station from 1951 to 1982, and Brent Skippen followed him.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives Skippen's start as 1981 in its text and 1982 in its roster. The station was fully automated in 1988.[^1] Until a trail was cut through the woods, supplies came from ships anchored offshore. A gravel road from Dyers Bay was extended to the station in the early 1960s.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1971 the light moved to a skeleton tower erected near the old lighthouse, once electricity reached the station. The old lantern room was taken off so that it would not block the new light.[^1][^9] The wooden tower stopped being the light then.[^9]

The Friends of Cabot Head received a two-year lease for the station in 1993. By 1995 they had put a new lantern room on the old tower and opened the building to the public.[^1] Before the closure, the Friends ran a gift shop, a museum and seasonal programming under a sublease from the municipality. The municipality held the site under a lease from the federal Department of Fisheries and Oceans, and it never paid to maintain the site.[^6]

The site was closed while contamination was cleaned up. LighthouseFriends and the Lighthouse Directory give 2017 as the year the lighthouse closed. The Owen Sound Sun Times of October 2020 says it had been closed since 2016, and that the federal government had done $1.3 million in repairs, including cleanup of contaminated soil and lead paint.[^1][^6][^9]

By 2020 the municipality had voted to end its lease with Fisheries and Oceans. The Friends still had access to the site, but their chair said the group had no standing to spend money on repairs. A second-floor bedroom ceiling had collapsed earlier that year. The Friends' chair, Ron Wheeler, said some of the cleanup work had contributed to the leaks.[^6]

## Today

The light is a Canadian Coast Guard aid to navigation. The Coast Guard list gives it as a white flash of one second every ten seconds, with a focal height of 25.0 metres, a range of 10 nautical miles, and a skeleton tower of 13.9 metres.[^10] The Lighthouse Directory names the Coast Guard as owner and Ontario Parks as site manager.[^9]

The road is the lighthouse's only overland access. It runs about eight kilometres from Dyers Bay to Wingfield Basin, was built in the 1960s with federal and provincial help, and has been closed to vehicles since the 2019 and 2020 storms washed it out. The Friends and bird observatory officials still have very minimal vehicle access.[^8] In July 2026 the Northern Bruce Peninsula council voted 3 to 2 for the option it called the most financially responsible, a formal road closure with conversion to a multi-use trail. The reports describe this as preliminary approval, with consultation still to come.[^7][^8]

The Lighthouse Directory says the site is open to cyclists and walkers, but that the museum and tower have been closed since 2017.[^9] Do not enter the closed buildings. Whether the road will be open to walkers and cyclists depends on the council's decision.
