Muskegon South Pierhead Light stands at the inner end of the south pier, where the channel from Muskegon Lake meets Lake Michigan.[^2] Its circular metal tower has been lit since September 1903, and until the 1940s three keepers ran the station and its pier lights.[^2] A light has marked this harbour mouth since 1851, when a dwelling with a wooden tower went up on the south side of the river.[^2]

## The place

Muskegon was a lumber port. LighthouseFriends says the town was nicknamed "the Lumber Queen of the World," and that "its lumber helped rebuild Chicago after the great fire of 1871."[^2] The pier light marks the harbour entrance: LighthouseFriends places it "on the south side of the entrance to the channel leading to Lake Muskegon from Lake Michigan."[^2]

In 1838 Lieutenant James T. Homans, reporting to the Secretary of the Treasury, recommended a light near the river's mouth.[^2] LighthouseFriends quotes his report: "I selected a point, on the south side of the river," as the best location "in the event of an appropriation being made for a light there."[^2] Congress set aside $3,500 for a light there in 1849.[^2] In 1851 "a one-and-a-half-story, rubblestone dwelling, surmounted by a wooden tower, was built."[^2]

## Building the light

Congress allocated $8,000 on 2 March 1867 to rebuild the old light, which by then was "old, and no longer worth repairing." While the work went on, "The light was exhibited from a temporary structure." The Board "decided to keep the light on the south side of the river," and a dwelling topped by a square tower was finished in 1871, with a fourth-order lens.[^2]

The pier tower came next. In 1872 "a wooden pierhead tower was built at the outer end of the south pier, with an elevated walk connecting it to shore." It showed a fixed red light and had a fog bell.[^2] The Board's fog-signal list for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 has a bell at "Muskegon Pierhead," a signal "struck by machinery" that was in operation 366 hours that year.[^4] The list does not say which pier it was on. It is probably this one, since the wooden pier tower was fitted with a fog bell.[^2] By 1891 a post light had been placed on the end of the south pier to form a range with the wooden tower.[^2]

In 1894 the Board's report notes that materials were "delivered at the station" for "a conduit for running a lantern out to the end of the pier." The Board lists this work under the "Muskegon Pierhead range, front" light, the front light of the range.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the 580-foot conduit took the lantern to the front light, which was displayed on 25 July that year.[^2]

In 1899 a fog-signal building went up at the outer end of the pier.[^2] LighthouseFriends says "A ten-inch steam whistle and the new front range light commenced operation on September 15, 1899."[^2] In 1903 the present tower went up near the inner end of the pier. "This new light was established on September 27, 1903," and the fourth-order lens from the old land-based lighthouse went into it.[^2] The sixth-order light on the wooden pier tower was discontinued and its structure taken down.[^2]

## The keepers

The first keeper of the land-based light, Alexander Wilson, was hired in 1851.[^2] "Keeper Wilson looked after the light from 1851 until 1859."[^2] William Monroe took charge in September 1859 and died in November 1862, "at which time his wife Jemima was made keeper." She kept the light until her resignation in 1871.[^2] LighthouseFriends calls her "the only female lighthouse keeper to serve at Muskegon."[^2]

The USLHS roster for the station begins in 1895, when John Erikson came as first assistant.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he "was assigned to the station as its first assistant keeper in 1895 to help maintain the station's three lights."[^1][^2] Thomas Robinson was principal keeper from 1906 to 1928.[^1] Joseph Edlund was second assistant on the roster from 1909, and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1913 names him as second assistant keeper at the station.[^1][^5]

Ransom A. Jakubovsky was first assistant from 1917 to 1937 and then principal keeper until 1946.[^1] The roster lists Frank M. Bourissau as principal keeper from 1948 to 1961.[^1] After the third assistant's post was dropped in 1909, LighthouseFriends says, "three keepers would be responsible for running the station from them until the 1940s."[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Between 1913 and 1919 the Bulletin records several times that the station's keepers went to boats in trouble.[^5] In August 1913 "Thomas Robinson, and Joseph Edlund, keeper and second assistant keeper, respectively, of the Muskegon Pierhead Range Light Station," towed the disabled motor boat *Ida L.* to port with the lighthouse boat.[^5] On 26 September 1914 Robinson towed "the motor boat Bohemian Girl, which with two boys on board was disabled," to safety on Muskegon Lake.[^6]

On 31 October 1915 the keepers helped check "a fire near the light station which destroyed two cottages."[^7] On 6 October 1917 Robinson, then keeper of the light, "rendered assistance to the tug Ida M. Stevens, which became disabled" south of the harbour.[^8]

The wreck came early on 28 October 1919.[^9] The Bulletin calls the ship the steamer *Muskegon*, which tried to enter the harbour and struck the south breakwater.[^9] LighthouseFriends calls her the wooden side-wheel steamer *City of Muskegon*, which "struck the south pier while trying to make the harbor in a powerful storm, and in less than fifteen minutes it had practically gone to pieces."[^2][^9] The Bulletin says the assistant keeper on duty sounded the fog signal and then went to the wreck.[^9] LighthouseFriends says he helped the passengers and crew reach the pier with a flashlight, and then "directed them to the safety of the fog signal building."[^2]

The Department commended the two keepers for the rescue.[^10] The Bulletin names "Ransom A. Jakubovsky, assistant keeper, Muskegon Pierhead Light and Fog Signal Station," and LighthouseFriends says the Lighthouse Bureau "commended Keeper Robinson and Jakubovsky for assisting in the rescue and helping recover the dead."[^10][^2] LighthouseFriends gives the toll as "Fifteen of the thirty-seven passengers perished along with seven of the crew." The Bulletin gives no count.[^2][^9]

## After the keepers

In 1925 "a project was adopted for creating an arrowhead harbor entrance," which meant "removing the greater part of the existing parallel piers."[^2] The old land-based lighthouse was moved in 1929 and placed on a new foundation.[^2] A steel tower on the outer end of the south breakwater, a separate light, followed in 1930.[^2] The land-based lighthouse was "torn down in the early 1970s to make way for a new Coast Guard station."[^2]

In June 2008 the pier and breakwater lights, "deemed excess by the Coast Guard," were offered at no cost to eligible entities.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the Michigan Lighthouse Conservancy "was announced as the new owner" in June 2010.[^2] The pier light was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 14 February 2006.[^11]

## Today

The Michigan Lighthouse Conservancy owns the lights.[^2] LighthouseFriends says "Grounds open, tower open during tours," and that the conservancy opened the pier light for regular tours in 2015.[^2] The conservancy's visitor page lists summer tower tours, "Open Memorial Day to Labor Day," on Friday through Monday from 1 to 5 p.m., and says "Post-Labor Day Tours To Be Determined."[^12]

The pier and breakwater are public.[^2] LighthouseFriends says "The public can freely walk the south breakwater," and that "The Coast Guard and NOAA both maintain active facilities at the shoreward end of the south pier."[^2] The conservancy runs docent-led tower tours, which is how the tower is open to visitors.[^12] Check the schedule before going.
