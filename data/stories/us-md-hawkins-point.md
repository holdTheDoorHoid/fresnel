Hawkins Point Light stood on a screw-pile foundation off the south side of the Patapsco River, on the approach to Baltimore Harbor.[^1] It was the front light of the Brewerton Channel range from 1868.[^1][^2][^3] A wooden lighthouse on iron screw piles carried it.[^3] In 1924 the wooden superstructure was demolished: the archive records a steel pole as its replacement, while the Chesapeake Chapter says an automatic light was installed on the old foundation.[^1][^2][^3] That light stood until 2015.[^2]

## The place

The light was built to mark the Brewerton Channel into Baltimore.[^1][^2] Its partner, the rear light on Leading Point, stood about one and an eighth miles (1.8 km) away, bearing north-west from it.[^1] When a ship was on the true course, the three lights would be seen in line, one above the other, the Board wrote in 1868.[^1]

Congress had appropriated $30,000 on 28 July 1866 to build the two beacons.[^2]

In 1886 the owner of the shore tract known as Hawkins Point Farm asked for the light to be removed. He said it interfered with improvements he proposed, and he claimed that the site lay in a tract of submerged land granted to a former owner under a patent issued by the governor of Maryland in 1861.[^1][^2] A Board committee recommended offering him $2,500 for two acres and an easement. The offer was made and declined, and the matter went to condemnation.[^1]

The range needed a clear line.[^4][^5] The Board's report of 1891 records that in July 1891 the brush and trees were removed from a strip 65 feet (20 m) wide across Hawkins Point, to open the range.[^4] The Board's report for 1896 recorded that trees and underbrush which obscured the range between the two lights had been cut down; the Chesapeake Chapter and LighthouseFriends date that entry to 1897.[^5][^2][^1]

## Building the light

Construction began in 1867.[^2] The Board's report that year said the iron foundation of the most important structure was finished, but the land for the rear beacon had not yet been obtained and the Maryland legislature had not passed the act ceding jurisdiction.[^1]

The front light was finished in 1868 as a frame superstructure on screw piles in six feet (1.8 m) of water, with two lights at 28 and 70 feet (8.5 and 21 m) above ordinary tides.[^1][^2] The Chesapeake Chapter describes a rectangular screw-pile structure with a 34-foot (10 m) tower on top.[^2] Both lights were first shown on 1 November 1868.[^1][^2]

The building did not keep out the weather.[^2] An 1870 inspection found that the structure leaked badly, and that the stovepipe made the stove smoke so much that a fire could not be kept going.[^2] By 1878 the Board reported that the upper framework was decaying so fast it would soon need rebuilding, unless one of the two lights was discontinued.[^1][^2] In May 1879 the upper light was discontinued, and the station showed one fixed white light from then on.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive's roster has seventeen stints from 1868 to 1915: eight head keepers and nine first assistants.[^3] The archive's first head keeper, John McDonaugh, is listed for 1869.[^3] Samuel Waterworth held the post from 1869 to 1877, then Thomas J. Waterworth from 1877 to 1882, then James M. Waterworth from 1882 to 1890.[^3] Kate L. Waterworth is listed as head keeper for 1890 alone.[^3]

Five people named Waterworth appear on the roster. The archive does not say how they were related, and this story does not assume a family.[^3] Hellen Isabella Waterworth is listed as a first assistant from 1871 to 1876.[^3] Thomas J. and James M. Waterworth also appear as first assistants before they became head keepers.[^3]

James M. Waterworth was the keeper when the landowner brought an ejectment suit in 1889, and he is named in the case.[^2] The court held that private rights are subject to the public right of navigation.[^1][^2] The Chesapeake Chapter says the judge ordered the landowner to receive $3,500 in damages in 1890.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives a $3,500 award for the easement between the two lights, which it says was paid to the owner in 1891.[^1] The sources differ on what the $3,500 paid for and when.

After 1890 the archive lists Herman Dehl as head keeper from 1890 to 1896, then John Peterson from 1896 to 1915. A Mrs. John Peterson is listed for 1915, the year John Peterson's stint ends.[^3] On 16 April 1915 William Raabe, head keeper at Leading Point, became keeper of both lights.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In a July storm the smokestack was blown down. It was replaced and repaired. LighthouseFriends and the Chesapeake Chapter date this to 1888.[^1][^2]

On 7 March 1913 the British steamer Alum Chine, loaded with dynamite, exploded and sank in about twelve feet (3.7 m) of water not far from the light.[^6][^7][^2] The Chesapeake Chapter says the lighthouse was damaged.[^2] The Washington Herald of 8 March reported that the ship had been at anchor in the Patapsco near Hawkins Point, and gave fifty lives lost and 100 injured.[^6] The Atlanta Georgian of 7 March gave a subhead, "Thirty Stevedores Killed," which counts the stevedores employed for the loading, so it does not give the total the Herald printed.[^7]

## After the keepers

In April 1915 the light was changed from fixed white to an automatic flashing light, flashing every second.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1915 lists the Brewerton Channel front light among the lights changed from oil to acetylene.[^8]

In 1924 the lighthouse was demolished, and an automatic light was placed on the remaining screw-pile foundation. The archive records the disposition as a steel pole that year.[^2][^3]

In 2015 the light on the old foundation was removed. A light made of solar panels was built in the river to serve as the front light of the Brewerton Range.[^2]

## Today

The wooden lighthouse is gone. The old screw-pile foundation no longer carries a light, and the front light of the Brewerton Channel range is the solar-panel light in the river.[^2] The archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^3]
