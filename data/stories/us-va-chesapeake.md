Chesapeake Light is an offshore light about fourteen and a half miles east of Cape Henry, Virginia, marking the entrance to Chesapeake Bay. From 1933 to 1965 a lightship held the station. In 1965 a "Texas tower" on steel pilings replaced the ship, and the tower ceased to be an aid to navigation in 2016.[^2][^9]

## The place

LighthouseFriends says the first structure "to mark the vitally important entrance to Chesapeake Bay was actually a steel lightship," and that five lightships served the station before the best-known one, LV-116.[^2]

LV-116 was built at Charleston, South Carolina, in 1930.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that year that No. 116 "will be placed at Fenwick Island Shoal, Del."[^3] LighthouseFriends says the ship's first station was off Fenwick Island in Delaware, and that in 1933 it was moved to Virginia to mark the entrance to the bay.[^2] The Chesapeake Chapter's timeline records that in 1933 it was "moved to Chesapeake Light Station," 14.5 miles east of Virginia Beach.[^9] The sources differ on the start date: the US Lighthouse Society's place record gives 1930 as the year the station was established.[^1] This story treats 1933, when the ship came to this place, as the start of the lightship station here.

The ship was built of steel, with a crew of sixteen in mind, LighthouseFriends says, and it had four diesel generators of 75 kilowatts each.[^2] Its first master was "a Norwegian named Alexander Andersen," LighthouseFriends reports.[^2]

## Building the light

The Coast Guard replaced the ship with a tower in 1965. LighthouseFriends quotes the Coast Guard's reasons: "reasons of economy and stability have dictated the replacement, but improved habitability is a factor which cannot be ignored."[^2] The tower was prefabricated by the Tidewater Raymond Kiewit Company of Norfolk. Its pieces came by barge and were "lifted into place with the help of a gigantic crane set in a platform embedded in the seafloor."[^2] Its steel pilings were set 180 feet (55 m) into the seabed.[^2]

The main structure stands 75 feet (23 m) above the water, and the light is raised another 42 feet (13 m) on a tower at one corner of the deck.[^2] The Light List gives the light a characteristic of Fl (2)W 15s, a height of 117 feet (36 m), and a range of 19 nautical miles.[^10] LighthouseFriends gives the range as sixteen miles.[^2] The lamp was a xenon tube, which the Coast Guard described as "a highly advanced engineering prototype, utilizing a glass tube filled with xenon gas."[^2]

"Chesapeake Light Tower was commissioned on September 25, 1965," LighthouseFriends says, with the lightship circling the new tower before it sailed off to Delaware.[^2] The tower could be manned by a crew of six.[^2]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists 24 people at the station between 1966 and 1979. Three of them are principal lighthouse keepers, and 21 are Coast Guard personnel whose positions the archive does not give.[^1]

James L. Brantley is the first principal keeper on the list, at least from 1966 to 1967. Burnett R. Hippler followed, from 1967 to at least 1968, and Milton Herring appears at least from 1970 to 1971.[^1] Coast Guard names on the same list include Lee D. Hembree, William B. Register and Bobby G. Winstead from the first years, and David W. Edson, John W. Filk and William D. Hickman from 1970 to 1971.[^1]

Before the tower, the lightship crew had its own names in the Bulletin's personnel notices. In 1937, under "Transfers and promotions," the Bulletin named Robert E. Tillette as mate of Chesapeake Lightship No. 116,[^7] and Herbert L. Garrison as first assistant engineer of the same ship.[^8]

LighthouseFriends says that when the Coast Guard took over the lightships in 1939, the pay of their enlisted men fell from "$62.50 a month to the enlisted man's standard of $36.00," and many older hands left the service.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In March 1936 the master of a steamship wrote to the lighthouse superintendent at Norfolk about a snowstorm on 29 December 1935. His ship was approaching the lightship in heavy snow with a strong northeast wind, and it was hard to locate the lightship by its fog signal or by sight. The ship fixed its position by radio bearing, steered for the lightship, and then saw its light. The master wrote, "I believe that it is a good practice," to keep the light burning in fog, haze, snow or heavy rain, and the Bulletin printed his letter under the heading "Exhibition of lights in daytime."[^5]

The lightship's own master had closed a report printed in 1935 by saying that he had never seen a steamer pass the ship at "a distance or speed calculated to constitute a hazard to the lightship."[^4] The Bulletin printed his report among reports from sixteen lightship stations on the Atlantic coast.[^4]

The Bulletin dates the September 1936 storm: "From the 17th to the 19th of September the Atlantic coast from North Carolina to Massachusetts was swept by a hurricane of wide area and unusual severity."[^6] Later in the same report the Bulletin says, "Chesapeake Lightship No. 116 also felt the full force of the storm." The ship parted her moorings, got out her spare moorings, and rode out the gale under power. "Damage was sustained to the launch and whale boat," and the ship was relieved and taken into port for repairs before going back on station.[^6] Diamond Shoals Lightship No. 105 was in the same storm.[^6]

LighthouseFriends records that a spare anchor had to be used on at least three occasions when storms broke the main anchor's chain, and that in 1962 "a very large wave severely damaged the pilothouse" of the lightship.[^2] Chesapeake Light has no wreck or rescue in these sources.

During the Second World War the ship, renamed WLV-538, was painted gray and armed with two 20 mm guns, and it "served as examination and guard vessel" off the north end of the Cape Cod Canal before it returned to the station in 1945.[^2][^9]

## After the lightships

The lightship's next assignment after 1965 was Delaware Bay, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] It was decommissioned by the Coast Guard in January 1971 and transferred to the National Park Service that September. In 1982 it was loaned to the city of Baltimore and moved to the Inner Harbor.[^9] The Chesapeake Chapter says it was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1989.[^9]

Automation of the station began in 1980. "In 1980, the Coast Guard commenced an ambitious, three-pronged phase of automation at the station," LighthouseFriends says, and in the same campaign "the Coast Guard removed all personnel."[^2] Since 1999 instruments for NASA's Clouds and the Earth's Radiant Energy System (CERES) have been mounted on the tower. In October 2012 the tower was transferred from the Coast Guard to the Department of Energy so that it could continue to be used for climate research.[^2]

In 2004 the Coast Guard considered demolishing the tower, but "subsequent testing found it to be structurally sound," and it stayed an active aid to navigation.[^2] In 2016 the General Services Administration auctioned the tower. The online auction ended on 3 August 2016 "with a high bid of $215,000," and the tower "ceased being used as an aid to navigation in 2016," LighthouseFriends says.[^2]

## Today

The tower is privately owned. "The lighthouse is privately owned," LighthouseFriends says, and its tower is closed.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the tower is "primarily accessed by helicopter," with boat hoists and boarding ladders as the alternative, and that it is best seen by boat. This story does not suggest a visit.[^2]

The lightship LV-116 is moored in Baltimore, where the Chesapeake Chapter places her at "Pier 3, Inner Harbor, Baltimore, MD."[^9]
