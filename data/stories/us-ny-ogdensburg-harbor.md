Ogdensburg Harbor Light stands on Lighthouse Point, where the Oswegatchie River meets the St. Lawrence in Ogdensburg, New York.[^1] Its limestone house and tower were finished in 1871 to replace an 1834 light.[^1][^3] The light was discontinued in 1961.[^3] Fifty years after that, its private owner relit it in October 2011, after the Coast Guard certified it as a private aid to navigation.[^4]

## The place

Lighthouse Point was the site of Fort de la Presentation, which the French erected in 1749.[^1][^3] The nomination calls the fort "the first permanent settlement on New York's forbidding northern frontier."[^1] The French held it until 1759, when it was abandoned. The British renamed it Fort Oswegatchie and used it through the Revolution, and they did not give it up until 1796.[^1] The first American settlement followed that year, under Nathan Ford, an agent for the landowner Samuel Ogden.[^1]

The nomination lists the river's natural hazards as reefs and shoals, sandbanks, fog and shifting currents, and it says that "Shipwrecks were a constant threat to navigation on the St. Lawrence River."[^1] The Digest says the harbor was hard to enter, because of cross currents and a narrow, winding channel between shoals.[^4] In 1834 the Ford family conveyed roughly an acre and a half of the point to the federal government.[^1]

## Building the light

Later that year the first lighthouse went up: a dwelling with a lantern room, which the nomination says held ten lamps and reflectors.[^1] The Digest gives 1835 for the completion of the first lighthouse.[^4] In 1838 Lieut. Charles T. Platt of the U.S. Navy inspected it. He found the river "well above normal levels," which had "made the facility inaccessible by way of land," and he asked for money to raise the foundation.[^1][^3] A fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps and reflectors in 1855.[^3]

In July 1870 Congress appropriated $13,000.[^3] The purpose is given differently. The nomination says the money was for building a new light station, while the Digest and LighthouseFriends describe renovation and repairs.[^1][^3][^4] LighthouseFriends says work on a new lighthouse began the following month.[^3] The nomination says the design was not original: it was modelled on the Stony Point Lighthouse on Henderson Bay, on Lake Ontario, and the Ogdensburg tower rose about seven feet (2 m) higher.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the difference as six feet eight inches (2 m).[^3] The limestone came from the quarries at Kingston, Ontario.[^1] The new building was completed in June 1871.[^1]

In 1900 the tower was raised.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for that fiscal year says the tower was increased by 20 feet (6.1 m), and that a 270° lens replaced the 180° lens "heretofore in use."[^5] The nomination says the tower was then "raised to a height of 65 feet" (20 m), with a brick extension.[^1] These figures do not reconcile. LighthouseFriends gives the 1870 tower as 39 feet 8 inches (12 m), and 20 feet more would make about 60 feet (18 m).[^3][^4]

## The keepers

The Society's roster lists twelve principal keepers, from Amos Wells in 1835 to Harold M. Cook in 1941.[^2] No woman is named on it.[^2]

Lewis C. Young was among the first to tend the light after its completion in 1871.[^1] In the 1880 census he was "keeping lighthouse" at the station, with his wife, Louise, and their son, William.[^1]

Samuel A. Penfield kept the light for twenty-five years, by the roster's dates of 1888 to 1913.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he resigned in 1913, at seventy-seven, after twenty-five years at the station.[^3] The nomination says his tenancy "ended in 1911," but it also has him resigning three years after the 1910 census, which is 1913.[^1] The roster and LighthouseFriends agree on 1913, and that is the date used here.

Amherst E. Gunn came from the lighthouse depot at Buffalo and retired on 1 January 1932, after forty-eight years of government service.[^3] The nomination says that during his tenure "the light was converted from oil to acetylene," and that its characteristic changed from fixed to occulting.[^1] The Digest gives a different sequence: whale oil lamps were replaced by kerosene in 1875, and kerosene by electric lamps in 1924.[^4] The 1924 Bulletin lists the harbor's light among that year's changes, from fixed to occulting and from oil to acetylene.[^10] The Bulletin's index for 1924 to 1929 has the entry "Ogdensburg Harbor, light changed to occulting."[^7] The same year's fog-signal changes include an electric siren at the harbor.[^6]

Daniel D. Hill had served at the nearby Crossover Island Lighthouse. He was the last official keeper, from 1932 to 1933, and then took over duties at the Huron Harbor Lighthouse on Lake Erie in Ohio.[^1] Harold M. Cook served as the property's caretaker until 1941.[^1] The roster lists Cook as a principal keeper for the same years, so the sources do not agree on his job.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In January 1922 the ferry Ferdinand ran aground on rocks in heavy fog near the light.[^1][^8] The nomination says the passengers were "luckily unharmed," though the crew who stayed aboard faced blizzard conditions.[^1] The boat was later freed and returned to Prescott, Ontario, across the river.[^1][^8]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard accepted a bid for the house in 1946, according to a Mount Vernon newspaper that the nomination cites.[^1][^9] That account describes the house as unoccupied since 1942.[^1][^9] By the time later tenants moved in, the nomination says, deferred maintenance had taken a toll, and the house "no longer had running water."[^1]

The sources disagree on when federal use ended.[^1][^4][^9] The nomination has the caretaker until 1941, the 1946 account has the house empty from 1942, and the Digest says the government stopped using it in 1942.[^1][^4][^9] LighthouseFriends gives 1 June 1961 as the date the light was discontinued.[^3]

In 1964 the General Services Administration offered the property to the city of Ogdensburg.[^3] The city declined to buy it for $900, although the property was valued at $24,000, and a private bid of $2,400 was accepted.[^1][^3] In 2007 the city denied a permit to demolish the building, because the application lacked asbestos-removal plans and structural reviews.[^3]

The Digest dates the relighting of the 65-foot (20 m) tower to 8 October 2011, after the Coast Guard certified the light as a private aid to navigation.[^4] LighthouseFriends describes a private gathering on 7 October 2011, at which the light was relit at 8:20 p.m.[^3]

## Today

The building is privately owned, and it is not open to the public.[^2][^3] The light is a private aid to navigation. The Digest says it guides traffic on the St. Lawrence from April through November.[^4] The Digest puts its range at fifteen miles.[^4] The building's National Register nomination is catalogued as 16000202.[^1]
