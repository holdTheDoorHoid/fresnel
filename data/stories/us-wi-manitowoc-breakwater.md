Manitowoc Breakwater Light is a steel light tower at the outer end of the north breakwater in Manitowoc Harbor, on the Wisconsin shore of Lake Michigan.[^1][^2] A light and a fog signal have guarded the breakwater since November 1895, but the tower a visitor sees today was built in 1918, and its permanent light was first shown in 1919.[^1][^2]

## The place

The harbor sits where the Manitowoc River runs into the lake.[^2] Congress appropriated $5,000 for a lighthouse at Manitowoc on 3 March 1837, and the early shore light was discontinued in 1877.[^2] Work on parallel piers at the river mouth began in 1867, and a pierhead light at the end of the north pier began operating on 10 December 1873.[^2]

The breakwater came later. A 400-foot (122 m) breakwater off the north pier was proposed in 1890 to shelter the harbor.[^2] In 1892 the Lighthouse Board asked for a steam fog signal for the steamers that carried freight from Manitowoc to railroads on the lake's eastern shore when the Straits of Mackinac closed for the winter.[^2] Congress provided $5,500 on 2 March 1895, and the breakwater light and fog signal were built that year.[^2]

## Building the light

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1896 says a fog signal was "established on the outer breakwater November 30, 1895," in a frame building.[^3] The same report gives the whistle's establishment as 28 November in its text,[^4] while its list of new lights gives the light itself as "A lens-lantern light, November 11,1895."[^5] LighthouseFriends gives 28 November for the light and fog signal together.[^2] The sources do not agree on the day, and this story does not choose one.

In 1915 the Commissioner of Lighthouses described the building: a frame structure with corrugated iron covering and a very small wooden lantern, "not large enough to admit a man."[^6] Congress appropriated $21,000 on 12 June 1917 for a new steel fog-signal and light station.[^8][^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index has entries for the station's preliminary plans and for its completed foundation.[^10]

The steel tower went up on a concrete basement, and the old frame structure was removed from the breakwater in 1918.[^2] A temporary oil light was exhibited from the new tower on 15 November 1918.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report lists a further $9,000 for "improving the light and fog-signal station."[^13] The permanent light, from a fourth-order Fresnel lens 46 feet (14 m) above the breakwater, began on 13 December 1919, the same day a diaphone fog signal was established.[^2]

## The keepers

Joseph Napiezinski, head keeper from 1911 to 1941, is named in the Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for fiscal 1915, in a table headed "Saving of Life and Property," as "J. Napeizinski, keeper, Manitowoc Breakwater Light Station, Wis."[^1][^7] That report spells the name Napeizinski; the roster and LighthouseFriends' text use Napiezinski.[^1][^2][^7]

Ross F. Wright was first assistant from 1911 to 1932.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1912 reports that "Ross F. Wright, first assistant keeper of Manitowoc Light Station, Wis., has been commended" for helping a party in a disabled motor boat.[^9] LighthouseFriends describes a 1913 rescue by Wright of a party of boys and girls adrift on Lake Michigan. Its account does not say whether this is the same event as the 1912 commendation.[^2] The Bulletin's index also lists a rescue by Mrs. Ross F. Wright, "wife of 1st asst keeper Manitowoc."[^11] LighthouseFriends says she rescued two bathers from drowning in 1919.[^2]

The roster lists Joseph E. Warren as first assistant from 1906 to 1911, after two stints as second assistant between 1902 and 1906.[^1] The keepers' double dwelling of 1895 was later replaced by a house for three keepers, which LighthouseFriends says was "finally completed in 1935 at a cost of $27,329."[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In January 1908 a wind storm, which the Manitowoc Pilot called a "BIG WIND STORM" on a Sunday, struck the harbor.[^12] The paper said the seas were "threatening large damage to property and to the fog signal station on the breakwater."[^12] It named the man on duty as "Warren, assistant light tender who was on duty at the station," and said that "Late at night the tug Arctic reached the breakwater and Warren was brought to shore."[^12] The paper gives his first name as Edward.[^12] LighthouseFriends identifies the man as Joseph E. Warren, first assistant on the roster at that time.[^1][^2] It also prints an account from an unnamed local newspaper, which says he "remained at the station all day and late into the night."[^2]

According to LighthouseFriends, on Navy Day, 6 August 1919, an exhibition seaplane crashed into the harbor, and the keepers rescued both airmen, who were taken to hospital with minor injuries.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1921 the wooden light on the stub of the north pier was electrified. A storm destroyed it in 1938, and a skeletal tower replaced it.[^2] The keepers' old duplex was sold in 1938.[^2] The diaphone was removed in 1964 and replaced by an electronic foghorn.[^2] The station was automated in 1971.[^1]

In 2002 a fifth-order lens was removed and placed on display at the Wisconsin Maritime Museum, nearby. LighthouseFriends describes the lens as fourth-order when the permanent light began in 1919, and the sources do not explain the change.[^2] In 2009 the Coast Guard deemed the light excess and offered it at no cost to eligible entities, among them government agencies, non-profit corporations and educational organizations.[^2] When no one took it, the government put it up for auction on 18 October 2010. LighthouseFriends reports two bids: "the minimum bid of $25,000 on December 6," and then a bid of $30,000 hours later.[^2] The winning bidder was a New York businessman, Philip Carlucci.[^2] He spent roughly $325,000 to renovate and paint the tower before its hundredth birthday in 2018.[^2]

## Today

The light is privately owned. Its grounds are open, and its tower is closed.[^2] Since 2019 the Manitowoc Sunrise Rotary Club has offered tours of the lighthouse to schoolchildren and other groups, arranged with the owner.[^2] Visitors who want to see the lens can go to the Wisconsin Maritime Museum, where it is on display.[^2]
