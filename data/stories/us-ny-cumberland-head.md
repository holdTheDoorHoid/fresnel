Cumberland Head Light stands on the point of land called Cumberland Head, on Lake Champlain near Plattsburgh, in Clinton County, New York.[^2] It was built to guide ships into Plattsburgh harbor.[^1] The stone tower there today was first lit in 1868.[^1] The light had shone from an earlier tower on the point since 1838.[^1]

## The place

In 1814 the British launched an invasion from Canada down Lake Champlain.[^1] On the morning of 11 September 1814 their fleet sailed round Cumberland Head, planning to take Plattsburgh. The Americans won the naval battle that followed.[^1]

On 3 March 1836 Congress provided $5,000 for the light, and the federal government bought 8.62 acres from Luther Hager for $398.20 as the site.[^1]

## Building the light

Peter Comstock built the first tower in 1837: thirty feet (9 m) of native rubble limestone, for $3,325.[^1] Lieutenant Charles T. Platt visited soon after it was first lit in 1838. As LighthouseFriends quotes his report, the light was "lighted with 11 lamps and as many bright reflectors." Platt found the light hidden from ships coming from the north until they were within fifty rods (about 250 m) of the point, but he called the location "a judicious one."[^1]

In 1856 the tower received a new lantern room and a fourth-order Fresnel lens with a fixed white light.[^1]

By 1866 the Lighthouse Board wanted the tower rebuilt, because it was too short to be seen from the north.[^1] On 2 March 1867 Congress provided $18,000 to rebuild the light on "a more eligible site."[^1] That year the first tower was taken down. Oxen hauled blue limestone from the Fisk quarry on Isle la Motte across the frozen lake to build the new one.[^1]

The new station had a conical stone tower fifty feet (15 m) high, joined to a two-story house in the Victorian Gothic style. Its light was first shown on 1 November 1868.[^1] Trees grew up around it and hid the light in the direction of Plattsburgh. Congress appropriated $3,000 in 1871 to buy fifteen acres and clear the trees, and $250 in 1879 for a smaller parcel and its trees.[^1]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Service roster names twelve principal keepers, from Samuel Emery, listed for 1838 to 1849, to Hans C. Anderssen, whose listed years run to 1941.[^3]

William H. Tabberrah began his service here in 1871.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a Confederate's bullet had struck him in the hip at the Battle of Fair Oaks on 31 May 1862, and that he kept at his duties for more than thirty years.[^1] In the summer of 1902 Rear Admiral Winfield Scott Schley, a former lighthouse inspector, paid a visit to his old friend, Keeper Tabbarrah.[^1] In early June 1903 surgeons operated on him at the Plattsburgh barracks hospital. An infection followed, and he died on 18 October 1904.[^1]

His wife Emma was appointed keeper in his place on 19 December 1904. She served until 1919, with two of her daughters helping.[^1][^3] The roster lists her as principal keeper for those years.[^3] Charles R. Riley's name appears for 1919 alone.[^3] William H. Fido kept the light from 1919 until 1934.[^3]

The roster also gives Hans C. Anderssen for 1939 to 1941, five years after the light was discontinued. The sources do not explain that span.[^2][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1921, Keeper Fido is named for helping a disabled motor boat. He "rendered assistance to disabled motor boat, aided the occupants ashore, and provided repairs for boat."[^4]

## After the keepers

In 1934 a forty-foot (12 m) steel tower carrying a battery-operated light went up between the stone tower and the lake, and the stone light was discontinued.[^1][^2] The stone tower was then abandoned.[^1] In 1946 its grounds were subdivided and offered at auction.[^1]

In 1948 a World War II veterans' land program brought a family to the abandoned, vandalized station, after a fire had destroyed their own home.[^1] They renovated the keeper's quarters and maintained the lighthouse for nearly fifty years.[^1] In 1984 the Town of Plattsburgh had put a drawing of the lighthouse on its official seal.[^1] The property passed to another private owner in 1996.[^1] In March 2003 the Coast Guard brought the light back to the stone tower, with the owners' help.[^1]

## Today

The station is private property, and the Lighthouse Society's record marks it as not open to the public.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says its grounds, dwelling and tower are closed.[^1]
