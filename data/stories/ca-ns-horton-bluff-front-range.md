The Horton Bluff front range light stands on a bluff at the western mouth of the Avon River, near Avonport in the Annapolis Valley.[^7][^8] For most of its life it was the front light of a pair of range lights.[^1]

## The place

The Avon River runs about 64 km through farmland before it empties into Minas Basin.[^1] Gypsum from the district around Windsor, Falmouth and Hantsport was shipped out in quantity, and Lighthouse Friends gives 50,000 tons a year by 1818.[^1]

The tide here is large. The Chief Engineer's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 gives the range at Horton Bluff as 14.6 m (48 ft) at spring tides and 12.2 m (40 ft) at neap tides, taken from the Admiralty list.[^2]

The pilotage tariff for fiscal 1911-12 covered vessels taken to Windsor, Hantsport, Avonport, Horton Landing, Wolfville, Port Williams, Picket Wharf and Canning, and vessels anchored below or above Horton Bluff light.[^11]

In 1840 a petition to the Nova Scotia House of Assembly called for a lighthouse on either Partridge Island or Horton Bluff.[^1] The commissioners visited both sites in 1841 and preferred Partridge Island. A petition from thirty shipowners, captains and merchants trading on Minas Basin then killed that plan, and funds for a beacon at Horton Bluff were not allocated until 1850.[^1]

## Building the light

The light was finished in 1851, and Robert King became its first keeper.[^1] Before 1851, according to the Hantsport society, Captain James Lockhart had hung a lantern from a pole and tended it twice a day.[^9]

The first lantern was a glazed window with iron sashes, set out from the roof of the house.[^1] Lighthouse Friends quotes the 1874 Department report on the lantern: it had three glazed sides, one flat-wick lamp with an 18-inch reflector showed down the channel to the north, and a second lamp with a 12-inch reflector showed up the river to the east.[^1] Lighthouse Friends also quotes an 1857 report that described a small square building, "painted white, and used for lighthouse and dwelling."[^1]

The replica history dates the first lighthouse to 1855 and names Captain James Rathburn as its keeper.[^10] Lighthouse Friends, the Hantsport society, the Nova Scotia society and the Rowlett directory all give 1851.[^1][^9][^8][^7]

After the fire of 1883, a replacement went into operation on 23 October: a square wooden pyramidal tower with an attached dwelling.[^1] Sydney S. Crowe and James Smith of Truro built it for $1,400.[^1] The rear light of the pair stood on a skeleton tower 539 m upriver.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists Robert King as keeper from 1851 to 1857.[^1] In 1854 Enos Brown wrote a letter noting King's absence from the lighthouse. King was dismissed three years later, and James Lockhart took his place.[^1] The Hantsport history gives a different order: it has Lockhart carrying on until 1860, when Captain James Rathburn became keeper, and it says King was dismissed in 1857.[^9] The sources do not agree on those years.

Lighthouse Friends counts five members of the Rathburn family as keepers between 1860 and 1940.[^1] James Rathburn died in 1865.[^1][^9] His wife, Matilda, kept the light until she died on 27 May 1870, according to the Hantsport history's quotation of the Department's annual report.[^1][^9] Their son, Charles E. Rathburn, was appointed keeper on 26 October 1870 at $232 a year, rising to $250.[^9]

Charles's wife, Harriet, died in childbirth at the lighthouse in 1871.[^9][^1] Charles then married Susan Monroe, a widow, in 1872.[^9][^1] Charles died in 1879.[^1][^9]

The Department's 1915 keepers' table gives "Mrs. S. M. Rathburn" an appointment on 3 September 1879 and a salary of $380 a year.[^4] Lighthouse Friends lists Susan M. Rathburn as keeper from 1879 to 1921, and says her tenure of over forty years was the longest of the family.[^1] The Hantsport and replica histories say she kept the light for 27 years.[^9][^10] The replica history also gives 1921 as the year of her death.[^10] The Department's 1906 keepers' table names Mrs. S. M. Rathburn at Horton Bluff.[^3]

Lighthouse Friends says her son March Rathburn replaced her as keeper in 1921 and served until 1940.[^1] The Hantsport and replica histories give his full name as LeMarchant Rathburn, the youngest son of Charles and Susan, and say he became keeper in 1906.[^9][^10] The sources do not agree on that date.

After 1940 Albert "Chick" Starratt was keeper until 1946. The replica history says his wife, Eva, performed many of the keeper's duties.[^1][^10] Henry Harper followed, in the 1940s and 1950s.[^1][^9] Rodney Henshaw was keeper in 1960 and 1961.[^10] Jack Hughes was appointed temporary keeper when the new light began operation in November 1961.[^10] Bill Crosby kept the light from 1962 to 1987, with Harris Hartlin as his assistant.[^1][^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 20 April 1883, while Susan Rathburn was keeper, the first light burned down.[^1] The Hantsport history says one child died in the fire, and the replica history says it was a Rathburn child.[^9][^10]

Some time before 19 December 1921 the schooner *Charlotte Comeau* was burnt and wrecked off Horton Bluff.[^5][^6] A shipping notice in the New York Herald of 19 December 1921 gives the wreck's position on the western end of the channel, and says a green can buoy would be placed on the channel side.[^6] The Department's report for fiscal 1922-23 says the owners removed the wreck, and that the green can buoy marking it was removed in June 1922, with the removal of the obstruction.[^5]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends dates the present tower, a concrete tower built beside two keepers' dwellings, to 1961.[^1][^7] The Nova Scotia society's list gives 1984 as the year the present light was lit, and says it replaced the second tower of 1883.[^8] The sources disagree on that date.

Lighthouse Friends says the 1883 tower was pushed over the cliff and burned after seventy-eight years of service.[^1] The replica history says it was bulldozed over the cliff and burned.[^10]

Automation began in 1969, according to the replica history.[^10] The assistant keeper's post ended in 1979, and the dwelling was sold and removed from the site.[^10] In 1982 the range light was moved from its former Martin Road location, because a shift in the sandbar necessitated a change in the alignment of lights.[^10] Bill Crosby retired in 1987 and was not replaced, by which time the light was fully automated.[^10] The Nova Scotia society also dates automation to 1987.[^8] The horn was turned off and the fog sensor removed in 2001 and 2002.[^10]

Gypsum shipping on the Avon ended in 2011, when the Hantsport loading facility closed.[^1][^9] The light was deactivated on 12 July 2013.[^1][^9] Rowlett says the rear light was probably demolished when the range was discontinued that year.[^7]

The Nova Scotia society's list still shows the light as operational, with a fixed green characteristic from 1992, and does not record the 2013 deactivation.[^8] The replica history says the light was downgraded to "a range light" in 2005, and that the Canadian Government declared it surplus in 2010.[^10]

## Today

Lighthouse Friends says the Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and Rowlett names it as site manager.[^1][^7] The replica history says it was declared surplus in 2010, and no source here says who owns it now. The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1][^7] The site lies across railway tracks, 0.5 km (0.3 miles) down Lighthouse Road.[^1]

Raye Myles, who owns land on Bluff Road, began a replica of the 1883 light in 2008.[^1] The Hantsport society dates its completion to 2009, and Rowlett to 2011.[^9][^7] Rowlett says the replica site and tower are closed, on private property.[^7]
