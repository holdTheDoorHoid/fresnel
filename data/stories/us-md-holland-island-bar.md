Holland Island Bar Light was a six-sided wooden house on a screw-pile foundation in Chesapeake Bay, close to Holland Island.[^1][^2][^7] It was first lit on 25 November 1889.[^2] In 1960 the wooden house was taken down and an automated light went up on the same foundation.[^1][^2]

## The place

The light stood on a shoal at the entrance to Holland and Kedge Straits.[^2][^3] The Light-House Board's report for 1899 still gave the station's name as "Holland Island Bar, entrance to Holland and Kedge Straits, Maryland."[^3] The sources do not agree on its position relative to the island. The Chesapeake Chapter places the light west of Holland Island and north of Smith Island, while LighthouseFriends describes a shoal running south from the island.[^7][^2] When Congress made the appropriation in 1888, the Board's report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, called the place "this long and dangerous shoal" and said the light would be "an important guide for both sailing vessels and steamers."[^2]

Holland Island, the land beside the shoal, was a working community.[^8] The Baltimore Sun, reporting the island's last house in 2010, said that by the 1880s the island was home to more than 360 people, with most of the men oystering, crabbing and fishing.[^8] The house collapsed into the bay that October. The same report said many boaters had used it as a navigational aid.[^8]

General context, not a record of this station: in 1889 Arnold Burges Johnson's account of the Light-House Service gave screw-pile light-houses a heading of their own and said there were then "more than seventy light-houses," principally in the Southern waters.[^9]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $35,000 for the light in 1888.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the appropriation to 2 October.[^2] The Board's report for 1889, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, records a new working scow, 100 feet (30 m) long and 28 feet (9 m) wide, built at the Lazaretto depot for the job.[^2]

A work party reached the site on 20 September 1889. Work began the next day, but, in the words of the Board's 1890 report, "continued rough and stormy weather" meant little could be done until October.[^2] By the end of the first week in November the house was "substantially completed," and the light was first shown on 25 November.[^2]

The house was hexagonal, on a screw-pile foundation, and showed a fixed white light of the fourth order.[^2] The lens was a fourth-order Fresnel, installed in 1889.[^1] The archive gives the light as 42 feet (13 m) above the water.[^1] During thick and foggy weather a bell was struck by machinery at intervals of ten seconds.[^2] In 1899 the Board reported that "New model fourth-order lamps were supplied," and that a zinc lens protector was provided.[^3]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists, for the years from 1889 to 1957, eighteen principal keepers and twenty-six first assistants at the station. It adds three Coast Guard personnel from 1957, whose positions it does not give.[^1]

Edward W. Coulbourn was the first principal keeper on the list, from 1889 to 1892.[^1] Josiah J. Hayman was first assistant from 1889 to 1892, and the archive also lists him as principal keeper in 1892.[^1] Edward R. Somers was principal keeper from 1892 to 1909, seventeen years, the longest term on the list that has an end year.[^1] The list also has an Edgar T. Somers as first assistant from 1896 to 1900.[^1] The archive does not say whether the two men were related, and this story does not assume it.[^1]

Ulman Owens was principal keeper from 1924 to 1931.[^1] Ulysses Sherman Todd was first assistant from 1925 to 1931 and principal keeper from 1931 to 1938, so the archive has him at the station under both posts.[^1] William F. McDorman, first assistant from 1914 to 1918, appears in the commendation described below.[^1][^4] The last principal keeper on the list is Arnold W. White, from 1957, with no end year given.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources record ice at the station in the winter before April 1918.[^2][^4] On 2 April the Department commended W. F. McDorman, the station's assistant keeper, "for faithful performance of duty under hazardous and arduous conditions" caused by that winter's ice.[^4] LighthouseFriends gives the same episode in one line.[^2]

Ulman Owens, the head keeper, was found dead at the station in March 1931. The date and the cause of death are disputed.[^2][^5][^6] The Evening Star of Washington printed a dispatch from Crisfield on 20 March saying that federal officials had ordered his body exhumed while investigators weighed a theory that rum-runners had killed him.[^5] The dispatch says the body was "found Sunday afternoon bruised and almost nude on the floor of the light house," and that it had been buried on Wednesday at Dames Quarter.[^5] It describes a room where "furniture in it was overturned," a calendar on the wall "torn to shreds," and an alcohol lamp, the one used to light the big light, that was in place but had not been used.[^5] The paper also said the light had been out on Saturday night, which first aroused the curiosity of Crisfield residents and led to the discovery of the body.[^5] A Bureau of Investigation agent came to Crisfield aboard the lighthouse tender Violet.[^5]

The newspaper gives weekdays, not dates.[^5] LighthouseFriends gives 15 March 1931.[^2] That date was a Sunday, which fits the paper's account.[^2][^5] A later magazine article begins with the light going dark on the night of 11 March 1931. It says an autopsy found a fractured skull and an enlarged heart, and that the death was ruled natural.[^6] The sources do not agree on the date. At the inquest, the Evening Star reported, it was said that Owens had "suddenly been seized with an insane fit," and that the fit had caused the bruises on his body and the wrecking of his room.[^5] Owens's family at Dames Quarter and citizens of Crisfield scorned the belief, the paper said.[^5]

On 19 February 1957 three Navy Skyraiders from the Naval Air Station at Atlantic City attacked the light.[^2][^6] LighthouseFriends says the pilots had mistaken it for the hulk of the Hannibal.[^2] The magazine calls the Hannibal a sunken target hulk four miles away.[^6] Three practice rockets hit the light and managed to tear holes in its roof and walls.[^2] Four men were evacuated, LighthouseFriends says, but they returned the next day to begin repairs.[^2] The accounts differ on injuries. The Chesapeake Chapter says the two keepers were injured, and the magazine says no one was hurt.[^7][^6]

## After the keepers

In May 1960 the wooden superstructure was removed, and an automated light was established on the screw-pile foundation.[^2] The archive records the change as "Dismantled 1960 and Replaced with Automated Skeletal Tower."[^1] The Chesapeake Chapter describes the present light as a steel skeletal tower on the original foundation.[^7]

## Today

LighthouseFriends reports that a modern light is still shown from the screw-pile foundation.[^2] The archive's record, however, lists the station as not an active aid to navigation.[^1] The archive also lists it as not open to the public.[^1] The Chesapeake Chapter says it is accessible only by boat.[^7] Holland Island's last house, which many boaters had used as a navigational aid, collapsed into the bay in October 2010.[^8]
