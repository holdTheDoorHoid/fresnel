St. Johns River Light stood at the mouth of Florida's St. Johns River, at Mayport, from 1830. Its third tower, a brick one first lit in 1859, still stands inside Naval Station Mayport, behind a fence. The sources date the end of the light two ways: the nomination says it was abandoned on 15 January 1929, and LighthouseFriends says it was discontinued on 5 May 1931.[^1][^2]

## The place

In 1828 Congress appropriated $6,500 for a lighthouse at the mouth of the St. Johns River, then in the Territory of Florida, and another $7,500 followed in 1829.[^1] The nomination says the site "may have been on or near the exact landing of Jean Ribault in 1565."[^1]

The river's mouth was not steady. Without jetties, the nomination says, the channel was "forever changing," and the first tower was built too close to the water and undermined.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says that by 1835 the sea had undermined it. The tower was torn down and replaced by a second one roughly a mile inland.[^2]

Sand then became the trouble. The nomination says the dunes had shifted until the keeper's house was almost covered, and that the light could not be seen when ships came close to the beach.[^1] Congress appropriated $10,500 in 1834 to rebuild the tower, according to the nomination; LighthouseFriends gives $10,550 for the same appropriation.[^1][^2] Congress also appropriated $10,000 in 1852 to try to make the site secure, and $15,000 on 3 August 1854 for a new light and a beacon light that would serve as a range for the channel.[^1][^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says the site could not be secured until 1857, and that work on the brick tower began that winter.[^2] The nomination describes it as "curved red brick and mortar having 3 foot thick walls," with granite steps built into the outer wall.[^1] It was first exhibited on 1 January 1859, with a third-order Fresnel lens.[^2][^1]

The sources disagree about the height. LighthouseFriends says the tower stood 74 feet (22.6 m) tall when it was built. The nomination gives the present height as 81 feet (24.7 m) and says the tower was raised fifteen feet (4.6 m) in 1887.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends says the 1887 raising was proposed but never made, because settlements on the island were steadily clearing away the obstructions.[^2]

In 1889 the nomination says the Lighthouse Board recommended a first-order light on Fort George Island, which Congress never funded.[^1] The Board's own 1891 report puts the proposed light on Mount Cornelia, a hill on the north side of the river 62 feet (19 m) above sea level, and gives its cost as $175,000.[^4] Its later estimates list $125,000 for a "Mount Cornelia light-station."[^5] LighthouseFriends says the Board wanted a focal plane of 200 feet (61 m) there and asked for $125,000 a year between 1889 and 1894. The tower was never built.[^2]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends says John Warren, appointed in 1835, was dismissed in 1838 after it was learned he had performed his duty by deputy while on a military tour against the Seminole Indians.[^2] Henry Maxey left the station a little over a month after he was hired.[^2] Matthew H. Philips was recommended as "a sober, industrious and moral man."[^2][^9]

John Daniels was head keeper from 1858 to 1870.[^9] The nomination calls him "caretaker before the War," and LighthouseFriends gives the same years for his keepership.[^1][^2] His wife, Josephine Daniels, is on the roster as first assistant in 1860 and 1861 and again from 1868 to 1870. LighthouseFriends says she served as assistant keeper in both periods.[^9][^2]

Frances McDonald was first assistant from 1871 to 1886, and LighthouseFriends calls her Mrs. Frances McDonald.[^9][^2] Augustus W. Brown was first assistant in 1867 and 1868, then head keeper from 1879 to 1890.[^9] Amos B. Buford kept the light from 1892 to 1925.[^9] His 1915 report survives in the Bulletin.[^7] Charles L. Sisson is the last head keeper on the roster, to 1931.[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The earthquake that struck Charleston, South Carolina, on 31 August 1886 was felt at the station. LighthouseFriends quotes Augustus Brown's report of it: the motion continued about fifteen seconds, and five shocks were felt, the first moderate, the second and third light, and the fourth and fifth very light.[^2]

The Lighthouse Board's 1891 report says that the light, a third-order light, had for years drawn "complaints that it was" inefficient as a seacoast light.[^3][^11]

In 1914 the Service experimented with a thermostat at the station to warn of a broken mantle.[^6]

On 4 May 1915 Amos Buford wrote a report, printed in the Bulletin, about a night when the thermostat bell woke him. The bell rang at 3:15 a.m. and he "found the lamp enveloped in flames," caused by overheating. He put the light out, changed the vaporizer and the heating tube, and had the light relit.[^7][^2] LighthouseFriends says the bell woke him again at 4:45 a.m. on 22 December 1917. This time a clogged vaporizer nozzle had put the light out, and he had to use his substitute light.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1928 a semiautomatic radiobeacon was placed in operation at St. Johns River. The Bulletin calls it the first of its type in the United States.[^8]

The nomination says the light was abandoned on 15 January 1929 and replaced by a lightship anchored about eight miles offshore, manned by the Coast Guard.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives a different sequence. In 1929 the lightship Brunswick was renamed St. Johns and placed about seven miles offshore, the lighthouse ran for a few months after that, and it was discontinued on 5 May 1931.[^2]

In the 1940s fill from Ribault Bay was brought in to build the runway of the naval station, and twelve feet (3.7 m) of fill buried the lighthouse's original door.[^2]

A separate light, Saint Johns Light, dates from 1954. The National Register lists it under its own number, 100002224, for the period 1954 to 1967.[^10]

A plaque on the lighthouse records a 1983 preservation award from the City of Jacksonville and the Jacksonville Historic Landmarks Commission for the rehabilitation of the lighthouse. A second plaque, LighthouseFriends says, shows that it was placed on the National Register in 1982.[^2]

The Mayport Lighthouse Association, founded in the 1990s, got Navy permission in 2000 to use a nearby building as a visitor center and museum. LighthouseFriends notes that the naval base has been closed to the public since 9/11.[^2] A study put the cost of moving the tower to the Mayport riverfront at $657,600.[^2] In 2023 relocation was discussed again, and LighthouseFriends lists three obstacles: Navy permission, raising $2 million for the project, and finding a way to avoid disturbing burial grounds near the lighthouse.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by Naval Station Mayport.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open by permission of the Navy, and the tower is closed.[^2] The nomination says a ground-level entrance had been sealed to keep the public out of the tower.[^1]

Access to the base is typically limited to people with a military ID or a sponsor on the base.[^2] A view from outside the base is possible from the Town of Mayport, near the St. Johns River Ferry: from A1A, go east on Palmer Street until it ends, and the lighthouse can be seen through a chain-link fence.[^2] Do not try to enter the base to see the tower.
