Country Island light stands on the south side of Country Island, the outermost of three islands just offshore from Country Harbour on the northeast shore of Nova Scotia. A light has shown there since 1873.[^1][^3]

## The place

Country Harbour is a sixteen-kilometre inlet on the northeast shore of Nova Scotia. Three islands lie just offshore: Harbour Island, Goose Island and Country Island.[^1] Country Island was once called Green Island. Lighthouse Friends says that, as the outermost of the three, it suited a light.[^1]

In 1865 John H. Kendrick visited the lighthouses of Nova Scotia east of Halifax and reported that a light was needed on Green Island. He wrote that “a light on this Island is not only highly necessary for a coast light,” and that it would be “a real service for a harbour light.” Lighthouse Friends quotes him.[^1] The light served as a coast light and as a guide into Country Harbour and Fisherman’s Harbour.[^1]

## Building the light

The tower was built in 1872. Its lantern room was damaged so badly in transit from Montreal that it went back to the manufacturer, and a replacement was sent later that year. The light was not put into operation until 1 May 1873.[^1]

James McDonald’s contract for the lighthouse was $1,295, and the lantern room cost $373.[^1] The tower was square and 8.5 m (28 ft) tall, joined to a small keeper’s dwelling. Four lamps in 20-inch reflectors gave a fixed white light 15.5 m (51 ft) above the sea.[^1]

In 1877 a larger lantern room and new lighting apparatus were installed. In 1884 the stone foundation was rebuilt in cement mortar and the dwelling was extended.[^1]

In the fiscal year 1907–08 the Department had a set of lighthouses repainted with white and red stripes or bands, to make them more conspicuous when snow was on the ground. Country Harbour is among those it names.[^4]

In 1910 the light changed from fixed white to a flashing light: two flashes nine seconds apart, then twenty-seven seconds of darkness.[^1]

The original lighthouse was replaced in 1927, after 55 years, by a two-storey square dwelling with a lantern room on its hipped roof.[^1]

By 1961 the second lighthouse had deteriorated significantly. The recommendation was a new lighthouse and three separate dwellings, so that the station could be watched around the clock by keepers on eight-hour shifts.[^1] Building began in November 1963 and finished in 1965. The contract covered three dwellings, a combined fog-alarm and radio-beacon building, an octagonal concrete light tower, and the demolition of the old lighthouse.[^1] The heritage statement says the tower follows a standard lighthouse plan developed by the Department of Transport in 1954, and that it illustrates the station’s rebuilding during the 1960s.[^2]

## The keepers

William B. Foster became the first keeper, at an annual salary of $400.[^1] When he died in 1875, his son James A. Foster was appointed in his place.[^1]

James A. Foster died in 1883. Spencer H. Giffin replaced him and served on the island for about twenty years, to 1902.[^1] The Department’s keepers’ table in its report published in 1902 gives “Country Harbour, Green Island” against Giffin’s name, with an appointment date of “Sept. 18, 1883” and a salary of $400.[^5]

Lighthouse Friends lists the later keepers as Henry Burke (1902 to 1924), Raymond V. Luddington (1924 to 1929), George L. Crooks (1929 to 1945), Ernest Langille (1945 to 1949) and Vernon Zwicker (1949 to 1960).[^1] After 1960 it lists Don Crooks (1960), Keith Guptill (1960 to 1977), Vernon Zwicker again (1977 to 1981), Terry Edmunds (1981 to 1985) and Clyde M. Lahey, as the last keeper.[^1] Jim Guptill is listed as an assistant from 1964 to 1974.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 24 August 1873 a hurricane known as the Great Nova Scotia Cyclone caused heavy damage along the province’s Atlantic coast. Lighthouse Friends says it killed 500 people in the province.[^1]

On Country Island a small shed at the landing was destroyed. Fallen trees blocked the road from the landing to the lighthouse, and a large quantity of plaster inside the tower was shaken loose.[^1] The foundation walls were quickly repaired and the plaster renewed. The tower was braced with wire rope against further hurricanes.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1971 the diaphone’s diesel engines and compressors gave way to a 400-watt electric horn, and the station was cut to two keepers.[^1] A submarine cable brought commercial power to the island in 1982. Lighthouse Friends says this made automation possible.[^1]

In 1986 the diesel generators and the fog detector were moved inside the lighthouse. Clyde M. Lahey, the last keeper, left the island on 29 September 1986.[^1] In 2017 the island and its surrounding waters were set aside as a protected area for roseate terns.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light. Lighthouse Friends says the grounds and tower are closed, and that the light is best seen from the water.[^1]

The Coast Guard list gives one white flash every 20 seconds, with a flash of 2 seconds and an eclipse of 18 seconds. The focal height is 16.5 m (54 ft), and the tower is 13.7 m (45 ft) tall.[^3] The heritage statement values the tower as a symbol of an earlier period in the fishing industry.[^2]
