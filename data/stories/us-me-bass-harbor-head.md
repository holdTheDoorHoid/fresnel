Bass Harbor Head Light stands on a rock ledge at the southwest tip of Mount Desert Island, Maine, where it marks the mouth of Bass Harbor and the way into Blue Hill Bay.[^2][^3] Lit on 1 September 1858, its brick tower is short, but the ledge lifts the lamp about 56 feet (17 m) above the water.[^1][^12] New England Lighthouses says that throughout its history as a staffed light it had a single keeper and his family, and the light was automated in 1974.[^12][^2]

## The place

In 1855 W.B. Franklin, the lighthouse inspector for Maine and New Hampshire, reported that a harbor four miles west of Mount Desert harbor needed a light to help vessels enter it, and he asked for $5,000.[^1] The light was there to warn ships off the Bass Harbor Bar, which lies at the harbor's eastern mouth, and to mark the southeast side of the way into Blue Hill Bay.[^12]

LighthouseFriends dates Congress's appropriation to 18 August 1856.[^1] New England Lighthouses says only that Congress soon provided the money, and that title to the land was secured in 1857.[^12] The site was about three acres at the southwestern tip of the island, bought for $80 in June 1857.[^1] The Attorney General had not approved the title in time for work to start that year.[^1]

## Building the light

The tower was finished in 1858, and LighthouseFriends says the $16.65 left over from the appropriation went back to the surplus fund.[^1] The first lantern held a fifth-order Fresnel lens.[^1] The sources disagree on when a fourth-order lens took its place. New England Lighthouses gives late 1901, made in Paris by Henry-Lepaute.[^12] LighthouseFriends gives 1902.[^1] The National Park Service dates the present lantern to 1901.[^3]

The keeper's house was a frame building joined to the tower by a covered walk.[^1] It was raised ten inches (25 cm) in 1878, and its one-story ell was lengthened ten feet (3 m) in 1900.[^2][^9] LighthouseFriends says an office was added to the house that year.[^1]

The station first had a hand-rung fog bell.[^12] LighthouseFriends says a tall pyramidal bell tower went up in 1876.[^1] The National Park Service describes its bell house as dating from 1897, and the nomination form describes a brick bell house erected in 1876.[^2][^3] In 1897 a brick fog-signal building was put up closer to the cliff, and the Lighthouse Board's report for 1896 to 1897 records a brick fog-signal house built that year.[^1][^8] New England Lighthouses dates the new 4,000-pound bell to 1898.[^12] The Board's 1901 report records a Stevens striking machine installed in place of the Shipman oil engines that had run the fog signal.[^10]

Until a road reached the station, all supplies came by boat.[^1] The Board's report for 1891 to 1892 records some 93 feet (28 m) of wire fence built along the bluff from the tower to the road.[^5] A boathouse and a 100-foot (30 m) slip were built in 1894 according to the Board's report for that year, though LighthouseFriends gives 1893. A boat winch followed in 1895.[^6][^1][^7] The oil house went up in 1902 and the barn in 1905.[^2]

## The keepers

The USLHS Digital Archive's roster lists 24 entries for the station, from John Thurston in 1858 to Cecil Mareno in 1972, one line for each stint.[^4] Thurston lit the lamp and kept it until 1861. New England Lighthouses says he was paid $350 a year.[^12] Seven men kept the light in its first 36 years. The longest of those stints was Charles F. Chase's, from 1880 to 1890.[^4]

Willis Dolliver held the light longest. The USLHS roster and LighthouseFriends give his service as 1894 to 1921. New England Lighthouses gives 1894 to 1912.[^4][^1][^12] LighthouseFriends says he had begun in 1888 as second assistant keeper on Mount Desert Rock, and that he was seventy years old when he retired from Bass Harbor Head. In 2016 ceremonies at Mount Height Cemetery in Southwest Harbor honored keepers buried there, and a Lighthouse Service marker was placed on his grave.[^1]

Joseph M. Gray kept the light from 1921 to 1938. New England Lighthouses quotes Robert Thayer Sterling's 1935 book, which says Gray did "not forget to salute all passing craft."[^4][^12] The same site also quotes Edwin Valentine Mitchell's 1940 book, *Anchor to Windward*, on the station's "fog dog": a keeper's dog that took the fog bell's rope in its teeth to answer salutes from the Maine Seacoast Mission's boat, the Sunbeam. The page does not date the dog or name its keeper. "It is a big bell," Mitchell wrote, "but the dog sometimes succeeded in ringing it."[^12]

Electric operation came in 1949, according to New England Lighthouses.[^12] Eugene Coleman kept the light from 1950 to 1955. That source says his career had included time at Boon Island, Popham Beach, Cape Neddick and Cape Cod's Nauset Light.[^4][^12] Morton M. Dyer, whom the same source calls the last civilian keeper, arrived in 1955 and retired at 70 in 1957. Earlier he had served at White Island, New Hampshire, and at the Cuckolds and Deer Island Thorofare in Maine. When he left, a Coast Guard keeper and his family took the house.[^4][^12] Gerry Moores is named as the last Coast Guard keeper.[^12]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In July 1938 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin's article "Unusual Fog Conditions on Maine Coast" printed a table of the stations that reported the greatest amounts of fog during that month. Bass Harbor Head appears in it with 399 hours of fog in the month and a longest continuous run of 302 hours.[^11]

## After the keepers

Automation in 1974 ended the resident keepers, and the keeper's house went on housing Coast Guard personnel.[^2][^1][^12] Later the house became quarters for the commander of the Coast Guard group at Southwest Harbor. It was vacant from 2012, when the Coast Guard's Southwest Harbor station stopped using the keeper's dwelling as a residence.[^1] The station was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1988.[^3]

In September 2017 the Coast Guard offered to hand the lighthouse and about two and a half acres to the National Park Service.[^1] The National Park Service formally accepted the transfer on 8 July 2020, and the Coast Guard kept the right-of-way to run the automated light.[^3][^1] New England Lighthouses gives the year of transfer as 2018.[^12]

## Today

The National Park Service owns the station and manages it as part of Acadia National Park, one of three lights the park looks after.[^3] The grounds and viewing areas are open to the public. The lighthouse interior is not.[^3][^1] The park says about 180,000 people visit each year, which makes it the most visited place on the west side of Mount Desert Island.[^3] Public parking is limited to one small lot, and the park asks visitors to respect neighbors and private property owners.[^3]

The light is still an occulting red, shown through the fourth-order Fresnel lens in the lantern.[^3] Shortly after the formal transfer, the Coast Guard replaced the white lamp with a red LED and removed the acrylic chimney that had covered the lens.[^3]
