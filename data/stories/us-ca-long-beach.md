The Long Beach Light, known as the Robot Light, is a beacon at the end of a breakwater at the entrance to Long Beach Harbor, California. It went into service in 1949 and has never had a keeper.[^1][^3] The Coast Guard owns it and keeps it closed. Visitors see it from a boat or from the shore.[^1][^6]

## The place

The light stands at the east end of a long breakwater that runs across much of the Los Angeles and Long Beach harbors.[^1][^2]

Before the 1949 light there was a light on the same breakwater. A Lighthouse Service Bulletin from 1931 describes the "LONG BEACH BREAKWATER LIGHT" as fed by electric current through a submarine cable "approximately 10,000 feet long," laid along the shore side of the breakwater and "installed by the city of Long Beach."[^4]

Heavy seas were the trouble. The same Bulletin says heavy seas had several times washed away part of the breakwater and broken the cable. A temporary acetylene light was then placed in service "on the end of the breakwater."[^4] The Bulletin says the equipment was fitted with a "fifth order lens" and a flasher, and that a change from electric to acetylene took "within approximately five minutes."[^4]

LighthouseFriends says the 1949 light replaced an earlier skeletal tower. Once the new light was active, that tower was moved across the harbor entrance to mark the western side of the channel.[^1] The sources read here do not say when that tower went up, or whether it was the same light the 1931 Bulletin describes.

## Building the light

A 1951 magazine article by Douglas Nelson Rhodes, in *Ships & Sailing*, says the station was designed by two men of the Eleventh Coast Guard District headquarters at Long Beach. They were Commander Edward W. Laird, head of the district's civil engineering section, and Chief Electrician's Mate John T. Hendrix. A civilian employee, Chester Shey, also helped develop the idea.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says the Guy F. Atkinson Company built the concrete structure for $82,000, and that Hendrix, spelled Hendix there, designed and built most of the station's equipment.[^1] Both sources give about $200,000 for the station. LighthouseFriends says roughly $200,000 for the completed station, and the 1951 article describes a new $200,000 station.[^1][^2]

The tower is a white concrete house, barely 20 feet (6 m) square at the base, set on piles so that the seas can wash over the breakwater below without damaging it. The light is raised 50 feet (15 m) above mean low water.[^2]

LighthouseFriends describes the light as a rotating airway beacon on the roof, controlled by an astronomical clock that turned it on at dusk and off at sunrise. It says the beacon rotated continuously so that the sun's rays could not concentrate on and melt its lamps.[^1]

The 1951 article describes one flash of one second every four seconds, from a 1,000-watt bulb in double lenses, visible for 13 miles. The article does not say whether those miles are nautical.[^2] It describes the fog signal as a diaphragm horn with a three-second blast every 20 seconds. LighthouseFriends instead describes two gasoline compressors that sounded 17-inch Tyfon foghorns, so the two accounts describe the fog signal differently.[^1][^2]

LighthouseFriends says two generators took turns at twelve-hour intervals. The 1951 article says they relieved each other every four hours, and that fuel came automatically from a 1,500-gallon reservoir.[^1][^2] The two accounts disagree on the interval.

## The keepers

There were no keepers at the Robot Light. An undated, unsigned typed station sheet in the same US Lighthouse Society file as the 1951 article says that although the light is "a primary seacoast aid," it "has never been manned."[^3]

The 1951 article says the light was operated by remote control from a mainland station five miles away, and it names no keeper at that station.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the foghorns were started by radio from the keepers at Los Angeles Harbor Lighthouse, five miles away.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sea damaged the breakwater and cable more than once. The 1931 Bulletin records the cable break and the temporary acetylene light that followed.[^4]

The Bulletin's report on the southern California earthquake is dated 1 April 1933. Its keepers' reports, "beginning on March 10," say that prolonged shocks "of considerable severity were felt," but that no damage to the structures was caused at the stations that reported.[^5] The report describes the Los Angeles Harbor light and the points nearby, but it does not name the breakwater light.[^5]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends calls the light a forerunner of later automated aids to navigation.[^1]

The typed station sheet says the light "was modernized" in 1972 and 1973, with new monitoring equipment installed. Staff at the Port Safety Station in Los Angeles and Long Beach now monitor the light, fog signal and radio beacon.[^3]

By 2019 the Coast Guard's Aids to Navigation Team for Los Angeles and Long Beach maintained the light. A senior Coast Guard petty officer told the *Beachcomber* that solar power runs it, that it comes on at dusk and goes off by day, and that the fog signal "blasts every 30 seconds."[^6]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light. LighthouseFriends says the grounds and tower are closed.[^1] The *Beachcomber* reports that the public cannot visit the two breakwater beacons, the Robot Light and the Angel's Gate light in Los Angeles Harbor. Visitors can see the light from a boat or from shore, for example from Ocean Boulevard at Cherry Avenue.[^6] The US Lighthouse Society's place record lists the light as not open to the public.[^7]
