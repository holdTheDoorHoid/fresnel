Little Denier Island Light, in Newfoundland and Labrador, stands on a small island off the tip of the Eastport Peninsula in Bonavista Bay, near the fishing community of Salvage. Its 7.6 m (25 ft) cast-iron tower was lit on 1 October 1888. Its heritage statement says the light is one of about 26 prefabricated iron and steel lighthouses, "thought to be the only remaining examples of their type in Canada."[^1][^2]

## The place

Lighthouse Friends says that by 1676 six English masters had a summer operation at Salvage, with their families and servants. The population was 181 at the 1836 census and had risen to 453 by 1869.[^1] That year the community sent ten vessels to the spring seal hunt, and seventy-three of its men sailed to the Labrador fishery in the summer.[^1]

The heritage statement says the light illustrates navigational aids in Newfoundland "when it was a British colony with an economy almost totally oriented toward the sea."[^2] The sources name no particular ledge or wreck behind the choice of site. Lighthouse Friends says the light was to serve Bonavista Bay.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1882 Inspector of Lighthouses John T. Nevill noted that "a landing had been made on Little Denier Island just off Salvage." The money set aside was not enough for a powerful light, a landing and a road, so Nevill recommended a minor light on Goodman's Island instead, at the entrance to Barrow Harbour. "In the end, no lighthouse was built in the area as a result of the 1882 inspection trip."[^1]

Nevill returned in 1887 and compared King's Cove Head with Little Denier. "Little Denier Island was selected, and a road was constructed from the landing place to the summit, where a store was built."[^1] Lumber for the keeper's dwelling was to be cut in Bonavista Bay, and the iron tower was ordered from Victoria Works.[^1]

The heritage statement says the lighthouse was built in 1888 under Nevill's direction. The structure was made in Britain or assembled from parts ordered from a catalogue, but "there is increasing evidence to suggest that Nevill functioned as overall designer."[^2]

Construction materials were landed in late May 1888. Mr. Cornick was in charge of erecting the iron tower, Lighthouse Friends says; Chance Heritage Trust spells the name Conwick.[^1][^5] William Stowe was foreman of the rest of the work, and skilled men from St. John's worked alongside men from the locality. The job took fourteen weeks, and the light was put into operation on 1 October.[^1]

The lens was a revolving, six-sided lens that gave a white flash every thirty seconds, from a focal plane of 90.8 m (298 ft).[^1]

When Nevill visited in August 1889 he reported the station's condition as "far better than any other station has been found in at a first visit."[^1] He recommended a concrete floor in the cellar, a water closet, and grass seed around the tower to hold back the blowing turf.[^1]

## The keepers

Robert Oakley, who had been serving as assistant keeper on Cabot Island, was placed in charge of the light.[^1] The 1894 entries in McAlpine's directory, as transcribed online, list "OAKLEY Robert lighthouse keeper" and "OAKLEY Samuel asst lighthouse keeper" at Little Denier. The transcription does not say how the two men were related, and this history does not assume they were.[^4]

Henry Squires followed Oakley in 1902 and, Lighthouse Friends says, served into the 1930s.[^1] In 1931 the light became unattended. "Robert Dyke followed by a family of Hunters made trips to Little Denier Island about four times a year to change the gas cylinders."[^1] James Brown is the last keeper Lighthouse Friends names, with his service ending in the 1960s.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1920 the light was changed to acetylene gas, which cut the range "from nineteen miles to ten miles," as Lighthouse Friends gives it.[^1] In 1927 the characteristic changed to "seven seconds of white light followed by a three-second eclipse." Lighthouse Friends thinks the source was then probably kerosene.[^1] In 1931 it went back to acetylene and became unattended.[^1]

The light is now solar powered, Lighthouse Friends says, and flashes once every three seconds.[^1][^5] The Coast Guard's list gives a long flash every six seconds, "LFl W 6s", and marks the light seasonal.[^3] The two do not match, and the sources read here do not settle which is current.[^3]

The tower was classified a Recognized Federal Heritage Building in 1990.[^1]

## Today

"Transport Canada, the Canadian Coast Guard, is the custodian," according to the heritage statement.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the grounds and tower are closed.[^1] Its suggested route runs east on Route 310 from the Trans-Canada Highway for 28.4 km (17.8 mi) to Salvage. From the Southern Head Trail above the village, it says, "you can get a distant view of Little Denier Island and its lighthouse."[^1]
