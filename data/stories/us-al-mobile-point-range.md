Mobile Point Light, at the western tip of the Fort Morgan peninsula in Baldwin County, Alabama, marked the way into Mobile Bay from 1822 until it was discontinued in 1966.[^1][^2][^3] Four towers have stood at the point or on the fort: a brick tower of 1822, a temporary wooden tower of 1864, an iron tower of 1873, and a steel skeletal tower of 1963.[^1][^2] The iron tower is in storage in Alabama, awaiting restoration.[^1]

## The place

The United States took control of Mobile Bay during the War of 1812.[^1] Work on a fort at the bay's entrance began in 1819.[^1] The fort was completed in 1834 and named for Revolutionary War general Daniel Morgan.[^1]

The light was a landfall light, the first light a ship would look for on its way in from the sea.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says it could be seen about ten miles at sea, but shoals kept ships about that far offshore, which made it hard to detect.[^1] In 1854 a pair of range lights was set up near it, to help ships get through the outer shoals and into the bay.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $9,000 on 14 May 1820 for a light to mark the entrance to the bay.[^1] Winslow Lewis completed a forty-foot (12 m) tower on the point by June 1822.[^1] The lamps were first lit on 29 September 1822.[^1]

The sources disagree about that first tower. The Encyclopedia of Alabama describes a 55-foot (17 m) brick tower that had a fourth-order lens from the start.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives its height as forty feet (12 m).[^1] The archive records reflectors, later replaced by a fourth-order Fresnel lens installed in 1858.[^3]

In 1835 Congress appropriated $8,000 for a taller tower.[^1] Lewis claimed that a new tower was not necessary, and offered to fit up the old one for $3,000 instead.[^1] He placed seven lamps on each side of a revolving triangular frame, so the light flashed once a minute.[^1] Mariners were not told of the change, and several sailors confused the light with the Pensacola light, roughly forty-five miles to the east.[^1] Two histories of the lighthouse service record the same alteration as a case of a contractor changing a light without instructions.[^4][^5]

By 1858 a 150-foot (46 m) tower on Sand Island, three miles out, had taken over as the landfall light.[^1] The Encyclopedia of Alabama dates that tower to 1856.[^2] Mobile Point became a harbor light, with a fourth-order Fresnel lens showing a fixed light.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive's list of people for the station runs from 1829 to at least 1970, and gives names, roles and years only.[^3] Tenure at the head of the light was often short.[^3] Thomas Harris is listed from 1831 to 1834.[^3] Robert Bruce and John Buchanan are each listed for the single year 1854, and H. P. Wilson's stint began that year.[^3]

Soldiers sometimes tended the light.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says that when attracting a keeper to this remote site proved difficult, a soldier from the fort was assigned to tend the light.[^1] The archive lists the fort's commanding officer as keeper for at least 1835 to 1839.[^3] Keepers and soldiers did not always get along.[^1] Lighthouse Friends reports that after some military men proved unreliable, a civilian keeper was assigned.[^1]

In December 1887 the chief engineer at Mobile, A. N. Damrel, wrote to the fort's ordnance sergeant.[^1] Damrel had been officially told that the lightkeeper's wife was living with the sergeant in his quarters at Fort Morgan.[^1] He said he did not know whether the charge was true, and that such conduct would not be permitted.[^1]

The archive also lists Annie F. Smith as first assistant keeper for 1895 to 1896, and Lighthouse Friends gives the same years.[^3][^1] Neither says more about her work.[^3][^1]

In November 1909 the Montgomery Advertiser reported a letter to the chief of police from "Lighthouse Keeper Olsen of Mobile Point light."[^8] The letter said that no barge was lost during the storm. It also reported on a man who had left the construction work at Fort Morgan.[^8] The archive lists Hans J.G. Olsen as head keeper at this station from 1900 to 1922, which covers the date of the letter.[^3] The newspaper gives only the surname.[^8]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1924 to 1929 lists a "Williams, J.C." as keeper at Mobile Point when he was awarded the efficiency flag.[^10] The index gives no year.[^10] The archive lists James Chester Williams as keeper there from at least 1924, and Lighthouse Friends lists James C. Williams from the same year; they may be the same man.[^3][^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Once the war began, Confederate forces took the fort, and the light was put out of service.[^1]

The Civil War put the station in the path of the battle for Mobile Bay.[^1][^2] At dawn on 5 August 1864 Admiral David Farragut's fleet approached the entrance to the bay.[^1] The Encyclopedia of Alabama says the lighthouse was a favorite target of Union gunners and suffered significant damage in the battle.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the tower was struck several times during the fight for the fort, and that one side of it was badly damaged.[^1] Lighthouse Friends places the tower beside the fort's hot-shot furnace, where cannonballs were heated until they were white hot and then fired at wooden ships.[^1]

Near the end of the war the old brick tower was demolished, and a temporary wooden tower on the fort carried a sixth-order lens.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates that tower's first light to 2 September 1864.[^1] The Encyclopedia of Alabama places the temporary tower after the war.[^2]

Lighthouse Friends says the light was put out for a time during the Spanish-American War, when military rules forbade ships to enter or leave the bay at night.[^1]

A storm report printed in the Montgomery Advertiser on 3 October 1906 lists "Mobile Point beacon, front range gone."[^7] That is a beacon, not the lighthouse, and the report says no more about it.[^7]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's account of the September 1926 hurricane says the storm was a short distance west of Mobile on the 21st, moving very slowly.[^9] The Bulletin lists Mobile Point Lighthouse, at Fort Morgan, among the stations that were also slightly damaged.[^9]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the light changed from red to white in 1892.[^1] A brick oil house was built at the station in June 1897.[^1][^6]

The Lighthouse Service itself became part of the Coast Guard in 1939.[^2]

In 1963 a steel skeletal tower was erected at the point, and a contractor was hired to take down the old iron tower.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says he put its pieces in storage rather than sell them as scrap.[^1] The archive gives 1966 as the year the light was discontinued.[^3] The Encyclopedia of Alabama dates a federal replacement tower to 1966.[^2] The Encyclopedia of Alabama also dates the iron tower to 1872, a year earlier than the 1873 that Lighthouse Friends and the archive give.[^2][^1][^3]

In 1977 a Pensacola firm owned by the contractor who had taken the tower down put the lighthouse on the market.[^1] The contractor's son offered to donate the 20,000-pound tower to the State of Alabama.[^1] The tower was given to the Alabama Historical Commission on the understanding that it would be restored and returned to Fort Morgan.[^1] That happened in 1991, after $64,150 in federal funding was secured.[^1]

In September 2003 the iron tower was removed from the fort for restoration by Robinson Iron, an Alabama firm.[^1] The Encyclopedia of Alabama dates the dismantling to 2004.[^2] In December 2014 the Alabama Historical Commission announced $400,000 from a Baldwin County lease tax for the work.[^1] As of 2024, Lighthouse Friends reports, no work had begun.[^1]

## Today

The iron tower is still in storage in Alabama.[^1] A steel skeletal tower from 1963 stands at the point.[^1] The grounds include the steel tower, the site of the earlier lights and the old keeper's dwelling, and visitors can walk around them.[^1]

The fort's visitor center displays the fourth-order Fresnel lens from the Mobile Point light.[^1][^2]

Lighthouse Friends gives the fort's hours as 8:00 a.m. to 5:00 daily, and the museum's as 9:00 a.m. to 4:30 p.m.[^1] It says the site is closed on Thanksgiving, Christmas and New Year's Day, and that a small admission fee is charged.[^1] The fort is reached by ferry from Dauphin Island, or by Highway 180 west from the eastern side of the bay.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the tower is closed and that the Alabama Historical Commission owns the lighthouse.[^1] Its most recent update is from 2024, so check the hours before going.[^1]
