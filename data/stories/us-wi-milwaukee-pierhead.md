A red steel tower of four storeys stands at the seaward end of the north pier at the mouth of the Milwaukee River, where ships have entered Milwaukee's harbour from Lake Michigan through a channel cut begun in 1857. The present tower dates from 1906, but a light has marked this pier end since 1872, and in the 1870s the keepers who tended it lived on a separate pier out in the lake.

## The place

Milwaukee's river mouth was a poor gateway for lake shipping. The National Register nomination says the river's natural channel was curved rather than straight and that a sand bar lay off its mouth, which limited the size of vessels that could come and go.[^1] Local commercial interests first asked the federal government for help in 1843, and Congress approved money in 1852.[^1] The work, directed by the Army Corps of Engineers and begun in 1857, cut a new channel half a mile north of the river's natural mouth, called the Straight Cut, and walled it with wooden piers on its north and south sides.[^1]

A small light had been put on the cut in the 1850s. LighthouseFriends describes the first, a 20-foot wooden tower with a sixth-order lens, and quotes an undated Light-House Board complaint about a pier light of that era: the pier stood only six feet above the water, so in heavy gales the sea broke over it and the keeper could not get to the light, and the Board's proposed remedy was a detached pier large enough for a lighthouse with a dwelling.[^2]

## Building the light

The Corps lengthened both piers seaward in 1868.[^2] Work then began on a new tower at the outer end of the north pier. LighthouseFriends dates the start to August 1872 and the first lighting to 30 October 1872: a 32-foot (10 m) tower, open framework at the base and enclosed at the top, showing a fixed red light through a sixth-order Fresnel lens.[^2] The National Register nomination, which is mostly concerned with the later steel tower, describes the 1872 light as a one-story skeletal tower carrying an enclosed service room and lantern.[^1] A 600-foot (180 m) elevated walkway, finished that November, linked the tower to the keeper's dwelling on a separate crib pier.[^2] In March 1876 a fourth-order lens replaced the sixth-order one, according to the same source.[^2]

The dwelling pier was the older part of the station. One keeper living there looked after both the pier light and the neighbouring North Cut Beacon until that beacon was discontinued in July 1875.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's keeper list for the station starts in 1857, years before the 1872 tower, which suggests it covers the whole pier station; the archive does not say so.[^3]

Pier work in 1905 changed everything. In preparation for rebuilding the outer end of the north pier, the old framework tower was moved 600 feet (180 m) west, and the fog signal was shifted to the offshore breakwater until it returned to the north pier on 20 June 1906, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The lighthouse that stands now was built in 1906 as the front light of a two-light range; the rear light was a taller skeleton tower several hundred feet west on the same pier. A ship lined the two up one above the other to find the channel.[^1] The front tower is a tapering cone of riveted steel plates in eight courses, about 42 feet (13 m) tall, with a ten-sided lantern. It was first fitted with a cylindrical lantern and a fourth-order Fresnel lens showing a fixed red light.[^1]

## The keepers

The roster lists fourteen principal keepers between 1857 and 1928.[^3] The longest by far was William M. Kynaston, listed as head keeper from 1871 to 1900.[^3] An Emma Kynaston is listed as first assistant from 1878 to 1888; the roster does not say how she was related to William.[^3] Before him the list shows Charles Bloom (1867 to 1870) followed by Jane Bloom (1870 to 1871), also listed as a head keeper; again, the list gives no relationship.[^3]

From 1907 the roster also lists a second assistant, making three keepers at the station.[^3] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 the Light-House Board altered the dwelling so that its four-room second story became the assistant keeper's quarters, entered through the old tower, where a hardwood staircase and front door replaced the iron stairs and the old iron lantern was taken down.[^4] William W. Foster served as first assistant from 1902 to 1907, Charlie E. Tesnow as assistant from 1909 to 1916 and head keeper from 1925 to 1928, and George Rogan as first assistant from 1918 to 1928.[^3] Oscar H. Knudsen was head keeper from 1911 to 1918.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sea reached this station repeatedly. The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 records that damage done "by the violence of the sea on the night of January 1, 1891" was repaired.[^5] Its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 says the elevated walk was damaged by a storm of 25 October, which, given the fiscal year, was October 1898, and was repaired. The crib that carried the keeper's dwelling was repaired, and the crib's sea wall was rebuilt a foot higher.[^4]

The worst-documented night is the gale at the end of November 1905, while the fog signal was temporarily on the breakwater. A news item in a Wisconsin weekly said the lighthouse and fog-signal building at the end of the breakwater pier "w'as battered by the high seas" and that Assistant Keeper William Foster was rescued with difficulty by the life-saving crew.[^6] LighthouseFriends, quoting Foster's own account, says a wave broke in the east wall of the building, carried him outdoors, and was stopped from sweeping him away only because he held onto a beam; the fog signal was out of action from 27 to 30 November.[^2]

In July 1917 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded that Keeper Oscar H. Knudsen and First Assistant Keeper Herman Schleif towed a disabled motor boat, the *Dewey*, into harbour.[^7] On 23 October that year a storm severely damaged the station, chiefly the stone-filled timber crib that supported the dwelling and the fog-signal building; the Bulletin put temporary repairs at about $2,500 and permanent repairs at about $9,500.[^8] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for 1921 lists $14,567 spent at Milwaukee Pierhead on rebuilding the superstructure and a new water-supply pipe, though it does not say that the money followed the storm.[^9]

## After the keepers

The breakwater changed the pier light's job. The Milwaukee Breakwater Light went into service in 1926, 0.7 mile (1.1 km) east, and took the pier tower's cylindrical lantern and fourth-order lens; a ten-sided lantern and a fifth-order lens went on the pier tower in their place.[^1] The nomination also says the rear range light was discontinued and dismantled in 1926, yet elsewhere says the range was discontinued in the early 1930s; LighthouseFriends puts the transfer in 1927.[^1][^2] The tower was repainted red in the early 1930s.[^1] In 1928 the three pier keepers joined the keeper of North Point Lighthouse as four keepers of a single Milwaukee Harbor station, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2]

The Coast Guard took over lighthouse keeping in 1939, and the pier light was looked after by the breakwater light's resident keepers until it was automated in 1966.[^1] In 1994 the breakwater light's fourth-order lens, the one first installed in the pier tower in 1906, was replaced with a modern beacon; the nomination says it is now on display at the Wisconsin Maritime Museum in Manitowoc.[^1] The pier tower's fifth-order lens was later removed: the nomination says its whereabouts are unknown, while LighthouseFriends says it was removed in 2005.[^1][^2]

## Today

The tower is still an active aid to navigation, showing a flashing red light every four seconds from a modern acrylic optic powered by batteries charged by a solar array on its gallery.[^1] As of the 2012 nomination the Coast Guard owned the lighthouse and the Army Corps of Engineers owned the pier, which was rebuilt in the 1970s and now reaches only about 150 feet (46 m) from shore.[^1] The tower is not open to visitors. The pier and tower can be seen from land at the southeast corner of Henry W. Maier Festival Park.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that the light was made available as excess property in May 2014 under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, and that the Corps then decided not to issue leases of its piers to private parties; the sources read do not say who owns it now.[^2]
