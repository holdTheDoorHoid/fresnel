Juniper Island Light is a cast-iron lighthouse on a steep, privately owned island in Lake Champlain, a little over three miles from the Burlington waterfront in Vermont.[^2] It was first lit in 1826 and was the first lighthouse built on the lake.[^1][^3] Its iron tower of 1846 still stands, but the navigation light has been carried on a steel frame since 1954.[^2][^3]

## The place

Congress allocated funds on 3 March 1825 for the first lighthouse on Lake Champlain, to help vessels reach the growing port at Burlington.[^2] Vermont bought the island for $200 from Moses Catlin and then ceded it to the federal government.[^2] Lake Champlain became a major route for iron ore and lumber.[^2] New England Lighthouses says the lake's location helped Burlington develop into the second-largest lumber port in the United States.[^3]

## Building the light

The first tower was a conical brick tower, 30 feet (9.1 m) tall, lit by ten lamps with reflectors. LighthouseFriends says it was lit for the first time on 11 May 1826.[^2][^3]

By 1838 the tower was in poor repair. LighthouseFriends quotes Lieutenant Charles T. Platt's inspection report of that year, which said the light-house was "much out of repair," and which asked for $500 to fix it.[^2]

In 1846 a new tower replaced the brick one. It was four cast-iron rings stacked into a 30-foot (9.1 m) cylinder, topped by a sixteen-pane lantern room.[^1][^2][^3]

A fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps and reflectors. The US Lighthouse Society's archive dates the lens to 1853, and LighthouseFriends dates it to 1856. The two sources disagree, and this story does not choose between them.[^1][^2]

Congress allocated $3,500 on 3 March 1871 for a landing and boathouse.[^2] In 1874 the fog-bell frame was rebuilt, using part of a $1,000 appropriation.[^2] The fog bell could be rung by a clock mechanism every fifteen seconds when needed.[^2]

In 1891 Congress appropriated $2,500 for "establishing a lighthouse and buoy depot" on the island.[^4] The island served as the Lake Champlain depot for buoys, boats and supplies.[^6] In 1893 its old wharf was remodeled and extended by 80 feet (24 m), and a combined boathouse and buoy shed was built at the inshore end.[^5][^6] By 1899 the depot had "a commodious wharf for landing at the light-station," which the report says it served.[^7]

The Commissioner's reports for the fiscal years ended 30 June 1918 and 30 June 1920 each sought money to rebuild the Juniper Island wharf so that it could accommodate a tender on the lake.[^8][^9] The requests were part of larger estimates for acetylene lights and a gasoline tender.[^8][^9] New England Lighthouses says a new concrete dock was erected in 1922.[^3]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists 18 principal-keeper entries for the station, which name 17 different men (Malachi Corning appears twice), from Frederick A. Sawyer (1826 to 1829) to Robert A. Brown (1943 to at least 1944). The list also includes first assistants, a laborer and some contractors.[^1]

The keepers also worked the depot. An 1894 report says that the depot "is kept by the keepers of the light-station on the island."[^6]

Frederick Sawyer served about three years.[^1][^2] He was replaced by Malachi Corning, a veteran of the War of 1812, who kept the light for twelve years.[^2] In April 1841 the Secretary of the Treasury wrote that Corning's "services as keeper of the Light House on Juniper Island are no longer needed by the Government."[^2] LighthouseFriends suggests that politics was behind the dismissal. Corning was a Democrat, and the recent election had put a Whig, William Henry Harrison, in the presidency.[^2]

William Bush kept the light for 23 years, from 1862 to 1885.[^1]

Frank Guyette was first assistant from 1893 to 1894 and head keeper from 1894 to 1905.[^1] The roster gives Herbert L. Perry as head keeper from 1905 to 1935. New England Lighthouses gives Perry's service on the island as 1903 to 1937, and the two sources do not agree.[^1][^3]

Peter J. Kirby was an assistant from 1901 to 1903.[^1] In late January 1903 he made the three-mile trek over the ice to Burlington to get medicine for his sick wife and child. He had planned to go back the next day, but the ice went out and he fell ill in town.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he died of tuberculosis on Valentine's Day 1903.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In December 1918 a northwest gale carried away 30 feet (9.1 m) of the old dock.[^2][^3] Herbert Perry reported the damage to the district inspector in a single-sentence letter. The letter also listed eight large sinkers and two spar buoys washed off the dock.[^2]

The Boston Sunday Globe of 3 January 1904, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, reported that Frank Guyette and his wife had set out that afternoon to row four miles to Burlington.[^2] Drifting ice cakes nearly capsized the boat, and the cold froze Guyette's hands and feet, so Mrs. Guyette took the oars and got them to shore.[^2] The article also said the light was not operated in winter, and feared the livestock on the island would perish, since no one was left there to care for it.[^2]

## After the keepers

The old iron lighthouse gave way in 1954 to an automatic light on a sixty-foot (18.3 m) steel skeleton tower.[^2] New England Lighthouses says the steel tower carried the light from 1954 to 2003, and that a new skeleton tower was installed in 2003.[^3] The US Lighthouse Society's archive records the discontinuance in 1954 and the lens as removed.[^1]

The island was sold at auction in 1956.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends says the highest of eight bids was $7,000, and New England Lighthouses says the buyer was a lawyer and state senator.[^2][^3]

According to LighthouseFriends, a fire started by an uncontrolled camper badly damaged the keeper's house in 1962. New England Lighthouses says careless campers destroyed it.[^2][^3] The iron tower and the wooden bell house survived.[^2]

Work to rebuild the keeper's house began in the fall of 2001, using bricks salvaged from the ruins.[^3]

## Today

Juniper Island is private property.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists it as not open to the public.[^1] The sources give no public access, and visitors should not land on the island.

LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard retained about 4,000 square feet (roughly 370 m²) around the light tower, with a right of entry to tend the automated light.[^2] The navigation light now stands on the 2003 steel tower.[^3] New England Lighthouses says trees around the property make the old lighthouse hard to see from the water.[^3]
