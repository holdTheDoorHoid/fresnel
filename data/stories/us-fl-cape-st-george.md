Cape St. George Light stood near the entrance to Apalachicola Bay, at the southern point of Little St. George Island on Florida's Panhandle coast. Its brick tower, built in 1852, fell into the Gulf of Mexico in October 2005 after erosion had left its base exposed. A rebuilt tower, put together from brick recovered from the water, now stands near the middle of St. George Island, where its tower is normally open for climbing.[^2][^11][^12]

## The place

Little St. George Island lies at the west end of St. George Island.[^2] The first tower on the cape went into service in 1833 near the entrance to Apalachicola Bay.[^2] The Florida Lighthouse Association places that first light near West Pass.[^11] A county history says it was hard for ships coming from the east to see.[^12]

## Building the light

The sources give different dates for the rebuilding, and they do not explain the difference. The National Register nomination says the first tower was rebuilt in 1847.[^2] The Florida Lighthouse Association and the county history both put a second tower at Cape St. George in 1848.[^11][^12]

The nomination points to the foundations, which on the earlier towers were only 18 inches (46 cm) deep.[^2] The tower of 1852 is brick, and the nomination gives its height as 70 feet (21 m). The nomination describes its walls as laid in hydraulic cement, "a mixture of lime and clay which hardens under water." The tower stands on pilings, which the nomination ties to the need for a deeper and stronger foundation.[^2]

For the lantern, the nomination names a contractor, "Mr. E. Bowden," who used some materials from the former tower. The nomination also says the old lanterns were reglazed with French plate glass.[^2] The Florida Lighthouse Association's page gives the builder's name as Edward Bowden.[^11] The two sources do not say outright that they name the same man. That page gives the lens as a third-order fixed Fresnel by Sautter et Cie, dated 1857.[^11] The archive lists the original optic as a third-order Fresnel and says the current optic was removed.[^1]

Later work on the reservation appears in the Light-House Board's reports. For the year ended 30 June 1895, the Board reported that a first-order oil house was completed in December 1894 under contract.[^4] For the year ended 30 June 1900, it reported that materials had been furnished for the keeper to build a storm house.[^5] In the year ended 30 June 1901, 32 feet (10 m) of wharf, 8 feet (2.4 m) wide, was built at the inner end of the old wharf.[^6]

The archive dates the keeper's quarters to 1878.[^1] The nomination dates a single-story wood-frame dwelling to 1880. It also lists an undated two-story brick dwelling and several small outbuildings erected between 1890 and 1939.[^2] The sources do not say whether the 1878 and 1880 dates refer to the same dwelling.

## The keepers

The archive lists eleven principal keepers between 1834 and 1854, beginning with John W. Smith, who appears for 1834 alone.[^1] Several men served a year or two. John Garrison is listed for 1835 alone.[^1]

Braddock Williams kept the light in two separate terms, 1854 to 1861 and 1868 to 1874. After his second term, the archive lists him as first assistant from 1874 to 1879.[^1] James A. Williams was first assistant from 1857 to 1861, and he was principal keeper from 1875 to 1893.[^1] The archive lists five men named Williams at this station. It does not say whether they were related, and this story does not assume they were.[^1]

Edward G. Porter was principal keeper from 1893 to 1913, the longest principal stint on the archive's list.[^1] The archive lists no principal keeper between 1861 and 1866, and it gives no explanation for the gap.[^1]

Thomas Clairmon Brooks was principal keeper from 1921 to 1924.[^1] On 31 July 1922, when an auxiliary schooner grounded close to the station, he gave assistance, according to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin.[^7] On 28 January 1923 he helped a British schooner that had too few supplies to finish its passage, according to the Bulletin.[^8] A Bulletin entry for 26 July 1926 names a "Clairmon Brooks" as keeper of the Apalachicola Range Light, who went into the water and rescued a boy from drowning.[^9] The Bulletin does not link that entry to this station, and this story does not assume the two men are the same.

The archive lists Walter Andrew Roberts as first assistant from 1926 to 1932 and as principal keeper from 1932 to 1938. It lists a Walter A. Roberts, Sr., in the 1890s and 1910s as well.[^1] The archive does not say whether these are the same man. In late December 1927 the Bulletin named W. A. Roberts, jr., an assistant keeper at Cape Saint George, as helping with a grounding near the lighthouse dock.[^10] The Bulletin does not say whether he was related to either man on the archive's list.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 17 February 1852 the New-York Daily Tribune printed a notice dated Apalachicola, 1 January 1852, listing lights that had been "entirely swept away during a severe gale." Cape St. George was among them.[^3] The notice said the lights probably would not be replaced within nine months or a year, and it gave the cape's latitude as 29 degrees 35 minutes north.[^3] The present tower was built in 1852.[^2]

During the Civil War, according to the nomination, rebels damaged the tower. It was repaired and went on serving as a light.[^2]

None of the three Bulletin items for this station describes a wreck on the island. Each describes help given to a vessel.[^7][^8][^10]

## After the keepers

The archive's last principal keeper is listed to 1946, and its last first assistant to 1949.[^1] The nomination says that in the late 1930s or early 1940s the light was changed from kerosene to dry-cell batteries.[^2]

A 1961 fire destroyed the buildings on the reservation.[^2] The archive also notes that an oil shack was buried in sand.[^1]

The Coast Guard discontinued the light in 1994.[^1][^11][^13] In 1995 Hurricane Opal tilted the tower. The Associated Press reported the tilt was fixed in 2002, but the Florida Lighthouse Association's page says the tower was righted in the summer of 2000 with a new foundation.[^13][^11] The AP, citing the St. George Lighthouse Association, said erosion then removed sand from around the base and left the tower about 20 feet (6 m) from shore.[^13]

The tower was found toppled on Friday, 21 October 2005.[^13][^11][^12] The pieces were salvaged and moved to a storage site on the mainland. The St. George Lighthouse Association acquired the 1852 plans from the National Archives, and volunteers cleaned the old mortar from the salvaged brick.[^12]

## Today

The light visitors see now stands at the middle of St. George Island. The Florida Lighthouse Association's page says the new tower opened in 2008 and was lit in 2009. A county history gives 1 December 2008 as the opening day.[^11][^12] The Coast Guard restored it as a private aid to navigation on 1 November 2009.[^11] The sources read do not say who owns the rebuilt light.

A replica of the keeper's house holds a museum and a gift shop.[^12] The Florida Lighthouse Association's page says the tower is normally open for climbing, and it advises checking the St. George Lighthouse Association for hours, full-moon climbs and weather closures.[^11]

The historic site on Little St. George Island was reachable only by boat, according to the Florida Lighthouse Association's page.[^11]
