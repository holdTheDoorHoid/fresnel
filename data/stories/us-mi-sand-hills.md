Sand Hills Light stands on the Lake Superior shore of Michigan's Keweenaw Peninsula, a yellow-brick light station that the Lighthouse Board wanted for more than a quarter century before it was built. The Board first asked Congress for the money in 1892. The light was first lit in 1919, and it has been dark as an aid to navigation since 1954.[^2][^3][^13]

## The place

Eagle River, to the east, was a copper-shipping harbor that needed a light to bring the vessels in. When the mine closed in 1873 the docks sat rotting and the river mouth silted up, and by 1890 the lighthouse tender was almost the only vessel that went in.[^13]

The Board's proposal was to move the light. Its report in the early 1890s said the light should go "from Eagle River, where it is of no use, to Sand Hills, where it would be of great service."[^2] The sources place the Sand Hills at different distances from Eagle River. Terry Pepper's history puts them "some twelve miles to the west" and says eastbound ships turned there, with the Sawtooth Reef just offshore.[^13] A Commissioner's estimate of 1915 described the station as "about 4 miles west of Eagle River."[^4] The Commissioner's 1919 account of the finished station puts it roughly three miles west of the town.[^8]

## Building the light

Congress authorized the move in 1893, but it did not pay for it. The Board reported that the move "was authorized by the act approved February 15, 1893, but no appropriation therefor has yet been made."[^3] The Board's estimate rose from $20,000 to $25,000 in 1899 and to $38,000 in 1902. Congress still voted no money, and the Board dropped the project in 1907. The Eagle River light was decommissioned in 1908.[^13]

Lighthouse work passed to the Commissioner of Lighthouses in 1910.[^13] Terry Pepper reports that the Commissioner's 1911 report counted ten vessels stranded on Sawtooth Reef in the previous decade, with losses above a million dollars, and asked for $75,000 for a coast light and fog signal at Sand Hills.[^13] LighthouseFriends dates the $75,000 request to 1912 and gives the ten-vessel count to the Lake Carriers' Association's report for 1910.[^14] The 1915 estimate used the same figure: "For establishing a light station and fog signal at or near Sand Hills, Mich., $75,000."[^4] The 1916 report noted that an act of 28 August 1916 "authorised this work, but no appropriation was made therefor."[^5]

Money came in 1917. "The act of June 12, 1917, appropriated $70,000 for the establishment of a light station and fog signal at or near Sand Hills, Mich."[^6] The site was surveyed and bought in the following season: "During the past season the site has been surveyed and purchased."[^7] Terry Pepper gives the reservation as 47 acres.[^13]

The Commissioner's account says work was actively commenced in the late fall of 1917.[^8] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin listed the preliminary plans as approved that year, and in 1918 it recorded that the acquisition of the site was under way.[^10][^11] A temporary light and an electric siren were established on 14 September 1917, and a gas and bell buoy on the reef was taken away.[^14] The Bulletin reported in 1919 that "Temporary light and fog signal are in commission and the work will be completed in June next."[^12] Terry Pepper dates the temporary lens-lantern light and electric fog signal to the spring of 1918.[^13] The sources do not agree on that date.

The permanent station was a combined tower and dwelling of yellow brick, with the tower in the middle and three apartments around it.[^13] The Commissioner's 1919 description calls the brick "buff-colored, pressed brick."[^16] The tower carried a fourth-order Fresnel lens made in Paris by Henry-Lepaute.[^13] The fog signal ran from a separate building with two diaphone signals; Terry Pepper says the trumpets projected the sound seven miles across the lake.[^13] The Commissioner reported that "the fog signal was placed in operation on May 15, 1919, and the main light on June 19, 1919," and that "All work at the station was carried out by hired labor."[^8] The Commissioner's report gives 18 June for the first showing in its entry on page 49 and 19 June in its account of the work on page 87, and Terry Pepper gives 18 June.[^8][^13] The Bulletin also recorded that "All work at this station practically completed."[^12] The Commissioner counted $68,714.69 spent by 30 June 1919 from the appropriation.[^8]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists fourteen keepers' stints at Sand Hills from 1917 to 1940: two principal keepers, seven first assistants and five second assistants.[^1]

Arthur De Champlain was the first head keeper. He was moved to take charge of the temporary light in 1917, and the archive lists him as principal keeper until 1919. Before Sand Hills he had most recently served as first assistant at Passage Island.[^14][^1] LighthouseFriends says he became ill in Bay City, Michigan, and died there on 6 June 1919.[^14] The sources disagree on who was keeper when the main light was first shown. Terry Pepper says William R. Bennetts "exhibited the light for the first time on June 18" of 1919,[^13] but LighthouseFriends says Bennetts was brought in to replace De Champlain in September 1919.[^14] The archive lists Bennetts as principal keeper from 1919.[^1] Terry Pepper also says that when the station closed as a manned light Bennetts retired, "after having served as the station's sole head keeper throughout all of its manned operation," which does not fit the archive's listing of De Champlain as principal keeper until 1919.[^13][^1]

Under them were assistants whose names the archive gives year by year: Emery D. Means, who served from 1919 to at least 1921; John E. Whelan, who served from at least 1922 to 1934; Richard E. Dissett; and Edward J. Tormala, who served from 1935 to at least 1940.[^1] The second assistants included Levi M. Whipple (1922 to 1924), Charles M. Miles (1924 to at least 1930), and Samuel A. Anderson (1936 to 1939).[^1] Terry Pepper says the station "remained manned for only twenty years."[^13]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The reef off Sand Hills was the reason for the light.[^13] Terry Pepper names no individual strandings, only the total from the 1911 report that ten vessels stranded there over the decade.[^13] The Commissioner described the station as built "to mark a dangerous locality on the south shore" of Lake Superior.[^9]

The one dated marker in the sources is the gas and bell buoy placed on the reef on 10 June 1916, which was taken away when the temporary light went up in September 1917.[^14] LighthouseFriends names one wreck on the same reef, the new steamer W. C. Moreland, which ran aground there on 18 October 1910 in smoky conditions from a forest fire. The Lake Carriers' Association cited her loss in 1911 as an argument for a light at Sand Hills.[^14] No wreck is named as happening at the station itself.

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over the aids to navigation in 1939. Terry Pepper says the Service "automated the light with the installation of an acetylene lamp with automatic sun valve," and that this eliminated "the costs associated with the station's three keepers."[^13] The archive gives 1939 as the automation year.[^1]

The station stood empty until 1942, when the Coast Guard used it as a wartime training facility for "over 200 trainees at a time," closing it again in 1943.[^13] The light was discontinued in 1954. Terry Pepper gives the reason as "improvements in weather forecasting and the adoption of radar."[^13][^1]

The station was sold in 1958. The Lighthouse Society's 2022 news item says the buyer was H. Donald Bliss, who "used it as a summer home for a few years."[^15] Terry Pepper says it was offered at public auction, and LighthouseFriends says the General Services Administration took sealed bids.[^13][^14] The archive records the sale in 1958.[^1] The property was offered for sale again in 1961, and a Detroit photographer and artist, Bill Frabotta, bought it; he converted the fog-signal building into a summer cottage.[^13] Between 1992 and 1995 he remodeled the station as the Sand Hills Lighthouse Inn, which opened in 1995.[^13][^15]

## Today

Sand Hills is privately owned, not open to the public, and closed for restoration.[^1] Bud Cole, a historical preservationist who owns the Eagle River Lighthouse, bought the station in 2019 and closed the property for a multi-year restoration.[^15][^14] The Society's 2022 news item also says Cole bought the property in 2014 and offered it as a vacation rental; the same item and LighthouseFriends give 2019 for the purchase.[^15][^14] He has also bought more than 1,000 acres around the light.[^14] The station is private land. Anyone who wants to see it should ask the owner, not walk onto the property.
