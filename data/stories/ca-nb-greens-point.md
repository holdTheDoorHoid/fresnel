Green's Point Light stands on a point at Letete, in Charlotte County, New Brunswick, where Letete Passage runs between the point and Macs Island into Passamaquoddy Bay.[^1] A steam fog alarm was set up there in 1879, and a lighthouse was added beside it in 1903. The light has been inactive since 1999, but the fog signal still sounds.[^8][^1]

## The place

Letete Passage was a thoroughfare for vessels entering Passamaquoddy Bay on their way to the wharves at St. Andrews, St. Stephen and St. George.[^1] LighthouseFriends suggests that the name comes from the French "la tête", "the head", for the headlands that bracket the passage.[^1] LighthouseFriends also says the point was then known on navigational charts as Mascabin Point, a name the report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1903 uses.[^1][^4]

The hazard the station answered was fog. In 1878 Parliament voted $1,500 for a steam-powered fog alarm at Letete Passage.[^1]

## Building the light

The next year, 1879, D. W. Clark built the fog-alarm building and a water reservoir for $2,774. A fog trumpet from the Neptune Fog Horn Company of Quebec, at $1,750, went into service the same year.[^1] In thick weather, fog and snowstorms, it sounded a seven-second blast every thirty seconds. LighthouseFriends estimates that each blast could be heard about 9 km (six miles) with the wind, and 3 to 5 km (two or three miles) against it.[^1]

The dwelling for the fog-alarm engineer went to Angus Fisher of St. George for $644.[^1] The boiler burned roughly ninety tons of soft coal a year.[^1] The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 says a new boiler had been set up at the fog-alarm station; the old one was an upright boiler and the new one a locomotive boiler.[^2]

The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 said: "A lighthouse will be built to be maintained at the existing fog alarm station at this point."[^3] The lighthouse was put in operation on 1 September 1903.[^4][^1] It stood a few feet north-west of the fog building, on a site 3.4 m (11 ft) above high water. Its octagonal wooden tower, painted white with a red iron lantern, was 13 m (43 ft) high to the top of the ventilator.[^4] The fixed white light came from a seventh-order dioptric lens, 15 m (48 ft) above high water. The report says it should be visible 19 km (12 miles) from all points of approach by water.[^4] C. L. McKeen of St. Andrews built the tower under contract for $800.[^4] LighthouseFriends says it was put together at St. Andrews in eight sections and carried to the point by scow.[^4][^1]

By 1906 the sea had cut into the peninsula that joined the point to the mainland, and LighthouseFriends says the Department needed a protection work.[^1] The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1906 describes it: square hemlock timbers, 73 m (240 ft) long, 2.7 m (9 ft) wide and 2.1 m (7 ft) high, well bolted and filled with ballast stone.[^5] The report for the nine months ended 31 March 1907 says three rooms were added to the keeper's dwelling.[^6]

In the fall of 1920 a new fog-alarm building went up to house a Class B diaphone powered by oil engines. LighthouseFriends says it was completed the following year at a cost of $7,338.[^1]

## The keepers

Samuel Craig was the first engineer assigned to the fog alarm, in 1879. Later that year he was moved to Head Harbour, and Neil Seely took his place.[^1] In 1882 Seely went to Head Harbour to replace Craig, and George Helms was assigned to the fog whistle at Letete Passage.[^1]

George Helms, a retired sea captain, kept the station until he died in the winter of 1907.[^1]

Sidney S. Dines grew up in the Helms household. LighthouseFriends says that when Sidney's mother died in 1869, his father, a sailor, could not take the three-year-old along on his voyages, and George and Elizabeth Helms adopted him.[^1] From 1883, when he was seventeen, Sidney was listed as an assistant engineer at Letete at $150 a year.[^1] The keeper list in the report for the nine months ended 31 March 1907 has an entry for a Dines at the Letete fog alarm.[^7]

When Helms died, Sidney Dines became head keeper.[^1] He retired in 1936, after 53 years at the station, 29 of them as head keeper.[^1] On 7 November 1936 Sidney Dines received a medal for long and faithful service from King George VI, presented by the Charlotte County Member of Parliament.[^1]

His son Prescott Dines took over in 1936 and kept the station until 1963.[^1] Electricity reached nearby Letete in 1937, but Prescott Dines recalled that it did not reach the station until 1952, because a neighbour wanted $10 a pole to let the line cross his land.[^1]

In the late 1980s Green's Point also monitored several other lights in the area, and four full-time keepers were needed to cover them all.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Prescott Dines later recalled a winter gale that blew his mother out of a one-horse sleigh onto rocks six feet below. A large buffalo robe she was wrapped in kept her from harm, as LighthouseFriends quotes his recollections.[^1]

In 1962 a gale put Prescott down on all fours among the rocks, and he retired the following year.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1995 news of a planned de-staffing leaked out. A few concerned citizens formed the Green's Point Light Association to keep the station as an educational and recreational place.[^1] In December 1996 the association signed an agreement with the Coast Guard to use the property. In July 1997 a marine and coastal interpretive centre opened in the keepers' dwelling.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the light had been fully automated since 1996. It was officially decommissioned on 8 September 1999, though the fog alarm stayed in use.[^1][^8]

Ownership of the lighthouse and station passed to the association in July 2008.[^1][^8] Since 2002 the former Coast Guard monitoring station on the property has been rented as a vacation rental to raise money for upkeep.[^1]

## Today

The Green's Point Light Association runs the site.[^8][^1] The museum in the keepers' dwelling is open in summer, from mid-June into early September, and the tower is closed.[^8][^1]

The fog signal is a white hexagonal tower with a red top, 4.0 m (13 ft) high. It gives a 3-second blast and is then silent for 27 seconds.[^9] The Coast Guard's List of Lights gives an entry at Letete Passage for the fog signal only; it lists no light there.[^9]

Russ Rowlett's Lighthouse Directory gives the octagonal wooden tower, painted white with red trim, as 12 m (39 ft) high.[^8] The 1903 report gives 13 m (43 ft) to the top of the ventilator. The two figures differ, and Rowlett's entry does not say what it measures to.[^8][^4]
