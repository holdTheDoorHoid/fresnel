Fox Point Lighthouse stands on the south side of the entrance to St. Anthony Harbour, on the east coast of Newfoundland's Great Northern Peninsula.[^1] It is a working Canadian Coast Guard light station.[^1] Its white tower with red bands flashes every ten seconds, and the station is also known as Fishing Point.[^4]

## The place

St. Anthony Harbour is a long, deep and sheltered anchorage.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Jacques Cartier reportedly named it in 1534, though French fishermen had likely used it before him.[^1] From the harbour the French fished the Straits of Belle Isle and the Grey Islands, until 1904, when they lost their fishing rights on the Newfoundland shore.[^1] LighthouseFriends also says the community really started to prosper after Wilfred Grenfell arrived in 1899 and chose St. Anthony as the site of a hospital for Labrador and northern Newfoundland.[^1]

The sources say little about what the point was lit for beyond the harbour entrance. LighthouseFriends places the point on the southern side of the entrance and also calls it the eastern side; the Town of St. Anthony puts the light at the south entrance.[^1][^2]

## Building the light

The first light on the point was a harbour light, set there in 1906.[^1][^2][^3] It was a lens lantern hoisted on an open white framework, and it ran through the navigation season, which typically lasted from June to the end of December.[^1]

In 1912 an iron tower replaced it.[^1][^2][^3] LighthouseFriends describes a circular iron tower painted with red and white vertical stripes.[^1] The Town of St. Anthony and the historical marker describe a cast-iron light tower, exhibiting a white occulting light 20.5 metres (67 feet) above the sea and visible for 10 miles.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends gives the occulting pattern as equal periods of 2.5 seconds light and 2.5 seconds dark.[^1]

A flat-roofed wooden fog alarm building went up in 1936.[^1][^2] The sources do not agree on when a new fog alarm followed. The marker dates it to 1952, and the Town's history to 1958.[^2][^3]

A bungalow, a landing and a storehouse were provided in 1955, and a second dwelling was built in the early 1960s.[^1][^2]

In 1960 the iron tower gave way to a combined light tower and fog alarm building, with a new aluminium lantern.[^2][^3] The acetylene light was converted to electricity generated on the station.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends gives the new light's focal plane as 26.8 metres (88 feet).[^1] The accounts of this 1960 building differ. LighthouseFriends describes a single-storey building with a square tower rising above one corner, while Rowlett describes a square skeletal tower covered with white siding.[^1][^6]

The old iron tower was lowered down over the cliff face.[^1] It jammed in a crevice, and the sea eventually washed it away.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that divers have since found it in about 73 m (40 fathoms) of water.[^1]

Commercial power reached the station in 1970.[^1][^2] From then on the keepers no longer had to tend the light at night.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

The sources name four keepers, and they do not agree on every date.[^1][^3]

- **W. Patey** was the first keeper of the 1912 light, according to the marker.[^3] LighthouseFriends also calls him the first keeper, and dates his service from 1909 to at least 1912, which would put him on the point before the iron tower was built.[^1]
- **Joseph Boyd** was keeper in 1938.[^3] LighthouseFriends also has him there at least that year.[^1]
- **Wesley Pynn** came to the station in 1952 with the Department of Transport, and LighthouseFriends has him as keeper until at least 1983. LighthouseFriends prints his recollections from 1983: "We worked 84 hours a week, 12 hours a day, seven days a week."[^1] He also remembered a big diesel engine that burned coal oil and needed regular cleaning. By 1983, he said, everything ran on batteries charged by a 40-horsepower motor.[^1]
- **B.S. Pynn** was a keeper in the 1960s. The marker names "W. and B.S. Pynn" as the lightkeepers at the time of the 1960 rebuilding.[^1][^3]

The keepers' children could attend school in St. Anthony, because the lighthouse was only a twenty-minute walk from the town.[^2] The families could also take part in its social functions.[^2]

## After the keepers

The station was de-staffed in July 1992 as part of automation.[^2][^3] It was staffed again in July 2002, when four keepers came back.[^2][^3]

One of the keepers' dwellings became the Lightkeepers Seafood Restaurant in 1993.[^1] The other was moved into town.[^1] The marker says one house was removed for residential use and the other is now a commercial property.[^3]

A modern lighthouse dating from 2002 or 2003 stands on the point now, with a small office nearby.[^1][^6]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse.[^1] It is a staffed lightstation.[^2][^5] A Fisheries and Oceans Canada brochure of 2005 lists Fox Point among the 23 staffed of the province's 55 major lightstations.[^5] In 2020 the Town of St. Anthony reported three keepers at the site.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the keepers no longer live on the point. They keep office hours, look after the structures and give tours to visitors.[^1]

Visitors can walk the grounds, which are open.[^1][^6] The tower is closed.[^1][^6] The Coast Guard's current list gives the light a focal height of 26.8 metres, a range of 17 nautical miles and a white flash every ten seconds.[^4] Rowlett gives the flash as every 15 seconds, and the sources do not explain the difference.[^6]
