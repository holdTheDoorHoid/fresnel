Off Bowers Beach, Delaware, a white cast-iron house stands on a drum of iron and concrete that was dug down into a sandbar in Delaware Bay.[^1] Fourteen Foot Bank Light, in Kent County, was the first American lighthouse built on a submarine foundation sunk by the pneumatic process, and it replaced a lightship that the winter ice kept driving off its post.[^1][^11][^3]

## The place

Fourteen Foot Bank is an oval shoal on the west side of the main ship channel, which the National Register nomination calls a critical turning point for ships navigating the bay.[^1] Major D. P. Heap of the Light-House Board gave its size as 5,720 feet (1,743 m) long and 1,300 feet (396 m) wide, measured between the 24-foot curves, with 20 feet (6 m) of water on it at the lowest tides.[^3] The Board's engineers bored 26 feet (8 m) into it and found very fine dark sand mixed with shells, packed so hard that a strong water jet was needed to drive the sounding tube down.[^3]

Sources disagree on how far offshore it is. The National Register nomination puts it some twelve miles east of Bowers, while Heap gives about three and a half miles from the Delaware shore, and the Cape Gazette also says about three and a half miles off Bowers Beach.[^1][^3][^21]

Ships bound for Philadelphia had to pass it, and the National Register nomination says imports into that port almost doubled in the 1870s. They also had to steer clear of the nearby Brown and Joe Flogger shoals.[^1] A lightship marked the bank from 1876, but floating ice kept it from staying on station through the winter months, when the shoal most needed to be marked.[^3] Lighthouse Friends adds that the vessel, Light Vessel 19, took shelter behind the Delaware Breakwater when the ice came.[^19]

The danger of that ice is on record from before the lighthouse existed. On 30 December 1880 the schooner *Charles Dennis*, bound from Port Richmond, Pennsylvania, to New Bedford with coal, lay at anchor off the bank when the ice cut through her and she sank at 5.30 in the afternoon. Her crew of five took to their boat and drifted with the floes. The next morning, about 11.30, the lookout at the Cape May life-saving station saw them about five miles off the New Jersey shore with a distress signal flying. The surfboat crew dragged their boat across the ice toward them, but a large tug steaming toward them through the ice reached the men first, and the surfmen turned for home.[^10]

In 1882, the year after the disaster to the first caisson attempted at Germany's Rothersand shoal, the Light-House Board began to consider replacing the lightship with a permanent structure.[^3][^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated money by acts of 7 August 1882 and 3 March 1883, and the Board had $175,000 at its disposal.[^3] In 1883 it settled, on Major Heap's recommendation, on a cast-iron cylinder about 35 feet (11 m) across, to be filled with concrete.[^3] The pneumatic-caisson method had been tried in lighthouse work at the Rothersand light in Germany in 1881 and 1882, with mixed results.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1937 adds that compressed air had been used at Waugoshance light in Michigan 21 years earlier, but only for a repair, and calls Fourteen Foot Bank the first United States lighthouse on a submarine foundation built by the process.[^11] The contractors' bid was accepted from Anderson & Barr of New York, at $38,900, with the government supplying the iron cylinder and cement for 2,000 cubic yards of concrete.[^3]

A wooden working chamber was built on the beach at Lewes starting in the last part of May 1885. The first three sections of the iron cylinder were bolted to it, and two tugs towed the whole thing about 20 miles to the shoal in six hours.[^3] The contractors had chartered the hulk of an old steamer, the *Moro Castle*, 200 feet (61 m) long, and moored it at the site with six anchors to carry the machinery, stores, and quarters for the officers and men.[^3] Concrete mixing began on 17 July, and on 23 July, once the structure weighed enough, air was pumped into the working chamber and the digging began.[^3]

Heap's report describes the work below. Gangs of eight men worked three shifts of eight hours, with candles in their hats. They gathered the sand at the bottom and the air pressure blew it out through a pipe, and each blow made the damp air condense so that the men could see only two or three feet; the blowing had to be limited to half a minute at most.[^3] There was no provision for circulating water through the air cooler, and he wrote that the air in the lock and upper shaft became intensely hot and "the workmen suffered considerably."[^3] For part of the sinking the cutting edge was not resting evenly on the shoal, and at times the cylinder leaned 12 degrees out of plumb.[^3] By 28 August the edge was 33 feet 4 inches (10.2 m) below the original surface of the shoal.[^3] The crew on the job comprised three foremen, two engineers, two firemen, thirty laborers and a cook, thirty-eight men in all.[^3][^1]

As the crew was preparing to leave, according to the National Register nomination (Heap says only "at one time"), a southerly blow tore the *Moro Castle* from her moorings and sent her drifting toward the cylinder, which stood only eight to ten feet above the water and was filled with concrete just to the waterline. Men sitting on its top flange dropped fenders to soften the blow, and the hulk struck only a glancing push. It still sprang the joints between the iron plates, and as they closed again they caught several men by the seats of their trousers. Heap says the men "tore their trowsers and jumped on board."[^3][^1] The contractors left the site on 1 September.[^3]

That autumn the Board decided the bids to finish the cellar story were not reasonable in amount. It built a temporary frame house of two rooms and a platform on the cylinder, from which a fourth-order light was first shown on 24 October. Two temporary keepers maintained it through the winter, and the report called it of much value to navigation because its range and visibility exceeded those of the lightship, which in winter was off station anyway.[^3] A thousand tons of riprap were placed round the cylinder against scour.[^3] During the following season the permanent building went up: a two-story cast-iron dwelling surmounted by a fourth-order lantern, with a cellar that held a Daboll fog trumpet with duplicate hot-air Ericsson engines, water tanks, and brick compartments for fuel, provisions and oil. The oil compartments had iron doors arranged to close automatically in a fire.[^3] Lighthouse Friends names the dwelling's fabricator as H. A. Ramsay & Son of Baltimore.[^19] Because the structure trembled in the shock of the waves, another 2,000 tons of riprap were laid round it.[^3] Heap gave the total cost, including everything, as $123,811.45, which was more than $50,000 under the sum appropriated.[^3]

When the light was finished is not agreed. The National Register nomination says the lighthouse was completed in the summer of 1886 and occupied that fall.[^1] Heap says the station was entirely completed in the spring of 1887, while Arnold Burges Johnson's *The Modern Light-House Service* says it was finished and lighted in the fall of 1887.[^3][^4] Lighthouse Friends gives 10 April 1887 for the first lighting of the permanent light, and 10 September 1887 for the start of the fog signal.[^19] The Delaware Bay Lighthouse Keepers & Friends Association gives 1 December 1886.[^20] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1937 says the lightship stayed on the station until 1887, when the present caisson lighthouse was completed.[^11] The keepers' roster in the U.S. Lighthouse Society archive begins in 1886.[^18]

The nomination, which names Major Heap as architect, notes the Classical Revival cornices and gables. It says the light was recognized in its time as a major engineering accomplishment and suggests that this may explain the more elaborate superstructure.[^1][^2]

The light was white, with red sectors. One covered Joe Flogger shoal, and Heap noted that, together with red sectors at Cross Ledge, it outlined the main channel up to Ship John Shoal. Another marked the Brown shoal.[^3] In June 1892 the characteristic was changed from a flash every fifteen seconds to a fixed light broken by short eclipses, produced by clockwork that raised and lowered a brass cylinder round the lamp chimney.[^5] Commander P. F. Harrington, inspector of the fourth lighthouse district, wrote that the old light had so short a flash that bearings on it "could be obtained with difficulty", that the new occulting lights at Fourteen-Foot Bank and three other Delaware Bay stations had given satisfaction, and that they marked "a distinct advance in the light-house system."[^5]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society archive, drawing on the National Archives' keeper registers, lists a head keeper and two assistants in the posts it records into the early 1950s.[^18] James C. Jones was the first head keeper, from 1886; Lighthouse Friends says he served until his death in 1895.[^18][^19] Joseph M. Harrison then held the post until 1910. The archive lists John H. Millis as first assistant from 1891 to 1901 and Thomas T. Moore as second assistant from 1887 to 1896.[^18]

Linwood Spicer is listed as second assistant from 1901 to 1904, and when he died in November 1930, keeping the Marcus Hook Range Rear light and having completed nearly 35 years in the Fourth District, the Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded that he had served at Fourteen Foot Bank, Ship John Shoal and Bellevue Range Rear. The Bulletin added that his father, William E. Spicer, also a keeper, had been rounding out his thirty-ninth year of service when he died in 1912.[^18][^14]

For the fiscal year 1916 the station won the district's efficiency flag, the award for the light station showing the highest general efficiency. The Bulletin lists Chester P. Joseph as the keeper.[^12] Chester Joseph's first assignment, Lighthouse Friends says, was Fourteen Foot Bank, and it reports that he was stranded at the station for three straight months in the winter of 1917-18, when heavy ice floes blocked relief, and that the ice moving against the foundation made loose objects on tables creep to the edge and fall.[^19]

Other long stays came later. The archive lists James G. Shockley at the station from 1919, as assistant and then, from 1922 to 1939, as head keeper, and Homer H. Burnett as second assistant from 1926 to 1936.[^18] By 1960 the roster names Coast Guard crews beside the civilian keepers, and the archive's list runs to at least 1970.[^18]

On 24 June 1911 the *Democratic Messenger* of Snow Hill, Maryland, reported that Keeper Lewis E. Robinson had taken his own life at the station the Wednesday evening before. The archive spells his name Lewis F. and lists him as an assistant keeper, not the keeper.[^16][^18] The paper reported that the crew working in the engine room heard him cry out, that no tender was at Lewes, and that the men set out in a dory through risky seas to carry his body to shore. It added that the year before he had broken his ankle at the lighthouse and, it was said, went six weeks without medical attention because he could not leave the station or sight a vessel near enough to signal; Lighthouse Friends gives two weeks.[^16][^18][^19] The paper said many thought the suffering he had endured impaired his mind.[^16]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The first winter of the temporary light was a hard one. Lighthouse Friends says the two temporary keepers helped save the crew of the tug *Buelton*, which had been cut open and sunk by floating ice. This account comes from that one source and has not been checked against a report.[^19]

A notice to mariners from the lighthouse inspector at Philadelphia, dated 26 June 1887 and reprinted in the *Morning News* of Savannah, Georgia, on 29 August, described a buoy with red and black horizontal stripes placed to mark the wreck of the schooner *David Lee*, which had sunk abreast of Fourteen Foot Bank Lighthouse and could not be seen at high water.[^15]

The annual reports give the hours the fog signal ran each year. The Daboll trumpet ran about 170 hours in the year ended 30 June 1894 and about 293 hours in the year ended 30 June 1896. In the years ended in 1899 and 1900 it ran about 429 and about 370 hours, burning about two tons of coal in each of the last three of those years.[^6][^7][^8][^9]

On 14 April 1914 the lighthouse tender *Iris*, Custis W. Atkins master, pulled the four-masted schooner *Margaret Thomas* off a shoal near Fourteen Foot Bank.[^23]

In March 1923 the *Milford Chronicle* reported that Elmer O. Mitchell, promoted from first assistant keeper here to keeper of another light, had had "quite an experience" coming in from the lighthouse the week before. He left the light at two in the afternoon, and when he was about three miles from the inlet his engine broke down.[^17]

On 16 November 1931 a party of three high-school teachers and five pupils from Millville, New Jersey, who had been lost in a motor boat in thick fog since the day before, found their way to the station with their fuel nearly gone and their food and water out. A Coast Guard search was under way, but nobody ashore knew they had reached the station until the next morning, when the depot keeper from Lewes landed during very thick fog and a rough sea to relieve a keeper for shore leave, and reported them to a searching Coast Guard vessel. The sea was too rough to take the women teachers off in a small boat; later that day a Coast Guard boat took the party off and towed their boat home to Millville.[^13] Lighthouse Friends adds that the party steered by the sound of the fog horn.[^19]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard kept crews here into the 1960s and 1970. The Delaware Bay Lighthouse Keepers & Friends Association says the light has been automated and without keepers since 1972, while Lighthouse Friends says the last Coast Guard crew left in 1973, after which a cable from the mainland carried power for the automatic light.[^20][^19] The light was later solarised and the Fresnel lens taken out of the lantern. The National Register nomination of 1989 describes the lens then in place as not the original: a drum-shaped Henry Lepaute lens from Paris, about 18 inches (46 cm) across and 2 feet (61 cm) high, dating from 1918.[^1][^19] In 2000 the Coast Guard loaned a lens from the station to the Lewes Historical Society.[^19]

The lighthouse was listed in the National Register of Historic Places in 1989, with the nomination calling it a landmark in lighthouse engineering.[^2][^1] In 2005 the Coast Guard declared it excess property and offered it to government bodies and non-profits under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act. The American Lighthouse Foundation applied, but Lighthouse Friends reports that its application was rejected, and the lighthouse was auctioned and sold on 4 September 2007 for $200,000.[^19]

## Today

Fourteen Foot Bank Light is privately owned. Michael Lynn Gabriel bought it at an online auction in 2007 for $200,000, and in 2025 the Cape Gazette reported that, after nearly two decades of little work, he hoped to begin restoration. He said he hoped the Coast Guard could take part and that the Army Corps of Engineers would be consulted about a dock, and that the first step would be a powered lift so that people need not climb ladders from the water.[^21] The light is still working as an aid to navigation, and the Coast Guard planned an upgrade of its batteries and solar equipment.[^21]

It cannot be visited. It stands in the open bay, its tower is closed, and Lighthouse Friends says it is best seen from the water on summer cruises that pass it.[^19] The Fresnel lens that served at the station is on display in the Lewes Historical Society's maritime museum in the Cannonball House in Lewes.[^22][^19] The University of Delaware also used the station as a base for a bay observing system, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^19]
