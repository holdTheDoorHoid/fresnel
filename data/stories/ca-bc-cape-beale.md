Cape Beale Light stands on a rocky headland at the southern entrance to Barkley Sound, on the outer coast of Vancouver Island, British Columbia. It was the first lighthouse the Canadian government established in the province, and its keepers are credited with several shipwreck rescues on a coast known as the Graveyard of the Pacific.[^1]

## The place

Cape Beale sits at the southeast point of the entrance to Barkley Sound.[^2] The light was meant as a landfall for ships bound for the Strait of Juan de Fuca, on a coast where the casualty rate for mariners was so high that it became known as the Graveyard of the Pacific.[^1] A Department of Marine report of 1906 described the shore between Port San Juan and Cape Beale, a distance of 35 nautical miles, as almost perfectly straight, and noted that west of the cape lay "broken ground with hidden reefs in the entrance to Barkley sound."[^3]

The cape is joined to the mainland by a tidal beach.[^4] Lighthouse Friends writes that in 1872 a party led by the province's assistant surveyor general, Benjamin W. Pearse, failed to land there to choose a site, and could not push through the rainforest on foot from Bamfield; the following year they landed and chose the spot.[^4]

## Building the light

Lighthouse Friends gives the construction crew's supervisor as Charles Hayward, of Hayward and Jenkinson, who hired First Nations people to raft materials to the cape and haul them up the slope to the site.[^4] That account describes a keeper's house and a 9.4 m (31 ft) tapered white tower with a second-order Fresnel lens, first lit by Robert Westmoreland on 1 July 1874, with the light 51 m (167 ft) above the sea.[^4] The federal heritage statement agrees that the station dates from 1874 and calls the original lighthouse the first established in British Columbia by the Canadian government.[^1]

That first tower was replaced on its old foundation. A Department of Marine report says the rebuilding was completed on 17 October 1906 and that a quick-flashing light was shown from the new tower from the beginning of 1907. The tower was "similar in size and colour to the old one," and the work was done by day labour under George Forrest for $2,576.50.[^5] The report adds that the old lighting apparatus was still in use, giving a revolving white light every 30 seconds with a red sector over the dangers in Barkley Sound.[^5]

A fog signal followed; Lighthouse Friends dates the fog alarm building to 1907, while the Department's report for the fiscal year labelled 1908 says the machinery was still being installed. The Department of Marine report records a rectangular wooden fog alarm building on a concrete foundation, with a diaphone plant bought from the Canadian Fog Signal Company of Toronto for $7,250, being installed.[^6] A later report records a 3-inch duplicate diaphone plant installed at Cape Beale by day labour for $523.44.[^7] Lighthouse Friends adds a 193 m (633 ft) tramway from the landing beach to the light in 1883 and a double dwelling for keepers in 1914.[^4]

The present tower is a replacement from the middle of the twentieth century. Lighthouse Friends says dry rot had eaten through the wooden 1906 tower by 1958 and a 9.8 m (32 ft) steel skeleton tower with an aluminium lantern replaced it.[^4] The federal statement describes a 13-metre, four-level galvanized-steel tower clad with asbestos board, with a slatted framework that works as a daymark.[^1] The two sources give different heights. The statement also lists related buildings: a spare dwelling of 1965, a junior dwelling of 1966, a senior dwelling of 1967, and an engine, fog alarm and radio building of 1970.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists the head keepers as Robert Westmoreland (1874 to 1878), Emanuel Cox (1878 to 1894) and Thomas Patterson (1895 to 1908), followed by a long run of others to the present.[^4] Its narrative says Westmoreland noticed that the supplies he received did not match the bills of lading and said so loudly, naming the marine agent at Victoria; the agent sued for slander and won, which cost Westmoreland the post after four years.[^4]

Emmanuel Cox came to Cape Beale, in that account, with his wife Frances and five children.[^4] John Mack, a First Nations man, was hired for $5 a month to help at the station and to watch for the Union Jack flown as a distress signal.[^4] In 1894, according to Lighthouse Friends, Mack saw the flag, found that Cox had died of a heart attack, and went to tell Cox's daughter Pattie at Port Alberni; the widow's request to take over the post was refused.[^4] The Lighthouse Friends keeper list gives Cox's years as 1878 to 1894, while its narrative says he moved to Cape Beale three years after 1874; the sources do not settle the year of his appointment.[^4]

Thomas Patterson's appointment shows in the Department of Marine's 1900 table of lightkeepers' appointment dates, which gives March 2, 1895 against Cape Beale.[^8] The same report lists "Mr. Patterson, keeper of Cape Beale Light, B.C.," among those thanked for kindly services to the American schooner *Winona*, which had been disabled and was brought to a safe anchorage.[^9] Newspapers of 1906 spell the name Paterson.[^11][^12]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

When the steamer *Valencia* was wrecked on the coast in January 1906, wire reports from Victoria said the first boat's crew from the ship, the boatswain and five seamen, had reached Cape Beale.[^10] The disaster led to public calls for a road along the shore from Port San Juan to Cape Beale, which the Department's engineer, after consulting local people including Mr. Patterson, the lightkeeper at Cape Beale, judged impractical.[^3]

In December of that year came the rescue that made the station's name. The American consul at Victoria reported to Washington that the barkentine *Coloma* was lost off Vancouver Island on 7 December and that her distress signals were seen early in the morning by the lighthouse keeper at Cape Beale. His wife, Mrs. Minnie Paterson, started for Bamfield, five miles away, where the government steamer *Quadra* "fortunately had steam up."[^11] The *San Francisco Call* told the rest on 30 December. She forced her way along the trail through bogs and undergrowth, and "staggered into the McKay House" near Bamfield; she and Mrs. McKay then rowed out until they met a boat from the *Quadra*, and the steamer was under way within minutes.[^12] The *Quadra*'s boat took off all ten men of the *Coloma*, the paper said.[^12] Lighthouse Friends names the second woman as Annie Cox McKay, daughter of the earlier keeper, and says the *Coloma* broke up on a reef just after the last of the crew was taken off.[^4] The *Morning Astorian* reported on 9 December that the *Quadra* had reached Victoria with the captain, Allison, and nine crew.[^13] Some retellings date the wreck to 6 December, the day the *Coloma* left Everett; the consul's report and Lighthouse Friends give 7 December.[^11][^4]

Lighthouse Friends says newspapers across the country carried the story and that Minnie Paterson received a cheque for $315.15 from the women of Vancouver and Victoria. It adds that she never fully recovered her health and died of tuberculosis five years later.[^4]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends gives the Cape Beale head keepers of the twentieth century, from William Lowell Thompson (1908) through Philip Gresley Cox (1923 to 1931) and on to Alex D. Thomson (1963 to 1982) and Norbert Brand (1982 to 2010).[^4] In November 1923, in the Department's fiscal year ended 31 March 1924, the Department of Marine put patrol men at Cape Beale, Pachena and Carmanah as extra lookouts for the coast and installed wireless telephones at the three stations.[^14] In 1968, according to Lighthouse Friends, two Airchime horns took over from the 1908 diaphone.[^4]

## Today

Cape Beale is still a staffed station. A U.S. Lighthouse Society News article of January 2025, an edited version of an interview first broadcast in July 2020, describes Cape Beale as one of 25 stations in British Columbia with a resident keeper, who maintains the equipment, monitors the weather and watches for emergencies, and names Karen Zacharuk as the keeper of more than 15 years.[^15] Lighthouse Friends gives her tenure as 2010 to 2023, followed by Mickey Dolan, and the two accounts are not easy to reconcile on how long she served.[^4] The light is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard; the grounds are open but the tower is closed.[^4] The way in is a strenuous 7 km (4.4 mile) hike from Bamfield that crosses the tidal beach and should be attempted only when the tide is six feet or less; check conditions and tides before setting out.[^4] The lighthouse and its related buildings have a statement of significance in the Canadian Register of Historic Places.[^1]
