Manitou Island lies about three miles off the tip of Michigan's Keweenaw Peninsula, at a turning point for Lake Superior shipping. Keepers lived at its eastern end from 1849, and the USLHS roster lists Coast Guard crews there until 1971. The light station now belongs to a land trust and is hard to reach.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

## The place

Lighthouse Friends calls the island three miles long and notes that its name is one of several places that carry the Algonquian name for spirit.[^2] Terry Pepper's history describes it as the marker for an important turning point for vessels passing the Keweenaw, with rocky shoals running out from its shores in every direction, a danger in thick weather. He adds that the opening of the new ship lock at the Soo, expected in 1855, was seen as the start of a boom in Lake Superior traffic.[^3] The Keweenaw Land Trust describes the island today as uninhabited, about 1,000 acres, and without a protected harbor.[^4]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $7,500 on 3 March 1849 for a light on the island's eastern tip, and a 60-foot (18 m) stone tower was built that season.[^2] Terry Pepper's account agrees on the $7,500 and the rubble-stone tower, and has the first keeper, Angus M. Smith, moving in on 5 September 1849.[^3] The Light-House Society's archive dates the station to 1850, and the Lighthouse Friends history says Smith was appointed on 4 September 1849, so sources differ by a year on when the light began.[^1][^2] Henry B. Miller, the Superintendent of Lights, inspected the tower on 29 July 1850 and, in the words quoted by Pepper, found it "a good one", complete except for the lamp frame, "which is missing."[^3]

Smith's household shaped the early staff. In 1855 he earned $400 a year and his wife Lydia $240 as his assistant, and the USLHS roster lists her as first assistant for 1855 to 1856.[^1][^2] A fourth-order Fresnel lens went in during 1856 and the light became a white flash every ninety seconds.[^2]

The stone tower did not last. Lighthouse Friends reports that in 1859 the Lighthouse Board asked for $45,000 to replace the towers at Manitou Island, Whitefish Point and DeTour Point, all barely a decade old, with iron ones, and the replacements were built in 1861.[^2] Pepper has the contracts awarded and the work begun in 1861 and the station complete in 1862, and adds that the old tower and dwelling were razed as unfit for other use.[^3] The surviving tower is a skeletal frame of iron braces round a central cylinder, 78 feet (24 m) tall by Lighthouse Friends' figure, with a covered walkway to the second floor of the keepers' frame dwelling.[^2] The Light-House Society's archive records 70 feet (21 m) from the base to the centre of the lantern, and a third-order Fresnel lens installed in 1861.[^8] Lighthouse Friends says the lens was by Henry-Lepaute.[^2]

## The keepers

Charles Corgan kept the light from 1866 to 1873, assisted at different times by his sons Hugh and James and, from 1868, by Henry Corgan, according to the USLHS roster; Pepper calls Henry the keeper's brother.[^1][^3] Lighthouse Friends adds that James became his father's assistant after Hugh drowned.[^2] James and Mary Corgan reached the island on 24 July 1873, and she served as his assistant. The station log he kept preserves family detail: she went to Copper Harbor in September 1873 and came back about a month later with their first son.[^2] On 15 July 1875 the couple set out in the station's steam launch, *Little Will*, for Copper Harbor in anticipation of an increase in the family; the log records that Mary gave birth to a boy on the water, 1.5 miles east of Horseshoe Harbor, in a dead calm.[^2] In August 1875 Corgan learned in L'Anse that he and his wife were to be removed and resigned instead.[^2] A Lighthouse Service bulletin of 1924 says that a Henry Corgan, retiring that year from St. Clair Flats Canal Upper Light, first entered the service at Manitou Island in 1866, though the USLHS roster begins his Manitou stint in 1868.[^7][^1]

The station's keeper was lost on 19 May 1882. Reuben A. Hart had been moved there from Huron Island in October 1881.[^3] The USLHS roster lists him as head keeper 1881 to 1882.[^1] According to Pepper, his two new assistants, Henry Ferguson and John Gustafson, had arrived on 29 March 1882.[^3] James Corgan, by then at Gull Rock, entered into the Manitou log what Gustafson told him: that Hart's boat, ballasted with stone, was swamped and capsized about 500 feet from the boat runway; that Hart climbed onto the hull and signalled his assistants; and that Ferguson would not help Gustafson get the station boat out. When they lit the lamp more than two hours afterwards, Hart was still clinging to the wreckage and drifting toward the Canadian shore.[^2] Pepper puts the capsizing only a few feet off the island.[^3] Corgan wrote that he hoped the log would never again record "heartless desertion of one keeper by another."[^2] Hart's boat was picked up by a steamer about fifty miles northwest more than three months later, and his body was never recovered.[^2] This account comes from one assistant's statement as Corgan recorded it, and Ferguson's version is not given.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Pepper calls Manitou's fog signals among the most active in the district.[^3] He reports that Congress appropriated $5,000 for it on 3 March 1871.[^3] Lighthouse Friends records that the signal first sounded for five and a half hours on 4 October 1874, in an engine house beside the tower, while Pepper has a pair of fog signal buildings erected in 1875.[^2][^3] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 the 10-inch steam whistle ran some 515 hours and burned about 28 tons of coal; the report printed before it in the same volume gives 360 hours.[^5] Pepper counts 627 hours in 1907, which he calls a station-high figure, with 40 tons of coal hauled and shovelled by the keepers that season.[^3]

Ice and a gale appear in the record. When James Corgan returned on 20 May 1875 there were icebergs ten feet tall outside the reef, and the light that evening was the latest opening of a season on record.[^2] A timber-crib dock built in 1897 was broken up in a gale in October 1898, Pepper reports.[^3]

On 28 June 1934, Keeper Herbert S. Mills saw a fishing boat on the rocks in a hard blow and used the radiotelephone installed in 1931 to call Marquette. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin says the call was made at 5 p.m. and a Coast Guard vessel had pulled the boat off by 10:30 that night.[^6][^2]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends gives the later equipment: oil vapour in 1913 raised the light from 15,000 to 220,000 candlepower; a radiobeacon began on 23 October 1925; electric lighting came in 1928; the fog signal changed to an air diaphone in 1930.[^2] Sources disagree on the concrete fog signal building. Lighthouse Friends dates it to 1938, after ice and storm damage and funded by the Public Works Administration, while Pepper and the Keweenaw Land Trust say 1930.[^2][^3][^4] The USLHS roster shows civilian head keepers to 1954, then Coast Guard crews listed through 1971.[^1] The Land Trust says the station was automated in 1953, yet the roster lists Coast Guard names until 1971, and the sources do not reconcile the two.[^4][^1]

Under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, the station was offered to eligible groups in 2002, and the Keweenaw Land Trust took it over in 2004.[^2][^4] The station is listed in the National Register of Historic Places (reference 84001773).[^9]

## Today

The 93-acre Manitou Island Light Station Preserve is owned by the Keweenaw Land Trust. The Land Trust says the grounds are open for rock collecting, kayaking and bird watching, but that reaching the island is hard because of treacherous water and the lack of a protected harbor. The buildings are generally closed except by prior arrangement with the trust.[^4] The Land Trust describes the modern lamp in the tower as an aid to navigation maintained by the Coast Guard.[^4] Pepper, writing in 2007, described a solar-powered acrylic optic as the light and could not trace the original Fresnel lens.[^3]
