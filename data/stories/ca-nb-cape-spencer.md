Cape Spencer Light stands on a headland of the Bay of Fundy, about 14 km (9 miles) south-east of Saint John, New Brunswick, and has shown a light there since 1873.[^1] The site has carried four towers in succession, and the one standing now is fibreglass.[^2]

## The place

The tower stands on high ground at the cape, which LighthouseFriends calls the "Pitch of the Cape", more than 61 m (200 ft) above the bay.[^1]

In 1870 John Harley, Inspector of Lights for New Brunswick, recommended a light here in a Department of Marine report. He wanted a "medium size flash or revolving light" for the bay and for the steamships that ran between Saint John and the Nova Scotia ports.[^1] Partridge Island, at Saint John, already had a fixed white light, so the new light was set to show a different signal from the start.[^1]

## Building the light

A contract for the tower went to Clark and Stackhouse in 1871.[^1] The light was first lit on 15 June 1873.[^1][^2] The first tower was a square, pyramid-shaped wooden tower, 11 m (35 ft) from base to the top of the lantern, with the keeper's dwelling attached.[^1] Its lantern and catoptric optics came from E. Chanteloup of Montreal, and the whole work cost $6,848.[^1]

The light used two banks of lamps that made one revolution every three minutes. It showed 45 seconds of red, 45 seconds dark, 45 seconds of white and 45 seconds dark.[^1] The white light could reportedly be seen 24 nautical miles, and the red only about 16 km (10 miles).[^1] After a year the station needed a small stove in winter to keep frost out of the turning machinery.[^1]

A 4 ha (10-acre) site was bought from John Carney, who owned the 40 ha (100 acres) around it.[^1] In 1875 the Department paid him $360 for that adjoining land, which had hardwood trees that would make good fuel for this station and nearby lights.[^1] A road 2 km (1.25 miles) long, through a swamp, took three years to build and was finished in 1877.[^1]

The fog signal came next. A keeper had been given a hand fog horn in 1898.[^1] The report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1908 records a wooden fog-alarm building over a large concrete cistern, to hold a three-inch diaphone. The building contract was $2,100, the cistern $550 more, and the diaphone plant, from the Canadian Fog Signal Company of Toronto, cost $7,250.[^5] The report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1909 records a new wooden dwelling for the fog-alarm engineer, built by J. E. Kanes of Saint John for $1,840.[^6] LighthouseFriends dates that dwelling to 1908.[^1]

Electric lighting came in 1913.[^1] In 1915 and 1916 a new fog-alarm building and oil shed were built, at a cost of $5,349.[^1]

The light itself was changed in the same period. The report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1910 says that "the alternating red and white light has been changed to a group revolving white light", with two peaks of brightness nine seconds apart.[^7] LighthouseFriends dates the change to 1909.[^1]

Later towers followed in order. In 1918 F. H. Carson of Saint John built an octagonal concrete tower to replace the wooden one.[^1][^2] A skeletal tower carried the light from 1971 to 1983.[^1][^2] The present fibreglass tower, red on top and white below, came into service in 1983.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

George C. Blacklock was the first keeper, from 1873 until his death in 1888.[^1] The Department's Marine Agent at Saint John, John H. Harding, praised his care and order. Because landing at the station was difficult and dangerous, the Department bought Blacklock a boat to carry supplies.[^1]

Fred G. Blacklock succeeded him and, according to LighthouseFriends, looked after the light for more than twenty years.[^1] The keeper list in the report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1904 puts his name against Cape Spencer.[^4]

LighthouseFriends then lists John E. Collins (1909 to 1921), Charles McKee (1912 to 1926), T. B. McKee (1926 to 1927) and Charles O. Mitchell (1927 to at least 1939).[^1] The Collins and McKee entries overlap, and LighthouseFriends does not say which of the two was in charge.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 29 November 1927 the fog-alarm building burned to the ground. A new one was built the following year.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse belongs to the Canadian Coast Guard.[^2] The present light flashes white every 11 seconds, with its focal plane about 62 m (204 ft) up.[^2][^3]

The sources disagree on the tower's height. The Coast Guard list gives 8.6 m for the red-and-white tower, and Russ Rowlett's Lighthouse Directory gives about 12 m (40 ft).[^3][^2] The fog horn sounds three 2-second blasts every 60 seconds.[^2]

The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1] LighthouseFriends advises extreme caution in this area of high cliffs.[^1]

Three keepers' dwellings from the 1960s stand beside the road to the light. LighthouseFriends says two are private residences and the third is abandoned.[^1] Rowlett's directory also describes two keepers' houses in private use.[^2]
