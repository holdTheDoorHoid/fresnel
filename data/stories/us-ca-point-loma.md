At the southern tip of the Point Loma peninsula, a skeleton tower of iron has marked the entrance to San Diego Bay since 1891, built to replace the old lighthouse on the ridge above, whose light fog too often hid.[^1] Today it is a Coast Guard station with officers' housing on the grounds, and the public cannot visit it.[^2]

## The place

Point Loma is a long finger of land that shelters San Diego Harbor on the west.[^3] The first lighthouse here, now called the Old Point Loma Lighthouse, was built in 1855 high on the ridge, 462 feet (141 m) up, and the Lighthouse Service later explained that it was abandoned because high fogs often hid its light.[^1] An NPS history by F. Ross Holland, Jr. adds that it was the highest lighthouse in the United States and that the coast below could be clear while the lantern sat in cloud.[^4] The answer was a second light at the water's edge, at the very end of the point.[^4]

The land was the Army's. According to Holland, the Secretary of the Treasury asked the Secretary of War in 1882 for ground for two new lighthouses, and in 1889 the Army turned the land over to the Lighthouse Board on condition that it be given up whenever the War Department needed it.[^4]

## Building the light

Holland says bids for the new sea-coast lighthouse were opened in August 1889 and most of the buildings were finished by June 1890.[^4] Lighthouse Friends adds that brick and lumber were landed in September 1889 and that by the following spring two Victorian cottages and a concrete foundation for the tower were finished.[^5] The tower itself was iron, made by the Phoenix Iron Company of Trenton, New Jersey. It reached San Diego by rail on 5 July 1890 and went out to the point by wagon; the stairs, central tube and framework were assembled that August.[^5] The same source calls it a 70-foot (21 m) pyramidal tower and the only one of its kind on the West Coast.[^5]

The lens caused delay. A third-order Fresnel lens with ruby panels had been made in France by Henry Le Paute and shown at the Paris Exhibition. The superintendent of lighthouse repairs arrived in October 1890, and a report to the Lighthouse Board dated 20 October, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it, recorded that "the lens furnished for the new tower was found to be too large for the space provided for it."[^5] Holland agrees that the prize-winning lens was never installed at Point Loma and says it went to the Chicago Harbor lighthouse.[^4] A replacement came on the steamer *Corona* on 3 February 1891. Its pedestal plate reads "ANCLOTE KEYS FLORIDA Henry Le Paute Engineer PARIS 1887", and Lighthouse Friends concludes it was first meant for Anclote Key, Florida.[^5]

The light was first shown on 23 March 1891, according to Holland, the National Park Service and Lighthouse Friends.[^4][^2][^5] The Lighthouse Board's own list of lights established that year records a third-order light at Point Loma in March 1891, though the scanned date is damaged.[^6] The NPS page also says San Diego celebrated "its first lighting" on 3 February 1891, but Lighthouse Friends gives that as the day the lens arrived, and the sources leave the discrepancy open.[^2][^5] Lighthouse Friends says the town marked the lighting with a sailing party to the point and a moonlight picnic.[^5] Holland says the new light kept the old station's alternating red and white flashes.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says the red panes came out around 1912, leaving a white flash, while the NPS dates the removal of the red glass to 1933.[^5][^2]

Fresh water was a recurring problem. The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 describes a patch of ground cemented as a catchment, with a concrete wall forming a basin to hold 12,000 gallons beyond what the tanks held.[^7] Joe Brennan, whose father kept the light from 1892, remembered in Lighthouse Friends that the catchment was seldom enough and that water was hauled to the station in barrels by wagon in dry years; Lighthouse Friends adds that the station's water supply was settled in 1906, when pipes were run to the Fort Rosecrans system.[^5] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 records a brick oil house built and the iron tower repainted white with a black lantern.[^8] The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1896 noted that the clay bank near the tower was eroding and might in time undermine it, and that the keeper had been authorized to move the barn to the other side of the dwelling.[^9]

A fog signal came in 1913. Plans and specifications for it were approved in 1912, and bids opened on 30 September.[^10] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin listed the siren as in commission on 1 March 1913.[^11] The fog signal and new keepers' quarters were completed at a cost of $17,464.48.[^12]

## The keepers

The first head keeper was Robert D. Israel, who had kept the old light and moved down the hill with it.[^4][^13] He did not stay long. Holland says that after nearly a year "a disagreement", whose facts "have been obscured by time", made him resign, and that George P. Brennan was appointed on 9 January 1892.[^4] Lighthouse Friends, followed in outline by the NPS, tells the story as a grudge: years earlier the station's boat had been lost by Israel's son and the assistant's son, and $50 was taken from the keepers' pay. A late-1891 inspection found the lens dirty and the grounds in disorder.[^5][^2]

Brennan came from Point Arena with his wife Mary and six children, according to Lighthouse Friends. The older children rode to school in a buggy.[^5] Later head keepers on the Archive's roster include Richard A. Weiss (1904-1908), William A. Beeman (1908-1920), John Kunder (1920-1924), George Cottingham (1924-1935) and James E. Dudley (1938-1952).[^13] In 1931 the Lighthouse Service named Point Loma, under Keeper G. Cottingham, as the station with the highest general efficiency in the Eighteenth District, entitling it to display the service's efficiency pennant.[^14]

James Milford Johnson, who had come from the Farallon Islands in 1931, served 21 years on the point. The Archive lists him as second assistant from 1931 and first assistant from 1938, and he became a Coast Guard Boatswain's Mate when the Coast Guard took over the lights in 1939. In 1952, offered the head keeper's post, he declined to move into the head keeper's dwelling and took early retirement.[^5][^13]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1912 reported that assistant keeper Malcolm Cady had been commended for helping an injured man who was in danger of drowning.[^15] Lighthouse Friends supplies the story: on 25 July 1912 a man walking the cliffs about a mile from the light fell onto the rocks, and Cady rode bareback to the spot, slid about thirty feet down the bank on a rope, and with a boy and a soldier from Fort Rosecrans hauled him up. The man's left thigh bone was broken.[^5] The Bulletin does not give these details.

On 27 June 1925 second assistant C. V. Wheeler went to help a fishing boat, the *Moon*, that had grounded on the reef near the light.[^16] During the southern California earthquake that began on 10 March 1933, the Point Loma keeper reported a severe shock but no damage at the station.[^17] In the Second World War, Lighthouse Friends says, a blackout order darkened the light and the keepers covered their windows with tar paper; the buildings, even the sidewalks, were painted olive drab.[^5]

## After the keepers

The Archive lists Coast Guard men on the station from 1946 to 1969, without detail about their duties.[^13] The NPS says automation in 1973 made the keeper's job unnecessary.[^2] Sources differ on when the lamp went electric: a Lighthouse Service Bulletin of June 1925 lists Point Loma among lights whose illuminant was changed from incandescent oil vapor to electric incandescent, while the NPS and Lighthouse Friends say 1933.[^18][^2][^5]

Lighthouse Friends says the Fresnel lens turned round the clock until rust and warping stopped it in 1997, when a replacement beacon took over; the lens was taken out of the tower in 2001 and put on display in 2004.[^5] The NPS says it can be seen today in the assistant keepers' quarters exhibits at Cabrillo National Monument.[^2] The prize-winning lens from Chicago Harbor also came back to San Diego in 1968, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^5]

An LED array was installed atop the tower in February 2013, and another went into the lantern room in March 2020, where the Fresnel lens had been.[^5] A Coast Guard release says the tower was put on secondary power in April 2017 for restoration that cost over $2 million and took about three years, and that the main light was re-lit in March 2020.[^19] Lighthouse Friends says about 20,000 pounds of cast iron had to be recast and replaced.[^5] The Coast Guard held a rededication on 20 August 2021, after a delay caused by the pandemic.[^19][^5]

## Today

The station is managed by the Coast Guard and is not open to the public; the NPS says it is now a residential area for Coast Guard officers.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the tower can be seen from a lookout near the Old Point Loma Lighthouse in Cabrillo National Monument.[^5]
