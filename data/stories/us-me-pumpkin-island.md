Pumpkin Island Light stands on a small island off the northwest end of Little Deer Isle, at the western entrance to Eggemoggin Reach in Hancock County, Maine.[^1][^4] The nomination dates its brick tower and keeper's house to 1854.[^1] The light was discontinued in January 1933, and the island has been in private hands since it was sold in 1934.[^1][^4]

## The place

The nomination describes Eggemoggin Reach as "a narrow body of water that separates" Little Deer Isle from the mainland.[^1] It calls the light a guide to "this heavily trafficked waterway linking Blue Hill Bay to the east and Penobscot Bay to the west."[^1] An 1852 Coast Survey report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, recommended a light there, as the reach was "used by all traders and coasting vessels to and from the eastward."[^4] Condon Point on the mainland was initially considered, but the Coast Survey suggested Pumpkin Island instead.[^4]

The island is small. The nomination gives its area as "Approximately 3 acres," while LighthouseFriends describes it as "less than two acres in size."[^1][^4]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $3,500 for the light in August 1852.[^4] Construction waited until the summer of 1854 because of difficulties acquiring the island from John Chester Tibbetts, according to LighthouseFriends.[^4] Tibbetts became the first keeper at a salary of $350 a year.[^4]

The nomination says the station was "established in 1854," and it names the Army Corps of Engineers as designer.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives a different first date: the light was first exhibited on 1 January 1855, with a fifth-order Fresnel lens, "one of the first Fresnel lenses to be used in Maine."[^4] The sources disagree on the first year, and this story does not choose between them.

The sources also disagree on the tower's height. The nomination describes a tower "twenty-two feet in height from its base to the middle of the lantern."[^1] LighthouseFriends says it "measures seventeen feet tall to the lantern deck."[^4]

The keeper's house was a one-and-a-half-story frame building.[^1] Its original board-and-batten siding "had probably been replaced by the 1880s."[^1] A boathouse was built in 1885 and an oil house in 1906.[^1] In 1890 the octagonal lantern replaced "a larger ten or twelve sided unit," according to the nomination.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the original Fresnel lens was replaced by another fifth-order lens in 1909.[^4]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster names seven principal keepers between 1854 and 1933.[^2] The first was John C. Tibbets, from 1854 to 1861, whom LighthouseFriends names as John Chester Tibbetts.[^2][^4] The roster then lists J. G. Walker (1861 to 1864) and Joshua Snow (1864 to 1870).[^2]

Charles L. Babson served from 1870 to 1902, the longest stint on the roster.[^2][^4] LighthouseFriends says he was a native of nearby Brooklin, began his lightkeeping career here in 1870, and was wounded at the Battle of Fredericksburg in the Civil War, after which he "ultimately lost his left leg."[^4] According to the family, his niece Josephine Herrick tended the light when he was away from the island.[^4]

The roster lists Charles S. Holt from 1902 to 1911 and Charles H. Newman from 1911 to 1930.[^2] LighthouseFriends says Newman "remained at the station for roughly twenty years."[^4] In 1923 Federal Court Judge John A. Peters wrote a letter, forwarded to the Lighthouse Bureau's commissioner, saying that Newman needed a telephone. The winter before, when the bay was frozen over, Newman "fell into a hole on the ice" and "came near dying." The letter is quoted by LighthouseFriends, which says that by the following year Newman had installed a telephone at his own expense.[^4]

Frank M. Rumery was the last keeper, from 1930 to 1933.[^2][^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Ice closed the light more than once.[^3][^5] On 28 January 1893 the light was "discontinued owing to close of navigation by ice," and it was relit on 2 April 1893.[^3] In 1894 it was shut again for ice on 26 February and reestablished on 5 March.[^5] An inspection report of 1875, a year after the district engineer's complaint about lights that caused disasters in ice, said the station was "discontinued when the ice makes," as LighthouseFriends quotes it.[^4]

The Board's annual report for the 1893–94 volume includes a note on fog signals: "The keeper of Pumpkin Island light-station hears Whitehead whistle a distance of 20 miles in a NE. snowstorm, and at no other time."[^6] In February 1899 the light was discontinued from 15 to 24 February on account of running ice.[^7]

Newman's rescues appear in the Commissioner's reports and the Service's bulletins.[^8] In the fiscal year 1916 list of assistance to vessels his name appears as "C. H. Newman, keeper, Pumpkin Island Light Station."[^8] In the fiscal year 1921 report he "assisted disabled power boat offshore, made temporary repairs, towed to safe anchorage, and furnished food, dry clothing, and lodging to occupant."[^9] The Bulletin dates a disabled power boat with one man aboard to 17 March 1921, and the two entries may describe the same boat.[^11]

In the summer of 1921 the Service's bulletin commended him for a rescue on 1 July of "two women and children."[^12] The Commissioner's report for the next fiscal year says he "went to the assistance of two ladies and two children who were adrift in a rowboat."[^10] LighthouseFriends dates that rescue to July 1921.[^4] On 9 November 1922 he "rendered assistance in extinguishing a fire aboard a motor boat" in the vicinity of the station.[^13]

In 1928 Newman assisted a power boat with two passengers on 4 August.[^14] On 1 September 1928 he rendered assistance to a vessel "which was ashore on Merrymans Ledge" near the station.[^15]

## After the keepers

The light was discontinued in January 1933.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the nearby Dry Ledge beacon was converted "to replace the lighthouse."[^4] The nomination says "A dry ledge beacon replaced the station in 1934, and the property was sold to a private citizen."[^1] The two dates differ, and the record does not say which is right.

In 1934 the government sold the island for $552 to George Harmon of Bar Harbor, according to LighthouseFriends.[^4] The Maine Historic Preservation Commission prepared the nomination in October 1987, and the station was listed in the National Register of Historic Places in 1988.[^1][^16]

## Today

LighthouseFriends says the island has been sold several times since 1934 and "remains in private hands."[^4] The grounds, dwelling and tower are closed.[^4] Do not land on the island or climb the tower.
