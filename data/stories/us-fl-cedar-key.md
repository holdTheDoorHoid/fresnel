Cedar Key Light stands on Seahorse Key, one of the Cedar Keys off the Gulf coast of Levy County, Florida. Its brick tower, with the keeper's house built into it, was lit in 1854 and put out in 1915. It sat dark for 104 years, until July 2019, when a replica lens brought it back.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Seahorse Key was a federal reservation before it had a light.[^2] In September 1850 Congress appropriated $8,000 for a lighthouse there, and President Millard Fillmore, Zachary Taylor's successor, reserved the island for lighthouse purposes in September 1851.[^2] During the Second Seminole War the island was used as a detention camp for Indians being moved west.[^2]

A railroad from Fernandina reached Cedar Keys in 1860. LighthouseFriends says that up to about 1890 Cedar Keys had been the largest port on that stretch of the Florida coast.[^2]

## Building the light

In January 1854 the Lighthouse Board approved George Meade's plans for the tower. Materials and a crew reached the island by the end of March.[^2]

The Gilchrist County Journal, reporting the 2019 relighting, gives the cost as $12,000 and says the lighthouse was built on a mound in the middle of the island.[^3] The Archive records a fourth-order Fresnel lens as the original optic, and the tower as brick, 18 feet (5.5 m) from base to lantern, with the light 74.5 feet (22.7 m) above mean high water.[^1] William Wilson, the first keeper, lit that lens for the first time on 1 August 1854.[^2][^1]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 records that at Cedar Keys a new sailboat was supplied, an oil house was built, and the dwelling was extensively repaired.[^6] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 says the light's characteristic was changed on 20 December 1899 and re-established on 20 March 1900.[^8] The same report says the revolving machinery was thoroughly repaired.[^5]

## The keepers

William Wilson served from 1854 to 1855, according to the Archive's roster.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he had served just under a year when he was buried on the island in 1866.[^2]

Andrew D. Hobday was principal keeper from 1871 to 1890.[^1] The roster also names one woman as a keeper: Catherine Hobday, first assistant from 1872 to 1879. LighthouseFriends calls her Mrs. C. Hobday.[^1][^2] The sources read here do not say how she was related to the principal keeper, or to the two other Hobdays on the roster.[^1]

Charles H. Gardner kept the light in the 1890s and again from 1907 to 1915.[^1] On 23 August 1914 he rescued a man who had drifted three miles off shore in a small rowboat. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin calls the man apparently insane.[^4] LighthouseFriends says Gardner put the light out for the last time on 15 February 1915, after eight years as its keeper.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends says the light went dark at the start of the Civil War. In January 1862 Union ships ruined the port at Cedar Key and destroyed the structures of military value on Seahorse Key.[^2] The light was returned to service on 23 August 1866, after repairs.[^2]

On the night of 31 August 1886 the Charleston earthquake was felt at the station at 9:31 p.m. LighthouseFriends prints the keeper's report, Andrew D. Hobday's, which describes two shocks about a minute apart.[^2]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1897 records repairs at Cedar Keys made necessary by the storm of 28 and 29 September 1896.[^7] LighthouseFriends says that hurricane destroyed many of the mills and factories in town.[^2]

## After the keepers

About 1903 to 1905 large additions were built on the lighthouse to house a new Navy wireless telegraph station, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The Archive gives 1915 as the year the light was discontinued.[^1]

President Herbert Hoover created the Cedar Key National Wildlife Refuge in 1929. Seahorse Key was made part of the refuge by Executive Order 7484 on 6 November 1936.[^2] Since 1952 the University of Florida has leased a three-acre parcel that includes the lighthouse for its marine laboratory.[^2]

On 5 July 2019 the light was relit after more than a century dark, with a replica of the fourth-order lens. The Gilchrist County Journal says Fresnel Lenses, LLC built the new lens and installed it on 20 May 2019. LighthouseFriends says Artworks Florida was contracted to build the replica.[^3][^2]

## Today

The island belongs to the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service and is managed by the University of Florida Marine Laboratory.[^2] The Archive lists the light as no longer an active aid to navigation.[^1] The lighthouse itself serves as a dormitory for the laboratory.[^2]

Landing on Seahorse Key is not permitted from 1 March to 1 July because of bird nesting.[^2] The lighthouse is open to the public during open houses, typically held in July and during the October seafood festival weekend, and Seahorse Key can be reached by boat from Cedar Key.[^2] Visit only on open-house days or on an authorized trip, and do not climb the tower at other times.
