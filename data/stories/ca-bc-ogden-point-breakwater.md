Ogden Point Breakwater Light marks the outer end of a granite breakwater that guards the entrance to Victoria's inner harbour, on Vancouver Island.[^3][^5][^8] The federal government built the breakwater and docks in the years around 1914 to 1917.[^3][^4] The first light on the breakwater was an unwatched acetylene beacon.[^1][^5]

## The place

The Victoria Heritage Foundation describes the harbour as comparatively shallow, with many hazards, and says it could not handle the traffic anticipated once the Panama Canal opened in 1914.[^3]

The Foundation names two earlier disasters, the loss of the SS Clallam off Trial Island in 1904 and the loss of the SS Sechelt in 1911. It gives 55 and 58 deaths for them, and says these wrecks deterred shipping companies from using the harbour.[^3]

The point is named for Peter Skene Ogden, a chief factor of the Hudson's Bay Company.[^3][^5]

In 1913 the federal government decided to upgrade Victoria's shipping facilities, after a preliminary study by the engineer Louis Coste.[^4]

## Building the light

A 1914–1915 appropriation of $1,100,000 provided the first money for the breakwater and two concrete piers.[^5] The Foundation names Louis Coste as design engineer, J.S. MacLachlan as supervising engineer, and D. Lafleur as chief engineer of Public Works Canada.[^3][^4] Sir John Jackson Ltd built the breakwater, and Grant, Smith and McDonnell built the docks.[^3][^4]

The breakwater is 762 m (2,500 ft) long.[^4] The Foundation says more than 10,000 granite blocks, each up to 15 tons, were stacked in a pyramid in nine layers to form the main wall.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the granite came from Hardy Island; the Canadian Society for Civil Engineering names Haddington, Nelson and Hardy islands.[^4][^5]

The sources give the year the breakwater was finished two ways. The Foundation and the Society say 1917, and LighthouseFriends and Tourism Victoria say 1916.[^3][^4][^5][^7]

A second breakwater, first recommended, was never built. Instead the docks were built on 53 concrete caissons, made in Esquimalt Harbour and towed to Ogden Point.[^3]

The light came in 1917.[^1][^5] LighthouseFriends says the breakwater was then marked by a square white pyramidal concrete tower, showing an occulting white light 12 m (40 ft) above high water.[^5] The Department's report for the fiscal year 1916–17 lists "Erection of an unwatched acetylene beacon" at Ogden Point, with "Messrs. Parfitt Bros." as contractor and a "Contract price, $1,655."[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the same firm and price for a tower, and says the unwatched beacon originally provided the light.[^5] The report ties the price to the beacon; LighthouseFriends ties it to the tower. This story does not settle which.

Later works followed. The Department's report for the fiscal year 1918–19 says an electrically operated fog alarm "was installed at Ogden point." LighthouseFriends dates it to 1919, and says a cable from shore to supply its electricity was laid in 1926.[^2][^5]

## Today

The breakwater is a public walk, and Tourism Victoria describes a flat, accessible path of nearly 800 metres.[^7] Rowlett says it has handrails and is a popular attraction.[^6] Both the grounds and the site are open, and the tower is closed to visitors.[^5][^6]

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light.[^5] The Coast Guard list gives a quick-flashing red characteristic, from a white tower with a red band at the bottom, with a focal height of 13.2 m and a range of 9 nautical miles.[^8] The 1917 description, an occulting white light, is LighthouseFriends' account of the first light. The sources do not date the change.[^5][^8]

The Foundation says the breakwater has not needed any major upgrades since it was built, and only 136 blocks have ever been repositioned.[^3] In 2001 the breakwater and docks were named a National Historic Civil Engineering Site, and a plaque was unveiled on the walkway that May.[^3][^4] The Foundation notes the site is not on the City of Victoria Heritage Register.[^3]

Cruise ships now call at the port in summer.[^5] The Foundation says Ogden Point was built with the foresight to accommodate today's large cruise ships.[^3]
