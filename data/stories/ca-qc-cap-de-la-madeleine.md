Cap de la Madeleine is an exposed point on the northern shore of the Gaspé peninsula, at Sainte-Madeleine-de-la-Rivière-Madeleine, and the white concrete tower on it is the second light to stand there. A wooden light was first shown in 1871; the present tower replaced it in the first years of the twentieth century, and its Fresnel lens is still in the lantern.[^1][^2]

## The place

Long before there was a lighthouse, ship captains used the cape to fix their position on the river.[^1] The federal heritage statement describes an exposed, treeless site bounded by water on two sides, in a historic fishing setting.[^7] It ties the light to the local economy: the settlement at the mouth of the Madeleine River was one of the principal fishing ports on the northern Gaspé coast, and once the Great Eastern Paper Company opened six miles upstream from the river's mouth, forest products became a mainstay of local trade.[^7]

## Building the light

Approval for a light came in 1868, with completion expected in 1870, but the schooner carrying the building materials was overcome by a heavy gale and disabled, and the light was not lit until August 1871.[^1] The first tower was a hexagonal wooden structure 16.5 m (54 ft) tall and about 5 m (17 ft) wide at the base, and it was lit on 21 August 1871.[^1][^2] Its apparatus was a revolving catoptric (reflector) light that showed alternate white and red flashes two minutes apart; the white could be seen up to twenty miles off and the red up to fifteen.[^1] The builder was John A. Cameron, and the whole station cost $6,170.17.[^1]

Quebec's heritage inventory places the station in the federal programme of marking the estuary and gulf coasts between 1870 and 1874.[^2] Over the next two decades the Department of Marine added to it. According to Lighthouse Friends, the tower was stayed with four wires and given a lightning rod in 1874, a semaphore for signalling ships by flag arrived in 1879, and a black vertical stripe was painted on its seaward side in 1881 so that mariners could tell it from other towers.[^1] A steam fog whistle began work on 24 September 1892, sounding an eight-second blast every thirty seconds when needed.[^1]

The wooden tower could neither carry nor house the heavier lens planned for the station, so it was taken down around 1907.[^2] The Commissioner of Lights reported in 1906-07 that a reinforced concrete tower was "almost completed" to receive a third-order triple-flashing light and lantern that were then at Quebec.[^3] The Steel Concrete Company of Montreal built it under contract for $3,300, and the report printed in 1909 says the circular iron lantern was to be fitted "early in the season of 1908".[^4] Sources differ on the date: Quebec's inventory says the wooden tower was demolished around 1907, Lighthouse Friends says the tower was built in 1906-1908, while the departmental reports show it nearly finished in 1906-07 and the lantern still to come in 1908.[^2][^1][^3][^4] A later report confirms the light "has been erected on the new tower", flashing white three times in each 30-second revolution, with a stated candle power of 55,000.[^5] The optics came from Barbier, Bénard & Turenne of Paris.[^1] The federal heritage statement calls it a very good example of an early reinforced concrete tower in Canada.[^7]

A new fog-alarm building went up too. The 1909 report describes a rectangular wooden building, 57 by 31 feet, for a 5-inch diaphone plant bought from the Canadian Fog Signal Company of Toronto, to be working in the summer of 1908.[^4] A report a year later calls it a 3-inch plant, "purchased last year", now installed in place of the fog whistle.[^5] Lighthouse Friends says the diaphone went into service in 1908.[^1]

## The keepers

Philip Savage lit the first light and was paid $300 a year.[^1] Quebec's inventory says he resigned in 1875 and was followed by Gualbert Lavergne, whose assistant, François-Joseph Sasseville, became keeper after him.[^2] Lighthouse Friends dates Lavergne to 1875-1886 and Sasseville to 1886-1923.[^1] The Department's 1915-16 list shows F. J. Sasseville at "Magdalen cape, light and fog alarm", appointed 9 June 1886, on $980 a year.[^6] On those dates he was keeper for some thirty-seven years, after first serving as Lavergne's assistant.

After Sasseville the list reads Madame Charlotte Léda Sasseville (1923-1924), Jean Batiste Caron (1924-1956), Raymond Caron (1956), Rémi Ferguson (1956-1965) and Jean-François Caron (1965-1987).[^1] Quebec's inventory gives the same later succession but calls the first Caron Joseph Baptiste.[^2] The sources give no more about their lives. Lighthouse Friends says the original dwelling housed both keeper and assistant until a two-storey keeper's house and a smaller assistant's house were built in 1956, a date Quebec's inventory repeats.[^1][^2]

## After the keepers

Quebec's inventory records that the light was electrified in 1957, and that in 1972 the diaphone was replaced by a fully automatic electric fog detector and horn.[^2] Quebec's inventory says the keeper's trade at Cap de la Madeleine ended with electrification and then automation; Lighthouse Friends says the keepers left when the light was automated, and its list of keepers ends in 1987.[^2][^1] The Fresnel lens's later history is stated differently. Lighthouse Friends reports that it was active until about 2000, then replaced by a modern beacon, and remains in the lantern room; Quebec's 2009-10 inventory and the Route des phares site describe it as still operating.[^1][^2][^11] The tower has a federal heritage building statement and a separate heritage lighthouse statement of significance, which says the lighthouse is one of the oldest reinforced concrete lighthouses in Canada.[^7][^8]

## Today

The tower stands in a small tourist site with a museum, a picnic area and food service; Lighthouse Friends names a local tourist association as owner, and the Quebec tourism listing names the municipality of Sainte-Madeleine-de-la-Rivière-Madeleine.[^2][^1][^10] The tourism listing offers guided visits in French and English, and Lighthouse Friends reports that the tower is open in season, with guided tours from early June to mid-October.[^10][^1] Hours change, so check with the site before going. The Canadian Coast Guard's list of lights still gives a light here, Fl(3) W 27 s, 44.5 m above the water and 20 nautical miles in range, in a white cylindrical tower 16.8 m high, classed as a private aid.[^9]
