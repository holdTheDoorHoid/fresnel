Point Aconi light stands on the northern tip of Boularderie Island, Cape Breton, on the eastern side of the Great Bras d'Or Channel.[^5][^6] Its first light was put in operation in 1874 a mile to the south, on High Cape, to mark the narrow channel of Little Bras d'Or.[^5] The light on the point today is a square skeletal tower, which Rowlett dates to 2012.[^6]

## The place

Little Bras d'Or is the narrower, more winding of the two channels that connect the Bras d'Or lakes with the Atlantic. The Great Bras d'Or was the main route in.[^5] By 1901 the Department's chief engineer, William P. Anderson, reported that the traffic had largely gone: "Since Little Bras d'Or has been spanned by a bridge, traffic by that route has almost entirely fallen off."[^1] The headland also held the Prince Colliery, which opened in the early 1970s and closed in 2001.[^5]

## Building the light

The light was put in operation on 20 November 1874, on High Cape, a mile south of the point.[^5] John A. Moore built the tower for a contract price of $500. Everything together, the tower and its lighting apparatus, cost $1,485.85. George Bonner was appointed keeper at $150 a year.[^5]

The station was improved over the following decades. Its lantern was reglazed in 1878, an oil store was added in 1885, and in 1893 the tower gained an addition ten by fourteen feet on its northern face.[^5] On 1 July 1902 the can buoy off the point gave way to an automatic whistling buoy of the Courtenay type. The Department said it would be taken up every winter.[^3]

## The keepers

George Bonner is the first keeper LighthouseFriends names.[^5] The Department's list of lightkeepers in its 1901 report still shows him at the station: "Bonner, George Point Aconi".[^2] That list gives his appointment as 18 April 1874, seven months before the light was put in operation.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives his service as ending in 1899, two years before the 1901 list. The sources do not say when he left.

John Charles Bonner appears by 1907, when the Department's keeper table prints "Bonner, John Charles Point Aconii" against the station, with an appointment date of 6 November 1903.[^4] LighthouseFriends dates his service from 1903 to at least 1937.[^5] The sources do not say whether the two Bonners were related.

LighthouseFriends says that as many as three keepers once served the station, with a foghorn, before it was reduced to one.[^5] Its later keepers are J. G. Mitchell (1965 to 1972) and Carl D. Goyetche (1979 to 1990), the last keeper.[^5]

## After the keepers

In 1901 the chief engineer had recommended moving the light out to the point: "The light would be much more serviceable if removed to the extremity of the point, and this is recommended."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the move was finally carried out, but many years later. Its account has the High Cape light in service through at least 1939 and gone by 1947.[^5] In the early 1960s a light went up on the point itself, on a skeletal tower about 10 m (34 ft) tall.[^5]

A photo essay on the Cape Breton coast gives other dates. It says the first light was built "here", at the point, and that the skeletal tower came between 1955 and 1968.[^8] The 1901 report, which asks for the light to be moved out to the tip, fits LighthouseFriends better on the first site.[^1][^5]

In 1989 a round white fibreglass tower, 11.5 m (38 ft) tall, took over from the skeletal tower, and the station was de-staffed in 1990.[^5][^6] Thieves broke into the station in September 2011 and took copper wiring, which put the light out for a time.[^6] Rowlett dates the move of the active light to a square skeletal tower, about 10 m (33 ft) high, to 2012.[^6][^7]

On 21 to 23 February 2014 the fibreglass tower and the building beside it burned to the ground.[^5] Because the site is so remote, LighthouseFriends says, nobody reported the fire until it was out.[^5] Rowlett describes the fire as one of suspicious origin.[^6]

## Today

The point is Canadian Coast Guard property. The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^5][^6] To reach the light, take Point Aconi Road, which runs 10.2 km (6.4 miles) from Highway 105 at Bras d'Or.[^5] The Coast Guard's list still gives the light as a long-flashing white light every six seconds, from a "square skeleton tower, red and white rectangular daymark", with a focal height of 26.9 m and a range of 14 nautical miles.[^7] Rowlett gives the focal plane as 25.5 m (84 ft).[^6]
