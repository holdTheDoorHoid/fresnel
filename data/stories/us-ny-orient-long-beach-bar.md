The Orient Long Beach Bar Light, which local people call Bug Light, stands on a shoal at the tip of Long Beach, off Orient, where it marks the entrance to Orient Harbor on eastern Long Island.[^2][^13] The first light on the bar was a wooden house on screw piles that, seen from a distance, looked like a bug walking on the water.[^2] Fire destroyed it on the Fourth of July in 1963. A working replica, built in 1990 on the old foundation, stands there now.[^3][^13]

## The place

The shoal, Long Beach Bar, lies between Orient Harbor and Gardiners Bay.[^13] In December 1869 the New York Herald called for a light there to mark the entrance to Peconic Bay for the fishermen of eastern Long Island. As LighthouseFriends quotes the paper, the bar was "the only difficult or dangerous place" those fishermen had to pass on their trips up and down the bay.[^2] The first light stood on a screw-pile platform sunk about ten feet (3 m) into the sandy bottom of Gardiners Bay.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress provided $17,000 for the lighthouse on 15 July 1870, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] In February 1871 the office of the Lighthouse Engineer for the Third District, at Tompkinsville, New York, advertised for granite, including granite for a wall at the Long Beach Bar light station.[^4] The archive dates the tower and its lens to 1871. The lens was a fifth-order Fresnel.[^1] The museum that now cares for the light says the original was built in 1870. The Suffolk Times says 1871.[^13][^12] The light was first lit on 1 December 1871.[^2]

In June 1872 a list of appropriations printed in the New-York Tribune included $20,000 for "Completing light-house at Long Beach Bar."[^5] LighthouseFriends gives a $20,000 appropriation of June 1872 as money for a breakwater to protect the light from ice floes.[^2] The sources do not agree on what the money paid for.

The Light-House Board also used the station for experiments. In August 1890 a committee of the Board chose the light station as its shore station for candle-power tests. Buoys were placed one to five miles from it, and a steamer and a shore party signalled each other with rockets and lanterns.[^7] Later work was smaller. The station had a fog bell that was struck by machinery once every fifteen seconds, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] In March 1899 a falling weight disabled the striking machine, and the signal stopped until 22 June 1899.[^8] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 the lower boat landing was rebuilt and 400 tons of riprap were placed to protect the station.[^9] The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1922 lists a concrete foundation and general repairs at Long Beach Bar, costing $5,742, among the works completed that year.[^11] LighthouseFriends says a reinforced concrete foundation was poured in 1926.[^2] The two dates disagree.

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists the station's keepers, assistants and crew from 1871 to 1946.[^1] The first winter is muddled in the sources. LighthouseFriends says William W. Thompson, the first keeper, wrote in the first logbook entry that he had only 70 gallons of oil on hand. It says he resigned the following April, after a harrowing winter.[^2] The archive's roster is more tangled. It lists Thompson in 1871, then Cornelius Oakes from 1871 to 1872, then Thompson again in 1872.[^1]

George M. Fenton was an assistant in 1872 and 1873 and then keeper until 1887.[^1] In 1881 ice packed under the house from the east on a flood tide, broke three pilings and nearly all the braces. The ice was two feet (0.6 m) thick, and the lamp lenses had to be steadied by hand to save them. LighthouseFriends quotes Fenton's own description of the damage.[^2] Judging from the station logbook, LighthouseFriends says, Fenton spent most of his time catching lobster and blackfish.[^2]

LighthouseFriends calls Charles M. Fenton the son of the keeper George M. Fenton, and says Charles became keeper on 18 May 1887, after his father's death.[^2][^1] Charles had been an assistant from 1882 to 1887.[^1] He played the flute, violin and accordion, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] In 1889 Charles B. Moore arrived as assistant keeper with his daughter Ruth. She and Fenton married, and LighthouseFriends says they went ashore for the ceremony and came back to the light for their honeymoon.[^2][^1] Charles resigned in 1905, when a doctor forbade Ruth to live at the station any longer because of her health.[^2]

John A. Miller was an assistant from 1911 to 1912, and the light was his first station.[^1][^2] George Ehrhardt was keeper from 1913 to 1919. A table of lighthouse employees who aided vessels in the fiscal year 1915 names him as keeper, with James Hamilton as assistant keeper, at Long Beach Bar Light Station.[^1][^10] The roster's last keeper is Albert C. Brennecke, from 1943 to 1946.[^1] The Suffolk Times says a lightkeeper lived in the house from 1871 to about the mid-1940s.[^12]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In March 1872 the New York Herald printed a notice from a correspondent at Greenport. The correspondent reported that the light on Long Beach Bar had been deserted on a Saturday morning, when a floe of ice broke away in Orient Harbor. He passed on the keepers' report that the heavy masses of ice had started the piles on the west side. A steamer later reported the building all right and said the keepers, who had already left their post once, had been needlessly alarmed. The notice said the light was still without a keeper.[^6]

On 3 August 1911 the steamer Shinnecock came up Long Island Sound in a heavy fog and ran aground right in front of the station. LighthouseFriends says the keeper did not start the fog bell until almost half an hour later. The ship and its passengers were not injured, but the keeper was reprimanded for his inattention.[^2]

On 30 December 1911 Miller, who had been stormbound onshore for several days, set out in a small sailboat for the light. The captain of the oyster dredge Cupid, J. Frank Terry, saw the small boat making little progress in the heavy seas and changed course to help. The sailboat capsized before he reached it. Miller was thrown a line and drawn aboard, and he fell unconscious. He came to in time to go back to the light on New Year's Day.[^2]

The Suffolk Times calls the 1938 hurricane the storm that "pretty much marked the end of Bug Light." The keeper and his grandson, it says, huddled in a corner on the second floor. Waves flooded the first floor and crashed against the second.[^12] The museum's board chairman told the paper that the damage was such that no one wanted to live there.[^12]

On the night of 4 July 1963 fire destroyed the building. LighthouseFriends says arsonists set it.[^2] The museum's own account gives the same date and says the fire destroyed the light that night.[^13] The Suffolk Times reports that some said arson and some said accident, and that the building burned to the waterline.[^12]

## After the keepers

The archive gives 1948 as the year the station was discontinued.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the sandbar had moved so far past the light that the beacon no longer marked the channel, and that this was why the station was taken out of commission that year.[^2] The Suffolk Times quotes the museum's chairman saying it was decommissioned in 1948.[^12]

In 1955 the federal government put the structure up for sale.[^12] LighthouseFriends says it was auctioned that year, and that the Orient Point Marine Historical Association won with a bid of $1,710, well above other bids of $52 to $212.[^2] The Suffolk Times gives the winning bid as $1,700 and the others as about $200.[^12]

The fire of 1963 left only the foundation.[^12] A Marine Foundation was formed in 1989 to raise money to buy the site for eventual restoration, and it later merged with the East End Seaport Museum.[^12] LighthouseFriends says a fundraising campaign led by Merlon E. Wiggin raised $140,000 for a working replica.[^2] The replica was built on land at the Greenport Yacht and Shipbuilding yard in the summer of 1990. On 5 September 1990 it was moved onto the old concrete foundation, and its solar-powered light was lit that day.[^2][^13] The Coast Guard recognized the beacon as a federal aid to navigation in 1993.[^2][^12]

In April 2005 two local groups that had feuded over control of the light signed an agreement. They divided the maintenance and repair work and agreed to raise money together, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] Superstorm Sandy flooded the building again in 2012.[^12]

## Today

The East End Seaport Museum & Marine Foundation owns the lighthouse structure and cares for it.[^2][^13] The Coast Guard maintains the light as a federal aid to navigation.[^13]

The light stands about 0.1 mile (160 m) offshore. LighthouseFriends says it is best seen by boat, and its tower and dwelling are closed.[^2] The museum's lighthouse cruises stop at the light.[^2] To get as close to it as you can on land, LighthouseFriends says, drive into Orient Point State Park, take the park road to its end, and walk 2.4 miles (3.9 km) over sandy trails to the point.[^2]
