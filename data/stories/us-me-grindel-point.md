Grindle Point Light stands at the entrance to Gilkey Harbor, beside the village of Islesboro and the ferry landing.[^1][^2] The first light there was a brick tower of 1850, which was rebuilt in 1874 and 1875.[^1][^3] The square brick tower standing now is thirty-nine feet (11.9 m) tall.[^2] The nomination dates the light's automation to 1934, the light was relit in 1987, and the tower has been open to visitors since its reopening in 2025.[^1][^2][^8]

## The place

Islesboro is an island in Penobscot Bay.[^2] The nomination notes that it was first included on a steamboat line in 1847, and the island later became a summer resort of hotels and cottages.[^1] The light was built to serve seafarers entering Gilkey Harbor, which LighthouseFriends considers one of the best in Maine for its access and deep water. The harbor was named for John Gilkey, a settler who arrived in 1772.[^2]

On 3 March 1849 $3,500 was appropriated for a lighthouse at Islesboro.[^1] The site was a three-acre tract bought from Francis Grindle for $105, and the nomination says that gave the light its name.[^1] The Light-House Board's reports spell the name "Grindel."[^4][^5]

## Building the light

The first tower and keeper's house were built of brick. The tower was twenty-eight feet (8.5 m) high, and the buildings were completed in the summer of 1850.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the one-and-a-half-story brick lighthouse, with its lantern, cost $3,100.16 to erect.[^2] A fifth-order Fresnel lens was placed in the lantern in 1856.[^2][^3]

The station was rebuilt in 1874 and 1875. An appropriation for the rebuilding was made on 23 June 1874. The nomination quotes the Board's 1875 annual report as saying the light was "determined advisable to re-build instead of repairing it." A one-and-a-half-story dwelling and a square brick tower were erected, and the old house was taken down to near the ground and used as a covered way between them.[^1] The nomination says a Boston contractor, not yet identified, did the work, and that a machine shop in Portland refurbished the replacement lens.[^1]

The land's boundary caused trouble in 1889. The deed of July 1849 started the boundary at "a heap of stones at low water mark," a marker that LighthouseFriends says was long ago destroyed. A line drawn from the most probable starting point would pass through the dwelling.[^2] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 says a question about the boundary, arising from an error in the deed "which was on February 7, 1889," was referred to the United States attorney for settlement.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the attorney for Maine was trying to get a correct deed from the grantor's heirs.[^2] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 records $79.30 for legal services in correcting the error in the title, and says the light-house premises were enclosed.[^5][^2]

Other work followed in order. A brick cistern went into the covered passage in 1887, and a barn was rebuilt in 1892.[^1][^2][^5] LighthouseFriends dates a new oil house, which still stands, to 1905; the archive gives 1906.[^2][^3] The Board recorded that a hand bell was changed to a 1,000-pound bell struck by hand on 3 October 1896.[^6][^1] LighthouseFriends dates the bell to 1897.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists thirteen principal keepers from 1850 to 1933.[^3] The table on this page carries every stint. The stories below are the ones the sources tell in detail.

Rufus Dunning was appointed keeper on 20 August 1850.[^1][^3] The nomination says Francis Grindle replaced him on 3 April 1853.[^1] The archive lists a Francis Grindell for 1852.[^3] LighthouseFriends quotes a history of Islesboro written in 1893, which names Dunning as the first keeper, Mansfield Clark second, and Captain F. Grindle third, and says those three men served about three years.[^2]

Charles Nash kept the light from 1856 to 1861, and Nelson Gilkey from 1861 to 1869.[^3] LighthouseFriends says Nelson Gilkey also worked as a postmaster and town clerk, and that an inspection in 1869 found the light "in good condition."[^2]

Nelson's older brother Avery kept the light from 1869 to 1872.[^2][^3] In his 1872 inspection report, Inspector Mayo said he had watched the light during one night for two hours, and that it "burned dimly all that time." At three in the morning it "had not the brilliancy of an ordinary family lamp, at four miles distant." LighthouseFriends quotes the inspector recommending Avery's removal, and says Avery Gilkey was removed later that year.[^2]

Ira D. Trundy kept the light from 1894 to 1908.[^3] The archive also lists Valeria I. Trundy as principal keeper for 1908. The sources do not say how she came to the post, or whether she was related to Ira Trundy.[^3]

James E. Hall moved to Grindle Point in 1908 from the head keeper's post at Matinicus Rock.[^2] He remained there until 1916.[^3] LighthouseFriends says that a blasting accident at the station caused his death in 1916.[^2]

Edward K. Tapley kept the light from 1916 to 1920, and William H. C. Dodge from 1920 to 1933.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The only death the sources record at the station is James E. Hall's, which LighthouseFriends attributes to a blasting accident in 1916.[^2] No wreck or rescue tied to the light appears in the sources read.

## After the keepers

Plans to automate the light were made in 1923, but local objections held the move up until 1934.[^1] The sources differ on the year the light was taken out of service. LighthouseFriends says the light was decommissioned on 30 June 1933, and that its Fresnel lens was removed afterward. A lighted bell buoy was set up nearby as a replacement.[^2] In 1934 nine Maine lights, described as abandoned during the national economy program, were announced for sale, and Grindle Point was among them.[^2] The nomination says that in 1934 a 21-foot (6.4 m) steel tower was built to carry a flashing white light.[^1]

The nomination says the Town of Islesboro acquired the station in 1935 for use as a public park and to house a museum. LighthouseFriends says the town bought it for $1,200 in 1934, and that the town established the Sailors' Memorial Museum in the lighthouse in 1938.[^1][^2]

The station was entered in the National Register on 13 March 1987.[^1] Islesboro residents later lobbied the Coast Guard to reactivate the light, and the steel tower came down. A solar-powered optic that flashes green went into the old lantern room, and a relighting ceremony was held on 4 November 1987.[^2] The museum received a 1,000-pound fog bell similar to the one the station had used.[^2] An LED optic was installed in the lantern room in January 2017.[^2][^3]

## Today

The Town of Islesboro owns the station, and the Digital Archive lists it as open to the public.[^2][^3] The town's page gives the museum's hours as 1 July through Labor Day, Tuesday to Saturday from 9:30 a.m. to 4:30 p.m., and Sunday from 12:30 to 4:30 p.m.; LighthouseFriends gives Friday-to-Sunday hours.[^2][^7] The town's page says the lighthouse tower is open to the public, with a view of the west side of Penobscot Bay and Gilkey Harbor.[^7]

The tower reopened on 29 June 2025, after six years closed for safety reasons. The work cost $228,000, LighthouseFriends says, including replacing rotted floor joists, reinforcing the foundation and repointing the brick.[^2][^8] Summer visitors may climb to the top of the tower.[^2]

The station is reached by the ferry from Lincolnville, and the lighthouse stands right by the Islesboro ferry landing.[^2]
