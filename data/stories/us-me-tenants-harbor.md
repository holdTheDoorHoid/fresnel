Tenants Harbor Light stands on Southern Island, at the entrance to Tenants Harbor on the Maine coast of Penobscot Bay. It is a circular brick tower joined to a wooden keeper's house. The light was built in 1857, discontinued in 1933, and has been in private hands since.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Southern Island is a rock ledge. LighthouseFriends gives its size as about twenty-two acres, while the nomination gives about twenty acres for the nominated property.[^3][^1] The light stands on its east side, where it helped ships find the two entrances to Tenants Harbor.[^1][^3] The village is the largest community in the Town of St. George.[^1] An active shipbuilding industry there produced more than seventy schooners between 1820 and 1870. The nomination says many of them carried ice, stone, lime and lumber from nearby granite quarries.[^1]

Mariners had proposed a light there as early as 1838. LighthouseFriends says the government did not act until 1854, when it provided $4,500. It gives two reasons for the delay: the harbor had only six houses, and a new light might be confused with Whitehead Light, two miles to the northeast, or with Marshall Point Light, five miles to the southwest.[^3] The village's name has been spelled many ways over the years, including "Tarrent's," "Tarance," "Terrance" and "Tennants," LighthouseFriends says.[^3] The Lighthouse Service's 1914 *Bulletin* spelled it "Tennant Harbor."[^4]

## Building the light

A one-acre parcel and a right-of-way on Southern Island were bought in 1855, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] In 1857 a brick tower twenty-seven feet tall was finished, attached by a service room to a five-room wooden keeper's dwelling.[^3] The nomination describes the tower as just over twenty-seven feet from its base to the center of the lantern, and says the tower was "built in 1857."[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives the height from base to lantern center as 25 feet, so the two sources differ on the height.[^2]

The original optic was a fourth-order Fresnel lens, which LighthouseFriends says showed a red flash that could be seen for thirteen nautical miles.[^2][^3] The sources disagree about when the light was first shown. LighthouseFriends says it was first lit on January 1, 1858. The US Lighthouse Society gives 1857 as the year the station was established and the lens installed.[^2][^3] This story cannot say which date is the first night of the light.

By 1863 a fifth-order Henry-Lepaute lens had been fitted in the tower. The light then showed a fixed red, varied by a red flash each minute from two panels turned by clockwork, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] The dwelling was listed as brown in 1870, and by 1879 it was white to match the tower.[^3] A boathouse went up in 1880, a fuel house in 1895 and an oil house in 1906, after the light was changed to kerosene.[^1][^3] The nomination describes the 1906 oil house as "a diminutive gable roofed brick building."[^1]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists thirteen principal keepers at Tenants Harbor, from Levi Smalley in 1857 to Leonard B. Dudley, who left in 1933.[^2]

Levi Smalley, of St. George, was the first keeper. LighthouseFriends says he lived in the small red house with his wife, Lucy, and their daughter, Sarah.[^2][^3] He was followed in 1861 by Ebenezer Farnham, a master mariner, who in 1865 handed the post to his son, John A. Farnham, a blacksmith. In 1872 John Farnham was removed for negligence, after the lighthouse inspector wrote that "the condition of his charge is faulty."[^2][^3]

Eben D. Carleton kept the light from 1880 to 1890. William H.H. Wyman followed, and then Thaddeus A. Wallace, who had come to Southern Island in 1892 from Matinicus Rock, where he had risen from third assistant to first assistant in four years, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2][^3]

Wallace died in September 1898. LighthouseFriends says that his wife, Ellen, then took over his duties until Joseph Jellison arrived from Whitehead Lighthouse. The roster lists Ellen Wallace as principal keeper for 1898.[^2][^3] Jellison had been an assistant at Whitehead for two years, under his brother Frank, LighthouseFriends says.[^3]

Jellison kept the light until 1920. Under him the station's hand bell, rung for vessels' signals since about 1884, was replaced in 1908 by a bell in a pyramidal wooden tower.[^3] The bell house standing today is not the original. The nomination says the current bell house "was built by the current occupant."[^1] The next keeper, Willie W. Corbett, served from 1920 to 1923. His son Myron later recalled the coal deliveries, which came in heavy burlap bags of 200 pounds or more. The bags were carried through knee-deep surf and dumped near the boathouse, and the family then had to move them to the storehouse, LighthouseFriends reports.[^2][^3]

Leonard B. Dudley, the last keeper, served from 1923 to 1933. LighthouseFriends says his daughter June helped tend the light and kept the lantern room glass clean.[^2][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On September 17 and 18, 1914, Joseph W. Jellison, keeper of Tennant Harbor Light Station, towed two disabled motor boats to their moorings, the Lighthouse Service's *Bulletin* reports.[^4] LighthouseFriends dates a similar tow by Jellison to 1915, and says he towed another disabled boat to safe anchorage in 1918.[^3] The sources read for this story tie no wreck to the station.

## After the keepers

The light was discontinued on August 30, 1933.[^3] Nearby, a lighted bell buoy was set about 250 yards from the lighthouse, at a cost of $5,869, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] The nomination says the station "was decommissioned and sold to a private individual" that year.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the station sold at auction to a Rockland resident.[^3] In 1935 three businessmen bought it. Adriel Bird, president of a coffee company, had begun sponsoring the Flying Santa flights in 1933.[^3] In 1936 those flights delivered Christmas packages to keepers from Minot's Ledge in Massachusetts to Grand Manan in Canada.[^3]

Walter Whitehead owned the lighthouse from 1953.[^3] In 1978 the painter Andrew Wyeth and his wife, Betsy, bought it from him. Andrew Wyeth painted the station in several works, among them *Easterly*, *Signal Flags* and *Fog Bell*, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] The nomination judges the station to be significant under National Register criteria A and C, for the Maine maritime connections of its place and its mid-nineteenth-century design. It gives the period of significance as 1857 to 1933.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is privately owned, and the US Lighthouse Society lists it as not open to the public.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the light is best viewed from the water, and that its grounds, dwelling and tower are closed.[^3] Southern Island is reached by boat, and LighthouseFriends notes that a keeper's family relied on a boat to reach the mainland.[^3] The island and the lighthouse are private. Visitors should not land there.
