On a bluff on Simmons Island, north of the channel into Kenosha's harbor, stands a cream-colored brick tower built in 1866.[^1][^5] Its keeper's house, finished the next year, stands beside it.[^7] The harbor's main light moved from this tower to a new one on the north pier in 1906.[^8][^7]

## The place

The National Register nomination says Kenosha's harbor "would actually need a great deal of work to become useful."[^1] It quotes an older history of the Great Lakes on the basin: "In the basin the original depth of water was about six feet."[^1] The town was called Southport until 1850.[^7] Its people began petitioning Congress for harbor money in 1837.[^1]

Meanwhile they made their own light. A fire burned every night of the navigation season on flat stones laid atop the stump of an oak tree ten feet (3 m) high, and community members took turns tending it, rotating on a weekly basis.[^1]

In 1840 John Stryker collected sixty dollars for a more modern light: a platform on four posts, twenty-four feet (7.3 m) high, with a three-foot-square lantern on top.[^1] Henry Dodge, the first governor of the Wisconsin Territory, asked Congress for a lighthouse at Southport in 1842, 1843 and 1844. A bill in 1845 appropriated $4,000 for the project.[^7] The government bought lot thirty on the island in 1847 for the tower.[^1]

The island had another name: the Kenosha History Center calls it Simmons Island, formerly Washington Island.[^8] The Center also says the light was originally the first navigational light a mariner saw on entering Wisconsin from Chicago.[^8]

## Building the light

The first federal lighthouse at Southport went up on the island in 1848.[^7] Its brick tower carried five lamps in fourteen-inch reflectors.[^7] By July 1850 it was failing.[^7] Henry B. Miller, superintendent and inspector of lights on the Northwest Lakes, found that half the deck had crumbled and the upper tower was so badly cracked that he thought it would need new brick.[^7]

The bricks were too soft, LighthouseFriends says, and a second tower, a cylinder fifty feet (15 m) tall, replaced the first in 1858.[^7] The History Center calls the 1866 tower the third government lighthouse on the island, which fits a second tower in between.[^8] The nomination mentions only the 1848 tower,[^1] so the 1858 tower rests mainly on LighthouseFriends.[^7]

LighthouseFriends quotes the Lighthouse Board's report for 1865 as saying the inner wall at Kenosha was "literally crumbling to pieces."[^7] Congress appropriated an extra $4,000 in 1866 for a new tower.[^1] Considerable excavation was needed for a firm foundation, and much of the old light station's material went into the new tower.[^1] In the meantime a beacon on a pier, put up that year, carried a fourth-order lens as the harbor's main light until the tower was ready.[^7]

The new tower rises 55 feet (17 m) above the ground.[^1] The nomination says its light stands 76 feet (23 m) above the lake, while the Kenosha History Center gives 74 feet (23 m).[^1][^8] Its walls are four feet (1.2 m) thick, and inside is a cast-iron spiral stair.[^1] It was fitted with a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^1]

According to the 1867 Annual Report of the Lighthouse Board, as the nomination reports it, soil was brought from the mainland and spread on the lot to prevent shifting of the sand.[^1] The federal government declared the station complete in 1867, and the tower "commenced its long watch over Kenosha's harbor."[^1] The keeper's dwelling was completed in 1867.[^7]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society's archive lists the station's keepers from 1848 to 1906, including principal keepers, first and second assistants, an acting assistant and laborers.[^5] The first name on the list is Jeremiah Jordan, for 1848 and 1849.[^5]

Joseph Merrill, who had served in the Civil War, was appointed keeper in 1865.[^7] His daughter Emma served as his assistant and taught in a local school.[^7] When Joseph died in 1871, his wife Lorinda was appointed keeper.[^7][^5] The archive lists her as principal keeper for 1871 and 1872.[^5] In 1872 the two women were turned out of their home, and their jobs went to two men.[^7] LighthouseFriends says the local newspapers tried, without success, to have the Merrills reinstated. The sources do not say why they were dismissed.[^7]

Emma never married. She later worked as a bookkeeper and died in 1931.[^7] Her grave in Greenwood Cemetery was unmarked until 1999, when local historian Ed Werner championed an effort that put a stone with a lighthouse on it.[^7]

Louis Napoleon De Diemar, whom the archive spells De Diemar and LighthouseFriends spells De Deimer, replaced Lorinda Merrill as principal keeper in 1872, and held the post until 1906.[^5][^7] LighthouseFriends says he was born in Montreal around 1840,[^7] and the Lighthouse Digest says the same.[^9] After enlisting in 1861 he lost his right arm at the Battle of Chaplin Hills, and he was discharged in November 1862.[^7][^9]

His wife Mary became his assistant in 1874.[^7] The Lighthouse Digest says she held the post until 1882, and LighthouseFriends gives the same end date.[^9][^7] Assistant keepers came back in 1889, and a second assistant was added in 1901.[^7]

In 1906, when the tower's light was discontinued, De Diemar was transferred to the Kenosha North Pier light.[^9] He later became keeper of the South Haven South Pierhead light in Michigan, where the Digest says he died in 1913.[^9][^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

No storm, wreck or rescue in the sources is tied to the tower itself. The nomination describes the 1848 tower's walls as "crumbling and cracked from the harsh weather conditions along the water's edge."[^1]

The Life-Saving Service also kept a station at Kenosha, Station No. 13 of the Eleventh District.[^10] Its report for 1882 records that on 7 September the crew discovered a steamer about six miles away, apparently disabled.[^10] That event belongs to the life-saving station, not the light, so it is not told here.

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Board changed the tower's signal in the 1890s.[^2] In December 1892 the flash went from every ninety seconds to every forty-five.[^2] In January 1901 the characteristic changed again. The Board's report records only the change; LighthouseFriends says the flash became every fifteen seconds.[^4][^7] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 records a new flagpole, 80 feet (24 m) long, set due south of the tower.[^3]

In 1903 the Lighthouse Board discontinued the tower light.[^1] The nomination says the station was far from the actual harbor area, that pierhead beacons served the area much better than the tower, and that the flashing white light was moved to the breakwater beacon.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives 23 May 1906 as the permanent end, and says the lens went to a metal tower on the north pier.[^7] The Lighthouse Digest gives 28 May 1906.[^9] The sources disagree on the final date, so both 1906 dates are given here, beside the nomination's 1903 date for the tower light.

The keeper's dwelling stayed in use, because a keeper was still needed for the harbor lights.[^1] The nomination says Congress appropriated $2,500 in 1907 for additions so that three men could live there.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates a brick addition to 1908.[^7]

In 1913 the lantern room came off the tower, LighthouseFriends says.[^7] The nomination says a fifteen-foot (4.6 m) steel tower and flagstaff were secured to the top that year, for storm warning signals.[^1] LighthouseFriends and the Kenosha History Center say a twenty-five-foot (7.6 m) tripod mast replaced the lantern room.[^7][^8] The nomination instead dates the removal of the cupola and lantern to 1959.[^1]

The Coast Guard's dates also differ. LighthouseFriends says it left the station in 1940, after the breakwater and pierhead lights were automated, and that the city received the property in 1955.[^7] The nomination says the Light Service handed care of the station to the Coast Guard in 1942.[^1] An inspection in 1941 rated the remaining buildings "only fair."[^1] In 1966 the city's chief building inspector reported the tower in "poor condition."[^1]

The station was listed on the National Register in 1990.[^7] The city and the county historical society began restoration in 1991.[^7] A replica lantern room went up on 7 May 1994, helped by a state grant.[^7] LighthouseFriends dates the restoration of the keeper's house from 2004, but the History Center says work on the 1867 residence began in 2001.[^7][^8] The Southport Light Station Museum opened on 15 May 2010.[^7]

## Today

The tower and keeper's house are the Southport Light Station Museum, which consists of the 1866 lighthouse and the 1867 keeper's residence.[^8] The Kenosha History Center says the museum is open from mid-May through October.[^8] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is owned by the City of Kenosha, and that the tower and dwelling are open in season, typically staffed by the History Center from Thursday through Sunday.[^7]

A beacon was switched on inside the replica lantern for Fourth of July celebrations in 1996, and LighthouseFriends says it is not a navigational aid.[^7] The Lighthouse Society's archive still lists the station as an active aid to navigation.[^5] The sources disagree on whether the light is still an aid to navigation. The nomination describes the tower's windows and door as boarded up in 1989, so ask the museum about climbing before going up.[^1][^7]

The Kenosha History Center also says the 1866 light is listed on the Wisconsin and National registers.[^8]
