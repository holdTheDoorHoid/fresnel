Quaco Head Lighthouse stands on the headland above St. Martins, New Brunswick, on the Bay of Fundy coast east of Saint John.[^1][^8] The light on the head is the third built for the station: a tower on a ledge off the headland from 1835, a replacement on West Head after a fire in 1881, and the square tower standing now, which Lighthouse Friends dates to 1966.[^1]

## The place

The hazard was a reef and ledges running out from the headland.[^1][^2] In 1834 the New Brunswick government set money aside for a buoy or beacon on the Quaco Ledges.[^1] In 1835, Lighthouse Friends says, the Bay of Fundy commissioners of lights won approval to spend that money on a manned light on Thrum Cap, a ledge west of St. Martins that lies off the headland.[^1]

The reef still needed marking later.[^2] The Commissioner of Lights' report printed in 1901 says that when the bell buoy at the end of the reef was placed that spring, "its position was changed so as to better mark the extremity of the reef."[^2]

St. Martins was a shipbuilding town.[^1] In the 1800s, by Lighthouse Friends' reckoning, it was New Brunswick's third-largest shipbuilding centre, with over 500 vessels launched there.[^1]

## Building the light

The first tower was octagonal and banded red and white, with a dwelling attached. At low tide it could be reached on foot.[^1]

Its six lamps, each set behind a 61 cm (24 in) reflector, turned once every twenty seconds: fourteen seconds of light, then six of dark.[^1] The machinery ran on weights that the keeper wound up every four hours, and a bell rang with each turn to show the light was working.[^1]

Work went on through the 1840s and 1850s.[^1] In 1846 a lumber breastwork went up on the crumbling rock.[^1] In 1848 the tower was raised by 6 m (20 ft), to 14 m (46 ft), and in 1849 six new lamps came from England.[^1] The revolving machinery was replaced in 1850.[^1] In 1875 Chanteloup of Montreal supplied a new lantern and revolving machinery for $2,469, and the revolving light went into service on 18 October 1875.[^1] In 1877 a frame tower was built near the lighthouse to carry a fog bell.[^1]

After the fire, the Department had a new light built on West Head, a contract for which went to D.W. Clark at $2,890.[^1] The square, pyramidal tower stood 15 m (49 ft) from base to vane and had a keeper's dwelling attached.[^1] The white revolving light was first shown on 20 April 1883, with a focal plane 34 m (110 ft) above high water, and it could be seen for sixteen miles.[^1]

The fog signal came later.[^1] An iron bell buoy was moored off the headland in 1886, and a steam fog alarm began operating on 1 September 1887.[^1] The Department's report published in 1907 records a new six-room wooden dwelling for the engineer, about 46 m (150 ft) west of the station; Lighthouse Friends dates it to 1906.[^4][^1]

## The keepers

Thomas Lamb was keeper from 1835 until he retired on 1 October 1868.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says he had first been keeper at Gannet Rock.[^1] His salary of £100 a year was raised to £150 in 1841 "due to the great privations and dangers" of the offshore post, where he could not raise a garden or keep a cow.[^1]

William Love, his assistant, was appointed head keeper in 1868 at $400 a year and retired in 1884.[^1]

Charles Brown took over in 1884 and kept the light until 1919.[^1] The keeper table in the report published in 1906 lists Charles Brown against the "Quaco West Head Light"; the report published in 1907 calls the same post the "Quaco West End Light".[^3][^5] The same table lists a separate "Quaco Pier Light", so the station's several names need checking before a keeper is matched to it.[^3]

The fog alarm had its own engineer.[^1] Lorenzo B. Bradshaw, hired in 1887, appears in the 1906 table as "Quaco West Head Fog Alarm".[^1][^3] Lighthouse Friends then lists Mrs. M. Bradshaw in 1916 and Harry Bradshaw from 1917 to 1948 for the fog alarm, and also gives a Harry Bradshaw as keeper from 1919 to 1948. The sources do not say whether these are one man or two.[^1]

The keepers' list runs on to 1987, when Joe S. Russell is the last name Lighthouse Friends gives.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A storm in 1842 washed away part of the rock under the light, along with two of the boats used to reach it.[^1] Lamb petitioned for money to replace the boats and received £20 in 1844.[^1] The next year he was paid £10 for helping the crews of vessels stranded near the light.[^1]

The Saxby Gale of October 1869 damaged the lighthouse, and $298 in repairs was completed.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the soft sandstone was eroding so fast that the light would soon have to move to the mainland.[^1] A severe gale in November 1872 carried away part of the protection works, and $730 was spent on repairs.[^1]

In the end it was fire, not erosion, that brought the first station down.[^1] On 17 June 1881 a faulty flue between the ceiling and roof of the keeper's dwelling started a fire that burned every building at the station.[^1] A temporary light on a pole went up on West Head on 9 July.[^1]

The Department's report published in 1909 lists, among its hydrographic notes under "Affecting the Atlantic Coast," that a "Quaco lighthouse" was destroyed by storm.[^6] The same report describes work on the breakwater light at Quaco, and the notice does not say which light it meant.[^6] Rowlett's directory lists a separate Quaco Pier light, which stood from 1887 to 1908, and says a storm carried that pier and light away in 1908.[^7] The sources do not settle whether the notice refers to that light. Neither Lighthouse Friends nor Rowlett mentions a storm loss of the head light in this period.[^1][^7]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends dates the present square tower to 1966, when it went up on one corner of a concrete fog-signal building.[^1] Rowlett dates it to 1976, with a question mark.[^7] Rowlett also reports that around 2017 the Coast Guard pulled down the fog-signal building and left the tower alone, and that the tower was later clad in composite aluminum panels.[^7] The Coast Guard's list still carries a fog horn, blasting three seconds in every thirty.[^8] The sources do not agree on what the fog signal is now.

The 1883 lantern room is preserved on a replica tower in St. Martins that now serves as the visitor information centre.[^1] Rowlett dates the replica to 1983 and places the lighthouse's Fresnel lens at the Quaco Museum, also in St. Martins.[^7]

## Today

The Coast Guard's list gives a white flash every ten seconds, with the focal plane 26 m above the water and a nominal range of 21 nautical miles.[^8] It describes the tower as "White rectangular tower with red top," 11.7 m high.[^8]

Lighthouse Friends says the Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and that the grounds are open but the tower is closed.[^1] The road runs from Route 111 toward St. Martins, then along West Quaco Road to Lighthouse Road.[^1]
