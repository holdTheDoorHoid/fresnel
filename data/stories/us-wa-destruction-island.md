Destruction Island Light is a 94-foot (29 m) dressed-stone tower on a rocky island about three miles (5 km) off the Washington coast, and its first-order lens first showed on New Year's Day, 1892.[^1]

## The place

Destruction Island is about 30 acres (12 ha) of rock, and it is the only offshore island on Washington's outer coast.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory puts it 3.5 miles (6 km) off the coast, where HistoryLink says about three miles.[^12][^1] Its name is older than its lighthouse. The Spanish naval officer Juan Francisco de la Bodega y Quadra passed it about 18 September 1775 and recorded it on his charts as Isla de Dolores.[^1] In 1787 the English fur trader Charles William Barkley lost six men when his ship's boat was attacked near the mouth of what is now the Hoh River. Barkley named the river "Destruction River."[^1] Five years later George Vancouver applied that name to Quadra's island.[^1]

The island is a landfall light for ships heading for the Strait of Juan de Fuca. It also warns coastal ships of rocks and ledges that lie up to a mile offshore to the south.[^1] The Lighthouse Board first reserved its 30 acres for lighthouse purposes on 8 June 1866.[^1] In 1882 the Board asked for an $85,000 appropriation for a first-order light and fog signal, and it eventually received the money.[^1]

Wrecks added to the case. The bark *Cassandra Adams*, sailing from San Francisco to Tacoma, ran into fog and struck a reef near the island at 8:15 in the morning of 10 August 1888. Heavy seas soon broke her up.[^1] A Port Townsend paper, the *Puget Sound Weekly Argus*, reported her loss on a reef near the island in its issue of 23 August 1888, and called the bark a total loss. The date of the wreck is hard to read in the scan. The paper's text seems to give the 16th of the month and a Thursday, which would be 16 August, not HistoryLink's 10 August (a Friday).[^7][^1] The US Lighthouse Society's historian counts several wrecks around the island in 1888 and 1889.[^3]

## Building the light

Construction began in August 1888, after Congress added $10,000 to the appropriation, too late to help the *Cassandra Adams*.[^1] When Christian Zauner arrived in August 1889 to become head keeper, the tower was not yet up. His house, the assistants' house, a cistern and a barn were finished.[^1] The 94-foot tower, sheathed in iron, went up from October 1890 and was ready for occupancy in November 1891.[^1]

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 says that defective work by the contractors on the keepers' dwellings had been remedied or replaced by good work.[^4]

The lens was made in Paris by Henri LePaute and Sons in 1888. Its five wicks each burned two gallons of oil a night, and the keepers carried every gallon up a 115-step circular staircase.[^1] The Light-House Board's report gives 1 November 1891 as the day the fog signal was put into operation. Its list of new fog signals dates the siren to 15 November 1891. The light was exhibited continuously on and after 1 January 1892.[^4]

A Portland newspaper's paragraph, reprinted in the Seattle *Post-Intelligencer* on 1 December 1891, said the light was ready to be lit the night before. The paper said the Lighthouse Board had put it off until New Year's Day, and asked, "Just what is to be gained by this." It noted that the keeper was on the spot.[^5] On 1 January 1892, at 4:26 in the afternoon, Zauner lit the five wicks for the first time.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1891 as the year the station was established, and the Lighthouse Directory also gives 1891 for the light.[^2][^12] The Light-House Board's report and HistoryLink give 1 January 1892 as the first lighting.[^4][^1]

## The keepers

HistoryLink says the head keeper typically had two assistants, and married keepers could bring their families to the island. The families made a small community with its own school, vegetables and chickens, and milk and butter from cows kept on the island. Government rations came on periodic tender visits.[^1] On 8 January 1892 the lighthouse steamer *Manzanita* discharged ten tons of coal for the light, a week after the first lighting.[^6]

The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists 135 stints at the station between 1889 and 1965: 16 principal keepers, 25 first assistants, 38 second assistants, 42 third assistants, one laborer and 13 Coast Guard entries.[^2] The first on the list is Christian Zauner, who is given as principal keeper from 1889 to 1898.[^2]

Albert Beyer is the principal keeper the archive lists for 1919 to 1922.[^2] In the Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1921, he is named for assisting the launch *Myrtle May*, which had a scow in tow, and for giving its crew and passengers shelter and provisions.[^10] In the report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1922, Beyer, keeper; William Hill, assistant keeper; and Harvey A. Walker, third assistant keeper, are named for helping a disabled launch that had drifted helplessly for 20 hours. The keepers cared for its three men "until it was practicable for them to leave the station."[^11]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's list of rescues for the fiscal year 1913, which ran from July 1912 to June 1913, names keeper Clifford B. Hermann and his assistants, Carl Lien and Anders G. Berner. On 30 November 1912 they gave aid to the officers and crew of a schooner that had anchored in distress near the station.[^8]

On 1 May 1927 keepers Harry Oakley, Karl Luoma and Jens Pedersen made an effort to rescue the two men in a fishing boat that had been wrecked on a reef at the west end of the island. The Bulletin commended the three keepers, but it says the effort was unsuccessful.[^9]

During the Second World War, the island's Coast Guard crews added watching for enemy ships and submarines to their routine. HistoryLink reports that the sentries did not spot any Japanese submarines.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1939 the Coast Guard absorbed the Lighthouse Service. Rotating crews gradually replaced the long-serving keepers. Those on the island spent six weeks on duty, then two and a half weeks ashore.[^1] Electric bulbs and a motor replaced the oil lamps and clockwork sometime during the Second World War.[^1]

In 1963 the Coast Guard proposed closing the light, but mariners protested and it stayed on. The light was automated in 1968, and the last resident keepers left in the early 1970s.[^1]

In 1995 a small solar-powered beacon replaced the Fresnel lens, and the Coast Guard took the lens off the island that year for security reasons. It went on display at the Westport Maritime Museum in 1998.[^1][^12]

The Lighthouse Directory says the light was extinguished in April 2008, despite some protests.[^12] The US Lighthouse Society's record still lists the light as an active aid to navigation. The two sources disagree, and this story does not settle the question.[^2]

## Today

The 1891 lighthouse and fog-signal building remain, along with the marine railway, a cart-storage shed and two small buildings once used for oil storage. Most other buildings of the old keepers' community have been burned or demolished.[^1]

The island is part of the Olympic National Marine Sanctuary and a federal wildlife refuge, and visits are restricted to protect seabirds and sea mammals.[^1] The site and tower are closed. The Lighthouse Directory says the island is "accessible only by boat in very dangerous seas," that the Coast Guard owns the light, and that the Quillayute Needles National Wildlife Refuge manages the site.[^12] The US Lighthouse Society record says the station is not open to the public.[^2] From U.S. 101, between Kalaloch and Ruby Beach, the island and its light can be seen. HistoryLink described that view in 2004.[^1]
