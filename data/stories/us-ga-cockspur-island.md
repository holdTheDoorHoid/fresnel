Cockspur Island Light is a brick tower on an oyster-shell islet at the edge of Cockspur Island, in the Savannah River about twelve miles from the port of Savannah.[^1][^7] It marked the South Channel below Fort Pulaski.[^1] It was put out for navigation in 1909 and relit in 2007 with a solar-powered beacon.[^1][^7]

## The place

The river reaches the sea past a string of islands. Cockspur, Long and Elba sit between the river's mouth and the port, and between them the river splits into a North Channel and a South Channel.[^7] To accommodate large freighters, the busy port of Savannah sent its traffic by the deeper North Channel, and the National Park Service says the light stopped guiding ships up the shallow South Channel.[^1]

The islet is oyster shell and marsh grass and is often covered at high tide.[^1] The light stood off the southeastern tip of Cockspur Island, near the fort.[^1][^7]

## Building the light

The sources do not agree on when Congress first paid for a light here. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1934 says the first appropriation was made in 1834 and the money was reappropriated in 1837.[^3] LighthouseFriends gives an earlier act, dated 3 March 1832, for an unlit beacon on the oyster beds.[^7]

Documented references suggest the first brick tower, used as a daymark, was built between March 1837 and November 1839.[^1] In 1848 the New York architect John Norris was hired to supervise an illuminated station, and his commission included a keeper's house.[^1] The NPS describes the first light as a fixed white light from five lamps, with 14-inch reflectors, visible for nine miles.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives three lamps for the same light.[^7]

The 1854 hurricane destroyed that tower.[^1] It was rebuilt on the same foundation the next year.[^1] The Bulletin says restorations were made in 1855 and the keeper's house was rebuilt.[^3] LighthouseFriends puts the rebuilt tower in 1856 and gives it a fifth-order lens.[^7] The USLHS archive gives 1857 for the tower and a sixth-order Fresnel lens installed that year.[^2] The standing tower is the rebuilt one, but the year and the lens are disputed.[^1][^7]

## The keepers

The first keeper of the Cockspur and Oyster Beds lights was John H. Lightburn. He lived on Cockspur Island near Fort Pulaski and went out to the towers each day to service them.[^2][^7]

Cornelius Maher drowned in 1853 when his boat capsized in the river, according to LighthouseFriends.[^7] His wife, Mary Maher, took over the light and stayed three more years.[^7] The USLHS roster lists her as principal keeper from 1853 to 1856.[^2][^7]

Patrick Egan was principal keeper twice, from 1856 to 1867 and again from 1868 to 1877.[^2] The roster also lists John, Thomas and Robert Egan among the first assistants between 1867 and 1876.[^2] In August 1871 Patrick Egan set out in a heavy storm with two of his sons, Michael and Thomas.[^7] Their boat capsized.[^7] Thomas drowned, and his body was not recovered.[^7] The roster has a Thomas Egan as first assistant for 1870 and 1871, but the sources do not say whether he was the son who drowned.[^2][^7]

The keepers' dwelling needed repair more than once.[^3][^7] The Bulletin says that in 1880 the dwelling was again out of repair.[^3] LighthouseFriends says lightning struck it in April 1880.[^7]

In 1893 the keeper of Cockspur Island and the keeper of Oyster Beds Light were living in casemates at Fort Pulaski.[^3] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year 1900 called those quarters "damp and unsatisfactory."[^4] Congress was petitioned in 1902 for a dwelling on top of the fort, and in 1906 the War Department let the keepers occupy a converted dwelling there.[^7][^2]

The Board also spent money on the landings.[^5][^6] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 it built a boathouse and a landing place on Cockspur Island, set on piles, and in the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 it extended both boat landings by 20 feet (6 m).[^5][^6]

Gustaf Ohman was principal keeper from 1901 to 1911 on the roster, two years after the light was put out.[^2] The sources do not explain why the roster runs on that long.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The 1854 hurricane destroyed the first tower.[^1][^7] On 27 August 1881 a storm raised the water on Cockspur Island 23 feet (7 m) above sea level, according to the National Park Service.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it was 23 feet above normal.[^1][^7] The surge filled the lighthouse and destroyed the keeper's residence.[^1][^2] The Savannah Morning News of 30 August 1881 printed a report of wreckage scattered through the woods and fields, including headboards "probably from the soldiers' graves." It said that most of this wreckage "probably came from Cockspur Island."[^8]

In the great hurricane of 1893, the head keeper, Jeremiah Keane, the assistant keeper Charles Sisson and two Fort Pulaski caretakers took refuge in the northwest stair tower of the fort.[^1][^2]

At the start of the Civil War the light was temporarily extinguished.[^1] On 10 April 1862 Union batteries on Tybee Island opened a bombardment of Fort Pulaski, with the tower in direct line of fire.[^1] The National Park Service says the tower suffered only minor damage.[^1] The light was relit and painted white as a daymark on 25 April 1866.[^1]

## After the keepers

The beacon was put out on 1 June 1909.[^1] The Bulletin reported in 1934 that a pair of bald-headed eagles had nested in the old tower and perched on its balcony railing.[^3] The Bulletin called the tower Cockspur Island Beacon and noted that visitors on the way to Fort Pulaski saw it.[^3]

The Coast Guard transferred the lighthouse to the National Park Service by presidential proclamation on 14 August 1958.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the tower was repaired in a two-stage restoration from 1995 to 2000, with a new lantern room and repointed brickwork.[^7] The light was relit on 18 March 2007 with a solar-powered beacon.[^7] In 2007 shipworms were found in wooden timbers that erosion had exposed.[^7] The Georgia Trust for Historic Preservation put the tower on its 2008 Places in Peril list.[^7] The money for a thorough restoration took several years to come, but it allowed the Army Corps of Engineers to take measures to preserve the land around the lighthouse.[^7] A Fort Pulaski preservation team stabilized the masonry in the summer of 2021.[^7]

## Today

The lighthouse and its island are closed to the public.[^1] The National Park Service offers an overlook trail, which gives visitors the best chance to get a closer look at the tower from land.[^1] The trail is about 0.65 miles long and starts just north of the fort.[^7] To get close to the tower you need a boat or a kayak.[^7] The tower was closed to climbing in 2015 for safety.[^7]
