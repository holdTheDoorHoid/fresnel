Long Island Head Light stands at the northeast end of Long Island, the largest island in Boston Harbor, where it marked the Broad Sound approach to the city.[^1][^3][^10] The brick tower there now was first lit on 1 November 1900, according to the Board's 1901 report, but the 1981 National Register nomination dates the present tower to 1819.[^4][^2] Long Island is closed to the public today, and LighthouseFriends says the light is best viewed from the water.[^11][^3]

## The place

Long Island sits in the middle of the harbor, and its shoreline runs about a mile.[^10] Ships entering Boston Harbor from Broad Sound passed the island, and a committee of the Boston Marine Society petitioned Congressman Jonathan Mason for a light there, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] In 1879 the Lighthouse Board called the light "the only guide into Boston by the Broad Sound passage," as LighthouseFriends quotes the Board's report.[^3] The National Park Service says a rudimentary lighthouse was built on the head in 1794, before the more prominent tower of 1819, and that the light was later moved to make room for coastal fortifications.[^10]

The 1981 National Register nomination says the light was known for many years as the Inner Harbor Light, that it was in two other locations before it was built on the highest point at the north end of the island, and that the remains of Fort Strong lie "a short distance from the tower."[^2]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $11,500 on 3 March 1819 for a light-house on Long Island Head, among other works, LighthouseFriends reports.[^3] Keeper Jonathan Lawrence first lit the lamps on 9 October 1819, in a seven-foot (2.1 m) lantern on a stone tower twenty feet (6.1 m) tall.[^3]

The sources do not agree on the date of the brick tower. The nomination's chronology dates the present tower to 1819, but its statement of significance says the brick tower was "Erected in 1817."[^2] The Board's 1901 report describes a new brick tower built in 1900 at a new site.[^4] Both dates cannot describe the same brick tower.

Inspections in the 1840s were "less than satisfactory," the National Park Service's account says.[^9] Hard winters had cracked and broken the glass and rotted wood inside the tower, and "It was clear the lighthouse needed a total overhaul."[^9] LighthouseFriends says an 1842 inspection found the tower leaky, its walls cracked by frost, and its construction "rude."[^3] In 1844 a cast-iron tower, 34 feet (10 m) tall, went up on the old site. The NPS account calls it the first cast-iron lighthouse in the United States.[^9][^3]

In 1857 a fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps and reflectors, and the tower was lined with brick, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] The sources differ on the optic. The US Lighthouse Society's record gives a fourth-order Fresnel as the original optic, installed in 1857.[^1] The Board's report for the fiscal year to June 1901 says the light in the old tower was a three-and-a-half-order light.[^5] The NPS account also gives a three-and-a-half-order Fresnel as the original lens.[^9]

The Board's 1879 report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, called the light "one of the most important on this part of the coast" and proposed a new dwelling and tower with a third-order light.[^3] The new iron tower and dwelling were finished in 1881, with a three-and-a-half-order lens that gave a fixed white light 128 feet (39 m) above the water.[^3] In the fiscal year ended June 30, 1892, the Board reports that a brick oil house was built at this station; LighthouseFriends dates it to 1892 as well.[^6][^3]

The 1900 move was made because of the guns on a new coast battery.[^13] The Board's 1899 report said the battery's guns endangered the light whenever they were fired, and proposed a move.[^13] The Board's 1901 report says the new brick tower was built where the light "is not exposed to injury by the firing of guns in the new seacoast," with a $4,500 appropriation under the act of June 6, 1900.[^4][^7] The old iron tower was sold at auction, and the light was shown from the new tower on 1 November 1900.[^4] The Board records that on 30 October the light was changed from a three-and-a-half-order light in the old tower to a fourth-order light in the new one. On 6 November the three-and-a-half-order light was reestablished and the fourth-order light discontinued.[^5]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster names ten principal keepers for Long Island Head, from 1819 to 1918, and one contractor with no years.[^1] The first was Jonathan Lawrence, who kept the light from 1819 to 1825. LighthouseFriends says he was a veteran of the War of 1812 who died at the lighthouse in October 1825 from complications of injuries he had received at the Battle of Fort Erie.[^3]

Charles A. Beck kept the light from 1825 to 1853, the longest stint in the roster.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he was a native of Sweden who had served aboard USS Constitution, and that he died in the keeper's residence in August 1853.[^3] The nomination says that in 1845 Beck ran a signal system for pilots, hoisting a black ball when more pilots were needed to guide ships in.[^2] A visitor that year, James Lloyd Homer, wrote that Beck had "discharged the duties of his office with fidelity for many years," as LighthouseFriends quotes Homer's 1848 book.[^3]

After Beck came George Henchman (1853 to 1857), Richard Nichols (recorded for 1857 only), John H. Litchfield (1857 to 1861), John Spear (1861 to 1864), Pliny B. Small (1864 to 1881), Thomas H. Lyndon (1881 to 1894) and John B. Carter (1894 to 1906).[^1] Carter's stint covered the move to the brick tower.[^1][^4]

Edwin Tarr came to the light in 1906 and was its last keeper.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he passed away on 8 January 1918, "as he sat surveying the sea."[^3] The National Park Service's account says his position was never refilled, and that custodians kept the light lit until it was automated in 1929.[^9] LighthouseFriends names one of them: in 1921 Mervin E. Wright was paid one dollar to serve as custodian.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources give few dated storms for this station. LighthouseFriends says a walkway on trestles was built out to the boathouse in 1886, and that three years later the boathouse was washed away.[^3] The source does not say what washed it away.[^3]

LighthouseFriends also tells of Tarr's funeral, which was held in the keeper's dwelling.[^3] A storm struck during the service and coated the hillside in ice. As the four pallbearers went down the path to the wharf, the coffin hit the ground and slid, and the men were carried down the slope on it. LighthouseFriends attributes this account to a Fort Strong soldier who was one of the pallbearers.[^3]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1929, the National Park Service's account says, when the custodians stopped.[^9] In 1918 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin had already listed a change at Long Island Head from incandescent oil vapor to electric incandescent.[^8] The nomination records that in 1966 the light was a three-and-a-half-order Fresnel lens giving 14,000 candlepower, and that in 1979 the Coast Guard reported water damage to the tower.[^2] The NPS account says the light was discontinued in 1982, and that "in 1985, the US Coast Guard installed a solar-powered light, which still operates today."[^9] The nomination says the light was re-established in 1985 and converted to solar power.[^2] The tower was added to the National Register in 1987, and in 2011 the National Park Service acquired the lighthouse.[^9]

## Today

The National Park Service owns the lighthouse. The NPS account says it is the only property on the Boston Harbor Islands solely owned by the federal government.[^9] The NPS page describes the island as home to the light and to "the now closed City of Boston social services facilities."[^10] The Long Island Bridge, which connected Long Island to Moon Island, was closed on an emergency basis in 2014, and its superstructure was removed in 2015.[^12] The park's page says Long Island is closed to the public, and that visitation is restricted because of hazards from aging structures.[^11]

The brick tower stands 52 feet (16 m) tall, and its solar-powered light gives a white flash every 2.5 seconds.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the light is best viewed from the water.[^3]
