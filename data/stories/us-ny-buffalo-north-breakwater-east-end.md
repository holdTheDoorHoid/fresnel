The "bottle light" is a boiler-plate beacon, named for its resemblance to a glass milk bottle, that was built on Buffalo's new breakwater in 1903 and marked an entrance to Buffalo Harbor until 1985.[^2] It is listed on the National Register as the Buffalo North Breakwater South End Light.[^1] Since 1985 it has stood beside the 1833 Buffalo Main Lighthouse, on the Coast Guard grounds at the mouth of the Buffalo River.[^2]

## The place

The breakwater was new in 1902.[^2] LighthouseFriends says it was being finished just north of the Buffalo River entrance, and that the Lighthouse Board put a red post lantern on its southern end, to mark the northern side of the harbor's northern entrance, while a permanent beacon was being designed and built.[^2] The Board's report for 1900 describes the works as the main southern entrance of the new breakwater, and its report for 1901 heads the entry "Buffalo Breakwater, southern entrance."[^3][^4] The sources do not agree on which entrance the light marked, and this history does not choose between them.

The older Buffalo Breakwater North End light station has its own record. The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive dates it to 1872 and lists it as no longer existing.[^6] The Lighthouse Service's Bulletin for 1912 reports a new concrete foundation completed at that station.[^7] It is a separate station, and this history does not use its keepers.

## Building the light

The act approved on 6 June 1900 appropriated $45,000 for light and fog-signal stations at the entrance.[^3] By the end of that fiscal year, the plans for the towers, the fog-signal house and their foundations were complete, and plans for the keeper's dwelling were being prepared.[^3]

The Board's report for the following year says the completed plans had to change. Two severe storms made it necessary to modify them "to provide greater strength," and the modified plans were practically complete when the year ended.[^4] The report does not date the storms.

The permanent light went up in 1903.[^2] LighthouseFriends says it was installed on the breakwater that year, and it gives the tower's build: 3/8-inch boiler plate, a base about ten feet three-quarters of an inch (3.2 m) across, four round windows and a curved iron door. A winch and cable inside the base hauled the lens lantern up, the keeper climbed to it by an outside ladder, and at the top an egg-shaped ventilator ball rested on four iron posts. With the ball, the total height is 23 feet 3/4 inch (about 7.0 m).[^2] LighthouseFriends says the nickname "bottle light" came from the tower's resemblance to an old glass milk bottle.[^2] The National Register record gives 1903 as the light's significant year.[^1]

## The keepers

No keeper is named for this light in the sources. The Society's place record classes it as a minor aid to navigation and lists no keepers for it.[^5] In 1909, LighthouseFriends says, the fixed red light from its lens lantern was maintained by the keeper of Buffalo Main Lighthouse.[^2] The Society's keeper roster for the older North End station runs from 1872 to 1914, and it belongs to that station, not to this one.[^6]

## After the keepers

In 1960 the light was given a battery-operated, 12-volt lamp, and its ventilator ball was taken off.[^2] The National Register record, dated 4 August 1983, lists the light under its breakwater name.[^1] In 1985 it was removed from the breakwater and given a new home next to the original 1833 Buffalo Main Lighthouse. It was then restored and capped with a reproduction of its ventilator ball, and it is now part of a 1,400-foot (430 m) promenade with signs about Buffalo's waterfront.[^2]

## Today

The light stands on the Coast Guard station grounds beside Buffalo Main Lighthouse, on the south side of the Buffalo River entrance.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the Buffalo Lighthouse Association owns it. Its visitor notes differ: one says the grounds are open and the tower closed, and the other says the grounds are open with a Coast Guard escort.[^2] Check before you go. After 9/11 the Coast Guard station admitted visitors only with an escort. Under a 2010 deal the Coast Guard gave up the land around the light, and access was restored in late 2011.[^2] The tower is closed, and it should not be climbed.
