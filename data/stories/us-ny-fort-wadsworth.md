Fort Wadsworth Light is a brick tower on top of Battery Weed, on the Staten Island shore of the Narrows, the strait that leads into New York Harbor.[^1][^6] The Lighthouse Service lit it in 1903 to replace the Fort Tompkins light nearby.[^1] It was discontinued in 1965, and volunteers restored it and relit it in 2005.[^1] It stands nearly under the western end of the Verrazano-Narrows Bridge.[^1][^6]

## The place

The harbor entrance had a light on the bluff at Fort Tompkins long before the bridge.[^1] In 1826 Congress appropriated $30,000 for a lighthouse on the bluff near Fort Tompkins.[^1] Its first tower was conical and rose forty feet (12 m) to the base of its lantern room.[^1] It opened in 1828.[^1]

The tower and keeper's dwelling were positioned so close to Fort Tompkins that practice artillery firings often broke the lantern room glass.[^1] In an 1843 report, E. Curtis, the New York Superintendent of Lights, wrote, as LighthouseFriends quotes him, that "The light is not a good one."[^1] Along with the fort's expansion, that problem required a search for a new site, and on 20 December 1873 a second tower in a gingerbread style went into operation there.[^1]

## Building the light

By 1892 the Lighthouse Board judged the Fort Tompkins light too far back from the point it was meant to mark.[^2] Its report for fiscal 1900 quotes the Board's 1892 statement: "The light at Fort Tompkins at present is well back of the point it is intended to mark." The Board proposed moving it "to an angle of the stone fort at Fort Wadsworth."[^2]

Congress appropriated $1,500 on 11 June 1896 for the move.[^1] That sum was too little, so the Board sought permission from the War Department to put a fog bell on the sea wall instead.[^1] The Board's report for fiscal 1899 records that "a fog bell with striking apparatus was established May 16, 1898, on the easterly angle of the sea wall at Fort Wadsworth."[^3] LighthouseFriends says the bell weighed 1,282 pounds, was struck by machinery every fifteen seconds, and had William Boyle as its keeper.[^1]

On 3 March 1901 Congress provided $12,900 for the new light and fog signal.[^1] The Board's report for that year gives the appropriation as made "to remove this light and to establish a light and fog-signal at Fort Wadsworth."[^4] The Board had first chosen a site for the keeper's dwelling on the Appleton tract, but the War Department had since occupied it, so another site was selected.[^4]

Work on the new tower began on 16 October 1902. The new light and fog signal began showing on 11 May 1903, replacing the Fort Tompkins light and the Fort Wadsworth fog signal.[^1] The fog bell and its machinery went to the new station on Battery Weed.[^1] The old Fort Tompkins lighthouse was moved to serve as the keeper's dwelling.[^1] Its contractor began work on 19 October 1903 and finished the following summer.[^1]

## The keepers

The first head keeper of the new light was William B. Davis, Jr.[^7] LighthouseFriends's list of head keepers, which begins with the Fort Tompkins keepers in 1828, gives him as head keeper from 1870 to 1903.[^1] The Lighthouse Service roster starts his Fort Wadsworth stint in 1903.[^7] After him came Adolph Obman (1903 to 1904), William H. De Luce (1904 to 1906) and Nelson L. Ackerman (1906 to 1911).[^7]

Ernest J. Larsson served as first assistant from 1908 to 1911, then as head keeper from 1911 to 1929.[^7] Thomas Pickup kept the light from 1929 to 1942.[^7] Edrick W. Farnham was first assistant for most of 1916 to 1935. His stint had a gap in 1917, when George F. Reid filled in from June to December.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 4 September 1915 the Lighthouse Service tender Gardenia, under Eugene P. Shevlin, master, "took in tow and beached the schooner Highland" near Fort Wadsworth "when the schooner was found to be in danger of sinking."[^5]

## After the keepers

The Verrazano-Narrows Bridge opened in 1965 and made the light obsolete, so it was discontinued.[^1] The lighthouse then passed to the Army, then the Navy, and in 1995 to the National Park Service.[^1] Coast Guard personnel lived on at the fort after the light was discontinued.[^1]

When volunteers took the tower on, it was in poor shape.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes the lantern room as rusted, the roof gone, and every window broken by vandals long before.[^1]

Joe Esposito, a Staten Island man who had served as a volunteer keeper at the Staten Island Lighthouse for nine years, began a campaign to save it.[^1] In 2002 he presented a restoration plan to the Park Service, which approved it.[^1] For more than two years volunteers led by him worked on the tower.[^1] They carried materials up sixty-six steps to the roof of Battery Weed, and the Park Service contributed $27,000 for materials.[^1] The glass in the curved windows was replaced with Lexan.[^1] On 24 September 2005 the now solar-powered tower was relit in a ceremony.[^1]

## Today

The National Park Service owns the lighthouse.[^1] The grounds are open and the tower is closed, though occasional tours bring visitors close to it.[^1] Fort Wadsworth itself is open daily from 6 a.m. to 9 p.m.[^8] The Lighthouse Society's record lists the light as an active aid to navigation.[^6] It records the current optic as removed.[^6] The original optic was a fourth-order Fresnel lens installed in 1903.[^6]
