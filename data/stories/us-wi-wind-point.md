Wind Point Light is a tall brick tower on a low headland of western Lake Michigan, a few miles north of Racine, Wisconsin. It was first lit on 15 November 1880 and, with a modern beacon in place of its Fresnel lens, still shows a light today.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The Light-House Board began urging a light on Racine Point in 1870. The point lies about three and a half miles (5.6 km) north of Racine and eighteen miles (29 km) south of Milwaukee, and the Board argued that the Racine light was hidden from ships coming down from the north by the point itself.[^1] The 1979 Historic American Engineering Record inventory puts it the same way: the point could not be seen well by vessels approaching Racine from the north, and it blocked the Racine lights from view.[^2] The Board also wanted a light that would help mariners clear Racine Reef, which lies well offshore, and cut the wrecks near the point.[^1] One account says the point was named for a tall, windblown tree that mariners used as a landmark.[^1]

## Building the light

The Board repeated its request each year until Congress paid. The two accounts differ on the details: the lighthouse history on the station's own website and LighthouseFriends give an appropriation of $40,000 in 1878 for a lake-coast light and fog signal, while the HAER inventory says Congress funded the project in 1877 and that it cost about $100,000 when finished.[^1][^2][^3] In May 1879 the government bought a lot measuring 295 by 292.5 feet for $1,000, with a right-of-way to the public highway.[^1] The station's website credits the tower and its attached dwelling to Orlando Metcalfe Poe, who had been engineer secretary of the Lighthouse Board.[^3]

The tower is brick with an inner and an outer wall, tapering as it rises, and stands on a stone foundation ten feet deep.[^1][^2] Its height is given as 108 feet (33 m) by the HAER inventory and the station's website, and as 110 feet 3 inches by LighthouseFriends.[^1][^2][^3] A cast-iron spiral stair of 144 steps climbs to the watchroom and lantern.[^1] A covered way twenty-two feet long joins the tower to the brick keepers' dwelling.[^1]

The original lens was a third-order Fresnel lens made by Barbier and Fenestre of Paris. It turned once every six minutes to give a six-second flash every thirty seconds, driven by a clockwork whose weight dropped through a tube between the tower's two walls.[^1] A second, red light, shown from a watchroom window, warned of Racine Reef.[^1] The HAER inventory calls this a fifth-order light; LighthouseFriends says a sixth-order lens was replaced in 1897 by a locomotive headlight with a parabolic reflector, and that the reef light ended on 24 January 1907 after a light was established on Racine Reef itself.[^1][^2] Two ten-inch steam fog whistles stood in twin buildings east of the tower.[^1]

A brick fog-signal building went up in 1900 for duplicate compressed-air sirens, and the Board's fiscal 1900 report records the work: three holes 12 feet deep drilled for extra boiler water, materials bought for a new plant, and the building begun.[^1][^6] The sirens were established on 8 December 1900, replacing the 10-inch steam whistles.[^7] In that fiscal year the steam whistle ran about 631 hours and burned about 45 tons of coal and 3 cords of wood.[^6]

The oil house is another place where the records differ. LighthouseFriends says a circular iron oil house was put up in 1893 and the present concrete one in 1910.[^1] The Board's report of 1891-92 says the metal work for an iron oil house for Wind Point was built under contract and delivered to the Detroit depot, and the report for fiscal 1894 lists Wind Point among the stations where oil houses were built during the year.[^4][^5]

The first nights went badly. Within days of the light and fog signal starting on 15 November 1880, the revolving machinery failed, and the first keeper, Alfred B. Finch, and his son Asa, the first assistant, turned the lens by hand for five cold nights.[^1]

## The keepers

The Finches, father and son, opened the station in 1880.[^1][^8] After them came a run of principal keepers whose terms the archive's keeper registers give as Lawrence A. Easson (1881-1883), John V. Sandell (1883-1891) and Peter J. Peterson (1891-1913).[^8] Henry R. Bevry took over in 1913 and stayed until 1944 by the archive's reckoning, or 1945 by LighthouseFriends.[^1][^8] He had been at Wind Point before, as second assistant in 1899 and first assistant from 1902 to 1907.[^8]

The archive lists a second assistant from 1896, and LighthouseFriends suggests that addition likely prompted the 1899 extension of the dwelling: in 1899 the dwelling was extended by 34 by 20 feet with a back building. By 1910 it held seven rooms for the head keeper and five for each of the two assistants.[^1] The station's website says the remodelling let three keeper families live on site.[^3]

Bevry had the same two assistants for most of his time. Julius Lonne was second assistant from 1908, then first assistant from 1910 until his death on 23 December 1940, which LighthouseFriends reports as sudden, from a heart attack. William H. Nash was second assistant from 1910 to 1938.[^1][^8] LighthouseFriends passes on Bevry's recollections of the place: two of his children were born at the station, all three daughters were married in its living room, and weekend crowds could reach 1,000 people. By his own estimate, it says, he climbed the tower about 3,109,800 steps on official business.[^1] Oscar L. Franseen joined as second assistant in 1938 and became head keeper in 1954; he retired in March 1964, a few months before the light was automated.[^1][^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources name small-boat rescues at Wind Point rather than large wrecks. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin commended Bevry, Lonne and Nash for towing a disabled motor boat with two boys aboard to shore on 10 August 1913.[^9] Nash alone helped the hydroplane Fire Fly, disabled on Lake Michigan on 4 July 1913, and helped the life-saving crew tow it in.[^9] On 23 August 1914 the three brought in a disabled motor boat "in a dangerous position."[^10] LighthouseFriends adds a 1918 aid to the auxiliary cruiser Driad, aground on a reef.[^1] On 28 May 1921 the three were commended for rescuing two boys on a raft that had been blown out on the lake.[^11]

One night in April 1925 the light was seen across the whole lake. The officer in charge of the Coast Guard station at St. Joseph, Michigan, about 80 miles away, wrote to the keeper that at about 8:30 p.m. on 17 April his lookout had called him to see regular flashes in the northwest. After watching the unvarying intervals, he launched the motor lifeboat, and after more than two hours and about 17 miles the engine was stopped to look at the lights of Milwaukee, Racine, Kenosha, Waukegan and Chicago, each showing as points of light. Everybody aboard, he wrote, was "thankful to old Wind Point for having coaxed us out to witness this the grandest of displays." The mirage was over by about 11:15 p.m.[^13]

## After the keepers

The light was electrified in the 1920s. LighthouseFriends puts the electrification on 5 December 1923, calling it the second on the Great Lakes, while the station's website says 1924; the Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1924 lists a change to electric incandescent at Wind Point among that year's changes.[^1][^3][^12]

In June 1964 a DCB-24 aerobeacon replaced the Fresnel lens, a few weeks before the station was automated. The fog signal was dropped, and the buildings were leased to the Village of Wind Point.[^1][^3] The tower was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1984, according to LighthouseFriends, though the station website gives 1980 in its own timeline.[^1][^3] The Coast Guard transferred the property to the village in 1997.[^1][^3]

## Today

The Village of Wind Point owns the lighthouse and the park grounds, which are open daily; the tower is open for tours on certain dates, and the Coast Guard keeps responsibility for the light itself.[^3] Part of the old dwelling serves as a hall and village meeting place, and the caretakers live in the keepers' quarters.[^3] The original third-order Fresnel lens is on display in the dwelling, on loan from the Racine Heritage Museum.[^1][^3] The DCB-24R beacon failed in 2007 and was replaced by a VRB-25, which local boaters complained was too weak.[^1] The tower is not open to climb outside the village's tour dates.[^3]
