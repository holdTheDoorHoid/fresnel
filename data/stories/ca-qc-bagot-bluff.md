The Phare de l'escarpement Bagot, known in English as the Bagot Bluff light, stands on the bare coast of Anticosti Island, in the Gulf of St. Lawrence, in the Minganie Regional County Municipality of Quebec's Côte-Nord region.[^2] The Department's report printed in 1906 called it "Anticosti South Point," and the one printed in 1913 called it "Bagot bluff."[^7][^8] Its reinforced concrete tower, finished in 1912, has six flying buttresses, and a small skeletal tower beside it now carries the light.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

LighthouseFriends says that, although the government could not change the currents, reefs and fogs of Anticosti, it set up navigational aids and provision caches on the island.[^1] The Department's annual report for 1900, printed in 1901, says that Mr. Menier, proprietor of the Island of Anticosti, had a surveyor mark off the government property at West Point. It adds that at South Point, Heath Point and Southwest Point, according to Mr. Menier's surveyor, "some government buildings are beyond the government property."[^5]

LighthouseFriends also tells of the *Renommee*, which grounded on a reef a mile from South Point on 14 November 1736, before any light stood there. Its account names no primary source.[^1]

## Building the light

By the 1870s, LighthouseFriends says, the growing traffic and the continuing wrecks made another light on Anticosti necessary. Quoting the Department's 1870 report, LighthouseFriends says contracts had been made for a lighthouse and a powerful steam fog whistle on the south point of the island.[^1] The Quebec heritage register says the station was set up in 1870, and it adds that little is known about that first tower.[^2] Les Amis des phares du Saint-Laurent gives the first light's construction as 1871.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the light first shone on 15 August 1870.[^1]

According to LighthouseFriends, that first tower was a hexagonal wooden building, painted white with a red roof, and it carried a catoptric light. A steam fog whistle stood about 91 m (300 ft) east of it. LighthouseFriends gives the cost as $7,492.50 for the lighthouse and $7,242.22 for the fog alarm.[^1] The station had two dwellings, one for the keeper and one for his assistant, a stable and a provision depot.[^1]

The concrete tower came later. The heritage register says the flying-buttress concrete design was developed and used by the Department of Marine and Fisheries between 1906 and 1912 under Colonel William Anderson, its chief engineer. It also says the 1912 tower replaced an earlier one built "sur la pointe est de l'île dans les années 1870" (on the island's east point, in the 1870s; translation).[^3] The sources disagree on where the first tower stood. LighthouseFriends places the first light at South Point, while the federal register places the tower it replaced on the island's east point. No source read settles the question.[^1][^3] The Quebec register gives the concrete tower as built around 1906 to 1912, 24.3 m (80 ft) high.[^2] Les Amis des phares gives 1912 and the same height.[^4]

## The keepers

David Tetu was the first keeper, from 1871 to 1880, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] It describes him as an inventor, a mapmaker and the Federal Receiver of Wrecks for Anticosti. In 1878 the Marine Agent at Quebec asked him to draw a map of Anticosti Island showing the shipwrecks during his time as keeper. LighthouseFriends says the map, which still exists, shows 87 wrecks between 1870 and 1879.[^1]

The keepers after him, according to the same list, were William Carter (1880 to 1888), Jean Nadeau (1888 to 1894), Alphonse Nadeau (1894 to 1903) and Emile Laprise (1903 to 1922). The Department's report printed in 1906 lists Laprise against "Anticosti South Point Light and Fog Alarm," and the report printed in 1913 lists "Bagot bluff" with E. Laprise.[^7][^8] J. H. Delisle followed, from 1922 to at least 1937.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says cows seldom lived past eighteen months on the island, so the stock of cows for the lightkeepers was renewed every two years. After the cattle were butchered, their hides went to Quebec tanners and came back to the station the next season, to be made into shoes for the keepers' families.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 27 October 1873 a barque was wrecked at South Point. Her master and eight men stayed at the lighthouse for the whole winter and were fed from the station's depot.[^1]

In the winter of 1878 Keeper Tetu gave some of the depot's provisions to islanders who were starving, as LighthouseFriends tells it.[^1]

LighthouseFriends quotes the Quebec Saturday Budget of July 1890 on another wreck on the Anticosti coast, the steamer *Idaho*, carrying cheese and produce.[^1] It also quotes a dispatch from South Point saying that a two-masted steamer was ashore a little above the lighthouse and that the keeper had gone aboard. LighthouseFriends does not say whether that steamer was the *Idaho*.[^1]

On 10 August 1903 the British steamship *Manchester Trader* stranded near South Point. A formal investigation found that the stranding came through the master's wrongful act, in steering improper courses after receiving "two convincing proofs that he was very much out of his reckoning."[^6]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the station was automated in 1963.[^1] In 1980 the lantern room was removed from the concrete tower and the light moved to a skeletal tower about 15 m (50 ft) high nearby. That tower's focal plane is 19.5 m (64 ft), and LighthouseFriends describes its light as a white flash every six seconds. The keepers' duplex was demolished in 1980.[^1] Only the concrete tower, stripped of its lantern, and the skeletal structure still stand on the site.[^2]

The site was recognized as a federal heritage building on 30 May 1991.[^3]

## Today

The federal register describes the tower as a hexagonal white reinforced concrete column, braced by buttresses.[^3] The Quebec register says it stands on an open point jutting into the Gulf of St. Lawrence (translation of "pointe dégagée").[^2]

LighthouseFriends says the Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, that the grounds are open and the tower is closed, and that the lighthouse is best seen by boat. From the island's roads a distant view is possible, but LighthouseFriends advises against walking in, because the ground is marshy.[^1] Listing in the national register does not make a site open to the public.[^3]
