Fourchu Head Light marks the eastern end of a headland on the eastern side of Cape Breton Island, in Richmond County, Nova Scotia.[^1][^2] The Department of Marine and Fisheries established the light in the year to 31 March 1908.[^3] The Canadian Coast Guard owns it now, and its last keeper left in 1988.[^1][^2]

## The place

Fourchu is a small settlement on the eastern side of Cape Breton. Boats making for its harbour had to avoid the Fourchu Rocks and Flat Ledge offshore, then pass north of Fourchu Head to enter Fourchu Inlet.[^1] A breakwater 260 m long now joins the head to the land.[^1]

The name causes confusion. Cape Forchu, sometimes spelled Cape Fourchu, is a different headland near Yarmouth, Nova Scotia, and Lighthouse Friends warns against mixing the two.[^1]

## Building the light

The Department's report for the year ended 31 March 1908 records that "A lighthouse was established on Fourchu head (the point on the south side of Fourchu inlet), Cape Breton." The tower was an enclosed wooden building with sloping sides and a square wooden lantern, painted white, and it stood 9.8 m (32 ft) to the top of the lantern ventilator. The work was done by contract by Lawrence Mury of Amherst West, Nova Scotia, for $700.[^3]

The light was a fixed red dioptric light of the sixth order, 14.6 m (48 ft) above high water, and the report gives its range as "visible 8 miles from all points of approach."[^3]

The same report's list of new lights erected in Canada during 1907-8 gives the head a different lens: "Fourchu. Head. — 360° 5th order French lens, duplex lamp, red."[^4] The narrative says sixth order and the list says fifth order. This story does not choose between them.

The dates disagree too. Lighthouse Friends says the Department announced the lighthouse's completion in 1908.[^1] The Nova Scotia society gives 1907 as the year the light began.[^2] The Department's list covers the fiscal year from April 1907 to March 1908, so either year fits.[^4]

The first keeper, Albert Hooper, was appointed on 18 May 1908.[^1][^5] Lighthouse Friends says he served just over a year before George Hardy took charge.[^1]

In August and September 1950 the oil shed was repaired and a temporary light was shown from its roof while the tower was rebuilt. New joists, corner posts and studs went in, and the tower was sheathed and shingled again.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends names three keepers for the station: Albert Hooper (1908 to 1909), George Hardy (1909 to at least 1923), and Robert Spears (1987 to 1988).[^1] Spears was the last. "The station remained staffed up until 1988, when Robert Spears, the last keeper, left," Lighthouse Friends says.[^1] The two dwellings were subsequently disposed of, leaving the tower and an equipment shed on the head.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 23 February 1921 a vessel struck a ledge off Fourchu, Cape Breton. A preliminary inquiry sat at Louisburg on 28 February 1921, and the formal investigation at Halifax on 15 March. The finding was that ice pressure had carried the vessel inshore to starboard, and that weather conditions and darkness meant it was not perceivable by those in charge.[^6] The court was of opinion that "had master been aware of non-existence of light on Fourchu Head the casualty would not have occurred." The master was cautioned.[^6] The inquiry does not say why there was no light on the head. The Nova Scotia society's list gives the light a seasonal schedule, April to January, but only for its 1922 to 1943 lens, which was after the wreck.[^2]

In February 1967 the Iceland II, a dragger from Souris, Prince Edward Island, was lost near Fourchu Head. Lighthouse Friends says she had sailed from Souris on 15 January to fish the waters off Nova Scotia until the ice broke up.[^1] A crewman telephoned his wife on 17 February. "Five days later, a terrible storm hit the Maritimes," the account says.[^1] The dragger had left the fishing grounds and was seeking harbour to escape the storm when she ran aground.[^1] A few days after the storm, news reached her family that the wreck had been discovered near Fourchu Head. "There were no survivors," Lighthouse Friends says. A memorial in Souris bears the names of the ten men who died.[^1]

Staff at the light helped the search. "Personnel at Fourchu Head relayed radio-phone messages to help coordinate the search for bodies from the wreck and recovered two bodies themselves," Lighthouse Friends reports.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Nova Scotia society dates a fog signal here to 1955. Lighthouse Friends says a fog alarm building was put up in 1956 to house a diaphone horn, which sounded a pair of two-second blasts each minute. Before that, a bell was tolled every twenty seconds when needed.[^1][^2]

Commercial power reached the head in 1970, and an electronic foghorn went into service in 1974.[^1] In 1979 a round fibreglass tower with a beacon on top replaced the traditional lighthouse.[^1] The society dates the fibreglass tower to 1985 and says it replaced the 1907 tower.[^2] The sources disagree on the year, and this story does not settle it.

In 2015 the Coast Guard put up a galvanized square skeletal tower to replace the fibreglass one. The characteristic stayed the same, a two-second white flash followed by four seconds of darkness.[^1] In May 2015 the Coast Guard announced it would discontinue the fog signal and gave the public three months to comment. The signal was silenced afterwards.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light. Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] The route it gives is east from Fourchu along the road to its end, then across the breakwater.[^1] The Nova Scotia society lists the site as requiring a hike.[^2] The Coast Guard's List of Lights gives the light as a long white flash every six seconds, near the eastern end of the head.[^7]
