Sanibel Island Light stands at the eastern tip of Sanibel Island, on Florida's southwest Gulf coast in Lee County, across San Carlos Bay from the mainland port of Punta Rassa.[^1][^2] Its skeleton iron tower showed a light 98 feet (30 m) above the sea from 20 August 1884, and two keepers' houses stood beside it until Hurricane Ian swept them away in 2022.[^1][^2][^10]

## The place

In 1833 a small group of settlers arrived from New York. In December of that year "thirteen residents of Sanibel Island petitioned the U. S. Treasury Department for the construction of a lighthouse on the island," but the national government showed no interest.[^1] Within five years the island was nearly deserted, and it stayed that way for about twenty-five years.[^1]

A request for a reservation of land for a lighthouse reached the General Land Office in December 1856, and it was repeated in December 1877. By then, the nomination says, "seagoing commerce in the area had reached important proportions."[^1] The Lighthouse Board argued that there was no lighthouse between Key West and Egmont Key, and it recommended in 1878 and again in 1879 that $40,000 be appropriated.[^1] The nomination says nearby Punta Rassa "was a busy cattle shipping point," which was "not specifically mentioned" in the request, "but this was an important consideration."[^1]

## Building the light

Congress was slow to provide the money. It appropriated $20,000 on 3 March 1881, and a further $30,000 on 7 August 1882 "for the completion of the lighthouse."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the full $50,000 was not obtained until 1883.[^2] The two accounts differ on when the money was complete, and this story reports both.

Reserving the island took longer. Florida claimed the land under the Swamp Land Act of 1850 until August 1883.[^1] The nomination dates the reservation order to 19 December 1883 and says it was "signed by President James A. Garfield." Garfield had died in September 1881, so either the signer or the date in the nomination is wrong, and this story does not choose between them.[^1]

Construction began in February 1884. The foundation was complete within three months, but the schooner carrying the tower's iron from Jersey City was wrecked just two miles from the island, and most of the cargo sank.[^1] Assistance came from Key West for the salvage, and all but two small pieces were recovered. A New Orleans firm made duplicates of the missing parts.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse tenders *Arbutus* and *Mignonette*, with a diver, fished up all the pieces except two small gallery brackets.[^2]

The tower was a skeleton of iron: four iron legs around a central cylinder with a spiral stairway, topped by a lantern room.[^1][^2] Two frame dwellings on iron columns, built in 1884 with wide verandas, were attached to the tower by a stairway.[^1][^3] The first lens was a third-order Fresnel.[^2][^3] The light was first shown on 20 August 1884.[^1] At the relighting ceremony in February 2023, the city manager said the light had been lit in 1885, which does not match the nomination or LighthouseFriends.[^9][^1][^2]

## The keepers

Dudley Richardson was the first keeper, from 1884 to 1892.[^3] The Digital Archive's list of principal keepers ends with William Robert England, who served from 1946 to 1949, the year the light was automated.[^3][^2]

The Shannahans are the family these sources describe most fully.[^2][^3] Henry Shannahan (LighthouseFriends spells the name Shanahan) moved to Sanibel from Key West in 1888 and became assistant keeper in 1890.[^2][^3] When Richardson resigned in 1892, Shannahan applied for the head keeper's post. LighthouseFriends says the authorities first refused, because he was illiterate, and gave him the post when he threatened to resign.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends counts twenty-three years at the light before his death in 1913, and the roster dates his head keepership from 1892 to 1913.[^2][^3] His son Eugene served as an assistant from 1906 to 1912 and as head keeper from 1923 to 1925.[^3] LighthouseFriends dates Eugene's return to the station to 1924.[^2] The family also kept a pet deer that raced along the beach, LighthouseFriends says.[^2]

Richard T. Barry, an assistant keeper, was killed in June 1919.[^8] The *Punta Gorda Herald* reported that Barry was "killed Sunday evening by Jesse W. Lee of Port Myers," and that he had been "accused of having insulted Mrs. Lee." It gave "No other particulars."[^8] LighthouseFriends, citing the *Fort Meyers Press* of 23 June, puts the shooting at about noon on the day before, 22 June 1919, which was a Sunday. The two accounts agree on the day and disagree on the hour, and this story does not choose between them.[^2][^8] LighthouseFriends also says a jury in Fort Myers acquitted Lee of murder five months later, and that Lee admitted the killing and claimed self-defense.[^2] Keeper Charles H. Williams noted the news in the station logbook that day, LighthouseFriends says.[^2]

In 1923 the dwellings were modernized with indoor plumbing and bathrooms, and the light was converted from kerosene to acetylene gas.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In April 1925 keeper Eugene H. Shannahan and assistant William L. M. Demere helped tow the disabled boat *Florida* to safety.[^6] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded it.[^6]

In 1936 the Weather Bureau's office at Jacksonville had been receiving observations from Florida's reef lights since May. During a hurricane disturbance on 28 July, the senior meteorologist at Jacksonville told the Key West superintendent that reports from several lights, Sanibel among them, "were especially valuable."[^7]

LighthouseFriends says a hurricane in 1947 caused severe erosion on the island and left one of the two dwellings standing in a foot of water.[^2] The Florida Lighthouse Association instead dates a hurricane's erosion at the station to 1891.[^10] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 records that year's work at the station, including "New foundations and platforms ... put up under the cisterns," a new fence, and "various minor repairs." It does not mention a storm.[^4] The 1891 claim is not supported by that report, and this story does not repeat it.

Routine work went on as well. In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 a contract "was made for building an oil house," and minor repairs were made.[^5]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1949, partly because of erosion concerns, LighthouseFriends says. The Florida Lighthouse Association gives the same year.[^2][^10]

The Coast Guard electrified the light in 1962, when the causeway to the mainland was completed and electric conduit pipes were run along it to the island.[^2] LighthouseFriends' introduction says the causeway "was not constructed until 1963," so the two dates in its own account disagree, and this story reports both. The Coast Guard removed the third-order lens and installed a 300 mm drum lens that had been used on a lightship.[^2]

In 1972 the Coast Guard proposed to discontinue the light. Local residents and mariners persuaded it to keep the light lit.[^2]

The National Register nomination for the station is dated June 1974.[^1] The City of Sanibel took over management of the property, except the tower, in 1982, and restored the tower in 2013.[^10][^2] LighthouseFriends dates the formal transfer of the lighthouse to the city to a ceremony on 21 April 2010.[^2]

## Today

Hurricane Ian reached the island on 28 September 2022. It broke away one of the tower's legs, which the city's contractors replaced with a temporary wooden support.[^9] LighthouseFriends and the Florida Lighthouse Association both say the storm swept away the two keepers' dwellings and the oil house.[^2][^10]

The city held a relighting ceremony on 28 February 2023, and the U.S. Coast Guard replaced the light, the city reported.[^9] On 23 January 2024 the wooden support was replaced with a new, permanent metal leg. Repairs costing more than a million dollars were completed in the summer of 2024.[^2]

The City of Sanibel owns the lighthouse. The grounds are open and the tower is closed to visitors, and the Coast Guard still maintains the active beacon.[^2][^10] Drivers reach the light at the eastern end of the island, where metered parking is signed.[^2] After the 28 February ceremony the parking lot was closed to the public, and the city reported that the site repairs were not yet finished.[^9]
