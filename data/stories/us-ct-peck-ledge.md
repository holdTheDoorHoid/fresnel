Peck Ledge Light is a cast-iron tower standing in Long Island Sound at the northeast end of the Norwalk Islands, off the Norwalk and Westport shore.[^1][^10] It was lit in 1906 to mark two hazards on the approach to Norwalk Harbor. It was automated in 1933, after about twenty-seven years with resident keepers.[^2][^10]

## The place

The Norwalk Islands are a cluster of sixteen islands and countless rocks, and the light was built to mark two of the hazards among them.[^2] The National Register form describes them as "two hazards in the approach to Norwalk Harbor from the east": Peck Ledge to the southwest, and a shoal extending north from Goose Island.[^1]

Harbor traffic was the reason for the change.[^1] The form says that in the late nineteenth century, when commercial traffic into Norwalk grew, dredging and improvements to the aids to navigation followed, and deeper ships met underwater hazards that had gone unmarked.[^1] It says the old light on Sheffield Island was felt to be inadequate for guiding boat traffic around the east and west ends of the island chain.[^1] The answer was a light at each end: Greens Ledge at the west, and Peck Ledge at the east.[^2]

Lobbying for the two lights began in 1896, the form says.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for that year proposed "a light and fog-signal station on Pecks Ledge, Norwalk Harbor."[^4] Congress appropriated $10,000 on 3 March 1901 "for a light and fog-signal at this place," and the Board then decided the station had to be large enough for two keepers.[^5] That decision led to requests for a further $29,000, according to the National Register form.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives a total estimate of $39,000.[^2]

Some local mariners objected to the site.[^2] LighthouseFriends says George's Rock, at the southeast end of a reef that extends from Cockenoe Island, was deemed a better site, since a light there would have warned ships of that reef while also pointing the way to Norwalk Harbor. By then it was too late to change the site.[^2]

## Building the light

Construction ran in fits and starts. The foundation cylinder was sunk on 30 May 1905, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The National Register form says it was sunk that summer and filled with concrete: "Two hundred fifty bags of concrete were used to fill the foundation." LighthouseFriends says 250 bags of mortar went inside it.[^1][^2] Work stopped in July 1905 because the metal for the superstructure had not been delivered.[^1][^2] The foundation was covered over and fitted with a temporary light.[^2] Assembly of the superstructure resumed in March 1906, and within four months the tower was completed, painted and ringed with riprap.[^2]

The form describes a truncated cone of curved cast-iron plates, brick-lined for stability and insulation.[^1] Three stories of living space sat under a circular watchroom and lantern.[^1] The form calls the light a typical example of the prefabricated cast-iron conical lighthouses that the Board developed for difficult sites, including "wave-washed shoals, reefs, ledges and newly created breakwaters."[^1]

The station went into operation in 1906 with a fourth-order Fresnel lens, which showed a two-second white flash every ten seconds.[^2] The two sources give different heights. The form gives the tower as 54 feet (16.5 m) high above mean high water at the focal plane, and LighthouseFriends gives the lantern room as 61 feet (18.6 m) above the water.[^1][^2] The figures measure different points, the focal plane and the lantern room.

## The keepers

The first head keeper was George W. Bardwell, from 1906 to 1907. Louis F. Toocker was the first assistant that first year, as the roster spells the name.[^3] Later head keepers were August Lorenz (1907 to 1909), Conrad Hawk (1909 to 1914), Ferdinand Heizman (1914 to 1919), William Hardwick (1919), Charles J. Kenney (1919 to 1925), George Scheer (1925 to 1927) and George H. Clarke (1927 to 1933).[^3] Heizman had been first assistant from 1906 to 1914 before he became head keeper.[^3]

The station's work grew in the 1920s.[^2] Starting on 1 January 1926, the keepers became responsible for three more lights, White Rock Reef, Round Beach and Long Beach, in addition to Grassy Hammock, which they already tended. The illuminant of all four was changed from gas to acetylene that day.[^2] The keepers were to visit the lights every two weeks, record the gas pressure, clean the lantern glass and relight any that went out.[^2] LighthouseFriends notes that the keepers' salaries were not increased for the extra work.[^2]

Clarke's career at the station ran through every rank.[^2] He came as second assistant in 1921, was promoted to first assistant in 1925, and became head keeper in 1927.[^2] When Peck Ledge was automated in 1933, despite vocal protests from local citizens, Clarke was transferred to Greens Ledge, where his duties included keeping an eye on the Peck Ledge light.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 17 February 1913 a fire broke out in the kitchen at Peck Ledge.[^2] Keeper Conrad Hawk had set a tub of hot tar, for tarring lobster-trap funnels, on newspaper near the stove, then rowed out to adjust a post lantern at Grassy Hammock.[^2] On his way back he saw smoke pouring from the windows.[^2] He dragged the tub across the floor with a clam rake and knocked it out a window.[^2] He then climbed the outside wall to an upstairs bedroom for a blanket to cut the draft, and drew seawater up in a bucket on a rope to douse what was left of the fire.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says Keeper Kenney was recognized on at least five occasions for rendering assistance to people in distress.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports the official commendation of the rescue of four men from the sunken steamer J. C. Austin.[^7] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report names Charles J. Kenney, keeper, George H. Barker, first assistant, and George H. Clarke, second assistant, for rescuing four men who were drifting in a small boat from the sunken steamer J. C. Austin, of Brooklyn, and who were suffering severely from exposure.[^6] The Bulletin gives the date of the rescue as 5 December.[^7] LighthouseFriends places the recognition in 1922. The Bulletin issue that reports the rescue is dated 3 January 1922, so the rescue most likely took place on 5 December 1921.[^7][^2] New England Lights, a secondary web guide that cites no source, gives five men rather than four.[^12]

On 20 August 1923 Kenney saw a power boat drifting about a mile (1.6 km) east of the light, the Bulletin reports.[^8] The keeper went out in his power boat, found one occupant and towed the boat to Westport.[^8] LighthouseFriends tells the episode differently: a barge captain whose newly bought launch was leaking and who was exhausted from bailing waved for help, and Kenney set off in the station's launch.[^2]

On the night of 3 July 1928 the keepers heard cries for help.[^2] Clarke and First Assistant Keeper Percy L. Oppel lowered a boat and found two girls and two young men clinging to an overturned canoe. The keepers took them to the lighthouse, where they were given dry clothes.[^2]

On 19 May 1930 First Assistant Oppel rescued the occupants of the catboat Eleanor, which was in distress near the light, and gave them lodging for the night, with help from the second assistant, Milton Hague.[^9] The roster lists Percy L. Oppel as first assistant for these years, but the Bulletin prints the name as Lewis P. Oppel, and LighthouseFriends's 1932 report says "Lewis Oppel." The sources do not settle the first name.[^3][^9][^2]

On 4 March 1932 a power boat caught fire within a quarter of a mile (400 m) of the light.[^2] Clarke's report, which LighthouseFriends prints, says five men were aboard.[^2] When they jumped into a skiff being towed astern, the skiff swamped.[^2] Four of the men climbed back into the burning boat and put the fire out.[^2] One man who had left the skiff to swim back was found exhausted by the keepers, who had launched a boat.[^2]

In September 1913 the steamship Middletown, on its way from Hartford to New York, ran into dense fog near Peck Ledge and grounded on a rocky ledge near Goose Island.[^12] New England Lights, a secondary web guide that cites no source, reports that none of the nearly 250 passengers was in danger, and that four tugs took them off and transferred them to another steamer.[^12]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1933.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the change was made over local protests.[^2] The roster lists three Coast Guard men at the station in 1942 and 1943, without roles.[^3]

In 1980 and again in 1989, crews from the Coast Guard buoy tender Redwood worked on the light.[^2] In 1980 they replaced the light and power source and sealed the tower. In 1989 they sandblasted and repainted the caisson and cleaned the interior.[^2] Superstorm Sandy washed away the lower gallery in 2012.[^2]

In 2014 the Coast Guard declared the light excess.[^2] When no new custodian was found, an online auction was held in 2015.[^2][^12] LighthouseFriends reports that the high bid of $235,000 came from the Cockenoe Lighthouse Preservation Group, an LLC formed to acquire, restore and preserve the light.[^2][^12] A restoration began in 2016, and the owners' website says it was completed in 2021.[^11][^10]

Since 2023, the light has offered limited artist engagements and residencies, through a partnership with The Norwalk Art Space.[^10]

## Today

The light is privately owned. Its owners appear as the Cockenoe Lighthouse Preservation Group, a Delaware LLC, and as the Lighthouse Preservation Group, LLC.[^10][^11] The owners' website describes workshops for artists and says access is limited by logistical and environmental restrictions.[^10] LighthouseFriends lists the tower as closed, and says the light is best seen from the water, with distant views from Calf Pasture Park in Norwalk.[^2] Do not land on the light or try to climb it.
