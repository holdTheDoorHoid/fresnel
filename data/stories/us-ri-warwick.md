Warwick Light has marked the narrow water between Warwick Neck and Patience Island since 1826.[^3] The tower that stands there was built in 1932.[^4] In 1938 a hurricane undermined its foundation, and in 1939 the tower was moved about 50 feet (15 m).[^4]

## The place

Warwick Neck is a point at the southern tip of Warwick, Rhode Island. It divides Greenwich Bay from the western approach to the Providence River.[^2] The nomination says all traffic from the West Passage of Narragansett Bay en route to Providence had to pass through the narrow straight that separates the Neck from Patience Island.[^2] It also gives the reason for the light: "Because of the importance of Providence as an early center of trade, a light was needed at Warwick Neck to guide ships through the narrow straight."[^3] The nomination calls the straight a "narrow half mile" wide.[^2] LighthouseFriends puts the northern entrance of the western channel at three-quarters of a mile wide.[^10] Three acres at the southern tip of the Neck were bought from the Green family on 15 May 1826.[^3][^10]

## Building the light

Congress had provided $3,000 for the first light, LighthouseFriends says.[^10] The two accounts of that first light do not agree. The nomination describes "an 18-foot clapboarded tower" that rose through the roof of a long, one-story stone keeper's dwelling.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes a stone tower thirty feet (9 m) tall, with a small stone house attached. It says the first beacon was eight lamps and nine-inch (23 cm) reflectors arranged around part of a circular table.[^10] The sources do not settle the height or the materials.

The nomination says the wooden tower on the dwelling "was completed the following year," which would put it in 1827.[^3] LighthouseFriends dates the station's completion to 1826.[^10] The archive gives 1827 as the year the station was established.[^11]

A fourth-order Fresnel lens went into the lantern in 1856.[^10][^11]

As early as 1878, according to the nomination, the Lighthouse Board's annual report described the keeper's dwelling as "in a dilapidated state."[^3] The nomination says "$8,000 was appropriated in 1889 to construct a new dwelling."[^2] The sources then disagree about the house that still stands. The nomination calls the 1899 keeper's dwelling "the earliest surviving structure," yet elsewhere it refers to "the 1889 dwelling."[^2][^4] LighthouseFriends and the archive date the house to 1889.[^10][^11] The old dwelling was "placed on a new foundation in 1892 and remodeled into a barn."[^3]

## The keepers

The first keeper was Elisha Case. In July 1831 Daniel Waite arrived to take over the light and found that Case, the station's first keeper, refused to leave.[^10] Case wanted to harvest crops he had planted at the station, and he wanted compensation for a barn he had built there. He was allowed back to harvest the crops, but he received no money for the barn.[^10] The archive lists Case as principal keeper from 1827 to 1831, and Waite from 1831 to 1832.[^11]

Waite died in November 1832, in his second year of service.[^10] His wife, Abby Waite, took charge of the light and stayed on the point for several more years. The archive lists her as principal keeper from 1832 to 1838.[^10][^11] By 1838 she had married George S. Armstrong, who took charge of the light on 29 June that year.[^10][^11]

The archive lists 32 principal-keeper stints between 1827 and 1985.[^11] Nathaniel Dodge held the post twice, in 1898 and in 1905.[^11]

Jorgen Bakken was principal keeper from 1912 to 1932.[^11] LighthouseFriends says he was transferred to Warwick from New York's Shinnecock Lighthouse, and stayed as keeper there for twenty years.[^10][^11] LighthouseFriends reports, from a 1986 interview, that his daughter, May Bakken Chrietzberg, recalled the old tower. She remembered that her father received several commendations, one of them from President Herbert Hoover, for rescues at Warwick Neck.[^10]

Edward Murphy served as first assistant from 1920 to 1933, and as head keeper from 1933 to 1953.[^10][^11] When he was promoted, LighthouseFriends says, the post of assistant keeper was dropped.[^10]

Harry A. Wilbur, a civilian, served as keeper from 1953 until he retired in 1967.[^10][^11] He was followed by Donald D. Pereira (1967 to 1970), Wally G. Wise, Russell P. Richards, Luther M. Jacobson and Alan Penney. William Knight's stint, from 1982 to 1985, is the last the archive lists.[^11]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1869, according to the nomination, "a large portion of the bank was washed away by a gale."[^4] In 1896 "250 feet of fencing was moved back from shore to prevent further undermining," and in 1924 a concrete retaining wall was built.[^4]

The Lighthouse Board's report for fiscal 1894 renewed a request, made in its previous four annual reports, for a fog signal.[^6] It gave the estimated cost as $5,000.[^6] LighthouseFriends says a fog bell placed on the point in 1882 was replaced by a blower siren.[^10] The Board's report for fiscal 1900 says the siren "was established on June 1, 1900."[^7] The report for fiscal 1901 says the siren "was in operation about 336 hours" that year, and that the characteristic "was changed June 15, 1901."[^8]

On 17 July 1922 Keeper Jorgen Bakken rescued "two men whose canoe had capsized," and recovered their canoe, according to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin.[^9]

LighthouseFriends says the 1938 New England hurricane left the tower only inches from falling into the water.[^10] The nomination adds that "the hurricane also destroyed the fog signal building," and that a new horn was set on the new base of the tower.[^4]

LighthouseFriends reports that on 13 July 1962 lightning struck the metal tower during a dry electrical storm of three flashes, as Keeper Harry Wilbur noted. Wilbur found flakes of paint on the lantern deck when he went to light the beacon.[^10]

LighthouseFriends also reports that May Bakken, as a child, lay awake at night listening to Coast Guard boats chasing rum-runners on Narragansett Bay.[^10]

## After the keepers

The first tower and dwelling were torn down in 1932, after the present tower was built.[^2] The nomination says the new tower was built "nearer to the shoreline than its present location."[^4] It describes a cylindrical cast-iron structure, 51 feet (16 m) high.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes a thirty-one-foot (9.5 m) steel tower, completed in June 1932.[^10] The archive gives 28 feet (8.5 m) from base to lantern centre.[^11] The sources disagree on the height and the metal.

Before 1932 two keepers were needed to tend the kerosene lamp.[^4] After 1932, the nomination says, one keeper was enough because the new light "was outfitted with an easier-to-operate electric lamp."[^4]

In 1939 the tower was moved.[^4] The nomination says it was "moved 50 feet to the north of its original location in 1939 because its foundation had been undermined by the 1938 hurricane."[^4] LighthouseFriends says massive jacks lifted the tower and moved it farther inland, a job that took one day, and that the light kept shining throughout.[^10] The direction differs: the nomination says north, and LighthouseFriends says inland.[^4][^10] The nomination treats the period of significance as running "from 1899, when the lighthouse keeper's dwelling was constructed," to the 1938 and 1939 events.[^5]

In 1958 a 1¼-acre lot west of the light was "sold for the construction of a private residence."[^4]

The archive dates the automation of the light to 1985, and LighthouseFriends says a modern lens replaced the Fresnel lens that year.[^10][^11] The nomination, which led to the station's National Register listing in 1988,[^12] still describes a keeper and his family living on the grounds, and says the station was "scheduled to be automated by 1990."[^2][^4] The sources therefore disagree on when the keepers left.

## Today

The federal government owns the property.[^1] The archive lists the light as an active aid to navigation and as not open to the public.[^11] LighthouseFriends says the grounds, dwelling and tower are closed.[^10]

The property's future is in question. In May 2023 the General Services Administration offered Warwick Neck Lighthouse at no cost to eligible public and non-profit bodies.[^10] LighthouseFriends reports that, according to the GSA, "more than a dozen" organisations expressed interest, and that the City of Warwick was a leading candidate.[^10] The GSA announced in 2025 that the lighthouse would be offered at auction.[^10] An invitation for bids dated 18 June 2026 sets an online auction with a starting bid of $500,000, a registration deposit of $50,000 and bid increments of $20,000. The invitation gives the start date as "TBD."[^12] The Coast Guard keeps an easement to maintain the aid to navigation. The invitation says bids "will be received continuously."[^12]

The invitation also says the cast-iron tower "was erected in 1889, replacing an earlier stone structure." That conflicts with the nomination and LighthouseFriends, which date the present tower to 1932.[^12][^2][^10]

LighthouseFriends says the best views are from the water.[^10] From the street at the end of Warwick Neck Avenue there are partial views, and Save the Bay runs cruises that pass the lighthouse.[^10] Please do not go onto the station grounds.
