Fuller Rock Light is a small automatic beacon on an old granite pier in the Providence River, the channel that leads up to Providence, Rhode Island. The first light on the pier was a wooden tower built in 1872. That tower was destroyed by an explosion and fire in 1923, and the pier still carries the light, under a tower the Coast Guard installed in 1997.[^1][^2][^5]

## The place

In the nineteenth century the Providence River became one of New England's busiest industrial waterways, and its upper reaches had shoals, rocks and narrow channels that made them treacherous.[^1] Fuller Rock sat nearly in mid-channel. A shoal off Sassafras Point, on the western side of the river, was another danger.[^1] The two lights were placed on opposite sides of the river, about a mile apart.[^1] In July 1870 Congress set aside $20,000 for three lights above Sabin Point: Pomham Rocks, Fuller Rock and Sassafras Point.[^1][^2]

## Building the light

Fuller Rock and Sassafras Point were planned to the same design: a granite pier, a fourteen-foot (4.3 m) hexagonal wooden pyramid tower and a small cast-iron lantern.[^1][^2] The Fuller Rock light was to stand twenty-eight feet (8.5 m) above mean high water.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive gives the light's height as 26 feet.[^8] An early light list gave the tower as 17 feet tall, where later lists say 14.[^2] The bid specified piers eighteen feet across at the base, fifteen at the top and twelve feet high. Fuller Rock itself was bare at ordinary high water, while Sassafras Point needed riprap built up to low water first.[^1] Work went ahead under contract in 1871.[^1]

Both first showed fixed white from sixth-order Fresnel lenses. The Sassafras Point light was changed to red on 1 October 1872 to tell the pair apart.[^1][^2] Both were in operation during 1872.[^2]

## The keepers

Neither beacon had living quarters, so one keeper tended both. He rowed between them every day in all weathers.[^1][^2] The Lighthouse Board secured $5,000 in 1874 to buy a site and build a keeper's house. Landowners feared a keeper's house would diminish property values, and despite repeated efforts in 1875 and 1876, no site could be obtained. The dwelling was never built.[^1]

By 1879 the wooden towers showed considerable evidence of decay. The Board asked for $1,000 to replace them with cast-iron towers, but the money did not come.[^1][^2] In 1889 the Fuller Rock pier was repointed, cavities under it were filled with concrete, and seventy-five tons of riprap were added around its base.[^1][^2]

The first keeper, Lorenzo D. Clark, was hired on 11 July 1872 at $320 a year and removed that November.[^1][^8] Charles H. Salisbury, the keeper at Pomham Rocks, looked after the lights for a few weeks, and the roster also lists Joseph Pollard that year.[^1][^8] John S. Whittemore and then Edward Beard came and went, and both resigned.[^1][^8] Samuel D. Heard, whom the roster also spells Hurd, was the first keeper to stay more than a year, from 1873 to 1882.[^1][^8] Edward P. Hoxie (1882), William Dunwell (1882–1886) and Patrick Fitzpatrick (1886) followed.[^8]

John J. Mullen was appointed in 1886 at $550 a year and kept the lights until 1912, more than twenty-five years.[^1][^8] LighthouseFriends calls him a local legend, a keeper who was also a celebrated clog dancer.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On New Year's Eve in the 1890s, according to LighthouseFriends, Mullen was returning after lighting the beacons in his yawl when a gust overturned the boat near Kettle Point. He was left clinging to the upturned hull in freezing water until a neighbor, Ed Grogan, spotted his plight and rowed out to rescue him. LighthouseFriends says the story was later recounted in a Providence Journal article, so this account is second-hand, as LighthouseFriends gives it.[^1]

On 5 February 1923 a crew from the lighthouse tender Pansy came out to change the acetylene tanks that ran the light. Six heavy new tanks had been fitted, and the crew had gone aboard for lunch. When they returned for a final inspection, an explosion caught them.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports that the light was destroyed by the explosion and the fire that followed, injuring several members of the Pansy's crew, none of them seriously.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the blast ignited the structure, that a newspaper reported five crew members hurled through the air, and that no one died.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1900 the Lighthouse Board changed the color of many Narragansett Bay lights. The Board's report records a color change at Fuller Rock on 27 June 1900, and LighthouseFriends says the light changed from white to red.[^3][^1]

In 1912 dredging removed the shoal that had marked Sassafras Point, and that light was discontinued in July.[^1] The Bulletin's list of 1912 changes shows Fuller Rock converted to acetylene.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the light then became an unmanned acetylene beacon, flashing every three seconds, and that resident keepers ended with the change.[^1] A second history, which dates the automation to around 1918, gives a different year.[^2]

After the explosion, a steel skeleton tower went up on the old foundation.[^2] The Bulletin's December 1924 notice, reprinting a Providence paper, describes the old framework at the Kettle Point beacon being taken down and a new tower erected in about an hour and a half, with the lantern 50 feet above the water level.[^6] LighthouseFriends gives the new lantern as forty feet above the water, twice the old height, and says plans for the rebuilt beacon were announced partly to serve a growing oil terminal at Kettle Point.[^1][^7] The two sources disagree on the height.

In 1997 the Coast Guard refurbished the granite pier, added 25 tons of riprap, and erected a new 20-foot (6.1 m) light tower on it.[^1][^2]

## Today

The light is now automatic and solar-powered, showing a red flash of three seconds alternating with three seconds of darkness.[^2] The Coast Guard refurbished the pier and installed the present tower in 1997.[^1]

The pier stands in the river channel.[^1] No visitor access to it is recorded, so do not land on the pier or climb the tower.
