Cape Henry is the point on the south side of the entrance to Chesapeake Bay in Virginia, and two lighthouses stand there within sight of each other. The older is an octagonal sandstone tower first lit in 1792, the first lighthouse the new federal government authorized and completed; the younger is a black-and-white cast-iron tower first lit on 15 December 1881, which is still in service.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

## The place

The National Register nomination says that for decades efforts to light the entrance to the bay failed: the bay was divided between Virginia and Maryland, the regular Virginia coast made the need seem less pressing, and the assemblies, the British government, Lord Baltimore and British merchants could not agree.[^1] LighthouseFriends traces the effort back to a Virginia resolution of 1720, and says that after Maryland joined in 1772 more than 4,000 tons of stone were delivered to the cape before the Revolutionary War left them forgotten and buried in sand.[^5] Governor Randolph of Virginia told President Washington on 18 December 1789 that the state "some years ago placed upon the shore at Cape Henry nearly a sufficient quantity of materials to complete such a lighthouse as was at that time thought convenient, which have been in the course of time covered by sand."[^6]

The land was ceded by Virginia as a plot of two acres.[^3] The nomination notes that the Assembly's 1789 grant was for "not more than two acres" and that the tower was somehow built only partly on it, a defect put right in 1881 when more land was furnished.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress's first appropriation for lighthouses, the act of 26 March 1790, covered this one.[^6] On 31 March 1791 Alexander Hamilton, as Secretary of the Treasury, signed the first contract the federal government ever made for a lighthouse, with John McComb Jr., a New York bricklayer.[^7] It called for a "Light House of Stone, Faced with hewn or hammer dressed stone": an octagon with a foundation sunk 13 feet (4 m) below the water table, a base 26 feet (8 m) across, walls 6 feet (1.8 m) thick at the bottom, and 72 feet (22 m) of stonework above the water table. The price was $15,200.[^7] A separate contract set one Cornick, apparently of a Princess Anne County family, to superintend the building for $2 a week.[^3]

The sand made the foundation larger than planned. A supplementary agreement, with McComb's estimate dated at Cape Henry on 29 July 1791, recited that the ground made it necessary to give the foundation "a greater depth & Width than at first intended". McComb estimated sinking it 20 feet (6 m) below the water table, with a diameter of 33 feet (10 m), for $2,500; Washington approved the agreement on 6 December 1792.[^8] LighthouseFriends quotes Hamilton's representative on the scene describing the drifting sand as "truly vexatious": at least fifty cart loads fell into the cleaned foundation pit in an instant, and McComb cleared it without complaint about payment.[^5]

The light was first shown in 1792. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1939 says only 1792; the National Register nomination says the oil lamps were first lit in October of that year.[^3][^1] The finished tower is an octagon of hewn and hammer-dressed sandstone, 26 feet (8 m) across at the base and 16.5 feet (5 m) at the top, 72 feet (22 m) high, with walls 6 feet (1.8 m) thick.[^1] Putnam's history of the service lists among the contracts President Washington personally approved one for spermaceti oil "immediately wanted for the use of the Light House on Cape Henry in Virginia."[^6]

The engineer Benjamin Latrobe visited in 1798. LighthouseFriends reports that he faulted the wooden staircase, soaked with oil, and sympathised with the keeper, whose home sat inside a rim of blown sand on a dune; he called it "one of the dreariest abodes imaginable."[^5] The same source lists the later changes: a new keeper's house in 1835; a new lantern by Winslow Lewis with eighteen lamps and reflectors in 1841; a fog bell tower in 1855; and in 1857 a brick lining for the tower and a second-order Fresnel lens in place of the lamps.[^5]

By 1872, according to LighthouseFriends's quotation of the district engineer, large cracks "extending from the base upward" ran through six of the tower's eight walls, and he judged it "in danger of being thrown down by some heavy gale." A new dwelling and a first-order light were recommended.[^5] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin says funds were first appropriated in 1878, and LighthouseFriends gives $75,000 in June of that year.[^7][^9]

The new tower went up about 350 feet (107 m) southeast of the old one.[^1] The Light-House Board's own description, printed in its report volume for 1893-94, calls it an octagonal pyramid with its top cut off, 157 feet (48 m) high, with an outer shell of cast iron and an inner stair cylinder of wrought iron joined by radial cast-iron abutments, and says it cost $125,000.[^10] Putnam, writing in 1917, gives its height as 165 feet (50 m).[^6] LighthouseFriends gives 157 feet as the focal plane of the light in 1881 and calls the tower itself 165 feet, so the two figures may measure different things.[^9]

The work had troubles. LighthouseFriends, drawing on the Board's reports, says a pier finished in August 1880 for landing materials was found to be eaten by boring worms, that its bridge broke under a loaded car, and that the pier collapsed hours after the ironwork was taken off it; the Board then ran a tramway four miles (6 km) to Lynnhaven Inlet and landed materials there by scow.[^9] A Preservation Virginia marker gives a different account, saying the sections were shipped by barge from Philadelphia.[^29] The steam fog siren in its brick building began working on 1 December 1880. Work resumed on 30 May 1881, the tower's iron sections were set between 8 July and 8 September, and the first-order lens was tested on 27 November.[^9] The Bulletin gives the lighting as the night of 15 December 1881.[^7] The old tower was left standing as a daymark and for coast survey triangulation.[^9]

The tower's painted pattern is already described in an 1889 handbook, which gives the base, service room and lantern as black and the shaft as half white and half black on each face, alternating so that the upper and lower halves of the faces show black and white in turn.[^11]

## The keepers

The archive of the U.S. Lighthouse Society lists the principal keepers of the old tower from 1792 to 1881.[^12] Who was first is disputed. The archive's roster opens with William Lewis in 1792, followed by Lemuel Cornick in 1792-93 and Laban Goffigan in 1793-94.[^12] LighthouseFriends says Washington gave the post to Lewis, a veteran of his army, who died a month later, and that Cornick, who had overseen the building, held it for under a year.[^5] The 1939 Bulletin instead says the first keeper "appears to have been Laban Goffigan", who was serving in 1794 and had lived in Portsmouth, Virginia, nine years earlier.[^3] Henry James then kept the light until 1807 and Travy Burroughs from 1807 to 1837, according to the roster.[^12]

The conduct of a Cape Henry keeper once reached President Jefferson. On 31 December 1806 Thomas Jefferson endorsed a letter about the conduct of the keeper of Cape Henry: "I think the keepers of light houses should be dismissed for small degrees of remissness, because of the calamities which even these produce," and the opinion of Colonel Newton was enough authority for removal.[^6] The documents Putnam prints do not name the keeper; the roster lists Henry James until 1807 and Burroughs after.[^12]

From 1855 the roster shows a first and a second assistant beside the principal keeper.[^12] It also names women in those posts: Julia A. P. Harding, first assistant from 1865 to 1869; Mary A. Creekmore, second assistant in 1869-70; and Carrie Richardson, second assistant from 1870 to 1872.[^12] The roster does not say how they were related to the principal keepers of the same years, who were William Harding, Horatio Creekmore (also spelled Creekmur) and Henry Richardson.[^12]

Jay D. Edwards was the last keeper of the old tower and the first of the new. The 1939 Bulletin says he was appointed on 5 March 1880 and moved his attention to the new tower when it was ready.[^7] The roster shows him rising through the old station, as second assistant from 1877, first assistant from 1878 and principal keeper in 1880, and then principal keeper of the new light from 1881 to 1885.[^12][^13] By 1893-94 the Board described three keepers living at the station: the principal keeper was paid $820 a year, the first assistant $550, and the second assistant $500 with the use of the dwelling, and the light, near a much-frequented seaside resort, was visited yearly by many people.[^10]

Milton L. Odell was principal keeper from 1885 to 1890.[^13] By late 1887 he was at the centre of a political fight. A Richmond newspaper summarised the report of a board of light-house officers on charges brought against him by H. L. Worthington, Philip Morrissett and others of Norfolk. The board found that the charges Morrissett brought were part of a scheme to oust Odell so that Morrissett could secure the place himself, and that Worthington's object was to strengthen his political party. It found that Odell had not neglected his duties and was a good and efficient officer, and that the testimony that he was "extremely offensive and obnoxious as a Republican partisan" came down to his having taken part in a convention in 1885 and acted as a judge of election that year.[^14] In January 1888 the same paper printed a letter to the chairman of the Light-House Board in which "the department" said that, in acting against this pressure, it wished it stated that Odell was a good and efficient officer who would not be removed simply on account of his politics.[^15]

Meanwhile a violent episode was reported. A Norfolk dispatch, printed in the Staunton Spectator on 28 December 1887, said an attempt had been made on the keeper's life on the Saturday night before, and that he received three rifle shots in his clothing while riding.[^16] On 30 December the Richmond Dispatch reported that two men had been arrested on warrants sworn out by Odell, a man named Gwynn and Charles Sampson, charged with the shooting.[^17] The affair did not end there. In February 1888 Odell was taken before a justice at Princess Anne Courthouse and bailed to answer a charge of perjury, in connection with the recent trial of several young men whom he had charged with trying to kill him; William Morrisett swore out the warrant.[^18] The newspapers cited here do not report how the charge ended. The papers spell his name Odell, O'Dell and, in print errors, with other initials; the archive's roster gives Milton L. Odell.[^13]

The Board's reports give a few figures for the station's working. The steam siren ran 256 hours in the year ended 30 June 1893 and burned 13 tons of coal; the Board's report for 1899 gives 417 hours and 22 tons.[^10][^19] The siren's signal was a blast of five seconds followed by a silence of ninety.[^10] In its report for 1897 the Board recorded that the keepers' dwellings were put in complete order and that sheet piling and a brick wall were built around the oil house "as a protection from sand drifts."[^20] The light showed a sector of red over certain shoals.[^10]

In the twentieth century the keepers' work changed with the equipment. Ephraim H. Riggs, principal keeper from 1907 to 1922 by the archive's roster (LighthouseFriends gives 1908 as the start), led the station that won the Fifth District's efficiency pennant for the fiscal year 1913.[^13][^9][^21][^22] On 24 June 1920 Riggs, first assistant Isaac C. Meekins and third assistant Barney Thomas rendered assistance to a disabled hydroplane.[^23] The Commissioner of Lighthouses, in his report for 1921, called Cape Henry "the most prominent and frequently visited station in the district" and listed $24,300 in his estimates for improving its grounds and structures.[^24] That year the Bulletin reported experiments at Cape Henry on whether commercial electric lamps could be used in old first-order lenses; the preliminary trials found a great increase in brilliancy.[^25] LighthouseFriends dates the electric conversion to 1922.[^9] The 1924 Bulletin says a spark fog-signal transmitter at Cape Henry had been sending signals in fog since June 1923.[^26] In 1929 an electrically operated oscillator replaced the siren. After a test on 26 February, a synchronised radio and air fog-signal pair was put in commission on 23 May; the Bulletin called it apparently the first synchronisation of a regular sound-in-air fog signal with a radiobeacon for distance-finding.[^27] A fog-signal testing laboratory was set up at the station in 1934, according to LighthouseFriends.[^9]

Meekins rose from first assistant (1919-22) to principal keeper (1922-38); William L. Geaslen served as first assistant from 1938 to 1954 and then as principal keeper until 1968; Utah C. Jennette was second assistant for 24 years, 1930-54.[^13] From 1947 the archive also lists Coast Guard personnel, and LighthouseFriends says the Lighthouse Service was folded into the Coast Guard in 1939.[^13][^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Civil War darkened the old tower. LighthouseFriends says that in April 1861 men from Princess Anne County seized the tower and destroyed the lamps and lens, that a lightship served the Union side off the cape, and that in 1863 the light was repaired, relit and put under military guard.[^5] The Bulletin of 1930 agrees that the lantern was destroyed and the light working again in 1863.[^2] A news item of May 1861 in a Nebraska paper blamed the darkness for a wreck: the British ship Albion "went ashore near Cape Henry and will be a total loss," and "Virginians having removed the Cape Henry lighthouse and built a fire on the beach some half a mile away, deceived the Captain." The item is a second-hand wartime report, and it does not say what was removed.[^28]

## After the keepers

The sources disagree on when the last keeper left. The archive records automation in 1983, LighthouseFriends says the tower has been fully automated since 1984, and a historical marker erected by Preservation Virginia says the last light keeper retired in 1999 and calls Cape Henry the last manned light station in Virginia.[^13][^9][^29]

The new tower came near to being given up. LighthouseFriends reports that some in the Coast Guard proposed demolishing it, in the hope that a historical society would then step in, and quotes a late-1980s memo in which the Fifth District commander refused to replace it with a skeleton tower, writing that he would keep it as one of the major landfall aids for the Chesapeake Bay entrance.[^9] The Association for the Preservation of Virginia Antiquities asked for control in 2005; as of 2024, according to that source, no agreement had been reached. The Coast Guard hired a contractor in 2016 to restore the tower.[^9]

The old tower was saved earlier. On 26 April 1896 the Association for the Preservation of Virginia Antiquities placed a tablet on it marking the first landing of English colonists on Virginia soil.[^1] An act of Congress of 18 June 1930 authorised the transfer of the tower to the Association, and the National Register nomination says 1.77 acres of surrounding land went with it.[^2][^1] The nomination says wind erosion had threatened the foundation and that repairs followed the transfer.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that the tower was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1964, that it was closed after the earthquake of 23 August 2011 and reopened on 8 October that year, and that in 2018 the dune was raised and a granite retaining wall built around the foundation in an $845,000 project shared by the City of Virginia Beach and Preservation Virginia; the tower reopened in spring 2019.[^5]

## Today

The two towers stand inside Joint Expeditionary Base Fort Story at 583 Atlantic Avenue, Virginia Beach, so reaching them means passing base security.[^30] Preservation Virginia runs the old tower, which it can open only on guided, timed tickets; visitors 18 or older without base access must submit a security form in advance, and visitors over 16 must show a valid REAL ID.[^30] WHRO reported on 21 August 2026 that the old tower had been closed to civilians since February for security reasons and had reopened.[^4] The black-and-white tower is still in service as an aid to navigation but is closed to the public.[^30][^4]
