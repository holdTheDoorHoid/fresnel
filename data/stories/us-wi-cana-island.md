Cana Island Light is a brick tower on a small island on the Lake Michigan shore of Wisconsin's Door Peninsula, about five miles (8 km) northeast of Baileys Harbor.[^1] It was first lit in 1870.[^1] The island has been a Door County park since 2006, run in partnership with the Door County Maritime Museum, which keeps exhibits in the keepers' house and opens the tower to visitors.[^2][^3]

## The place

The Lighthouse Board began looking for a new site on the peninsula's Lake Michigan shore after deciding that the old Baileys Harbor light was badly sited for the two jobs it had to do, guarding the coast and guiding ships into the harbor.[^1] In July 1868, after President Andrew Johnson reserved Cana Island for the light, Congress approved giving up the Baileys Harbor light and building the new one.[^1] The National Register nomination calls the site "strategically situated between two good natural harbors, North and Moonlight bays, where ships in peril could readily find refuge from Lake Michigan storms."[^4]

The island is about nine acres. A causeway links it to the mainland, and at high lake levels water can cover the causeway.[^2][^3] The nomination says the causeway was a gravel one built around 1917, while LighthouseFriends calls it a natural causeway about 300 feet (91 m) long.[^4][^1]

## Building the light

The tower stood on an eight-foot (2.4 m) foundation and had double walls for ventilation. It was built of cream-colored Milwaukee brick, and when it was finished it was the tallest brick structure in Door County.[^1] The county gives the cost of the work as $12,792.55.[^2] The tower is 89 feet (27 m) tall.[^3]

The lantern was assembled in the fall of 1869, but the light was not shown until 24 January 1870. The county page gives 28 January for the first official lighting.[^1][^2] The original lens was a third-order Fresnel, installed in 1870.[^5]

In April 1890 Commander C. E. Clark came to oversee a renovation. A crew of twenty-five filled in the lowest half acre of the island with dirt and stone, built a 400-foot (122 m) breakwater, put up a fireproof stone oil house, and added a footbridge across the causeway.[^1] The Board's annual report for the fiscal years 1896 and 1897 says the boatways and the barn were rebuilt, and that "the well was deepened 28 feet, making its total depth 50 feet below lake level."[^6] A boathouse repair in fiscal 1901, the year that ended on 30 June 1901, moved the building some 40 feet (12 m) farther out toward the water and set it on a crib foundation.[^7]

The brickwork was deteriorating. Rather than repair it, the Board's engineers encased the tower in steel plates, riveted together around it, and filled the space between tower and casing with concrete.[^1] The sources give different years. LighthouseFriends dates the work from June through August 1902, and the USLHS record says 1902.[^1][^5] The National Register nomination says the tower was "encased in a steel shell in 1901."[^4]

## The keepers

William Jackson became the first keeper in November 1869. His wife Caroline was made assistant keeper, and the couple, with a daughter and four sons, lived on both floors of the dwelling.[^1][^5] On 29 August 1872 a storm capsized the family's boat. Jackson and one of his sons swam ashore, and the family left the island not long afterward.[^1]

Julius Warren succeeded Jackson in November 1872. His wife Sara was later appointed assistant keeper.[^1][^5] In 1875 the Warrens were "removed" from their posts. They went back to the family farm at Clay Banks and lived there for the rest of their lives.[^1]

William A. Sanderson kept the light from 1875 to 1891. His wife Sarah was assistant keeper from 1875 to 1882, and the family had three children.[^1][^5] LighthouseFriends says Sanderson kept a much more detailed log than Warren and recorded several storms.[^1] In October 1882 Congress failed to pay the salaries of the assistant keepers at eleven other lights, and Sarah's post ended with them. Two years later the Board reinstated the post and appointed the couple's son Clifford as assistant.[^1][^5] Sanderson resigned in 1891, complaining of the isolation and of the water that dripped from the house's walls all year round.[^1]

Jesse T. Brown, appointed in 1891, kept the light until April 1913, when he was transferred to the South Haven light in Michigan. His son Lawrence was the assistant from 1893.[^1][^5] Conrad A. Stram, who came with his wife and two sons, replaced him. LighthouseFriends says Stram kept the light lit all year, because stronger steel-hulled ships could break through the ice floes.[^1]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year 1916 lists C. A. Stram as keeper and M. Weiss as assistant keeper at the Cana Island Light Station. In its record of assistance to vessels, the same report credits the two with pulling a motor boat that had run on rocks out of danger.[^8] In March 1918 Stram was assigned as master of the Lansing Shoal lightship.[^1]

Oscar H. Knudsen replaced him in 1918. Knudsen and his assistant, Morris Weiss, cleared a good deal of brush from the island and did minor repairs.[^1][^5] The Commissioner's record of assistance for the fiscal year 1920 also lists O. H. Knudsen as keeper of the Cana Island Light Station.[^9] Knudsen moved to the Grosse Point light in 1924 to be near his son, then a student at Northwestern University.[^1]

Clifford William Sanderson had worked as his father's assistant from 1884 to 1889. He came back in 1924 to head the station he had grown up in.[^1][^5] On 1 July 1933 Ross F. Wright succeeded him. LighthouseFriends calls Wright the last civilian keeper at Cana Island.[^1] The roster runs Michael S. Drezdzon as head keeper until 1946.[^5] The county, however, says the light was automated in 1944, so the two dates disagree.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 16 October 1880 the lighthouse "survived one of the worst storms ever to hit the peninsula," the nomination says.[^4] It quotes Hjalmar Roland's history of Door County: "The waves ran so high that at the...lighthouse the sea frequently broke over the house."[^4] LighthouseFriends prints a report by Keeper Sanderson of the same storm. In it the water broke into the lower part of the lighthouse, the family fled to the boathouse, and the water drowned the keeper's chickens. LighthouseFriends calls the storm the "Alpena Gale."[^1]

In early October 1919 the wooden bulk carrier Frank O'Connor, bound from Buffalo to Milwaukee with about 3,000 tons of coal, caught fire near Baileys Harbor.[^1][^13] A Milwaukee newspaper of 3 October describes a fire "of unknown origin" that destroyed the steamer "late Thursday," which was 2 October, and says that the crew of 21 men was rescued by the Baileys Harbor coast guard crew.[^13] LighthouseFriends dates the fire to 4 p.m. on 3 October. In its account the keepers, Knudsen and his assistant, launched the station's boat and towed the crew's lifeboats to shore, and a caption on the same page says the Coast Guard arrived and took the crew onto Baileys Harbor.[^1] The Commissioner's record of assistance for the fiscal year 1920 lists Knudsen's station as having rescued the crew of the burned vessel.[^9] The sources disagree on the day and on who took the crew off. The county says the 300-foot (91 m) steamer, built in 1892, burned to the waterline in 1919.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index records that the Cana Island station received the Lighthouse Efficiency Flag, and LighthouseFriends dates the award to 1919.[^10][^1]

In a note on unusual distances, the 1933 Bulletin reports that the keepers at Cana Island saw the Point Betsie light, 47 miles away, and the Sturgeon Bay Canal light, 24 miles away, on the night of 3 August. The note gives no year.[^11]

## After the keepers

The acetylene light went in during mid-November 1924, and the station was to shut down for the winter.[^1] In early spring the keepers were to return and switch the light back to an incandescent oil vapor burner.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates electricity to 1934, when power lines were strung across the causeway.[^1] The county says the light was automated in 1944.[^2]

During the Second World War the Coast Guard used the station for training.[^1] After the war the Coast Guard leased the dwelling to a private family for summers, for about 25 years.[^2][^1] In 1970 the Door County Maritime Museum took control of the island and lighthouse.[^1] The county says the museum had worked with the Coast Guard on the site since that year.[^2] Louis and Rosie Janda served as caretakers from 1976 until 1995 according to the county, or from 1977 according to LighthouseFriends.[^2][^1] The lighthouse was listed in the National Register in 1976.[^2]

Ownership changed again in the 2000s. The county says the Bureau of Land Management transferred the land to Door County free of charge on 27 April 2006, to be used as a park.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the transfer to 2007.[^1] The tower opened to the public for climbing in 2008.[^2][^1]

## Today

Cana Island Lighthouse is a county park, managed by the county with the Door County Maritime Museum. The light remains a functioning navigation light.[^2] The lighthouse and tower are open from May through October, seven days a week, from 10 a.m. to 5 p.m. The last tower climb starts at 4:30 p.m., and the tower's spiral staircase has 97 steps to the gallery deck.[^3] When the lake is high, water sometimes flows over the causeway, and a complimentary tractor and hay-wagon ride carries visitors across.[^3] The wreck of the Frank O'Connor lies just off the island, and its steel parts remain at the bottom of the lake for divers to see, the county says.[^2]

A severe storm struck northern Door County on Saturday, 9 August, at about 4 p.m., with straight-line winds the museum puts above 70 mph. It trapped about 20 visitors and staff in the island's parking lot until after 11 p.m. and brought down dozens of trees, so the island was closed for three days. The museum says no historic structures were damaged, and the island reopened on Wednesday, 13 August.[^12]
