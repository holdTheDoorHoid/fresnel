Beaver Island Harbor Light, also called the St. James Light, is a brick tower at the entrance to St. James harbor, on the northern end of Beaver Island in Lake Michigan.[^2] The light station was established in 1856.[^1] The tower that stands there now dates from 1870 and still holds its fourth-order lens.[^1][^2] The keepers' brick dwelling was torn down in 1941 and 1942, and the tower, owned by St. James Township, is closed.[^2]

## The place

Beaver Island is the largest island in Lake Michigan, and one of fourteen islands in its archipelago.[^2] Elizabeth Whitney was four years old when her parents brought her to the island in 1848, the year James Strang arrived with his followers.[^2] Strang was shot on the island on 16 July 1856 by two of his followers, and died a few weeks later.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that four days before his death a drunken mob of non-Mormon islanders arrived and drove out all the Strangites.[^2]

The Whitneys were among the non-Mormon families who left the island in 1852, as tensions grew.[^2] They came back in 1857.[^2] As their ship entered the harbor, Elizabeth noticed a new lighthouse on the point.[^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends dates Congress's $6,000 appropriation for a harbor light to 3 August 1854.[^2] The brick tower went up in 1856 with a sixth-order Fresnel lens giving a fixed white light, according to the same source.[^2] The Lighthouse Society's record gives a fourth-order lens for 1856, so the order of the first lens is in dispute.[^1][^2]

By 1867 the Lighthouse Board judged the light poor.[^2] As LighthouseFriends quotes the Board's report, the harbor was "but indifferently lighted"; the brick tower walls were "badly cracked," and the tower, joined to the dwelling by a passage, did not rise above the dwelling's roof. The Board proposed "building a new and higher tower."[^2]

Congress provided $5,000 on 20 July 1868.[^2] A new cylindrical brick tower with an iron stairway was built in 1870.[^2] Williams records that "A new fourth order lens was placed in the new tower and the color of the light changed from white to red."[^2][^3]

Lyman Granger, the first keeper on the Lighthouse Society's roster (1857 to 1859), had come to "Beaver Island harbor light, just erected the year before," Williams writes. She says Mrs. Granger "seeing to the polishing of the lamp and fixtures herself."[^3][^1]

Williams's memoir, copyrighted in 1905, also names a Mr. Van Allen as "the first keeper when the light was first erected," and a Mr. Loaney as the second, after the Mormons were driven off the island. She gives no dates for either man. The memoir's "Beaver Island lighthouse at the head of the Island" may or may not be this light. The Society's roster does not name either man, and this story does not add them to it.[^3][^1]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society's roster lists ten principal keepers, from Granger in 1857 to Ernst E. Winter, who served until 1927.[^1]

Peter McKinley served from 1859 to 1869.[^1] Williams writes that he "lost his health soon after his appointment," and that his two daughters, Effie and Mary, kept the light: "the girls took charge of the light house."[^3] LighthouseFriends dates McKinley's resignation to August 1869, after which Clement Van Riper took his place. Van Riper had married Elizabeth Whitney in 1860.[^2][^1]

Van Riper's health was poor. Williams writes: "My husband having now very poor health I took charge of the care of the lamps."[^3] She describes the work: "We burned the lard oil, which needed great care, especially in cold weather, when the oil would congeal and fail to flow fast enough to the wicks. In long nights the lamps had to be trimmed twice each night, and sometimes oftener."[^3]

The roster ends Van Riper's service in 1872, and the memoir says he drowned in a storm described below.[^1][^3] Elizabeth Whitney Williams was appointed keeper after him, a few weeks later, and held the post until 1884.[^1][^2][^3] LighthouseFriends says she married Daniel Williams in 1875.[^2] In 1884 she asked to move to the mainland and was transferred to the new Little Traverse light at Harbor Point, whose lamp was "lighted the first time September 25th, 1884."[^3]

Five keepers followed her on the roster: Charles A. Lindstrem (1884 to 1886), Gustavus Umberham (1886 to 1890), John C. Gallagher (1890 to 1891), Joseph Fountain (1891 to 1892) and Nelson Knudsen (1892 to 1899).[^1] Ernst E. Winter, the last keeper on the roster, served from 1899 to 1927.[^1] LighthouseFriends says his was the longest stint of any keeper here, and that the 1930 census lists him, aged seventy-two, as custodian of the lighthouse.[^2][^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The memoir describes a night storm that brought the schooner *Thomas Howland* toward the harbor in distress, but it gives no year. This story dates the storm to 1872, the year the roster ends Van Riper's service, and no other source gives a date.[^3][^1] "One dark and stormy night we heard the flapping of sails and saw the lights flashing in the darkness," Williams writes. "The ship was in distress. After a hard struggle she reached the harbor and was leaking so badly she sank." Clement Van Riper, Williams's husband and the keeper, was drowned in his efforts to help the crew, together with the schooner's first mate. Williams writes: "My husband in his efforts to assist them lost his life." "The bodies were never recovered."[^3][^1]

On 19 July 1888, in a southwest blow, the coal-laden schooner *A. Boody*, of Detroit, was being towed to shelter in Beaver Harbor. She got out of the channel and stranded about four hundred yards southeast of the Beaver Island Station of the Life-Saving Service.[^6] The memoir's list of illustrations includes "The Light House and Life Saving Station at Beaver Island Harbor, Michigan," but the sources do not say whether that is the station the report calls the Beaver Island Station.[^3]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Board's report for 1892 records work on the grounds: "Some 800 running feet of fence were built around the station buildings and provided with four gates."[^4] In 1895 the dwelling's cellar was re-excavated, and "a concrete floor 5 inches thick was laid."[^5]

The keeper's brick dwelling came down in the winter of 1941 to 1942, LighthouseFriends reports. In 1942 the light took the name St. James, and it was electrified in 1949.[^2] The township took a renewable ten-year lease of the brick tower in 2000, and the lighthouse itself passed to it in 2005.[^2]

## Today

St. James Township owns the lighthouse, and its tower is closed.[^2] Ferry service runs to the island from Charlevoix, and LighthouseFriends notes that it is possible to walk from the ferry dock to the lighthouse.[^2] The Lighthouse Society's archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^1]

In March 2011 a representative of the Great Lakes Lighthouse Keepers Association visited the island to discuss restoring the harbor lighthouse at Whiskey Point. The estimate of $196,000 covered masonry, doors, windows, lantern work, drainage and painting. By October 2011 the long-term restoration was still unresolved, and the estimate is not evidence that the work was funded.[^7]
