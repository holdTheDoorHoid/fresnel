Point Sherman Light stands on a point on the east shore of Lynn Canal, north of Juneau, Alaska.[^1][^2][^3] The point was named in 1869 by the naval officer Richard W. Meade, after General William Tecumseh Sherman, and a shallow ledge and a reef called Sherman Rock lie off it.[^2] The Alaska marker puts the light about 33 nautical miles (38 statute miles) from Juneau,[^3] and CoastView about 40 nautical miles (46 statute miles) to the north-west.[^2] The station was first lit on 18 October 1904.[^1]

## Building the light

The Alaska marker says Point Sherman "was among the lighthouses hastily built in 1904 to mark hazards found during the gold rush."[^3] Between 1896 and 1899 the traffic to the Klondike goldfields through the boom towns of Skagway and Dyea made Lynn Canal a busy route, and a run of lighthouses went up along the Inside Passage, with Point Sherman among them.[^2]

The station was built during the summer of 1904 with hired labor. LHF says it was the eleventh lighthouse built by the United States in Alaska.[^1][^2] The light sat on a white, hexagonal wooden tower six feet (1.8 m) high, with a black hexagonal lantern room, and a one-and-one-half-story keeper's dwelling and a boathouse stood just to the east.[^1][^4] The archive gives a focal height of 38 feet (11.6 m) above high water for the original light.[^4] LHF and CoastView describe it as a fixed white oil light until 1911.[^1][^2] The Alaska marker, however, lists its original optic as an "acetylene lantern," which conflicts with LHF and CoastView.[^3]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists four people for the station. Andrew Jackson, the first keeper of the light, was principal keeper from 1905 to 1909.[^1][^4] Olaf Otteson served in 1909, and David H. Somerville from 1909 to 1911. Patrick Cannon was acting principal keeper in 1908.[^4]

LHF prints a report that Andrew Jackson submitted on 10 November 1907, on the seeds and plants he had been sent from an experiment station. It covers apple trees, currants, raspberries and vegetables. The report says, "My greatest success has been with potatoes," and that the light-house inspector, P. J. Werlich, had told him his potato tops were the largest he had seen in Alaska.[^1]

In 1911, according to LHF and CoastView, the oil light gave way to an acetylene light flashing white every three seconds; the station became automatic and the last resident keeper left.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Daily Alaskan of Skagway reported on Thursday, 31 March 1910, that two keepers of the Eldred Rock light had been drowned. The paper said they had left Eldred Rock "for Sherman light, about six miles distant," and that the two "were drowned last Tuesday somewhere between Eldred rock and Point Sherman." The paper names them as Adams and "Scotty" Curran, and says an overturned boat was sighted drifting off Sherman point.[^5] These men kept the Eldred Rock light, not Point Sherman, and they are not on this station's roster.

## After the keepers

CoastView says the 1911 change raised the light's focal plane "from 38 feet (11.6 m) to 42 feet (12.8 m) above high water."[^2] The Alaska marker says the keeper's dwelling was "destroyed in the 1940s when a gold mine caretaker set fire to it to attract a passing vessel."[^3]

The sources do not agree on when the light was given up. The marker says it was "Disestablished: before 1917." The archive gives 1932 as the year it was discontinued, and LHF says the Forest Service took the station that year and a buoy replaced it.[^3][^4][^1]

In 1981 a day marker and a light were placed on the site of the original lighthouse, and the Coast Guard operates them.[^2]

## Today

The day marker displays a pair of red-and-white diamonds, and its light has a focal plane of 47 feet (14.5 m).[^2] The archive lists the station as not open to the public, and LHF says the grounds and dwelling are closed and that the Coast Guard owns the site.[^4][^1] Ships and ferries passing through Lynn Canal can see the light, but the site itself is not open to visitors.[^1]
