Scotch Cap Light stood on the southwest corner of Unimak Island in the Aleutians, where it marked the entrance to Unimak Pass. The first light went up in 1903. On 1 April 1946 a tsunami that followed an earthquake destroyed the lighthouse and killed its five-man Coast Guard crew. The tower on the site today, the Society's record says, dates from 1950.[^2]

## The place

Unimak Pass was the passage from the Pacific Ocean to the Bering Sea.[^8] In 1918 the Commissioner of Lighthouses called the station "an important coast station located on Unimak Pass," and said that "all vessels passing from the Pacific Ocean to Bering Sea" used the pass.[^8]

The 1999 Senate tribute says the station's only contact with the outside world was a visit from a buoy tender "every three months or so."[^3] A 1923 Lighthouse Service Bulletin describes a mail boat instructed to land mail at Scotch Cap, and says that the Cape Sarichef and Scotch Cap stations "exchange code messages with the naval radio station at Dutch Harbor and are thus in touch with the outside world."[^14] The two accounts do not agree.

Fog was a steady hazard.[^11] A 1930 issue of the Lighthouse Service Bulletin called the region "one of the foggiest in the world."[^11] A 1931 issue, which printed its list of the foggiest stations after its account of the year ending 30 June 1930, gives Scotch Cap 1,249 hours of fog against an average of 1,003 hours over 23 years.[^12]

The sea also threatened the buildings.[^8] In 1918 the Commissioner said shore protection was needed because "the dwellings are threatened by encroachment of the sea."[^8]

## Building the light

The 1999 Senate tribute calls Scotch Cap "the first light put in place on the outside coast of Alaska."[^3] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record gives 1904 as the year the original lens was installed, a first order bivalve.[^2]

In the fiscal year 1917 the Commissioner reported that four air diaphones had been installed on the Pacific coast "in place of sirens or whistles," and Scotch Cap was one of the stations named.[^7] In 1918 the Commissioner proposed a flashing lens in place of the fixed one, which would "render it distinctive from other fixed lights."[^8] In 1921 the Commissioner reported plans for "radio telephones at Scotch Cap and Cape Sarichef Light Stations," to be installed that summer.[^10] A 1923 Bulletin says the Navy had installed radio telephones at both stations in 1921.[^14] The Lighthouse Service reported in 1930 that a radiobeacon had been established at the station.[^11]

A new concrete tower went up in 1940. A museum caption shows the old light standing behind "the new concrete lighthouse built in 1940."[^6] Rozell describes the tower destroyed in 1946 as a "reinforced concrete structure."[^1] The Society's record calls it an "Old Art Deco Tower."[^2]

## The keepers

The Society's roster for the station runs from 1903 to 1971 and includes principal keepers, assistants and later Coast Guard crews.[^2] The first principal keeper, Eugene M. Walters, served from 1903 to 1907.[^2]

Edmund Moore served as principal keeper from 1913 to 1916 and again from 1917 to 1921.[^2] A 1920 Commissioner's report names him as keeper here.[^9] Odin Bernard Lokken was principal keeper from 1920 to 1927 and again from 1928 to 1931.[^2]

Nicholas Kashevaroff served in three posts between 1918 and 1935: second assistant, first assistant and principal keeper.[^2] Oscar Lindberg joined as a second assistant in 1924 and then spent most of the years to 1943 as first assistant.[^2] George Alexius was principal keeper from 1934 to 1936 and again from 1937 to 1940.[^2] Harry W. Chambers moved from second assistant in 1935 to first assistant in 1939 and principal keeper in 1940 and 1941.[^2]

The principal keeper when the tsunami struck was Anthony Lawrence Petit, a Chief Boatswain's Mate, who held the post from 1945 to 1946.[^2][^3] The 1999 tribute says he "was born and raised on Michigan's Upper Peninsula, in the town of Hancock," enlisted in 1926, and was quoted as saying, "I hope to serve at as many Coast Guard ships and stations as I can before I retire in ten years."[^3]

The work was hard.[^3] The tribute says one keeper "froze both his hands" trying to walk from the tower to his quarters in a blizzard, and that "no families were allowed" on the island.[^3] It says that in the early days keepers were allowed "a full year off for every three years they spent on the island."[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

At 1:30 a.m. on 1 April 1946 the crew woke to "an earthquake lasting about 30 seconds."[^3] Hoban Sanford, a Coast Guard electrician, was in the radio direction-finding building on a terrace "about 100 feet above the lighthouse."[^1] He wrote that the building "creaked and groaned loudly."[^1] A second quake followed about twenty minutes later, and in his memo, quoted by Rozell, "a second severe quake was felt."[^1]

At 2:18 a.m. the wave came.[^1] Sanford's memo reads: "a terrible roaring sound was heard followed almost immediately by a very heavy blow against the side of the building."[^1] He went outside and "saw no lights below," and "the foghorn was silent."[^1] His memo then says, "The Light Station had been completely destroyed."[^1] Rozell writes that the wave left "nothing but the foundation of the reinforced concrete structure."[^1]

The sources do not agree on the height of the wave. The Navy's figure, printed in the Juneau *Daily Alaska Empire* of 5 April, was a "90-foot tidal wave" that demolished the light.[^5] The 1999 tribute gives "over 100 feet," Rozell gives 130 feet (40 m), and the museum gives "well over 120 feet."[^3][^1][^6]

The early news also disagreed on the time. The *Nome Nugget* of 1 April printed a Seattle dispatch reporting that "a tidal wave hit there at 5:30 a.m." (Pacific time) at the Unimak station, with no word on damage.[^4] A Ketchikan dispatch in the same paper said the district Coast Guard officer had reported that a tidal wave "destroyed the strategic Scotch Cap lighthouse station with the loss of all its personnel."[^4] The Navy's count, given on 5 April, was five dead or missing, "instead of the 10 feared possibly lost."[^5]

The men who died were Anthony Petit, Jack Colvin, Dewey Dykstra, Leonard Pickering and Paul Ness.[^1][^3] The radio station on the bluff had a crew of 25 men.[^6] The museum's account, drawing on the recollections of a radioman who was at the station that night, says "The men worked by kerosene lamps until 5:50 a.m., when an emergency generator was brought online."[^6]

Three days later, the survivors buried their dead just before most of the men left on a Coast Guard cutter.[^1] Rozell gives the graves' position as "300 yards from the site of the light."[^1]

The cause is disputed. Scientists long thought the earthquake itself made the wave.[^1] Rozell reports that a USGS scientist has since shown "a mountain of rocks on the sea floor that appears to be from a massive underwater landslide," which may have made it.[^1]

The Alaska Historical Society's collection, held at the University of Alaska Anchorage, includes "seven pages from a high frequency direction finder logbook" kept at the radio station from 27 to 31 March 1946. The pages were "found in debris behind where the lighthouse was located."[^13]

## After the keepers

Within two weeks, the Coast Guard buoy tender Clover "established a temporary unmanned light at Scotch Cap."[^6] The museum says the radio station crew "remained on Unimak Island until the end of April."[^6] Rozell's account has most of the men leaving Unimak three days after the tsunami, so the sources differ on when the crew went.[^1]

The museum's photo caption describes the replacement as "Automated lighthouse built at Scotch Cap after the tsunami to replace the former manned lighthouse."[^6] None of the sources read gives a year for automation. The Society's record dates the present tower to 1950 and describes it as concrete and skeletal: "Year Tower Established: 1950" and "Tower Shape: SKELETAL."[^2]

In January 1999 Senator Murkowski marked "the christening of the USCGC Anthony Petit," a cutter named for the keeper who died at the station.[^3][^6]

## Today

The Society's record says the station is not open to the public ("Open to Public: No") and that the aid to navigation is not active ("Active Aid to Navigation?: No").[^2] The museum's caption describes an automated light built after the tsunami, but no source read says whether that light shows today.[^6]

Rozell calls the men's posting "their lonely Coast Guard assignment on Unimak Island."[^1] It is not open to the public, and visitors should not try to land on the island to see it.[^2]
