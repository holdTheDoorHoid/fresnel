Panmure Head Lighthouse is a white, eight-sided wooden tower on the north-east point of Panmure Island, on the eastern shore of Prince Edward Island, built in 1853 to guide ships into Georgetown Harbour and keep them off a dangerous reef. It was the island's first wooden lighthouse, and its keepers were drawn largely from the farming and fishing families of Panmure Island itself.[^1][^4]

## The place

Georgetown, also called Three Rivers, lies on a peninsula bounded by the Cardigan, Brudenell and Montague rivers. Boughton Island to the north and Panmure Island to the south mark the entrance to Georgetown Harbour and give some shelter from the open water of Northumberland Strait.[^3] The hazards were Bear Reef and Panmure Ledge, shoals just south of the point.[^1][^5]

In 1849 inhabitants of Kings County petitioned the House of Assembly of Prince Edward Island, pointing to the many vessels that used Georgetown Harbour and the risk to them without a light. A committee agreed a lighthouse would be useful and recommended a grant of 150 pounds, but nothing was done. Similar petitions followed in the next two years, and in 1851 a committee asked the Lieutenant Governor to obtain plans and estimates, at first for a light on Boughton Point or another site near the harbour entrance.[^3]

The Lieutenant Governor asked Captain Henry W. Bayfield, then surveying the Gulf of St. Lawrence, where it should stand. Bayfield, as LighthouseFriends reports his answer, favoured Panmure Point, the north-east end of Panmure Island: a light there would lead vessels into Georgetown, guide them clear of Bear Reef, and, by disappearing behind Boughton Island, serve as a guide to ships along the coast.[^3]

## Building the light

The 1851 specification called for a lighthouse and keeper's dwelling of "sound wood and island stone", estimated at about 620 pounds. Work began in 1853, and 647 pounds was spent that year, including 20 pounds to Archibald MacDonald for a one-acre site and right-of-way, and 5 pounds to William MacDonald for clearing the land.[^3] The heritage register gives the same 20 pounds for the land.[^1]

Peter Stewart was paid 384 pounds for building the tower. Thomas Robinson and Benjamin Williams were paid for the lantern, and a contract to finish the lighthouse went later in the year to Henry Williams.[^3] The tower is a heavy timber frame on a stone foundation, built by local craftsmen who used shipbuilding methods; the Panmure Island Lighthouse Association says it was put together with notched beams and wooden pegs (trunnels), without nails in the original work.[^5][^2]

The first keeper drew pay from 20 November 1853, so the light probably went into service around the end of that year.[^3] It is the second lighthouse built on the island after Point Prim (1845), and the first of wood.[^1][^6]

The tower's later history is one of repair and upgrade. A new copper roof was put on in 1858.[^3] In 1861 the Superintendent of Public Works, Thomas Alley, reported leaking windows and snow drifting into the tower, and recommended a fence to keep pigs from the neighbouring farm from rooting at the foundations, and a boat so the keeper could reach Georgetown in an emergency without the long way round.[^3] In 1885 a new lantern was fitted and a fourth-order lens replaced the reflectors; on the night of 6 September 1887 the lamp inside the lens exploded, badly damaging the lens and the lantern glass, and a portable lamp was shown until a new lens arrived.[^3] The Department of Marine's report listing changes to existing lights, in the volume printed in 1909, records a 270-degree fourth-order French lens, 35 mm, with a Chance vapour installation at Panmure Island.[^7]

## The keepers

The sources do not agree on who kept the light, and the differences are worth stating plainly. The Panmure Island Lighthouse Association's list, which matches the list on LighthouseFriends in most respects, gives William MacDonald (1853 to 1859), David O'Brien (1859 to 1865), John Duff (1865 to 1866), James MacDonald (1866 to 1867) and William MacDonald again (1867 to 1901).[^4][^3] The Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society, compiling from Department of Fisheries and Oceans and local records, instead lists David Byron as keeper from 1853 to 1887 and a William Archie MacDonald from 1887 to 1908, and says its own list is incomplete.[^6]

The first keeper was William MacDonald, son of Archibald MacDonald who sold the land, on a salary of 50 pounds a year.[^4][^3] An obituary transcribed on Find a Grave records the death of a "William A. McDonald" at his home at Panmure on Monday night, 11 February 1901, after a two-day illness. It says he had been keeper of the Panmure lighthouse "for a number of years" and had tended the lights on the Saturday before he died. The transcription gives his birth year as 1829 and the memorial page gives 1820; the newspaper is not named.[^12]

Colin Steele followed in 1901; the Department of Marine's salary list gives his date of appointment as 3 June 1901, which agrees with the Association's list; the Society dates his term 1908 to 1910, although the Department's list of lightkeepers printed in 1911 still names Colin Steele at Panmure.[^13][^9][^6] Wallace E. Graham took over in 1910 (the Association gives 1910 to 1912; the Society says he served until about 1920).[^4][^6] William Albert MacDonald then kept the light until he resigned or retired in 1936, at about seventy years old.[^3][^6] The Association says that in all five Panmure Head keepers were MacDonald descendants.[^4]

His granddaughter Therese later recalled that around 1 May 1937 her grandfather, normally not excitable, called the family out of the kitchen to see the airship Hindenburg crossing the horizon, and let each of them look at it through his spyglass. The recollection is dated to about May 1937, a year after the retirement date the sources give for him, so the dates do not fully agree.[^3][^6] Therese MacDonald, writing in August 2003 and quoted by the Society, described a keeper's work in 1930: her grandfather and his assistant, Matt Condon, shared a seven-day, round-the-clock watch, one lighting the lamp at sundown and the other putting it out at sunrise, with hourly checks that the lens was turning, and they also ran the machinery for the fog horn, which sounded every two minutes. She also wrote that he used a spyglass to watch for rum-runners during Prohibition.[^6] The keeper climbed four flights to wind the weights that turned the lens and lit an Aladdin lamp.[^6]

Charles A. Steele kept the light from 1936; the Society says he held the post until his death in 1939, while LighthouseFriends gives 1946 as the end of his term.[^6][^3] George Creed became keeper in 1946 after returning from the Second World War, where he had been a prisoner of war. The Association says he served 34 years; the Society dates his term 1946 to 1967, and LighthouseFriends to 1981.[^4][^6][^3] He and his wife Theresa had five children, and the family moved into the new keeper's house built in 1957. The Society, drawing on the memories of Liz Dalton and Leona Creed, says the children helped wind the weights, switched the light on and off once electricity arrived, and turned on the fog alarm. Supplies arrived by ship or helicopter, and the children took tourists up the tower in the 1970s because visitors were not allowed to climb alone.[^6]

William J. MacDonald, grandson of William Albert MacDonald and Creed's wartime friend, first served as Creed's assistant and then, from November 1984, as the last keeper. He retired on 31 March 1985, when the light was automated.[^6][^1]

Fog-alarm engineers are recorded separately. Mathias Condon was appointed on 4 August 1908; the Association dates his service 1908 to 1938, followed by Augustus Jameson (1938 to 1940) and his son Louis (1940 to 1948), though the Society spells the name Augustine Jamieson and gives Louis's term as 1940 to 1946.[^9][^3][^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources tie few incidents to Panmure Head. In 1903 the can buoy marking Panmure Shoal, "locally known as Wheeler bar buoy", was replaced by a spar buoy moored in eight fathoms, 12 and a half cables from Panmure Head lighthouse.[^10] In March 1906 the steamer Minto, working the Pictou to Georgetown route, grounded in fog "off Panmore island" and was pulled free the next day by the Stanley, according to the Marine Department's report, whose spelling of the island's name is likely a misprint.[^11] During the Second World War the lighthouse was kept dark so that German submarines could not use it for navigation.[^4]

## After the keepers

Fog signalling arrived in 1908, according to LighthouseFriends and the Society.[^3][^6] The Department's report records that B. D. Huntley of Charlottetown erected a wooden fog-alarm building, 9.4 by 7.3 m (31 by 24 ft), to house a 3-inch diaphone plant, for a contract price of $2,800.[^7] The Department's next report records a dwelling for the fog-alarm engineer under construction by J. Clark of Summerside, contract price $1,900.[^8] A separate two-storey keeper's house was built in 1957.[^1][^5] The fog-alarm building was declared surplus in 1980 and moved to an adjacent property.[^3] The light was automated in 1985, and the 1957 house was sold and is a private residence.[^1][^5]

In the late 1950s a bull's-eye Fresnel lens and a wooden tube for the rotating weights were installed, and around 2010 the lens was replaced by a modern beacon.[^3] Prince Edward Island recognised the lighthouse as a heritage place on 3 October 2012.[^3] Ownership passed to the Panmure Island Lighthouse Association, a volunteer group, in December 2015, with a one-time grant of $80,000.[^3][^5] Its restoration began in 2016 by removing later interior additions and exposing the original timbers.[^6]

## Today

The tower is automated; the Canadian Coast Guard List of Lights gives its characteristic as a white flash every four seconds, 25.0 m above the water, with a range of 19 nautical miles.[^14] The Association runs a gift shop on the ground floor and a museum of lighthouse and maritime artifacts on the upper floors.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the tower is open daily from mid-June to mid-October and the grounds are open; check with the Association for current hours.[^3] The neighbouring keeper's house is a private home.[^5]
