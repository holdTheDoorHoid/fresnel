Sugar Pine Point Light is a navigational light on the west shore of Lake Tahoe, ten miles south of Tahoe City, California, inside what is now Ed Z'berg Sugar Pine Point State Park.[^1] The first tower on the point went up in 1921, replacing a light that had stood at Rubicon Point. The light there today is a small post.[^2][^3]

## The place

Lake Tahoe lies on the border of California and Nevada, in the Sierra Nevada range.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for fiscal 1917 gives the lake's length as 21 miles and notes that "a great many passengers are carried on numerous steamers and launches there annually."[^4]

In July 1921 the Associated Press reported that the light at Rubicon Point, "two miles from Emerald bay," was to be moved to Sugar Pine Point.[^3] The wire story said experience had proved that a light "to warn boats away from the dangerous" shoals at Sugar Pine Point was "more necessary." It added that the station "will be visible from more resorts," according to the Service's superintendent of lighthouses for California.[^3]

The park's history puts the land in the hands of one family from the turn of the century until 1965: the financier Isaias W. Hellman, and then his daughter, Florence Hellman Ehrman.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress authorized aids to navigation on Lake Tahoe in an Act of 28 August 1916.[^4] The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1917 says "no appropriation was made therefor."[^4] Two years later the report still said that funds "have not been available to properly light the lake, on which are now many power boats."[^5]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1 October 1919 lists "Rubicon Point, Lake Tahoe, Calif., flashing white" among the lights established.[^6] The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1920 names a "Rubicon Point Light, Lake Tahoe, Calif." in its list of construction and repair work.[^7] Russ Rowlett's Lighthouse Directory dates the Rubicon Point light to 1916 and calls it inactive from 1921.[^2] The sources read for this story do not agree on when the Rubicon Point light was first lit. Rowlett gives 1916. The 1919 bulletin lists it as newly established. The Commissioner's reports for fiscal 1917 and 1919 say no appropriation had been made for the lake's aids and that funds had not been available to light the lake.[^2][^4][^5][^6]

On 1 November 1921 the bulletin listed "Sugar Pine Point, Lake Tahoe, Calif." among the lights established.[^8] Rowlett describes the first structure there as a wood pyramidal tower that replaced the Rubicon Point light in 1921.[^2]

## After the keepers

Rowlett's directory says the original tower was "reportedly removed in 1985."[^2] The present light is a 13 ft (4 m) post with a red and white diamond-shaped daybeacon.[^2] Its focal plane is 15 ft (4.5 m), and it gives a white flash every four seconds.[^2] Rowlett's entry gives the station's establishment as 1921 and the date of the present light as "Date uncertain."[^2]

## Today

The light is listed as active, and Rowlett names the state park as its site manager.[^2] Ed Z'berg Sugar Pine Point State Park has "nearly 2 miles of lake frontage," and its Hellman-Ehrman Mansion, also called Pine Lodge, is "a summer home built in 1903."[^1] The park says its historic estate is open for tours in the summer, for a separate fee.[^1]

The park's information sheet says the Edward F. Dolder Nature Trail passes a light it describes as "the world's highest—in elevation—operating maritime navigational light."[^9] Rowlett notes that this has been claimed as "the world's highest working lighthouse," but that "there are much higher lights on Lake Titicaca in Peru and Bolivia."[^2]
