Alcatraz Island Light marks a rock in San Francisco Bay just inside the Golden Gate. A lamp first shone here on 1 June 1854, the first navigational light lit on the West Coast.[^1][^2] The tower that carries the light today was built in 1909, beside the cottage-style lighthouse it replaced, and it has outlasted the keepers, the prison around it and an occupation of the island.[^1]

## The place

The light came out of the Gold Rush. Thousands of people rounded Cape Horn for San Francisco, ships ran into trouble on the coast, and Congress responded with acts in 1850 and 1851 that funded eight lighthouses along the West Coast.[^1] The National Park Service says the Alcatraz light was meant to guide ships directly in through the Golden Gate.[^3] A modern marker at the Alcatraz Landing departure pier in San Francisco describes rock outcrops in the bay as hazards to Gold Rush ships.[^4]

The lighthouse shared the island with the military. The Alcatraz Citadel, a fortified barracks, was finished in 1859 on the island's upper plateau just north of the lighthouse.[^1]

## Building the light

Most of the eight lighthouses were planned as a one-and-a-half-story cottage with the tower coming up through the roof, which let one stair serve both the upper rooms and the lantern.[^1] An advance party began work on the foundation at Alcatraz in early 1853.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the building was complete by July 1853 but waited almost a year for its lens; George R. Putnam's 1917 history likewise says the Alcatraz tower was complete and "awaiting apparatus" when the Pacific Coast work was organised in 1853.[^1][^5]

The wait was deliberate. The new Lighthouse Board had decided to use Fresnel lenses, so the contractors were told to hold off installing the lamps and reflectors first intended.[^1] The fixed third-order lens was lit on 1 June 1854, with Michael Cassin (spelled "Kassin" by LighthouseFriends) paid $1,100 a year as head keeper and John Sloan $700 as his assistant.[^1] The keepers' roster in the US Lighthouse Society's archive dates Cassin's service from 1853, a year before the lighting.[^2]

That lens was replaced in 1902 by a revolving fourth-order lens.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for fiscal 1901 explains why: in a joint report dated 13 May 1901 the district officers recommended changing the light from fixed to flashing "in order that it may be more readily distinguished from the electric lights surrounding it."[^6] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1915 agrees that the 1854 lens was used until 1902, and was on show at the San Francisco exposition of that year.[^7]

The cottage lighthouse lasted 55 years. Putnam gives the reason: a prison building that would have hidden the light from seaward.[^5] LighthouseFriends adds the detail: the new cell house, with 600 cells, was built where the Citadel had stood, and Congress appropriated $35,000 for a new concrete tower on 4 March 1909. Work began on 20 July 1909, and the lens from the old lighthouse was lit atop the new tower on 1 December 1909, after which the old lighthouse was torn down.[^1] The sources do not agree on the height: LighthouseFriends calls it ninety feet (27 m), the National Park Service gives 95 feet (29 m), and the Alcatraz Lighthouse Preservation Project says 84 feet (26 m), with 112 steps to the lantern.[^1][^8][^9]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists keepers at Alcatraz from 1853 to 1963.[^2] The early posts turned over fast. A modern marker in San Francisco says that long hours and low pay meant high turnover, and that up to three families lived close together in the lighthouse.[^4] The principal keepers after Cassin were John Sloan, Underhill Van Wagner (1856 to 1862) and Hartford Joy (1862 to 1872).[^2]

Women appear on the roster as assistants. The archive lists "Sarah Joy (1862-1872)" as first assistant, the same years Hartford Joy was principal keeper, and "Alpha Bushnell (1872-1873)" and "Sophie Huie (1878-1882)" in the same post.[^2] LighthouseFriends prints the second name as "Mrs. Alpha Rushnell", but the sources do not say how any of these women were related to the men who held the principal keeper's post in those years. The Preservation Project records that in 1872 "Keeper Rushnell" became the first US postmaster for the island; the roster's principal keeper that year is spelled Bushnell.[^2][^9]

Benjamin F. Leeds was first assistant in 1887-88, acting keeper in 1888, and then principal keeper from 1888 to 1905, about seventeen years.[^2] Frederick A. Harrington was first assistant from 1901 to 1903 and returned as principal keeper from 1919 to 1938.[^2] Edward H. Schneider, a keeper at Alcatraz for twenty-eight years by LighthouseFriends' count, was second assistant from 1931, first assistant from 1936 and principal keeper from 1950 until 1959, when LighthouseFriends calls him the station's last civilian keeper.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says he played Santa Claus for some seventy children of prison guards on the island, and the Preservation Project repeats that he did so for many years.[^1][^9] (LighthouseFriends says he arrived as first assistant; the archive roster lists him first as second assistant.)

In 1901 a second assistant was assigned to the station. By LighthouseFriends' account the head keeper then had four rooms in the lighthouse and each assistant two, with a shared kitchen.[^1] The Light-House Board's report of 1896-97 records rebuilding the kitchens, with an upper room and an outside stairway added, and removing the "unsightly outhouse" so the grounds would "conform in neatness to the military post in which it is situated."[^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The fog signals changed several times over the years. LighthouseFriends says a 3,340-pound bell cast in 1883 replaced the old one at the southeast tip.[^1] An early-1890s Light-House Board report records an electrical-bell system put up between the signal and the dwelling.[^11] In the fiscal year 1900 the bell machinery was reported out of order and not sounding on 27 September 1899, and repaired on 4 October.[^12] The Board's report for fiscal 1901 describes a new fog-signal station on the island's northern end, with a 3,000-pound bell, and lists an Alcatraz fog-signal station with a bell struck by machinery, established on 6 April 1901.[^13] In 1913 the bells were replaced by electric sirens at either end of the island, established on 1 May 1913.[^1][^14][^15] LighthouseFriends reports that the prison guards helped the keepers run the fog signals: from the watch towers they could see fog come through the Golden Gate and would tell the keepers.[^1]

In its report in the volume for 1893 the Light-House Board noted that a green buoy marking the wreck of the *Autocrat*, about 200 yards west of the island, had been discontinued because the wreck had completely disappeared.[^16]

On 18 April 1906 the station logbook recorded a violent and continuous earthquake at 5:30 a.m. LighthouseFriends reproduces the entry, "is this the end of the world?", and says the quake put a slight crack in the tower and toppled the dwelling's chimneys; it names Henry W. Young as head keeper, while a modern marker credits the entry to "Captain B.F. Leeds".[^1][^4] The archive roster shows Young as principal keeper in 1906.[^2]

In the "Battle of Alcatraz", which began on 2 May 1946 when inmates seized weapons in the cell house, Marines were landed; Cellblock D was just 150 feet (46 m) from the lighthouse.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the head keeper, Harry Davis, probably wrote the log entries, including that shooting was "almost continuous" and, at the end, "The end of 44 hours of living hell."[^1]

## After the keepers

The Justice Department acquired Alcatraz in 1933 for a federal penitentiary; keepers and prison ran side by side until the light was automated on 22 November 1963.[^1] The National Park Service dates the automation to 1963, when the penitentiary closed and a modern beacon was added.[^8] A modern marker says no one has lived in the lighthouse since.[^4]

In November 1969 about ninety Native Americans landed and claimed the island, and a varying number stayed for nineteen months. The Preservation Project says they painted the top of the lighthouse red and made the tower a symbol of the "Red Power Movement".[^1][^9] On 1 June 1970 a fire destroyed the warden's house, the keepers' quarters and other buildings, and scorched the lighthouse; the occupation ended in a raid on 11 June 1971.[^1] Alcatraz became part of the Golden Gate National Recreation Area in 1972 and a National Historic Landmark in 1986.[^1] In 2015 a donation of $25,000 from Lands' End paid for a historic structure report to guide the lighthouse's preservation.[^1]

## Today

The tower is still an active aid to navigation: the National Park Service says the light "is still operational today".[^3][^9] The lighthouse stands on an island administered by the National Park Service as part of the Golden Gate National Recreation Area; the Park Service lists the lighthouse itself as not open to the public.[^1][^8]
