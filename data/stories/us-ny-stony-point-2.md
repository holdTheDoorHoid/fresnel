Stony Point Light stands on Stony Point, on the west bank of the Hudson at the southern entrance to the Hudson Highlands, in Rockland County, New York.[^3][^4] Its octagonal stone tower was first lit in 1826 and was in service for almost a century.[^3] The Parkinson and Rose families both kept the light.[^4] From 1857 to 1904 its keeper was one woman, Nancy Rose, a widow with six children.[^1][^4]

## The place

The light was built to mark the southern entrance to the Hudson Highlands. The Erie Canal had opened the year before, and LighthouseFriends says the canal sent more traffic onto the river.[^3]

The point had a military history before it had a light. The Tribune of 1903 described the lighthouse as built on the foundation of Stony Point Fort, whose old walls had been filled in.[^1] The Tribune also reported that "Dozens of bullets and grapeshot" had been found about the old fort ground, and that a flagpole marks where Wayne "is supposed to have fallen." The Tribune says Jacob Parkinson, a great-grandfather of Mrs. Rose, was wounded there.[^1] The Evening World of 1904 recalled that Mad Anthony Wayne "stormed the heights" there.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates Wayne's night attack to 15 July 1779.[^3]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says Thomas Phillips of New York City built the tower of blue split stone for $3,350. It was thirty feet (9 m) tall.[^3] The station's original optic was eight patent lamps with 12-inch reflectors.[^4] The keeper Cornelius W. Lansing first lit it on 1 December 1826.[^3]

In 1838 Lieutenant George M. Bache reported on the station. As LighthouseFriends quotes him, the tower "at present is in a very bad condition," and its mortar had not hardened.[^3] The lamps gave way to a fifth-order Fresnel lens in 1856, and a more powerful fourth-order lens followed in 1902.[^3]

A wooden fog-bell tower was added in 1857. In 1890 a new bell tower went up about 200 yards (180 m) east, near the water.[^3] The Tribune puts the move at "something like an eighth" of a mile (about 200 m) nearer the water, which roughly agrees with the 200 yards.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1891 noted that the boat landing near the fog-signal tower had been completed.[^5] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1915 proposed to rebuild the light and fog signal at Stony Point, "which is in poor condition and far from the edge of the channel."[^6]

The first six-room stone dwelling was torn down in 1879, and a replacement was built on the flat land just west of the lighthouse.[^3] In 1903 the Roses lived in a cottage that the Tribune called "only twenty-three years old."[^1]

## The keepers

Robert Parkinson became keeper in 1829. LighthouseFriends says he was the grandson of Jacob Parkinson, who had been wounded in the battle of 1779.[^3][^4] The roster gives Sarah Parkinson as keeper for one year, 1834, between Robert Parkinson and Leonard K. Baker.[^4] No other source says more about her.

The Tribune dates Alexander Rose's appointment to the spring of 1852. The roster and LighthouseFriends give 1853.[^1][^3][^4] LighthouseFriends says that while he was carrying timber for the new bell tower, he ruptured a blood vessel and died a few weeks later.[^3] His widow, Nancy Rose, then kept the light.[^4] The Tribune calls her "a young widow and the mother of six little children," and says "Of these six children only two are now living, Melinda and Alexander."[^1]

The work was constant. The Tribune says she could not leave the house without telling the inspector of her intended absence, and that she had to keep a record of the weather day and night.[^1] The Evening World says that in thick weather she had to climb the tower at least once in every three hours and three-quarters to wind the clockwork that rang the fog bell.[^2] In winter, it adds, the upper tower took the full sweep of the gale, and the walk to the water was icy and blocked with snow.[^2] The Tribune says the most Mrs. Rose ever received was $500 a year.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that once, in a dense fog, she stayed at her post for fifty-six hours, tolling the bell.[^3]

The Tribune gives her tenure as forty-seven years, and the roster's dates, 1857 to 1904, agree.[^1][^4] The Evening World, reporting her death in May 1904, called her "the oldest lighthouse keeper" in the United States and gave her forty-eight years at the post.[^2]

Her daughter Melinda became keeper after her death. LighthouseFriends says she was told at fifty-three that she was too old, sought help from her congressman, and served one year before resigning. She left because the pay of $560 was "too little for the amount of work and responsibility."[^3] The roster lists Melinda Rose from 1904 to 1905, then Frank Guyette from 1905 to 1918 and Millard Caler from 1918 to 1941.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The only wreck that either newspaper describes was that of the Poughkeepsie, a steamer of the Central Hudson Steamboat Company. The Tribune says it happened one March night, between one and two in the morning, while Mrs. Rose was changing out of storm-soaked clothes. "Forty or fifty persons, among them seven women, asked for admittance," and the family gave them "hot coffee until the next down train was due."[^1] The Evening World says the steamer ran on the rock in fog, and that an effort to pension Mrs. Rose failed.[^2] The two accounts disagree about the weather. The Tribune's year is garbled in the scanned text, and LighthouseFriends gives none.

In 1923 Keeper Caler rescued two men whose boat overturned about twenty feet (6 m) from shore. LighthouseFriends says he gave them warm clothes and hot drinks and sent them home.[^3]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says that in 1925 Keeper Caler put out the beacon at the top of the lighthouse for good, when a steel tower built near the shoreline replaced it. The steel tower was operated manually until 1973 and was "the last manned light on the Hudson River."[^3] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's place record gives the years discontinued as 1925-1995.[^4]

The Hudson River Lighthouses thematic nomination for the National Register, which covers Stony Point, describes the building as unoccupied, with access "restricted."[^7] LighthouseFriends says restoration began in 1986 and was complete on 7 October 1995. The light was activated that day for the first time in seventy years, on solar power, with a flash every four seconds. A fourth-order Fresnel lens was placed in the lantern in 1995. LighthouseFriends says it was removed around 2011 and put on display in the museum at the battlefield site.[^3]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by the Palisades Interstate Park Commission. It stands at the Stony Point Battlefield State Historic Site. LighthouseFriends credits the battlefield site, the park commission and the state parks office with the restoration.[^3] The lighthouse has exhibits and a restored interior. The grounds are open, and the tower is open during tours. LighthouseFriends advises calling ahead to confirm the hours.[^3] The lighthouse itself no longer shows a light. The Coast Guard maintains a flashing light on a skeletal tower at the end of the point.[^3]
