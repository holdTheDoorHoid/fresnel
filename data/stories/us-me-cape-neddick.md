Cape Neddick Light, known as the Nubble, stands on a rocky island just off York Beach, Maine.[^1][^3] Its cast-iron tower, lined with brick, went up in 1879 and is still an active aid to navigation.[^2][^3] About half a million people a year come to Sohier Park on the mainland to look across the channel at it.[^3]

## The place

The Nubble is largely bare rock, and the National Register nomination says a bar connects it with the mainland at very low tide.[^1]

Local mariners asked for a light on the Nubble as early as 1807, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] In 1837 Congress appropriated $5,000 for one. Captain Joseph Smith, a former revenue cutter captain who knew the coast well, advised against it. He wrote that the only object of a light there that he could see was to guide coasting vessels through an inshore passage between York Ledge, a dangerous shoal, and Stone's Rock. He thought an additional light would tend to embarrass rather than aid navigation, and he proposed a beacon on York Ledge instead.[^3]

In November 1842 the bark *Isidore* was wrecked north of the Nubble, near Bald Head Cliff. LighthouseFriends says that wreck showed the need for a light.[^3] Congress appropriated another $5,000 in 1852, but the money was withdrawn after a coast survey found the light unnecessary.[^3] In 1874 the Lighthouse Board asked for $15,000 to build one.[^3]

The island had multiple owners, and plans for a hotel there complicated the purchase. The government bought the Nubble for $1,500 in February 1879.[^3]

## Building the light

The tower is 41 feet (12.5 m) tall to the center of the lantern, according to the archive. The National Register nomination gives its height as 39 feet (11.9 m), so the two sources disagree.[^2][^1] Its cast-iron sections were made in Portland and carried to the island on the USS *Myrtle* in April 1879. The inside was lined with red brick.[^3]

The archive gives the first light as 1879, with a fourth-order lens. The nomination says the tower is equipped with a fourth-order light.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends says the fourth-order red light was first shown on 1 July 1879.[^3]

The tower was white at first and was later painted reddish brown. It was painted white again on 1 September 1899, a change the Board's report for fiscal 1900 records.[^3][^5]

The first fog bell hung in an A-frame tower. In 1890 a striking machine was moved from Pond Island and set up at the Nubble.[^3] The Board's report for fiscal 1896 records that the fog bell was disabled on 16 April and repaired on 20 April 1896.[^4] A boathouse and boat slip were built in 1888. LighthouseFriends says a telephone line to the village of York Beach followed in 1898.[^3] The Board's report for fiscal 1900 records that the boat slip was lengthened by some 40 feet (12 m), that about 50 large boulders were taken from its approach, and that a storm porch was built at the tower.[^6] The red brick oil house was added in 1902.[^2][^3] The frame bell tower was replaced in 1911 by an enclosed pyramidal tower, which stayed on the island until 1961.[^3]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists thirty-five principal keepers and one temporary keeper, from 1879 to 1987.[^2] LighthouseFriends calls Nathaniel H. Otterson the light's first keeper. It says an illness forced Simon Leighton, who had been given the post first, to step aside.[^3][^2]

Otterson served until 1885, on $500 a year.[^3] LighthouseFriends notes that he was a cousin of New Hampshire Governor Natt Head, and suggests that the connection probably helped him get the post.[^3] LighthouseFriends quotes a *Portsmouth Journal* notice that the keeper's son would row visitors over for ten cents, and says the ten-cent fare lasted for many years.[^3]

Brackett Lewis kept the light from 1885 to 1904. LighthouseFriends says he was able to tune out the fog bell so well that he sometimes forgot to switch it off. In 1898 he was forbidden to let visitors onto the island on Sundays. He said that was because he was making too much money. LighthouseFriends thinks the real reason was that crowds were getting in the way of his duties.[^3]

William M. Brooks kept the light from 1904 to 1912. He sold fishing tackle and bait to visitors, and his wife gave a five-cent tour of the station. His superiors complained that 200 to 300 people at certain times were roaming the reservation with only the keeper's wife to look after government property.[^3]

James M. Burke came in 1912 with twenty-five years of lighthouse service behind him.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the family kept a cow and chickens and ate local mussels, crab, lobster, fish and duck. His daughter Lucy later wrote of a six-room, gingerbread-trimmed house with a large parlor stove and no bathroom.[^3]

William P. Richardson kept the light from 1919 to 1921. LighthouseFriends says he was fired for ferrying sightseers to the island.[^3]

Fairfield H. Moore kept the light from 1921 to 1928. He used a 35-millimeter mantle in the incandescent oil vapor lamp for 142 nights, from 8 September 1922 to 27 January 1923, and reported on it to the Service.[^11]

LighthouseFriends reports that an explosion in 1928 damaged the fourth-order lens, and that a duplicate was brought from another station.[^3]

LighthouseFriends remembers Eugene L. Coleman, the keeper in the 1930s and 1940s, for the station cat. A large tomcat called Sambo Tonkus, or Mr. T, had been left at the station by its first keeper-owner. Coleman was shocked one day to see the cat paddle to the mainland and come back with a fat mouse.[^3] The archive gives Coleman's stint as 1930 to 1942. LighthouseFriends gives 1946.[^2][^3]

In 1967 a Coast Guard keeper's son was photographed being taken to school in a basket that ran on a line over the water. The picture ran in newspapers across the country, and the Coast Guard then ruled that no family with school-age children would be stationed on the Nubble again.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The bark *Isidore* was wrecked north of the Nubble in November 1842, according to LighthouseFriends. Nothing further about the loss appears in the sources cited here.[^3]

On 12 September 1913 Burke towed a disabled launch to the light station in a heavy southwesterly wind, and took care of its occupants overnight.[^7] On 13 June 1914 he saved a man from probable drowning, and gave him dry clothing and shelter.[^8]

On 10 October 1920 Richardson helped two men who were drifting out to sea in a rowboat.[^10] The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1921 describes a rowboat rescue of two men, which may be the same event. It also says Richardson towed a disabled sloop with one man aboard to anchorage, made repairs, and piloted her to York Harbor. The report gives no date for that tow beyond the fiscal year.[^9]

On 10 July 1928 Moore helped tow a speed boat and its occupant to the light station.[^12]

On 26 December 2010 a blizzard caused the power lines to the island to arc during the Christmas lighting. The beacon switched to battery power, but it went out two days later, LighthouseFriends reports.[^3] In the winter of 2013 a storm destroyed the overhead tram used to bring supplies to the island, and damaged the shed that housed its mechanism.[^3]

## After the keepers

The station was electrified in 1938, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] The light was automated in 1987, and the archive gives the same year.[^2][^3] When the light was automated, a local historian said, the town had not been so upset about anything since the Second World War broke out.[^3] The townsfolk organized, and in 1998 the town adopted the station under the Maine Lights Program. The town was deluged with applications from people who wanted to be live-in caretakers, but water and sewer problems have kept anyone from moving in.[^3]

The Coast Guard kept the station staffed until the late 1980s. In 1986 and 1987 a keeper's family lived on the island. Everything they needed had to be loaded into a basket that ran on a line from the mainland, LighthouseFriends reports.[^3]

The nomination was prepared in January 1985 by the Maine Historic Preservation Commission. At that time the Coast Guard owned the station, and the nomination says it was one of the few on the Maine coast still manned.[^1]

In 2017 the lighthouse was repainted and its overhead zipline, now used to deliver supplies, was replaced. A 2021 project of $42,000 replaced the back deck and the tower windows, LighthouseFriends reports.[^3] A solar light was installed in April 2013 to give a more reliable power source.[^3] From late November each year the buildings on the island are decorated with Christmas lights, a big draw for visitors.[^3]

## Today

The Town of York owns the station.[^2][^3] The sources differ on access. The archive lists the station as open to the public. LighthouseFriends lists the grounds as open and the dwelling and tower as closed.[^2][^3] Visitors look at the light from Sohier Park on the mainland, reached by Nubble Road from York Beach.[^3] Scenic cruises from Ogunquit pass the light, LighthouseFriends says.[^3]
