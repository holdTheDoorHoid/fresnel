Cap Gaspé Lighthouse stands on a cliff at the tip of the Forillon peninsula, on the Gaspé coast of Quebec. Parks Canada describes the cliff as 95 metres high.[^1] The white octagonal concrete tower there is the third light on the site, built in 1949 and 1950.[^1][^2][^6][^9]

## The place

Cap Gaspé is a limestone headland at the tip of a long, narrow peninsula. Flowerpot Rock lies just off its southeastern shore, and vessels must round it to enter Gaspé Bay.[^3] Parks Canada says the light's clifftop site gives it a clear view over both the bay and the gulf.[^1]

Ships in the approaches had cause for care. The abbé Jean-Baptiste-Antoine Ferland, passing through Gaspésie in 1836, found the waters off the cape full of dangers. The Érudit article quotes him: "C'est ici un pays de tempêtes et de naufrages" ("This is a land of storms and shipwrecks", an English translation).[^4] Fog was a greater danger still. In general terms, the Érudit article explains that fog scatters and absorbs a lighthouse's beam, so that lights were of little use in it. Stations of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries tried sound signals to take over from the light in bad weather.[^4]

## Building the light

The first light, built in 1873, was a square wooden tower 9.1 m (30 ft) tall with a keeper's dwelling attached. It showed a fixed red light from four lamps, at a focal plane of 107 m (350 ft), and it was first exhibited on 6 October 1873.[^3][^4] Charles Esnouf, the first keeper (LighthouseFriends spells the name Esmouf in its text), was paid $800 a year. He had to hire an engineer to run the steam fog signal, which stood in a valley about 250 m (270 yards) from the light.[^3] The fog alarm began work on 22 May 1874 and sounded a ten-second blast each minute when needed.[^3] By 1883 the fog whistle had been judged ineffective and moved to Cap des Rosiers.[^3]

On 7 November 1890 fire destroyed the lighthouse and the keeper's dwelling.[^3] The Érudit article's caption puts it briefly: the first light "est détruit lors d'un incendie en 1890" (an English translation: was destroyed in a fire in 1890).[^4]

Tenders were called for a new light the following spring. Adolphus Rousseau of Ontario was the only bidder, at $2,980, but he refused to complete the contract. The second call drew a low bid of $3,480 from E. T. Nesbitt of Quebec.[^3] The second light, on the same site and built as a wooden combined lighthouse and dwelling, was first exhibited on 1 November 1892. Its tower was 14 m (46 ft) tall, and the whole structure cost $5,421.15.[^3] Parks Canada says it was "très semblable au premier" ("very like the first", an English translation).[^1] In 1894 guy-wires were put up to steady the tower, which the wind often buffeted.[^3]

From 1894 until a diaphone was installed in 1950, cotton-powder cartridges were exploded from the roof of the fog-signal building when fog came in.[^3] From 1 July 1901 the cartridges at several Gulf of St. Lawrence stations, Cape Gaspé among them, were fired "every 15 minutes, instead of every 20 minutes as theretofore."[^5]

The present tower is concrete.[^6] The Canadian Register of Historic Places says it was built as part of an upgrade to the coastal navigation system and replaced an earlier light in poor repair.[^6]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends lists the keepers as Charles Esnouf (1873 to at least 1878), C. D. Esnouf (at least 1889 to 1893), F. X. Smith (1894 to 1895), Francois Le Huguet (1896 to 1911), J. Smith (1912 to 1933), E. Reffous (1933), Alf. R. Minchinton (1934 to at least 1952) and Eylmer Langlois (1959 to 1972).[^3] The Department's annual report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1908 has a line reading "Cape Gaspe Frs. Le Huquet". The station and the keeper share a row in the printed table, so the line is probably the same keeper, although the spelling differs.[^7]

In 1961 two dwellings were built, one for the keeper and one for an assistant. The light was automated in 1972, and the dwellings were removed once the keepers had left.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The second tower collapsed in 1946. Parks Canada and the Quebec heritage record both give that year.[^1][^2] Parks Canada also says that the building, exposed to high winds and weather, deteriorated quickly.[^1]

The annual report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1919 prints the Quebec Salvage and Wrecking Company's record of its assistance to vessels. It notes that in October 1918 a wooden drifter, No. 57, was ashore off Cape Gaspé, and that an attempt to float her failed because of the falling tide and the ship's difficult position.[^8] The record places the drifter off the cape, not at the lighthouse, so it is recorded here only as a wreck near the station.

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1972.[^2] A diaphone fog horn had replaced the cartridges in 1950, and an electronic foghorn replaced the diaphone in 1970. LighthouseFriends says the electronic signal is no longer active.[^3] The Quebec heritage record says the light became a federal heritage building in 1991 and still does the job it was built for.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard's list gives the present tower as white and octagonal, 12.8 metres high, with a white flash every five seconds (one second of light and four of eclipse), a focal height of 106.9 metres and a nominal range of 14 nautical miles. The light operates year round.[^9]

The lighthouse stands in Forillon National Park.[^3] Parks Canada says it is 4 km from Anse-aux-Amérindiens and is reached on foot or by bicycle along the Les Graves trail.[^1] LighthouseFriends notes that an entrance fee applies to the park, and that the grounds are open while the tower is closed.[^3]
