Cape Decision Light stands on the southern tip of Kuiu Island in Southeast Alaska, a square concrete building with a tower rising from its roof, finished in 1932 as the last staffed lighthouse the United States built in Alaska.[^1] Its keepers lived just north of the junction of Sumner and Chatham Straits, and the light there was the first in an Alaskan lighthouse to be powered by electricity.[^1]

## The place

The station sits on the southwestern extremity of Kuiu Island, just north of where Chatham and Sumner Straits meet, about 63 miles (101 km) south of Sitka. The National Register nomination says that tidal currents there are strong, fog is frequent and dangerous rocks are numerous.[^1] The Lighthouse Service's own description of the site, printed in its *Bulletin* in 1932, makes the same case: fog is common at the cape "even when it is clear on each side", and the currents and off-lying rocks made it a hazard.[^3]

Ships came this way for a reason. Vessels that, because of their size or an unfavourable stage of the tide, could not use the shorter route through Wrangell Narrows went by Sumner Strait, Chatham Strait and Frederick Sound instead, which took them past Cape Decision; steamers and deep-sea fishing vessels also passed it on the way to and from the sea.[^3] The nomination adds that salmon canneries, herring salteries and reduction plants along Chatham and Sumner Straits multiplied during the 1920s.[^1]

Before the lighthouse, there was only an acetylene light on one of the Spanish Islands, about ten miles to the south.[^1] The nomination says shipping companies and fishermen appealed to the Lighthouse Service to improve on it.[^1] LighthouseFriends records that the wooden steamer *Ramona*, bound from Skagway to Seattle, ran aground in thick fog on the Spanish Islands on 10 September 1911, and that a newspaper blamed the lack of a lighthouse on Cape Decision; that is the website's account of the newspaper, not a clipping read for this story.[^4]

On 13 February 1921 an Executive Order signed by President Woodrow Wilson reserved about 216 acres (87 ha) of southern Kuiu Island for lighthouse purposes.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $59,400 in July 1929, and work began that September.[^1][^3] The sources do not agree on the money. The nomination and the Cape Decision Lighthouse Society both give a total cost of $158,000; the Lighthouse Service's 1932 *Bulletin* says the work was paid for from several allotments amounting to $160,000, and that the cost to date was $152,000.[^1][^5][^3] LighthouseFriends adds that the station had been approved in 1927 at an expected cost of $175,000.[^4]

The first season went to groundwork. According to LighthouseFriends, between September 1929 and the following July the crew levelled the site, put up a camp building, built a dock and tramway, set up a derrick and hoisting machinery, and finished a boathouse; by July 1931 the concrete work was done and mostly interior work remained.[^4] Poor weather and short funds delayed the finish.[^1] The station was placed in commission on 15 March 1932.[^1][^3]

The main building is reinforced concrete, one storey over a basement, 46 feet (14 m) square, with a tower rising from its centre.[^3] The Bulletin describes a three-storey tower 12 feet 6 inches square; the nomination measures the tower at 14 by 14 feet and 40 feet (12 m) above the roof.[^3][^1] The lantern held a third-order lens with a 300-watt electric lamp, showing two white flashes every 15 seconds at 24,000 candlepower, visible 15.6 nautical miles in clear weather; the Bulletin puts the light 96 feet (29 m) above the water.[^1][^3] The nomination calls it the first electric-powered light in an Alaskan lighthouse.[^1]

Inside were quarters for three keepers: the Bulletin states that "no family quarters are provided".[^3] The light shared the building with the fog signal and a radio beacon.[^1] The 1932 Bulletin describes a single No. 425 tyfon (a compressed-air horn), while the nomination says the original fog signal was two of them on the southwest corner of the roof, replaced by one Supertyphon in 1962.[^3][^1] The horn and the radio beacon were synchronised, so that a navigator timing the gap between the two sounds and dividing by five could estimate his distance from the station in miles.[^1]

The nomination lists a dock with a boom derrick, a tramway, a boathouse, a hoist house and a blacksmith shop, all put up during the 1930s.[^1] It also records two trails that keepers made over the years; one of about four and a half miles led to Port McArthur and, while the station was staffed, was occasionally used to carry supplies.[^1]

## The keepers

The USLHS Digital Archive, working from the registers of keepers, lists Edward M. Toman as the station's first head keeper, 1932 to 1937, and again from 1938 to 1939.[^2] William J. Donelly served as first assistant from 1932 to 1935 and again from 1937 to 1939.[^2] The post of second assistant turned over fast in the early years: the archive lists five men in it between 1932 and 1935, two more in 1935, and then Francis E. Craig from 1935 to 1942, the longest single stint on its roster.[^2] After Waddell's year in 1941-42 the archive shows no head keeper until Clair W. Dibble in 1945-46, and none between Dibble and Francis Woodward in 1957-58; whether that is a gap in the registers or in the staffing is not something these sources settle.[^2]

LighthouseFriends writes of H. O. Essig, who was stationed at Cape Decision from 1943 to 1946. It says a year's duty earned three months' leave, that most men would not sign up for more than one year, and that Essig found the place one that "requires a certain breed of cat to handle it".[^4]

From 1959 the archive's list changes to a Coast Guard crew: 27 men, each listed for a span of no more than two calendar years, up to 1966.[^2] The nomination says the Coast Guard automated the station in 1974 and stopped assigning keepers.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Ernest V. Evanson joined the Lighthouse Service in the summer of 1935 and was assigned to Cape Decision, according to LighthouseFriends, and the archive lists him as second assistant that year.[^4][^2] On 22 December 1935 he set out in the station's boat for supplies. The Juneau *Daily Alaska Empire* reported from Ketchikan on 23 December that the Coast Guard cutter *Cyane* had begun a search off Port McArthur for him, last seen adrift the day before in a small motorboat with its motor dead, after leaving the lighthouse for Port McArthur for supplies "with a northwest wind blowing".[^8] The paper's headline spoke of a feared swamping "by northeast blow", so the wind direction in that single page is not consistent.[^8] The next day the paper said the lighthouse tender *Hemlock* and the cutters *Cyane* and *Alert*, with 120 men aboard, were still searching in heavy seas and icy gales off Cape Decision, and that the crews believed there was slight hope his boat had ridden out the seas.[^9] None of the sources cited here says what became of Evanson; the archive's last mention of him is that year.[^2]

In August 1941, LighthouseFriends reports, the station's water supply failed after the region received 0.76 of an inch of rain in the month against an August average of 11.57; Coast Guard cutters were sent to bring water to Cape Decision and three other Alaskan lights.[^4] The nomination says the station's water was collected from the roof into two 10,000-gallon cisterns, and originally piped from a nearby stream.[^1]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends dates the departure of the last live-in crew to 1 September 1974.[^4] The nomination says the original lens was replaced by a 1,000-watt bulb on solar power, and in 1996 by a Vega VRB-25 aerobeacon.[^1] The Fresnel lens is now at the Clausen Museum in Petersburg.[^1]

On 11 October 1989 an Aids to Navigation team from Sitka was at the station. LighthouseFriends tells how the crew burned rubbish in barrels, which it thinks most likely set the pier alight; the three men fought it with extinguishers and a bucket line, a helicopter from Sitka and the buoy tender *Woodrush* arrived, fuel in the boathouse exploded, and the fire was out in about four hours. The official cause was listed as unknown.[^4] The Society says a burn-barrel fire destroyed the boathouse and part of the pier.[^6] The nomination lists more: the wood buildings, four fuel tanks and part of the dock, and says only the dock was rebuilt.[^1]

In 1997 the nonprofit Cape Decision Lighthouse Society leased the site from the Coast Guard, and in 2004 it received title to the buildings and structures.[^1] The station was listed in the National Register of Historic Places in 2005.[^4]

## Today

The Society owns the buildings and structures and says it looks after about 215 acres of Kuiu Island; the Coast Guard still maintains the light, which flashes white every five seconds and is visible up to 18 nautical miles.[^1][^7] The Society calls rebuilding the burned boathouse a high priority.[^6] The USLHS archive lists the station as open to the public, but LighthouseFriends notes that its remoteness keeps many visitors from reaching it, and it is private property of a nonprofit; anyone planning a visit should contact the Society first.[^2][^4]
