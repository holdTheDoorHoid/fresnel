The Ipswich Range Lights were two towers at the mouth of the Ipswich River in Massachusetts, set in line so that a ship keeping them aligned would cross the bar in the deepest water.[^8][^9] The front light was discontinued in 1932. In 1939 the cast-iron rear tower was floated by barge to Martha's Vineyard, and a steel tower on the same ground still shows a white light.[^8][^10]

## The place

The two lights served as a range for vessels coming in through the main channel to the river mouth.[^8] The channel was the trouble. The Life-Saving Service's report for 1895 records a vessel "Stranded on the bar outside Ipswich Harbor, 4 miles from station."[^11] A local history connects the problem with the years when Joseph Dennis was keeper, from 1841: the channel had moved so far that a ship following the lights would run ashore on Plum Island.[^10]

## Building the light

Congress voted the money in 1837. Lighthouse Friends says it appropriated $7,000 that year for two small lighthouses, to be built if needed.[^9] The government then bought four acres on Castle Neck, east of the river mouth, from John Baker and Tristram Brown for ten dollars.[^8][^9]

The towers were of brick.[^8] Historic Ipswich gives their height as 29 feet (8.8 m).[^10] Lighthouse Friends gives thirty feet (9.1 m).[^9] The two sources differ by a foot.

The station began operation on 1 December 1837, according to the New England Lighthouses history.[^8] Lighthouse Friends adds that a Notice to Mariners of 13 November 1837 had announced that the lights would be shown from 1 December.[^9] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's place records give 1809 as the year the station was established, and they date the two towers to 1838.[^1][^2] The histories put the money and the first light in 1837, and this account follows them.

The lights showed a fixed white, and each tower first carried ten oil lamps and reflectors.[^9]

The front light was moved in the 1860s.[^8] The New England Lighthouses history says that before 1867 the front light had been replaced by a shanty-like affair known as the "bug light," and that in 1867 it was moved 550 feet (168 m) as the channel shifted.[^8] Historic Ipswich gives the same 550-foot (168 m) move, in 1867, for the same reason.[^10]

Congress paid for rebuilding in 1879. Lighthouse Friends says Congress appropriated $10,000 on 3 March 1879 to rebuild the station and buy land for the front range light.[^9] The brick rear tower was badly cracked by 1878.[^8]

The sources differ on when that brick tower gave way to a 45-foot (13.7 m), conical cast-iron tower. New England Lighthouses and Lighthouse Friends give 1881.[^8][^9] The Lighthouse Society's disposition note gives 1880.[^2] Ellsworth's own journal, as Historic Ipswich prints it, says "In 1880 a new lighthouse was built of iron, the old one taken down."[^10]

The front light was out of service for six months.[^3] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 says the front range light "was discontinued August" 1898, and that it was established on a new line on 2 February 1899, with a new front beacon built.[^3] The next year's report says the front beacon "was moved to a new site to adapt the range to a change in the channel."[^4]

## The keepers

Thomas S. Greenwood, a former sea captain from Boston, became the first keeper in October 1837, at a salary of $400 a year.[^9] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster gives him three stints: 1837 to 1841, 1847 to 1849, and 1853 to 1861.[^2] The New England Lighthouses history says his tenure was interrupted three times by removal for apparent political reasons, which was common at that time.[^8]

Benjamin Ellsworth kept the lights for the longest stretch.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says President Abraham Lincoln appointed him in August 1861.[^9] He was a native of nearby Rowley and had worked as a fisherman and harbor pilot before coming to Ipswich.[^9] The roster has him from 1861 to 1902.[^2] Historic Ipswich's list of keepers gives Greenwood alone from 1838 to 1852 and Ellsworth from 1852. Its own text, though, says Lincoln appointed Ellsworth in 1861.[^10]

His daughter Susan was part of the work.[^10] Historic Ipswich says the keeper's daughter kept house at the station, and Lighthouse Friends describes her as his constant assistant and housekeeper.[^10][^9]

Ellsworth died in February 1902.[^9] The Springfield Weekly Republican reported on 28 February that he was "probably the oldest lighthouse keeper in the world" and that "For 41 years he alone tended the lighthouse at Ipswich beach."[^5]

The last official keeper was Carl Delano Hill, a Lighthouse Service veteran who spent about a decade at the station.[^8] The roster ends his stint in 1932.[^2] After that the roster lists a "Watchman / Caretaker" heading, with Frode Nordquist (1932 to 1934) and Leroy F. H. Lane (1934 to 1942).[^2] The New England Lighthouses history says the caretakers were paid by the nearby Crane Estate and did work on the estate during the day.[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

One wreck is described in several of the sources: the schooner Deposit.[^6][^7] The Rhode-Island Republican printed the news on 1 January 1840.[^7] The schooner Deposit, from Belfast, Maine, under Captain Cotterell, went ashore on "Lakeman's beach, in Ipswich Bay" on a Sunday night, and the paper says "four of the seven" aboard died of cold and exposure.[^7][^9][^10]

A Boston paper printed an account from the Ipswich Register on 25 December 1839, and a St. Johnsbury, Vermont, paper reprinted it on 31 December.[^6] It says that Marshall of Ipswich found the wreck on the Monday morning, and that he and Keeper Greenwood went down to it.[^6] Greenwood went into the surf and reached the vessel.[^6] The captain was lost when a wave upset the boat he had been lowered into with Marshall.[^6] The captain's wife was lowered from the stern on ropes, and Greenwood and Marshall, standing one on each side of her in the water, took advantage of an incoming wave to carry her ashore.[^6] Three people survived, including Mrs. Cotterell.[^6]

Later accounts tell the rescue differently: New England Lighthouses and Historic Ipswich say the two men persuaded the captain's wife to jump from the rigging into their arms.[^8][^10]

The dates do not agree. The Newport paper gives a Sunday night, the 15th of December.[^7] The Boston reprint of 25 December quotes the Register of a Friday, which must be 20 December or earlier, so the Monday it describes was 16 December or earlier.[^6] The New England Lighthouses history, Lighthouse Friends and Historic Ipswich give 23 December, which was a Monday as well, but a Monday after the Register had already printed the story.[^7][^6][^8][^9][^10]

Ellsworth's own rescues are recorded in less certain sources. Historic Ipswich quotes George H. Proctor's account of the fishing boat Garibaldi, "caught in the gale of Tuesday, March 11th, 1872, off Ipswich."[^10] In Proctor's telling, the two men clinging to the mast saw "the keeper of the Ipswich light go and return from his duties, yet no help came," and the rescued men were taken "to the house of Mr. Ellsworth." Historic Ipswich dates Ellsworth's medal to 1873.[^10] Lighthouse Friends puts a rescue of two fishermen in March 1874, when they had "clung to the frozen rigging" for nearly eighteen hours.[^9] March 11, 1872 was a Monday, not a Tuesday, so the Proctor date is not reliable as printed.[^10]

In October 1863 Ellsworth reached the passengers of an English schooner that had run aground.[^8] He later said he could "scarcely help from laughing" when he reached the wreck, because the passengers thought he had come to rob them.[^8]

A family recollection published by Historic Ipswich places the three-masted schooner Thomas H. Lawrence on the beach at Crane Beach in a bad storm in September 1939.[^10] New England Lighthouses dates the same wreck to September 1839, but says it came "close to the centennial of the wreck of the Deposit," which points to 1939.[^8]

## After the keepers

The front light was discontinued in 1932.[^8] The New England Lighthouses history gives November 1932 for the discontinuance and says the rear light was automated at the same time.[^8] Historic Ipswich adds that by 1938 sand had filled around the base of the tower.[^10]

Historic Ipswich says two keepers served the light after 12 November 1932, until the Coast Guard took over, and that the Crane family paid them.[^10]

A hurricane in 1938 had damaged a lighthouse at Edgartown.[^10] In 1939 the Coast Guard floated the Ipswich cast-iron tower by barge to Edgartown to replace it.[^10][^8] Susan Ellsworth, who had acted as keeper after her father's death, led an organized opposition to the move, which failed.[^10]

Lighthouse Friends reports that after town groups used the keeper's dwelling for several years, it was lost to a fire it calls suspicious, in 1973.[^9] Historic Ipswich says vandals burned the house in October 1973.[^10]

## Today

A steel skeleton tower stands at the site of the rear range light.[^9][^10] Historic Ipswich describes it as showing a white light every four seconds, and says it was erected at Crane Beach.[^10] The same page says Crane Beach belongs to the Trustees of Reservations.[^10]

New England Lighthouses says that apart from the modern tower, "there is no surviving reminder of the Ipswich Light Station on its former site."[^8] The sources do not say whether the tower itself can be visited, and none describes public access to it.
