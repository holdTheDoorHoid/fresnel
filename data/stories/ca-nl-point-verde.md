Point Verde Light stands on the point at the southern entrance to Placentia Roads, on the east side of Placentia Bay, about 3 km west of Placentia.[^1][^3] It has marked that entrance since the late 1870s, though the sources differ on the year it was first lit.[^1][^3][^4] The present light is a steel skeletal tower put up in 1990, and the grounds and tower are closed to visitors.[^1][^3]

## The place

The name means green point, and the headland is grassy.[^1] A causeway carried the road out to the point, cutting across what had been one pond; the two pools now lie near the headland.[^1]

The Encyclopedia of Newfoundland and Labrador says the point once held fortifications guarding the southern entrance to Placentia Harbour.[^4]

John and Robert Greene acquired the peninsula from Placentia merchants in 1803. By the first census, in 1836, the community had seventy-two people.[^1]

In 1877 Mr. Dwyer presented a petition to the House of Assembly from residents of Placentia and nearby communities, asking for a lighthouse at Point Verde.[^1] Lighthouse Friends records that the lighthouse was costed at about $4,000, and that it "would be very useful for navigation in Placentia Bay and Harbour."[^1]

## Building the light

A design was prepared and carried out in 1878. The tower was ready to receive its lantern room by the end of that year, but the lighting material had not arrived from Scotland in time.[^1]

The Encyclopedia says a lighthouse was established "two years later" than its 1874 population figures, which would put it in 1876.[^4] Rowlett gives 1879 as the year the station was established.[^3]

The first station was a flat-roofed dwelling of two storeys, rectangular in plan, on a cement base, with a square tower rising from its roof. A sixth-order lens gave a fixed white light, with a focal plane of 30 m.[^1] Before construction started, a brief road was cut down to the closest beach, and supplies came ashore there.[^1]

A September 1880 inspection found the concrete foundation walls losing their facing, with rot showing within. Repairs to the worst sections were done before winter; the rest followed a year later.[^1]

In 1912 a flat-roofed fog-alarm building went up 9.1 m north of the light. Its horn sounded for four seconds every ninety-four seconds when needed.[^1]

In 1920 a new light went up on a square, open-framed pyramidal tower raised on an enclosed base. Its acetylene lamp came from Chance Brothers of Birmingham.[^1] It flashed fourteen times a minute, one second on and 3.28 seconds off. The old enclosed wooden tower came down, though the dwelling stayed on for the keeper.[^1]

At some time during Joe Greene's service, a wooden tower went up: square and pyramidal, topped by an octagonal lantern room and painted in red and white horizontal stripes. Lighthouse Friends does not date it more closely.[^1]

In 1975 a fibreglass tower was erected. Lighthouse Friends gives its height as 3.7 m. Rowlett gives 6 m, and says it had to be removed because of shoreline erosion.[^1][^3]

In 1990 a square steel skeletal tower with an enclosed lantern was put in place.[^1][^3]

## The keepers

The first keepers named are the Crouchers. Lighthouse Friends gives James E. Croucher from 1878 to 1897, and Thomas Croucher from 1898 to at least 1912.[^1] Its keeper list starts James a year before Rowlett's 1879 date for the station.[^1][^3] The Encyclopedia says the light was "manned by the Crouchers and other local people."[^4]

Around 1922 the light passed to the Greene family. Joe Greene, a descendant of the Greenes who settled Point Verde, found fishing hard going and wanted to move his family to Boston. Lighthouse Friends says his wife Louise reportedly asked a relative in a high government position to press for the post, and that Joe began looking after the light around 1922.[^1]

Joe died in 1945, and the post went to their son Leonard.[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives Leonard as keeper from 1945 into the 1960s, and lists Gus Greene in the 1930s with no other detail.[^1]

The keeper list then runs to Thomas Green and Leo McGrath, both to 1977, and George Thomas from 1977 to 1991.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Lighthouse Friends describes the exposed site as one buffeted by many winter storms.[^1] By 1886 iron fastenings had been attached to the tower to limit its movement in gales. They were tightened in 1887.[^1]

In December 1889 a gale shook the lighthouse hard and blew the privy away.[^1]

Erosion was a long-running problem. In 1888 Inspector John T. Nevill reported the sea gaining on the bank near the light, and proposed a grass to hold the bank's face.[^1]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the light was staffed until the early 1970s, and the Encyclopedia says it was automated in the early 1970s.[^1][^4] Lighthouse Friends' own keeper list runs to 1991, so the sources do not agree on when the keepers left.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard list, dated 24 September 2026, gives the light as a white flash every four seconds, with a focal height of 29.9 m and a nominal range of 17 nautical miles. The tower is a square skeleton tower with a red and white square daymark, 11.3 m high.[^2]

Rowlett gives a white flash every five seconds and a focal plane of 30 m.[^3] The sources disagree on the rhythm. The Coast Guard list is the most recent official entry, so the figures above are its.[^2]

The Fisheries and Oceans brochure, with a page date of 2019-09-24, lists Pointe Verde among the unstaffed lightstations.[^5] The Canadian Coast Guard owns the station.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the only things left on the point are the present tower, a fog signal and a shed, all inside a chain-link fence.[^1] Lighthouse Friends and Rowlett both say the site and tower are closed to visitors.[^1][^3] Do not go onto the closed grounds, and do not climb the tower.
