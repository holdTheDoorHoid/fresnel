Santa Barbara Light was a brick tower rising from the roof of a keeper's house on the California coast, a few miles west of the city of Santa Barbara.[^3][^4] It was first lit in 1856.[^2][^8] An earthquake destroyed the tower and house on 29 June 1925.[^3][^5] The light that stands in the Coast Guard compound today is a 24-foot (7.3 m) enclosed steel tower, and the compound is closed to visitors.[^2]

## The place

Fog was frequent along this stretch of coast. The Service's 1925 proposal, as LighthouseFriends prints it, says northbound vessels "keep close inshore to avoid the prevailing northwesterly wind and sea."[^2] The same 1925 proposal stresses the traffic: "There is heavy traffic through the Santa Barbara Channel at all times."[^2]

The town gave the land. Santa Barbara's city authorities made it available free of charge, on condition that the grant not exceed thirty acres. LighthouseFriends says lighthouse authorities considered the choice of site "a matter of some delicacy," since they wanted one light to serve as both a seacoast light and a harbor light.[^2]

The Board's reports place the station "on the point 2 miles southeast of Santa Barbara Landing."[^7] The Service's 1925 bulletin puts it "a few miles westerly of the city."[^3] The two descriptions point in different directions.

## Building the light

Early in 1856 George Nagle moved his family from San Francisco to Santa Barbara to erect the light on a mesa about two miles west of the harbor, for which he was paid $8,000.[^2] The tower stood near the middle of a gabled roof on a house of a story and a half, a Cape Cod design that LighthouseFriends says most early West Coast lights shared.[^2]

On 19 December 1856 a fixed red light was first shown from a fourth-order Fresnel lens in the lantern.[^2] The Service's 1925 bulletin describes the tower as it stood before the earthquake: a story-and-a-half brick dwelling, with a brick tower "about 40 feet high from the ground" (12.2 m) rising from its roof. The bulletin says the tower was "practically the same as when built."[^4] The Archive's record gives the height as 34 feet from the base to the lantern's centre.[^1]

In 1880 the lamp changed from a lard-oil lamp to a mineral-oil lamp.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says the water supply was often insufficient.[^2] The Board had a well dug about 50 feet (15 m) deep, which reached "an ample supply," and it was walled with brick.[^6] In 1894 a "galvanized iron windmill was erected over the well," and a wooden cistern was set up beside it, with pipes to the house and grounds.[^7]

By 1901 the Board's language was blunt. The light was shown "from an old-fashioned lantern with triangular-shaped glass, built on top of the dwelling." The structure was "unsightly and uncomfortable," and in winter "the walls are damp." The Board estimated that a new structure could be had "for not exceeding $7,500."[^8]

## The keepers

Albert J. Williams was the first keeper, and the USLHS roster places him there from 1856 to 1860.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he was removed after four years and tried farming nearby. After "a string of three short-term keepers," the post was offered to him again in 1865. He declined, and his wife Julia took it.[^2] The three short-term keepers were Rafiel Guirado, Charles Talma and Samuel Robert I. Sturgeon, whom the roster places between 1860 and 1865.[^1]

The Los Angeles Times told its readers in 1893 that Julia Williams was a native of Campobello, New Brunswick, and gave her birth date as 12 July 1826.[^13] Forty years earlier she had set sail from New York with her small child to join her husband, who had come west in the gold rush.[^13] The roster lists her as principal keeper from 1865 to 1905.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that, since the station had no fog signal, she could tend the light alone, though she was also raising three boys and two girls there. When her husband Albert died in 1882, she stayed on.[^2]

A Los Angeles Herald correspondent, writing from San Pedro in August 1897, said that in all those years she "has never allowed any hand but her own to light the lamp."[^11] Once, he wrote, she was persuaded to stay away overnight, but she "suffered such torture of mind for fear some accident would happen the lamp and a life be lost at sea" that she never again could be induced to leave her charge.[^11] The writer gave her thirty-five years at the light. Counted from the 1865 appointment, the figure is nearer thirty-two.[^11][^1]

In 1905 she fell and broke her hip.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the fall ended her keeping at the age of eighty-one.[^2] That does not fit the birth date printed in 1893: someone born on 12 July 1826 was 78 or 79 in 1905.[^13] The roster lists Caroline Morse as principal keeper from 1905 to 1911, then George A. Hussey from 1911 to 1913 and Harley A. Weeks from 1913 to 1925.[^1]

Harley A. Weeks died in March 1925, and his son Raymond became acting keeper.[^2] The roster lists Raymond H. Weeks as principal keeper from 1925 to 1948.[^1] LighthouseFriends ends its head-keeper list in 1943, and describes his work as a laborer's, custodian's and lamplighter's job, held until the Coast Guard abolished the position in March 1948.[^2] He had one month to leave his quarters, which belonged to the Coast Guard.[^2] When the light was temporarily discontinued from 1 June to 11 July 1942, during the Second World War, his annual pay was cut from $480 to $240.[^2]

The roster also lists Harry A. Weeks as a laborer from 1912 to 1914, and Carrie R. Weeks as a laborer from 1914 to at least 1921 and as principal keeper in 1925.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

At about 6:45 on the morning of Monday, 29 June 1925, "a succession of sharp earthquake shocks was felt at Santa Barbara."[^3] The bulletin says the station "apparently was in the center of the most severe shocks."[^3] The tower, dwelling and oil house were wrecked.[^3] "The only portion of the quarters escaping destruction was the kitchen annex."[^3] A later Service summary says the station "was completely wrecked by earthquake."[^5] The Archive's place record gives the year of destruction as 1926.[^1]

The acting keeper, the son of the late keeper, had been sleeping in a small outbuilding.[^3] He found the house wrecked, with his mother, sister, brother and visiting relatives inside, and "he rescued them from their danger." They all escaped "other than serious fright and slight bruises."[^3] The bulletin does not give his name. LighthouseFriends and the roster give it as Raymond H. Weeks.[^2][^1]

A wire story printed in the Washington Daily News the next day said the lighthouse, "tended by Mrs. J. Weeks, widow of the former keeper, was one of the buildings wrecked."[^12] The Service's own account does not support that version.[^3]

LighthouseFriends quotes the station's logbook entry for the day: "Earthquake at 6:45 a.m. Tower and building down." It argues that the entry was written later, because the acting keeper "didn't find the logbook until July 6."[^2] "The tower and lantern came crashing down," LighthouseFriends says, and "The Fresnel lens was shattered."[^2]

By 3 July a temporary light was in commission on a frame tower 35 feet (11 m) high.[^3]

## After the keepers

The Board's recommendation for a new building first appeared in its report for 1894, LighthouseFriends says, and was repeated until 1904.[^2]

In 1912 the station received a high-power flashing lens that had earlier served at Alcatraz Island. The characteristic changed from fixed white to one white flash every fifteen seconds.[^2]

By 1921 the Commissioner of Lighthouses was making the same case in fuller terms. The combined dwelling and tower, built in 1856, was "entirely too small to accommodate the modern revolving lens now installed in it." The tower stood one-eighth of a mile (200 m) back from the shore, and trees on adjoining land obscured the light. The Commissioner asked for a new tower farther out near the shore line, and "a first-class compressed-air fog signal installed."[^9]

The 1925 earthquake changed the plan. LighthouseFriends prints the Service's 1925 proposal: a reinforced concrete tower of about 70 feet (21 m), set nearer the shore, a reinforced concrete fog-signal building, and "two substantial frame cottages" in place of the old dwelling.[^2] LighthouseFriends says these plans "were not fully realized": no fog signal was ever built, and the light stood on an enclosed steel tower 24 feet (7.3 m) tall.[^2]

The sources do not agree on when the steel tower went up. LighthouseFriends says "a more permanent tower was erected in January 1928," but it also gives 1935 to 1977 as the years the beacon was used atop the tower.[^2] A 1937 Lighthouse Service bulletin describes the revolving beacon at Santa Barbara as "mounted on an enclosed steel tower."[^10]

## Today

The light is inside "the active Coast Guard compound where the light is located," and the Coast Guard owns the lighthouse.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds and tower are closed.[^2] A distant view of the tower is possible from a lookout farther east along Meigs Road.[^2]

The 1856 tower and dwelling did not survive the earthquake. LighthouseFriends calls the structure "a total loss."[^2][^3] The revolving beacon that stood atop the steel tower from 1935 to 1977 is displayed at Point Vicente Lighthouse, LighthouseFriends reports.[^2]
