Bear Island Light stands on a small island about 1,200 feet (370 m) off Mount Desert Island, at the entrance to Northeast and Southwest harbors in Maine.[^2][^3] The light was first shown in 1839, when a wooden tower stood on the gable of a stone keeper's house.[^1][^3]

## The place

The National Register nomination says the station "was established in 1839 as a guide to Northeast Harbor on Mount Desert Island."[^1] Before that, in 1837, a Navy officer, Captain Joseph Smith, toured the coast of Maine looking for sites for lighthouses.[^3] LighthouseFriends quotes his report: "There is no place where there is a light so much required," on Bear Island, "at the entrance of Northeast and Southwest harbors," which he said were "much frequented by vessels."[^3]

The island was private property. LighthouseFriends says its owner, William Moore, asked the government $500 for the eleven acres, though he had bought the island for $101.17 only a few months earlier.[^3] A $3,000 appropriation was granted on July 7, 1838, and the light was built in 1839.[^3] Spurling Point, on Great Cranberry Island, had been looked at first and rejected.[^3]

## Building the light

The first station was a family station with a single keeper, according to LighthouseFriends. The tower was a wooden one set on the south gable of a stone dwelling. Its lantern held seven lamps with 13-inch (33 cm) reflectors and gave a fixed white light.[^3]

Fire damaged the station in 1852.[^3] The nomination says a brick tower was built off one end of the dwelling that year. LighthouseFriends says the station was rebuilt in 1853 with a cylindrical brick tower of 31 feet (9.5 m).[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends dates the fifth-order Fresnel lens to 1856. The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1858.[^3][^2]

The wharf was enlarged over several years. A crib wharf of 170 feet (52 m) went up in 1885, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] The Lighthouse Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1892, says arrangements were made for an addition to the northerly side of the wharf approach.[^5] The report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1893, says work extending the wharf on the north side, 50 by 100 feet (15 by 30 m), commenced on June 13, 1893, and was completed; it was "in excellent condition."[^6]

In 1889 the entire complex was "pulled down and rebuilt," according to the nomination.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a temporary skeleton tower showed the same light from September 1, 1889, until the new brick tower and gambrel-roofed frame dwelling were finished later that year "at a cost of $3,750."[^3]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends notes that *The Island of Mount Desert Register with the Cranberry Isles*, 1909-10, named William Moore, who had sold a parcel of the island to the government, as the first keeper. "Other sources list John G. Bowen as the first to tend the light," the page says.[^3] Bowen was dismissed in 1842. The Treasury Secretary's charges were "interference in elections" and "absence from the lighthouse for days in succession."[^3] LighthouseFriends says Bowen came back for three more stints.[^3]

When I.W.P. Lewis, a civil engineer, inspected the station in 1842, he wrote that the location "requires but one lamp, of a suitable form, instead of seven now used."[^3] The next keeper, Solomon L. Howes, added complaints of his own: the cisterns were in poor shape and the lantern's ventilation was bad.[^3] The station formed a school district of its own under Keeper Caleb L. Gould, whom LighthouseFriends calls Caleb S. Goold. The district lasted until 1871 and was re-established in 1880.[^3]

The roster runs from 1839 to 1981.[^2] It includes Alden H. Jordan, who served at Bear Island from 1883 to 1899, and who had earlier been keeper at Baker Island from 1867 to 1883. The archive lists the two stints under one person, which this story follows.[^2][^13] The Sawyers, Lewis F. and then Heber G., kept the light from 1899 to 1924. Heber G. Sawyer's rescues are described below.[^2]

LighthouseFriends quotes a keeper it names Steve Loiver, "one of the last Coast Guard keepers of Bear Island Lighthouse," who was concerned about the move to automate the light. He said he hoped that "in the need to economize we don't destroy the things that give flavor and uniqueness to life."[^3] The same page quotes Terry Stanley, a Coast Guard keeper on the island in 1959, on the fog bell's clockwork: it "worked like a Swiss clock."[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Board's fog-signal observations for the fiscal year 1894 record a bell "struck by a Stevens machine," heard with a southwest wind blowing two points in its favor.[^7] Before the fog bell was added, LighthouseFriends says, a Newfoundland dog began to bark when it heard the paddlewheels of an approaching steamer, according to an 1875 newspaper account that the page cites.[^3]

On March 2, 1900, a storm damaged about 30 by 40 feet (9 by 12 m) of the wharf, which was then raised and securely fastened, the Lighthouse Board reported.[^8]

Heber G. Sawyer, keeper from 1912 to 1924, is credited with several rescues.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1914 says that on April 20 Sawyer, "with his personal power launch picked up and towed" a disabled motor boat with a man aboard.[^9] The Commissioner's rescue tables for the fiscal years 1916 and 1918 name the keeper of Bear Island as "H. O. Sawyer." The roster has Heber G. Sawyer keeping the light in those years, so these entries are read as his.[^10][^11][^2] In the fiscal year 1921 the report describes "keeper of Bear Island Light Station, Maine," towing a disabled motor launch with one man aboard to port.[^12] LighthouseFriends credits Sawyer with rescues in 1914, 1916, 1918 and 1921.[^3]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over the station in 1939, though LighthouseFriends says it "remained a family light."[^3] The light was deactivated in 1981, and two lighted bell buoys were set offshore in its place.[^3][^2] The nomination, received by the Park Service in January 1983, says the station "no longer serves as an aid to navigation."[^1]

The National Park Service took ownership in 1987.[^3] In 1989 the Friends of Acadia relit the light as a private aid to navigation, showing a white flash every five seconds, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] After the station was listed on the National Register in 1988, the Park Service was allowed to lease the property.[^3] Sources differ on the present status. The park says the Coast Guard maintains the light as an aid to navigation.[^4] The US Lighthouse Society lists it as an active aid and as a private aid.[^2]

## Today

The light station is privately leased and closed to the public. The park manages only a small portion of the island, and visitors can see the station from the water, by boat or on a guided tour.[^4] LighthouseFriends notes that boat cruises pass the island most days in summer.[^3] The US Lighthouse Society's record also says the lighthouse is not open to the public.[^2]
