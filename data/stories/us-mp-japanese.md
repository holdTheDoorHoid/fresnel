The Japanese Lighthouse stands on Navy Hill above Garapan, on Saipan in the Northern Mariana Islands. The Japanese administration built the concrete tower in 1934, while Japan ruled Saipan under a League of Nations mandate, to guide ships into Tanapag Harbor.[^1][^2] It was damaged in the American invasion of 1944.[^1][^2] Its later history is one of neglect, a restaurant, and a new plan for a visitor center.[^2][^3]

## The place

Saipan's western shore has two harbors, Tanapag and Garapan. Both are rimmed by coral reefs that stretch south-southwest from the shore for some distance.[^2] The nomination says the light was built "to assist navigation into Tanapag Harbor through the coral reef and shoals south-west of Saipan."[^1]

Saipan had been Spanish and then German before the League of Nations gave it to Japan after the First World War.[^2] DeWire writes that Japan built twelve navigation lights on and around Saipan, at a cost of $312,533. This one, on a hill overlooking Garapan Harbor, was the best known of them.[^2]

## Building the light

The nomination dates the tower to 1934 and credits the Japanese administration.[^1] The nomination says the ash in the concrete came from burning coral and seashells, and that crushed coral served as sand and aggregate. It calls the concrete a relatively poor grade that "weathers easily."[^1]

DeWire describes a tower three stories tall, with a spiral stair around the beacon shaft, iron ladders to the lantern and a copper dome. She gives its height as about 43 feet (13 m), and says the tower stood against a keepers' dwelling.[^2] The nomination places the foundation of an auxiliary generator about fifty feet (15 m) north of the tower. DeWire says the generator was there to run the light in a power failure.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In June 1944 American forces landed on Saipan, and Navy ships and planes bombarded the island. Japanese forces put out the Garapan light so that it could not help the Americans. The tower was a major target, and Japanese soldiers used it as a lookout.[^2] The nomination says the bombardment stopped the light in 1944, and that "evidence of damage is evident on the lighthouse."[^1]

DeWire says the tower was still standing when the battle ended, but it was badly battered: its concrete walls were torn open, bullets had marked it all over, and the lamp was badly damaged.[^2] In 2015 Typhoon Soudelor took the fiberglass roof off the top of the tower, but the tower and its lower section stood. A photo caption in the same article says the typhoon tore off the cupola.[^2]

## After the keepers

After the battle, American troops mounted guns on the lighthouse and used it as an observation post.[^2] DeWire writes that Seabees of the 177th Battalion repaired the tower toward the end of the war, so that it could serve as an observation and communications post. A small light on a new cupola ran until at least 1947.[^2] She cites a 1946 U.S. Hydrographic Office report that describes the light as flashing white for one second and eclipsed for five.[^2] The Navy left Saipan on 18 July 1947, and the light was in use at least until then.[^2]

The 1974 nomination disagrees on the wartime history. It says the lighthouse "was never rebuilt nor utilized again after it was damaged during the war."[^1] The nomination's date for the light's end is 1944. DeWire's account has a small light in use until 1947.[^1][^2]

Neglect set in once the troops had left, and the empty tower became a favorite haunt of young people.[^2] In the early 1990s a business owner showed interest in the tower, and it was later refitted as a restaurant. The second level became an open-air terrace, and a fiberglass roof was placed on top.[^2] The restaurant closed about five years later, and the tower stood empty again.[^2]

David T. Lotz, a planner for the Marianas parks office, nominated the lighthouse to the National Register in 1974, and it was listed that December.[^1][^2] In 2006 citizens formed the Saipan Lighthouse Historical Society, and volunteers cleaned the site and painted the tower.[^2] By 2011 there were plans to restore the lighthouse as a visitor center for the new Mariana Trench Marine National Monument. DeWire writes in 2016 that funding had not materialized.[^2]

## Today

In July 2026 the Marianas Visitors Authority announced a concept to turn the lighthouse into a visitor and cultural experience center. The Department of Public Lands had recently awarded the property to the authority, for use as its headquarters. Site clearing began that month, and the authority expected to move in before December. Full development will depend on funding.[^3]

The sources give no opening hours for the site. Work on the property was under way in 2026.[^3] In February 2015 a visitor was warned that the site was not a safe place to go alone.[^2] Do not enter or climb the tower while the work continues.
