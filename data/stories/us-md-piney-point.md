Piney Point Light stands on the Maryland shore of the Potomac, fourteen miles up the river from Chesapeake Bay.[^3] A brick tower went up there in 1836 to replace a lightship that had marked the shoals since 1821.[^1][^3] The light was discontinued in 1963 or 1964, depending on the source.[^1][^3] The tower now stands in a county-owned park, with a museum you can visit.[^3][^10]

## The place

The shoals were the reason for the light. LighthouseFriends says a lightship had been stationed in the area since 1821 to mark the hazardous shoals at Piney Point and at Ragged Point on the far shore.[^3] In 1835 Congress set aside $5,000 for a land-based beacon to replace the lightship.[^3]

The point took its name from the pines along the river. LighthouseFriends says that for much of the nineteenth century many Washington notables summered there.[^3] The same source says the point gave Presidents James Monroe, Franklin Pierce and Theodore Roosevelt a nature retreat.[^3] The Lighthouse Directory warns that the claim that the light station itself was a presidential summer retreat does not hold up: "research has shown that this is not correct."[^3][^10]

## Building the light

John Donohoo, a builder of several Chesapeake lights, put up the tower at a cost of about $5,000.[^1] The land came from the Suters. LighthouseFriends says that in December 1835 Henry and Charlotte Suter sold 2.57 acres for $300 so the lighthouse could be built, and the National Register data sheet gives the deed date as 24 December 1835.[^3][^1] Henry Suter was reluctant to sell, but agreed if he could be appointed keeper of the light.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he died before the lighthouse was finished, and that his wife, Charlotte, served as the light's third keeper for seven months before being dismissed over drinking.[^3]

The light was first lit in September 1836.[^3] The National Register form describes the tower as "a conical structure of brick painted white, 30 feet high," with its focal plane 34 feet (10 m) above the base.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the tower's height as 26 feet (8 m), and says the light first used ten oil lamps in spherical reflectors.[^3] The directory gives 35 feet (10.5 m).[^10] The sources do not agree on the tower's height, and this story does not settle it.

After complaints about the light, I.W.P. Lewis visited it in 1837. LighthouseFriends quotes his account, which found "five out of ten lamps extinguished" and the rest nearly so.[^3]

On 5 June 1855 a fifth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps and reflectors.[^3] The archive gives the year as 1856.[^2] In March 1880 a 30-foot (9 m) wooden fog bell tower went up beside the tower. Its striker "tolled the bell every twenty seconds" in thick weather.[^3] A second story was added to the keeper's house in 1884.[^3] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 records that "New model fifth-order lamps were installed" at the station.[^4] A reed horn replaced the bell in 1936.[^3]

## The keepers

The archive's roster for the station runs from 1836 to 1963, and several of its keepers were women.[^2] The roster lists Charlotte Suter as keeper from 1844 to 1846, though LighthouseFriends starts her term in 1845.[^2][^3] Martha Nuthall kept the light from 1850 to 1861, and Elizabeth C. Wilson from 1873 to 1877.[^2] Helen C. Tune followed from 1877 to 1883.[^2] The roster lists Beatrice E. Goeshy twice: as a laborer from 1932 to 1946, and as keeper from 1955.[^2]

Thomas W. Costin kept the light from 1883 to 1910, the longest single term on the roster.[^2] Loch Humphreys served from 1911 to 1912.[^2] A son of his later recalled that his father was strict about "proper procedures." He also recalled a retired naval officer and inspector who would run a finger along the top of the door, "looking for dust."[^3]

William Yeatman Jr. was keeper from 1919 to 1931.[^2] LighthouseFriends prints his own description of a keeper's day, which he gave in 1928. He began by putting out his lamp at sunrise: "I look at my lamp in the lighthouse and put it out." Then he cleaned the lamps and wrote his journal entry, recording "conditions of the weather, rise and fall of the barometer." The rest of the day went to scrubbing floors, painting, and visitors. If fog set in he started the fog bell and stood by it. He changed the light late at night, "as the oil is nearly burnt out."[^3]

In December 1931 the Evening Star reported that Yeatman had been transferred to the Point Lookout light as an assistant keeper.[^9] William M. Goeshy kept the light from 1931 to 1955.[^2]

The archive also lists Coast Guard personnel from 1959 to 1963 under a heading that gives no roles. Their names are not repeated in this story.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1913 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that Henry C. Wingate, the keeper, "has been commended for rendering assistance to a schooner which had run aground."[^5]

On 2 August 1921 Yeatman was commended "in promptly anchoring" a power canoe. LighthouseFriends says the canoe had broken loose from its anchor and was sinking near the station.[^6][^3]

On 2 February 1926 Yeatman helped a man who had stopped at the station "after injuring his knee cap very badly." LighthouseFriends says the keeper called a doctor and provided dinner and breakfast for the man, and that his son took him on to Washington.[^7][^3]

In 1933 a storm reached the station. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin says water covered the station's grounds, and that the fog-signal apparatus was "out of commission and damaged."[^8] LighthouseFriends says the old bell tower was torn down after Hurricane Hazel heavily damaged it in 1954.[^3]

## After the keepers

In December 1931 the Evening Star reported that the Piney Point beacon, "claimed to be around 95 years old," would be run by electricity, along with the fog horn. The government had remodeled the keeper's house.[^9]

LighthouseFriends gives 1 November 1963 as the date the light was discontinued.[^3] The National Register form, prepared by the Coast Guard's Fifth District, says the Coast Guard discontinued it in 1964.[^1] In 1980 the federal government deeded the property to St. Mary's County. The lighthouse and other buildings were given to the Department of Recreation and Parks, which let a local museum group take over operation of the site.[^3] The lighthouse opened to the public in May 2002.[^3] Hurricane Isabel flooded the museum's building in 2003, and the museum moved to higher ground.[^3] In February 2021 a replica of the fifth-order lens was installed in a small lantern room at the museum.[^3] In 2022 Congress allocated $1.9 million to restore and protect the shoreline around the lighthouse.[^10]

## Today

Piney Point Light is a county museum site. The Lighthouse Directory lists St. Mary's County as owner and St. Mary's County Museums as site manager.[^10] LighthouseFriends says the National Park Service owns the lighthouse, although its own history records the 1980 transfer to the county.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open, the dwelling is closed, and the tower is open in season.[^3] The directory says the museum and tower are open daily except for a few holidays.[^10] The two sources disagree on the tower, so check before you go. There is also a pier for visitors who come by boat.[^10]
