St. Shotts Fog Signal Station stands on a headland southeast of the community of St. Shotts, on the Avalon Peninsula in Newfoundland. It is a fog signal rather than a lamp: the Coast Guard's list describes a white square building with a horn, and no light is listed.[^1][^2]

## The place

Cape Freels, where the first fog alarm stood, is two kilometres southwest of Cape Pine, where a lighthouse began work in 1851.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says that by 1894 a report had called a powerful fog signal at St. Shotts the most wanted on the whole coast. By then, it says, there had been numerous wrecks with fatalities along that stretch, which it blames on the strong currents that run into St. Mary's Bay.[^1]

## Building the light

No lamp is recorded for this station. The first structure that LighthouseFriends ties to it is a fog alarm on Cape Freels. In 1883 the steamer Cabot was sent to inspect the shore near St. Shotts, to help choose a site for one. The question was still being discussed three years later.[^1]

Canada had run out of patience waiting for Newfoundland to build one, and it obtained permission to put the alarm on Cape Freels.[^1] A notice to mariners gave the start date as 15 November 1910. The diaphone was to give one seven-second blast every minute in thick or foggy weather.[^1]

The Canadian Fog Signal Company of Toronto supplied the engines and equipment for a three-inch diaphone, at a cost of $7,535. Day labour, under the supervision of J. L. Colter, erected the buildings for $10,426.[^1]

The Department of Marine's report for the twelve months ending 31 March 1911 lists, at "Freels, cape," the completion of a wooden fog-alarm building, dwelling house and outbuildings, and the installation of a 3-inch duplicate diaphone plant. The report files this work under its Nova Scotia heading.[^5]

In November 1911 the signal was changed to two blasts of three and a half seconds each minute.[^1] In 1935 the fog alarm was moved to a spot down the slope from Cape Pine light, where it kept the same signal it had at Cape Freels.[^1]

In 1946 more than a hundred people from the fishing communities around St. Shotts petitioned the Newfoundland government for a foghorn. A manual horn, powered by bellows, was worked by Thomas Best, who was sixteen and was hired that year at $1.50 a day. That horn was used until 1957.[^1] In 1957 the Department of Transport installed a diaphone horn on the eastern head near St. Shotts. George Hewitt accepted a transfer from Cape Pine to look after it, with Gordon Finlay as his assistant.[^1]

LighthouseFriends treats the Cape Freels alarm, the 1935 move to Cape Pine and the 1957 horn as one line of succession. Whether the 1957 horn stands on the 1910 site is not stated.[^1]

## The keepers

The Department of Marine's keepers' list for 1911 has a "Cape Freels Newfoundland Fog Alarm" with Myrick as its keeper.[^4] LighthouseFriends says Michael Myrick was hired at the age of forty-five as the first keeper of the fog alarm and served until 1936. It also says he belonged to a Myrick family that served for several generations at Cape Race.[^1]

LighthouseFriends lists George Hewitt as keeper from 1957 to 1972, Walter Finlay from 1972 to 1995, Peter Myrick from 1996 to 2006, and Basil Molloy from 2006 to at least 2021.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

As LighthouseFriends tells it, three large vessels ran aground near Cape Freels in the summer of 1882. The steamship Gertrude had left New Orleans in mid-May, bound for Denmark. Her officers, navigating by dead reckoning through fog, believed they were well clear of the shore. On the evening of 4 June she ran aground on the western head at St. Shotts. Her twenty-two crew got into boats and were picked up the next morning by a fishing schooner. The brigantine Eagle was lost in the same place, and on 13 August the steamship Action wrecked nearby. No lives were lost in the three wrecks.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the incidents highlighted the need for a fog alarm along that coast.[^1]

## After the keepers

Commercial power reached the station in 1966. In 1981 its staff was reduced to one person.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard's list gives the signal as a fog horn: two blasts of two seconds, each followed by eight seconds of silence, then one more two-second blast followed by thirty-eight seconds of silence. The building is white with a brown roof.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the foghorn remains active.[^1]

The Coast Guard's April 2010 figures, printed in a 2011 Senate of Canada report, list two positions here on a seven-day rotation. The station is not counted as remote. The same report says eighteen of the 23 staffed Newfoundland stations can be reached by road.[^3]

This is a working Coast Guard station, and access is restricted. The sources give no public visiting arrangement, so do not go onto the grounds without permission.
