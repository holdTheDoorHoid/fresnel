Garden Key Light, also called Tortugas Harbor Light, stands on a bastion of Fort Jefferson, on Garden Key in the Dry Tortugas, about 67 miles west of Key West.[^5][^6] Its first tower was brick, and it was first lit in 1826.[^5] Hurricane damage led to its replacement by the iron tower on the fort, first lit in 1876.[^1][^5] The light was discontinued in 1921.[^5] In 2021 the iron tower was reassembled on its bastion after a restoration.[^5][^7]

## The place

The Dry Tortugas are a cluster of reefs, islands and shoals about seventy miles from Key West, at the far end of the Florida Keys.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the group was named Las Tortugas, for the turtles that were plentiful there when Juan Ponce de León anchored in 1513.[^5]

After Commander Matthew C. Perry surveyed the Florida coast in the early 1820s, he recommended lights for several places, including the Dry Tortugas.[^5] The site chosen was Garden Key, where a light could show over all the islands and mark the harbor just to the north.[^5]

The island later became a fort. Construction of Fort Jefferson began in 1846 on Garden Key, around the brick lighthouse built in 1825.[^2] The fort was never finished, and most of the work was abandoned by 1874.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the fort held more than 2,200 prisoners over ten years, with a peak of 900 in 1864.[^5]

## Building the light

In May 1822 Congress appropriated $8,000 for a light here. The money reverted to the Treasury, and the same sum had to be appropriated again on 26 May 1824.[^5] The ship carrying the building supplies was lost at sea on the way, and building did not begin until 1825.[^5]

The brick tower was 65 feet (19.8 m) tall.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it was finished in March 1826. The keeper did not arrive for several more months, and fifteen lamps in metal reflectors were first lit on 4 July 1826.[^5]

The first design had a flaw. The iron door of the lantern room created a gap in visibility for vessels approaching from the east. The door was soon removed, but the lamp kept giving trouble.[^1] Captains claimed that a vessel would run aground on shoals and reefs before seeing the light.[^5] The keeper wanted two new lights, one at each end of the shoals, to replace the Garden Key light, because of the continued shipwrecks and the light's general inadequacy. Congress sent inspectors to look at the Garden Key light in 1836 instead.[^1] New plate glass and lanterns were installed by 1838, and the new additions were shattered by 1839.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the fifteen original lamps were replaced by twenty-three new lamps and reflectors in 1838, and the complaints went on.[^5]

In 1858 the more adequate Dry Tortugas Light, now known as the Loggerhead light, was built on Loggerhead Key. The Garden Key light was downgraded to a fourth-order harbor light.[^1][^5] It was fitted with 21-inch (53 cm) reflectors and renamed Tortugas Harbor Lighthouse.[^1]

The Board's report for 1891 and 1892 says the positions for the proposed red sectors were laid out.[^9] The Lighthouse Board recorded that the light's characteristic was changed on 30 April 1893.[^3] LighthouseFriends says red sectors were added to the fixed white light that year.[^5] The Board's next report describes the northerly red sector being corrected.[^3]

Hurricanes in 1873 and 1875 damaged the old tower, and the Lighthouse Service advised a new light.[^1] LighthouseFriends says inspectors recommended that the tower be torn down and replaced by an iron structure on the fort's walls, and that Congress appropriated $5,000 on 3 March 1875.[^5] The new hexagonal iron tower stood atop Bastion C and was first lit on 5 April 1876.[^5]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends calls John R. Flaherty, keeper from 1826 to 1827,[^6] a "lazy and incompetent keeper" whose mismanagement let a thick coat of soot form on the lantern glass.[^5] His wife strongly disliked the heat, the boredom and the mosquitoes.[^5] After fourteen months he swapped posts with the keeper of Sand Key.[^5]

From 1826 to 1855 the roster lists ten principal keepers, most of them for a few years each.[^6] George Topham appears as first assistant from 1843.[^6] Henry Benners kept the light from 1855 to 1869. John A. Messina followed him until 1888, and then Louis Italy kept it from 1888 to 1908.[^6]

In 1899 the Board's report lists the station's upkeep. Some 212 feet (65 m) of brick walks were laid, some 465 feet (142 m) of iron fence was scraped and painted, and the washhouse and chicken house were rebuilt from the ground up. The tower's ironwork was scraped and painted two coats.[^8]

William H. Italy appears on the roster in 1908 and again in 1910 and 1911.[^6] Charles Johnson was the last keeper before automation, from 1911 to 1912.[^5][^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A hurricane in October 1873 was a blow to the Dry Tortugas, and the light was badly damaged, according to LighthouseFriends.[^5] The National Park Service says the tower sustained serious damage after the hurricanes of 1873 and 1875.[^1]

On the morning of 5 January 1912, a fire broke out in an outbuilding and destroyed the keeper's dwelling at the Tortugas Harbor light station. The Marine Barracks next to it also burned, the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported.[^4] LighthouseFriends says Keeper Johnson and his large family lost nearly all their household goods and clothing in the fire.[^5]

## After the keepers

The fire led to an automated acetylene light that same year. Johnson was transferred to Loggerhead Key as an assistant keeper, according to LighthouseFriends.[^5]

The automated light was discontinued in 1921, although a decorative light was later placed in the tower.[^5] The Lighthouse Society's record also gives 1921 as the discontinuance year.[^6]

President Roosevelt designated Fort Jefferson National Monument in 1935, and the site became Dry Tortugas National Park in 1992.[^5]

In October 2020 the National Park Service began a $4.5 million restoration of the tower, in three phases.[^5][^7] A crew dismantled the iron lighthouse piece by piece and carried the parts to the mainland. The metal was repaired in Alabama, and the tower was reassembled in its original location on its bastion in late summer 2021.[^5]

## Today

Garden Key Light belongs to the National Park Service and stands in Dry Tortugas National Park. LighthouseFriends says visitors can reach the park by seaplane or by ferry.[^5] You can walk around the top of the fort for a good view of the lighthouse. The lighthouse itself is not open to the public, and its tower is closed, so do not try to climb it.[^5]
