The Morris Island Lighthouse stands in open water off the south side of the Charleston harbor entrance. Its brick tower was lit in 1876 and is the third light on the site. The antebellum tower was blown up by Confederate forces in 1861, and the 1876 tower was retired in 1962, when a new light on Sullivan's Island took over.[^1][^2]

## The place

Morris Island lies on the south side of the entrance to Charleston Harbor, and the light was built to mark that channel.[^1] In 1767 the ground that became the island was three small islands. By the Civil War they had joined into one, which took the name Morris Island.[^1] In 1790 South Carolina handed the light and its island to the new federal government.[^2]

The Life-Saving Service, a separate agency, also invited proposals for a station on Morris Island. Its annual report for 1884 says proposals had been invited "for the construction of a station on Morris Island, South Carolina."[^8] This story is about the light.

## Building the light

The colony's first tower was an octagonal brick tower, designed by Samuel Cardy and built by the bricklayer Adam Miller on Middle Bay Island.[^1] The nomination says a second tower, with a revolving light, was built in 1837 and refitted in 1845 and 1857.[^1] The Charleston history site reports that the sources do not settle whether a new tower went up in 1837 or in 1857, or whether the colonial tower was raised and refitted.[^2]

Two sources give the lens installed in 1858 as second-order, while the 1982 nomination describes a first-order lens in the 1876 tower.[^1][^2][^4]

In 1873 the Light-House Board planned a replacement, a brick cone about 150 feet (46 m) tall, on piles driven through the island's mud and clay, under a timber grillage and concrete.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the piles were driven into sand.[^4] Work started with the foundations in the fiscal year that ended in June 1874, and bricklaying on the tower began in October 1875.[^2] The nomination gives the tower a base about 33 feet (10 m) across and a focal plane 158 feet (48 m) above the sea, and says the light could be seen for 18 and three-quarter miles.[^1]

Because the channel had moved, the new tower went to the south of the second light. The nomination says it was built "coincidentally directly on top of the 1767 tower," and that remains of the first light turned up during the building.[^1] The Charleston history site says otherwise about the site. It states that the 1876 tower "did not stand on the site of the tower blown up in 1861," and that the nomination's claim fits only if the wartime tower was a separate structure from the 1767 beacon, "which the sources do not settle."[^2] The light was first shown on 1 October 1876.[^2][^4]

The nomination describes a revolving lamp with a first-order Fresnel lens, which burned lard oil until 1881 and then mineral oil. It says the fuel became acetylene in 1934.[^1] The Charleston history site reports that the Board's 1876 report and its August 1876 notice describe a fixed white light.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the change from lard oil to mineral oil to 1884.[^4]

## The keepers

The USLHS archive lists 90 keeper stints at the station, from 1790 to 1938.[^6] The earliest is Thomas Hollingsby, principal keeper from 1790 to 1796.[^6] The name Mellichamp recurs: St. Lo Mellichamp was principal keeper from 1830 to 1856, and a Joseph Mellichamp appears as an assistant in 1859 and 1860. The archive spells that assistant's name two ways.[^6]

When the new tower was first lit, George W. Williams was principal keeper, with Richard M. Jones, George W. Veronee and John Barrett as his assistants.[^4][^6]

The keepers lived on a settlement, not a lone house. Nautical maps from the late 1800s show at least fifteen buildings, among them three Italianate keepers' residences.[^1] The residents kept a garden, chickens and pigs. A one-room school had a teacher who came out by boat at the start of each week and went home to Charleston at weekends. A boardwalk system eased travel around the island, and well into the twentieth century a car was imported for getting around it.[^1]

John Wieking was principal keeper from 1885 to 1912, and Lewis H. Bringloe from 1913 to 1933.[^6] William A. Hecker had been an assistant from 1925 and became principal in 1935. William A. Davis was an assistant there from the mid-1920s until 1938.[^6] A later account says that William Hecker and William Davis, with their wives and children, stood watch at the keepers' home until 1938. Mrs. Hecker recalled, "We loved the island, but the waves were coming in my door."[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the war the antebellum tower was blown up. The 1982 nomination says Confederate troops destroyed the lighthouse to hinder the Union forces' entry into the channel.[^1] The Charleston history site places the blowing-up on the night of 18 and 19 December 1861, as the first Union stone fleet gathered off the bar. Some popular accounts give 25 December 1861.[^2]

A Charleston newspaper of 26 December 1861 reported that the lighthouse "was blown up one night last week by order of our military authorities," as the history site quotes it.[^2] The Light-House Board's list of wartime damage, which the Board itself called only approximately correct, recorded "Charleston, lens and lantern destroyed." The newspapers say the tower itself was blown up.[^2]

Storms and earthquakes followed. The Charleston history site, citing the Coast Guard Historian's Office, dates a cyclone to 25 August 1885, which knocked down part of the brick wall around the tower and dwelling.[^2] An earthquake in August 1886 jarred the lens out of place and cracked the tower in two places, but the same account says it did not threaten the tower's stability.[^2]

The storm that crossed the coast between Savannah and Charleston on 27 and 28 August 1893 damaged stations across the Light-House Board's sixth district. The report says the beacons on Morris Island were swept away to sea so completely that nothing remained even of the sand hills on which they formerly stood. That passage speaks of beacons, not of the tower.[^7]

Erosion was also taking the island. The 1982 nomination says that jetties constructed in 1896 caused complete erosion of the shoal that protected Morris Island.[^1] The Charleston history site dates the harbor jetties from 1878 to 1895, one of them springing from Morris Island. It also says the shore was retreating long before the jetties were finished.[^2] The two sources disagree on the dates and on the cause. Since 1939, the nomination says, over 1,600 feet (490 m) of land has been lost.[^1]

## After the keepers

The nomination says the light was automated in 1938, and the keepers and their families left the island.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the change more precisely: the lens and its electric light source were removed, and a mantle-type acetylene light replaced them on 22 June 1938.[^4] The nomination dates the switch to acetylene to 1934, so the two sources differ on the fuel date.

The accounts agree that the keepers' buildings are gone but differ on how. Charleston Magazine says the buildings were razed when the light was automated.[^5] The nomination says all the outbuildings and associated structures have been destroyed, and that erosion has eliminated all traces of them.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the large keepers' dwelling was sold and dismantled, and that the other structures were razed before the water could wash them away.[^4]

A new light went into service on Sullivan's Island on 15 June 1962, and the Morris Island light was discontinued.[^2] The nomination says the old tower was then "slated for demolition," and that it was saved through the efforts of the Charleston Preservation Society.[^1]

After the government sold the tower at auction in 1965, it passed through private hands. A buyer at the 1965 sale planned to incorporate the lighthouse into a camping ground. A real estate entrepreneur bought it in 1966, and in 1996 a Columbia businessman acquired it through foreclosure.[^3] In 1999 Save The Light, Inc. bought the tower for $75,000. The State of South Carolina accepted title through its Department of Natural Resources in 2000, and Save The Light signed a 99-year lease.[^3] A 2006 project agreement with the Army covered storm-damage work at the tower.[^2] The tower was listed on the National Register on 18 June 1982.[^2]

## Today

The 1876 tower stands in open water. The nomination puts it "about 500 feet off the shore of what is left of Morris Island" (about 150 m), while LighthouseFriends gives roughly 1,600 feet (490 m).[^1][^4]

The tower belongs to the state. South Carolina has owned it since 2000, and it is no longer an aid to navigation.[^2] Save The Light has built a steel cofferdam and a concrete foundation.[^5]

The tower is closed to visitors. LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds are open and the tower closed, and that the lighthouse can be seen from the island's tip, about half a mile (0.8 km) on foot past the road barricade at Folly Beach, or from a boat tour that passes it.[^4] Nobody should climb the tower or wade out to it.
