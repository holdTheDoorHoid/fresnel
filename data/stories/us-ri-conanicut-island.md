Conanicut Island Light stands at the north tip of Conanicut (Jamestown) Island in Narragansett Bay, Rhode Island. Its wooden tower, joined to a Gothic Revival keeper's house, was first lit on April 1, 1886, and it guided southbound traffic until it was deactivated in 1933.[^1][^3][^6] The house is now a private residence, and the grounds are closed to visitors.[^3][^6]

## The place

The light sits on a point that marks where Narragansett Bay divides into its east and west passages. The National Register nomination says the light "served primarily to guide southbound traffic" from the Providence River and Greenwich Bay toward Newport and the Atlantic.[^1]

Before the government built a light there, a steamboat company made do with a private one. LighthouseFriends says the Wickford Rail and Steamboat Company ran a ferry between Newport and Wickford, and had an arrangement with island landowners to keep a lighted beacon at the north end, to help its ferry captains round the north end of the island.[^6] The company was apparently behind an 1882 petition for an official light, and LighthouseFriends says Congress approved $18,000 for one in 1884.[^6]

The Lighthouse Board bought a strip of land on the northern tip for $1,000. The title was delayed for a time because a member of the Conanicut Park Land Company, whose signature was needed on the title papers, was in Europe, according to the same source.[^6]

## Building the light

Work began in the summer of 1885 and stopped for the winter.[^6] The tower is a three-story, square wooden tower attached to the corner of a two-story keeper's dwelling. The dwelling is red clapboard, with a steeply pitched cross-gable roof, and the nomination says it is the only surviving lighthouse in Rhode Island built in the Gothic Revival style.[^1]

The lantern held a fifth-order Fresnel lens. The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1886 as the year the lens was installed.[^3] The light was first lit on April 1, 1886.[^1][^6]

Outbuildings followed over the next two decades. The nomination dates a barn to 1897, a brick oil house to 1901 and a brick fog-signal building to 1907.[^1]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society's roster names four people for the station.[^3] The first keeper, Horace W. Arnold, served from 1886 to 1914.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he was a native of the area, born and raised on Prudence Island, and that he had been keeper of Conimicut Light from 1874 until he won a transfer to Conanicut.[^6] The roster for Conimicut Shoal lists him there from 1874 to 1886.[^4] LighthouseFriends says Arnold kept the light at Conanicut for twenty-eight years. It also says the lantern was only forty-seven feet (14 m) above the water, so the keeper did not face the stair climbing typical of many lighthouse towers.[^6]

Arnold died in 1914, according to LighthouseFriends, and Philip Sidney Arnold kept the light that year.[^3][^6] Elmer V. Newton took charge in 1914. The Society's record has him there until 1932.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he was moved to the light on Prudence Island when Conanicut was deactivated in 1933, and the Prudence Island roster lists him there from 1933 to 1937.[^5][^6] The Society's record also lists a contractor, Linas Seeley, with no dates.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A wreck came before the light was lit. The Narragansett Herald of 6 February 1886 reported that the steamer *Eolus*, of the Wickford line, struck the rocky point at the north end of Conanicut on a Saturday evening. The paper gives no date beyond that.[^10] It said that, although the storm was a blinding one, "the usual fog horn and strontian red light, in lieu of the lighthouse now being built, apparently were not used."[^10] The thirteen passengers were taken ashore in the steamer's life boat and carried by team to a summer boarding house.[^10] The naval officials at Newport sent up a tug, the *Cohasset*, which could give no help, and the steamer was got off later.[^10]

Keeper Arnold's notes, which LighthouseFriends quotes, describe two storms. One, in April 1911, he called "the severest I ever saw in 37 years of keeping light." The other is undated; he called it a "very hard blow, hardest since I have lived here for the past 27 years."[^6]

The Light-House Board's annual reports list the changes made at the station each year.[^7][^8][^9] In the year ended June 30, 1897, the fog bell's signal was changed from a double blow every 30 seconds to a double blow every 10 seconds, and a small barn was built and the sea wall repaired.[^7] In the year ended June 30, 1900, a blower siren driven by a small oil engine was set up, with a water tank of 833 gallons.[^8] In the year ended June 30, 1901, the siren ran for about 336 hours and used some 116 gallons of oil.[^9]

## After the keepers

The light was deactivated in 1933. The lantern and lens came out of the tower, and a steel skeletal tower carrying an automatic beacon went up a short distance to the east.[^1] The lighthouse and grounds were declared surplus and sold at auction in 1934, LighthouseFriends says.[^6] The skeletal light stayed in service until 1982, and its tower was taken down in 1984.[^1][^6]

The nomination says the house "has been used as a summer residence" since 1933.[^1] The National Register asset record gives 25 February 1988 as the date published, under number 87001698.[^2] In 2023, LighthouseFriends reports, the federal government sold at auction for $166,000 a vacant lot that had been part of the station.[^6]

## Today

The lighthouse and its grounds are privately owned, and the Lighthouse Society's record says they are not open to the public.[^3][^6] LighthouseFriends notes that the best views are from the water, and that a bay cruise passes the station.[^6] Visitors should keep off the private land.
