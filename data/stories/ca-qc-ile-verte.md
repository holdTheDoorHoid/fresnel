Île Verte is a green, crescent-shaped island about 5 km (3 miles) off the south shore of the St. Lawrence, and its lighthouse, finished in 1809, was the first light on the river.[^1][^2] For 137 years four generations of one family, the Lindsays, kept it.[^3][^4]

## The place

Lighthouse Friends lists the hazards around the island as long foreshore flats that dry at high tide, strong currents where the Saguenay River meets the St. Lawrence, and frequent summer fog, and says reefs off the northeast shore were nicknamed "Les Couillions".[^2] Quebec's heritage register places the light at the entrance of the south channel, "caractérisé par de dangereux hauts-fonds et de forts courants" (a place marked by dangerous shoals and strong currents).[^1] The same register notes that the Maison de la Trinité de Québec, the Quebec Trinity House, was set up in 1805 to look after navigation on the river, and that in 1806 the government ordered the lighthouse built.[^1]

## Building the light

The tower was designed and built by Edward Cannon, a master mason of Quebec City, who began work in 1806.[^2][^4] The light became operational in 1809 and was the only lighthouse on the St. Lawrence until 1830.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the first lighting apparatus was thirteen lamps with reflectors burning porpoise, seal and fish oil, with kerosene substituted in 1865.[^2] It describes the tower as octagonal stone; the Quebec register describes a circular, tapered masonry tower about 18 m (59 ft) high, with a copper lantern, while Lighthouse Friends gives the height as 17 m (56 ft).[^1][^2] Both agree the stone was later boarded over: the register says horizontal planks went on in 1850 and vertical ones in 1904.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says a new lantern was fitted in 1897 and a fourth-order lens in 1913.[^2]

## The keepers

Charles Hembleton was the first keeper, in a dwelling of 6.4 by 7.6 m (21 by 25 ft) that he shared with ten family members, according to Lighthouse Friends; the Quebec register spells the name Hambelton and gives his end date as 1827.[^1][^2] Robert Noel Lindsay, a young pilot's apprentice and sailor from Quebec City, took over in 1827.[^2] From then until 1964 the Lindsay family held the post: Robert, his son Gilbert (Guilbert), Gilbert's son René (Irénée), and finally Joseph-Alfred "Freddy" Lindsay.[^2][^3] The sources disagree on when Gilbert's time ended. Lighthouse Friends says he fell from the tower while painting the lantern and was bedridden until he died in 1888, when René was appointed at sixteen; the Canadian Register gives his death as about 1891.[^2][^3]

In 1831 Robert Lindsay wrote a complaint about the new dwelling, saying the roof was badly built and the fireplace smoked excessively.[^2] Freddy Lindsay's wife later recalled that some islanders were jealous of the post and tried to have him dismissed.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 28 September 1831 the brig Acadia, bound from Dublin to Quebec, was wrecked on the island. Lindsay petitioned the government to be repaid for his costs, and the ship's master and seven passengers signed a statement supporting him, which says he provided for "not less than Two hundred and fifty souls" during their stay on the island, as Lighthouse Friends prints it.[^2] In fog and snow the station fired a gun: a nine-pounder, replaced in 1882 by a thirty-two-pounder, and from 1894 a device firing guncotton charges every twenty minutes.[^2] One December night in 1930, Lighthouse Friends says, Freddy Lindsay was covered in blood and woodchips when a charge went off as he connected it; he had forgotten to disconnect the battery that powered the detonator.[^2] The Department of Marine's report for the year ended June 30, 1900 called the station "the oldest station in the whole district" and recorded repairs to the dwelling, which was clapboarded.[^5]

## After the keepers

Armand Lafrance was the last keeper, and the station was automated, in 1972 according to Lighthouse Friends, in 1969 according to Quebec's heritage register.[^1][^2] The keepers' houses were rebuilt in 1960, and the land and buildings other than the lighthouse and fog-signal building passed to the municipality of Notre-Dame-des-Sept-Douleurs in 1983, according to Lighthouse Friends; the Quebec register says 1982 for most of the buildings.[^1][^2] The lighthouse was made a National Historic Site in 1974, and the site was classed as a heritage site by Quebec in 2022.[^1][^2]

## Today

The lighthouse belongs to the Canadian Coast Guard.[^2] The island is reached by ferry from Notre-Dame-des-Sept-Douleurs, and the two keepers' houses operate as a bed and breakfast, Les Maisons du Phare de l'Île Verte, from mid-May to late October.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^2]
