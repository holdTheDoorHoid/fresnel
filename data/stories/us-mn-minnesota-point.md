Minnesota Point Light stood on the long sandbar that shelters Superior Bay, beside the gap that ships used to reach the competing ports of Duluth and Superior. It was lit in 1858 and went dark for good in 1885. What remains is the lower half of its brick tower, on the point a walk from Sky Harbor Airport.[^1][^2]

## The place

Minnesota Point is a sandbar that projects about seven miles eastward from Duluth.[^1] In the early 1850s the bar had a single opening, Superior Entry, and every vessel bound for either harbor had to pass through it.[^3] The canal and locks at Sault Ste. Marie were completed in 1855, and both cities expected far more traffic to follow.[^1][^3]

Duluth's businessmen began cutting their own canal through the point in 1871. Pepper writes that after that, Superior Entry served only vessels bound for the Wisconsin port.[^3]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $15,000 for a light on the Minnesota side of the entry. The date is given two ways: LighthouseFriends gives 3 March 1855, and Terry Pepper gives 3 August 1854.[^1][^3] The site, about three-quarters of a mile from the entry, was surveyed in 1855.[^3] Work on the foundation began in the fall of 1856. Work resumed in 1857, but cold weather set in before it was finished, and the light was not activated until the spring of 1858.[^1]

The contract went to Captain R. G. Coburn of Superior, but LighthouseFriends gives most of the credit for the building to the stonemason Adam Dopp, a German immigrant.[^1] The foundation was blue rubblestone. The red brick for the tower and the attached dwelling came by ship from Cleveland.[^1] The tower was forty feet (12 m) tall, with a ten-sided lantern, and the whole station cost $13,675.[^1]

The lens was a fourth-order Fresnel that showed a fixed red light, burning kerosene.[^1][^2] Pepper gives the lens as fifth order.[^3] R. H. Barrett, a local merchant, was appointed the first keeper.[^1]

Terry Pepper says the Board's reports describe a station that needed constant repair. He quotes the Board's 1868 report on the dwelling: it "leaks badly around the chimneys."[^3] LighthouseFriends says the repairs were carried out in 1869.[^1]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster lists 22 entries for the station, from 1858 to 1913.[^2] Ruben H. Barrett, the first keeper, served from 1858 to 1861.[^2] Robert W. Sanborn's stint, from 1888 to 1902, is the longest on the roster.[^2] Thomas Doody followed him and is the last principal keeper listed, from 1902 to 1913.[^2]

The first assistants changed often. Joseph Restoule served from 1894 to 1900, and Arie Bakker from 1901 to 1906.[^2]

The roster lists one woman, Annie Doody, under the archive's acting-assistant heading, in 1906 and again in 1907. It does not say how she was related to Thomas Doody, the principal keeper in those years.[^2]

## After the keepers

In June 1878 work began on a pierhead light on the northwest side of Superior Entry. It was lit on 1 September 1878, and its keeper lived in the brick dwelling on the point.[^1] The tower light came back in 1880, and the pierhead light was discontinued.[^1][^2]

On 6 August 1885 the pierhead light was re-established, and the tower light went dark for good.[^3][^1] While it was in service, locals had called the tower the "Old Standby."[^1]

The sources disagree on what happened to the keepers' house. Pepper says it was refurbished and used by the keeper of the pierhead light after 1885, and that the Minnesota Point dwelling was abandoned in 1895, when new buildings went up on Wisconsin Point.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the keepers left the point in 1894, when the dwelling was moved to the Wisconsin side of the entry.[^1] The roster names keepers under this station until 1913.[^2] Neither history says which light those later keepers tended, so this story does not assign them to the tower.

In 1902 the Board chose the old lighthouse reservation as the site for a buoy depot. Pepper says construction began in 1903.[^3] LighthouseFriends says Congress appropriated $14,000 for the depot in 1903.[^1] The tower was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1974.[^1]

## Today

What stands is the lower half of the brick tower, surrounded by chain-link.[^1][^2] The concrete storehouse and oil house from the former depot site also stand on the point.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers owns the site, and that the grounds are open while the tower is closed.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society record marks the site as not open to the public, so check before you go.[^2]

Getting there is on foot. LighthouseFriends says driving past Sky Harbor Airport is not permitted, so park near the airport and walk the remaining 1.5 miles to the ruins.[^1] Pepper describes an unmarked trail, Park Point Trail, that runs alongside the airport fence.[^3] Do not climb the tower, which is closed.
