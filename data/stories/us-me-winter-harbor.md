Winter Harbor Light stands on Mark Island, about a mile off the town of Winter Harbor in Hancock County, Maine, near the entrance to Frenchman Bay.[^1][^4] Its brick tower dates from 1856.[^1] The light's official service ended in 1933 or 1934, depending on the source, and the station has been in private hands since it was sold in 1934.[^1][^4]

## The place

The light was built "as a guide to nearby Winter and Mosquito harbors."[^1] The nomination calls it a primary navigational aid "to the local fishing fleet as well as nearby maritime transportation routes," and says that the summer tourist industry on Mount Desert Island "increased the number of pleasure craft that plied the waterways in the area."[^1]

LighthouseFriends dates the light's official service from 1 January 1857, a year after the nomination's 1856 date for the tower.[^4] Both dates appear in the record, and this story does not choose between them.

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $4,500 to build the light, according to LighthouseFriends.[^4] The nomination says the brick tower "was erected in 1856," and that its "original octagonal lantern with its domed polygonal roof and spherical ventilator survives."[^1] LighthouseFriends describes the tower as nineteen feet (6 m) tall, with a fifth-order Fresnel lens giving a focal plane of thirty-seven feet (11 m) above high water.[^4]

The first dwelling was replaced in 1876.[^1] The nomination says that "This dwelling and its long connecting ell were built in 1876 to replace the original house."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the old frame house had decayed past repair by then.[^4] A wooden boathouse followed in 1878.[^1][^4]

The Board's reports record later repairs.[^3] The report for 1891–92 says that the boat-slip was partly renewed and that minor repairs were made to the dwelling, and a later entry in the same volume says the boat-slip was extended 15 feet (4.6 m) and refastened.[^3][^5] In the 1893–94 volume "The boathouse was remodeled and enlarged."[^6] In 1899 a partition was built in the L of the dwelling, providing another room.[^7] The stone oil house was built in 1905.[^1]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster gives nine principal keepers for the station between 1857 and 1933.[^2] The first was Frederick P. Gerrish, from 1857 to 1860.[^2] LighthouseFriends says Gerrish lived in the house with his wife Susan and their eight children until 1861, when W. N. Wasgatt took over. The roster puts Wasgatt in charge from 1860.[^2][^4]

The roster lists William N. Wasgatt from 1860 to 1866, Richard Higgins from 1866 to 1868, Amaziah Southard from 1868 to 1870, Allen H. Cole from 1870 to 1875, and James B. Wright from 1875 to 1888.[^2]

Benjamin Maddox, whom the roster also prints as Maddocks, was keeper from 1888 to 1896.[^2] LighthouseFriends calls him a Civil War veteran who lived on the island with his wife Olivette until 1896.[^4]

The roster lists Adelbert C. Leighton from 1896 to 1924.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives the same span in its keeper list, but its narrative says he served to 1927.[^4] The last principal keeper on the roster was Lester Leighton, from 1924 to 1933.[^2] LighthouseFriends calls him "Captain Lester 'Cap' Leighton" and says he never returned to Mark Island after leaving in 1933.[^4]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends dates the end of official service to late September 1933, when a lighted bell buoy was set southeast of the lighthouse.[^4] The nomination says instead that "This light was decommissioned in 1934 and sold to a private citizen."[^1] The two dates differ, and the sources read do not settle them.

In 1934 the station was auctioned, and LighthouseFriends says George Harmon of Bar Harbor bought it.[^4] In 1939 Harmon sold the island to Bernice Richmond, a writer who published two books about her years there, *Winter Harbor* and *Our Island Lighthouse*.[^4] In 1956 a couple of children's-book authors bought the island and lived there full time for eighteen years, according to LighthouseFriends.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the tower deteriorated in the years after that, and that duck hunters shot out its windows.[^4]

The station's keeper logbooks, dating from 1856, went missing around 1983, and the island was bought for $87,500 that year.[^4] The deed passed to another private owner in 1995.[^4] In 1996 an orange light was shown from the tower, because it was a private light and not an official Coast Guard aid.[^4]

## Today

The lighthouse is privately owned, and its grounds, dwelling and tower are closed, according to LighthouseFriends.[^4] LighthouseFriends reports a sale of the island in October 2004, and a later listing whose outcome the page does not confirm.[^4] The nomination was prepared in October 1987, and the station was listed in the National Register of Historic Places in 1988.[^1][^8]

Do not land on the island or try to reach the tower.
