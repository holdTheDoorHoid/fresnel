Point Retreat Light marks the northern tip of Admiralty Island in Alaska's Inside Passage, where Lynn Canal meets Stephens Passage. A concrete building with a short tower on its roof, it has been a staffed light, an unwatched acetylene lamp and an automated station in turn, and for part of its life it was a place where keepers could bring their families.[^1]

## The place

The station sits on the Mansfield Peninsula at the northern end of Admiralty Island, near the junction of Lynn Canal and Stephens Passage, which the National Register nomination calls an important intersection for ships and boats using the Inside Passage.[^1] Its case for a light rests on the traffic of the gold-rush years: the nomination says that over three hundred accidents were reported in Inside Passage waters in 1898, and that fog, rain, strong tides and a rocky shoreline made the route hard for heavily loaded steamers.[^1] In 1901 President McKinley reserved land for lighthouses in Alaska by executive order, and one reserve was 1,505 acres at Point Retreat.[^1][^2]

## Building the light

The first light was a white hexagonal wooden tower with a black hexagonal lantern, lit on 15 September 1904. It had no fog signal; the dwelling stood to the south and a boathouse east of the dwelling.[^1]

The record is unclear on when the station lost its resident keepers. The National Register nomination says the station was not staffed before 1917 and was reduced to a minor light with a new acetylene light.[^1] Lighthousefriends dates an automated acetylene light to 1 September 1911, the point at which the station was "stripped of its personnel."[^2] The keeper roster in the Lighthouse Society's archive lists head keepers from 1905 to 1911 and none again until 1923.[^6] By 1918 the Commissioner of Lighthouses described the place as "an important station" that had "only an un watched acetylene light and no fog signal."[^3] The nomination adds that annual light reports for 1920 to 1923 found the lamp extinguished at each visit.[^1]

Money was slow. The Commissioner reported that Congress had authorized $32,500 for Alaska improvements including Point Retreat, "but no appropriation was made therefor," and that revised estimates showed the sum would not cover Point Retreat alone.[^4] The estimate for Point Retreat priced a reinforced-concrete fog-signal building with a light tower on top at $2.15 a cubic foot for 13,000 cubic feet, or $27,950, with $4,000 more to repair the existing dwelling.[^4] Lighthousefriends dates an appropriation of $125,000 for Alaska to 28 March 1922.[^2]

The present building dates from 1924, with its support buildings put up in 1923-24, and the nomination gives the cost as $58,242: a combined light and fog-signal house in reinforced concrete with an Art Moderne look, measuring 34 by 20 feet (10.4 by 6.1 m), with an eight-foot-square (2.4 m) tower rising eighteen feet (5.5 m) from the roof.[^1] Lighthousefriends gives the building as 20 by 36 feet and dates the work 1923-24.[^2] New keepers' quarters, support buildings including an oil house, a dock and a tramway were added.[^1] The Lighthouse Bulletin's 1924 volume lists an air diaphone at Point Retreat among the fog signals established.[^5] Lighthousefriends says the new light burned acetylene, kept the two-flashes-every-six-seconds pattern, and was paired with a diaphone sounding a pair of three-second blasts every thirty seconds.[^2] The nomination says the cupola removed in the 1950s had housed a fourth-order lens.[^1]

## The keepers

The nomination says keepers sought Point Retreat because it was one of the few Alaska stations where families could come too, and because Juneau and Douglas were close.[^1] The archive lists Charles B. Bohm as head keeper from 1923 to 1925 and Frank Edlin from 1925 to 1926; Eugene E. Mead held the post from 1929 to 1938.[^6] Mead was still named as keeper of Point Retreat in the Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1931.[^7]

One keeper's family is described in some detail. Lighthousefriends says Charles McLeod, who had sailed from Scotland to New York as a boy, worked on lighthouse construction in Alaska, married in Scotland in 1924, and brought his wife and infant son to Point Retreat in 1926.[^2] When the boy was two, it says, he fell forty feet from the dock to the rocks; he was taken to Juneau, where, it says, a doctor's poor treatment left him crippled for life, and the station launch was named *Hard Luck Charlie* after him.[^2] McLeod died of pneumonia in 1929.[^2] The sources disagree about his post: the archive lists McLeod as head keeper from 1926 to 1929 alongside Philip W. Harner, while Lighthousefriends calls him an assistant, and the nomination says he served as keeper from 1926 to 1930.[^6][^2][^1]

Among the assistants in the archive's list are August Waltenberg (1936 to 1940) and Paul Schuttpelz, Jr., from 1940 to at least 1941.[^6]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over lighthouse administration in 1939, and the nomination says staffing then shifted from keepers with families to single men; an inspection of 14 April 1944 counted ten men, including an officer in charge, and found the dwellings in poor condition.[^1] In the 1950s the lantern room was removed and an eight-foot block carrying a double-ended airways beacon, flashing alternately white and red, replaced it.[^1][^2] In 1966 the Coast Guard removed one keeper's house and put a helicopter pad over its foundation.[^1] The Coast Guard automated the station in 1973 and took off the remaining staff.[^1]

The Alaska Lighthouse Association, a non-profit, leased the buildings in 1997 and became the owner in 2002; Lighthousefriends adds that the transfer was disputed and carried a requirement of public access.[^1][^2] Lighthousefriends says all the structures were repainted in 2002, and a steel replica of the lantern room, built from drawings in the National Archives, was installed in 2004, the station's centennial.[^2] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2003.[^1]

## Today

The Association's website is no longer active, and Lighthousefriends says the station's current status is unknown.[^2] The nomination says the light is reached only by water or air, and the Lighthouse Society's archive records it as not open to the public.[^1][^6] Lighthousefriends says it can be seen from ferries on the Juneau routes, and lists the grounds as open and the dwelling and tower as open by arrangement with the Association; given the unknown status, that listing may be out of date.[^2] Anyone planning a visit should confirm access first; it is an automated aid to navigation on remote land.
