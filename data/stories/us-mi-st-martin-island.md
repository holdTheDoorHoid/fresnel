St. Martin Island Light is a six-sided steel skeleton of a tower on a wooded island in northern Lake Michigan, finished in 1904 and first lit in 1905 to mark one of the four channels that lead ships into Green Bay. Its beam was once red and white in turn, and today it is best seen from a boat.[^1][^2]

## The place

Green Bay is nearly closed off from Lake Michigan by two peninsulas, Wisconsin's Door County and Michigan's Garden Peninsula, with a chain of islands between them. Ships entered the bay through four passages; St. Martin Island sits between two of them, with Rock Island Passage between it and Rock Island and St. Martin Passage between it and the Gull Islands.[^1] After a railway reached the iron mines from Escanaba (completed in 1864 by Pepper's account, 1865 by LighthouseFriends), traffic out of that port grew, and ore vessels bound for southern Lake Michigan used St. Martin Passage, passing between St. Martin and Little Gull Island.[^1][^2]

The Light-House Board wanted the passage lit. In the report volume covering 1891 and 1892 it argued that a passage between St. Martin and Little Gull Islands, if marked by lights and fog signals, would be "much safer than Poverty Island Passage, as the entrance is capacious and unobstructed." It asked for a fourth-order light and fog signal on the northwest end of St. Martin Island, for $15,000.[^3] Congress authorised the work in 1893 but gave no money.[^4]

## Building the light

The money came in 1898. The Board's report for that year's work says an appropriation was made by the act of 1 July 1898, and that the island was visited in September 1898 to select a site; the OCR of the report prints the sum as $16,000, where later documents say $15,000.[^5] By the next report a site of about 42 acres had been purchased, but plans and estimates showed that the appropriation would not stretch. All bids for the metal work were rejected, and the Board now put the total need at $29,000, asking for $14,000 more.[^6] The following year's report records that plans for a metal tower had been drawn and three bids received, "which were rejected," and that the work must wait for a further appropriation.[^7]

Terry Pepper's history of the station adds that the owners were unwilling to sell, that the matter went to the courts and that clear title was not had until 1901. He places the chosen site on the island's narrow southeast point, though the Board's 1891 recommendation had named the northwest end, and LighthouseFriends locates the station on the northeastern side.[^1][^2] Congress gave $10,000 more on 28 January 1902, and bids were called early in 1903.[^1] The two accounts of the first building season differ. LighthouseFriends says materials came from Milwaukee, work began in the spring of 1903, and by July the boathouse was finished and the dwelling's brickwork had reached the second-storey window sills.[^1] Pepper instead has the fog-signal boilers landing on 2 September and the tower ironwork on 5 September 1903, with construction beginning then.[^2]

The finished tower is hexagonal, with six steel buttresses that run its length and fan out at the base to a concrete foundation. It stands 75 feet (23 m) tall according to LighthouseFriends; Pepper gives 57 feet (17 m) from foundation to the top of the ventilator ball, and a focal plane of 84 feet (26 m).[^1][^2] Its fourth-order lens produced alternating red and white flashes.[^1] The station also had a brick keepers' dwelling, a brick fog-signal house, an oil house and a boathouse linked to the dwelling by a tramway of some 800 running feet.[^1] The fog signal was a ten-inch steam whistle sounding a three-second blast every thirty seconds.[^1]

The sources disagree on when the buildings were done: LighthouseFriends gives 25 June 1904, Pepper 15 June 1904.[^1][^2] Both agree there was no money to pay keepers. Custodians held the station until the opening of navigation in 1905, when the light and fog signal were started.[^1] Pepper counts the first season's fuel and noise: 13 cords of wood fed the boilers to run the whistles for 110 hours.[^2]

## The keepers

Bernhard Pizzala was the first head keeper, from 1904, with Christian E. Christensen as first assistant and Niels P. Jensen as second.[^8] LighthouseFriends, which spells him Pizzalar, says he had earlier been an assistant at Poverty Island.[^1] He was head keeper until 1917, when Nels Engberg took over; he stayed on as first assistant until 1920 and then returned as head keeper from 1920 to 1924.[^8] Engberg's career is a ladder on one island: second assistant from 1907, first assistant from 1915, head keeper from 1917 to 1920.[^8]

In 1912 the Lighthouse Service's *Bulletin* commended Jensen, by then first assistant, for saving six motor boats from destruction by fire.[^9] The printed column is jumbled by the OCR, and no date or place for the fire is given there.

David P. Kincaide served the longest of the early head keepers, from 1924 to 1943, after time as first and second assistant.[^8] The Bulletin's list of lighthouses awarded efficiency flags for 1925 names St. Martin Island and Kincaide as the twelfth district's winner.[^10] Louis S. Bauchan moved from second assistant (1937 to 1941) to the Coast Guard roll in 1941, and then head keeper from 1943 to 1947.[^8]

Wartime brought Herbert and Eva Fadel in 1943, Herbert as first assistant. Eva later recalled that the well had been condemned and the water problem "was never solved during our stay," so they drew water from the lake when it was clear enough and calm enough. When they returned in spring 1944, ice kept the Coast Guard vessel a quarter-mile offshore and they carried their groceries over the floes.[^1]

Ernest R. Lockhart was head keeper from 1947 to 1969 in the archive roster, though LighthouseFriends says he was transferred to the island in 1946. He got a thirty-year service pin from a Coast Guard admiral in 1966. When he retired in May 1969 the Wisconsin Legislature issued a certificate of commendation for thirty-four years of service.[^1][^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the lake storm of November 1913, a United Press dispatch from Menominee dated 12 November said the tug *Martin* had reported the barge *Plymouth* off St. Martin's Island with seven men aboard; the same page's casualty list has the *Plymouth* sunk with seven lost, so the dispatch leaves her fate unclear.[^11] Separately, the steamer *Cepheus* went ashore near the island and the lighthouse tender *Hyacinth* pulled her off. The *Bulletin* dates this to 10 October; a Washington newspaper of 26 November 1913 tied it to "the recent storm" on Lake Michigan without giving a date, so the two accounts may not describe the same day.[^12][^13] Neither source gives the station's keepers a part in it.

## After the keepers

The light grew stronger in 1916, when an incandescent oil-vapor lamp replaced the oil lamp.[^14] LighthouseFriends dates that to 17 April 1916 and the strength to 24,000 candlepower.[^1] A submarine telephone cable reached the island in 1926, a radiobeacon began at the opening of navigation in 1931, and in 1933 the steam whistle gave way to an air tyfon.[^1]

The archive roster lists Louis Bauchan as Coast Guard personnel from 1941, and from 1960 on every name listed is Coast Guard.[^8] The station was one of seven still staffed on Lake Michigan in 1979 and was automated in 1980.[^1] Pepper says the Fresnel lens went to the museum at Point Iroquois and a small acrylic lens took its place; the buildings were boarded up.[^2]

## Today

The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1984 as part of the Coast Guard lighthouses of the Great Lakes.[^15] The Nature Conservancy bought nearly all of the island in 2013 and 2014, and the island joined the Green Bay National Wildlife Refuge on 21 September 2015. The Little Traverse Bay Bands of Odawa Indians own the remaining 57 acres and the lighthouse.[^16]

This is not a visitor site. The island is part of a national wildlife refuge that protects bird habitat, and the lighthouse stands on tribal land.[^16] An older page by Pepper (last modified 2007) says visitors were discouraged to prevent vandalism, knew of no boat tours past the island, and suggested a view from a private boat offshore.[^2] Sources disagree on whether the light is still lit: LighthouseFriends says the original flash pattern is kept, while the U.S. Lighthouse Society archive record does not list it as an active aid and does not list the station as open to the public.[^1][^8]
