Isle au Haut Light stands on Robinson Point, at the southern end of the Isle au Haut Thoroughfare, in Knox County, Maine. Its light was established on 30 December 1907.[^1][^2] The National Register nomination calls it "the next to last complex of its type established in Maine."[^1]

## The place

In January 1906 the *Ellsworth American* reported the light the Lighthouse Board had recommended. It would stand at Robinson's point, "on the west side of the island," and "would be of no value to vessels entering Bluehill bay." "The light would be primarily for the benefit of fishermen," the paper said.[^3] LighthouseFriends quotes the Board's 1902 report. It says fishermen claim the water seaward of Saddleback Ledge, for about ten miles, to be "exceedingly good fishing grounds," frequented by "fishing vessels ranging in size from 10 to 100 tons burden," and it calls Isle au Haut Harbor "the best harbor convenient to these fishing grounds."[^4]

The need was older than the Board's report. In 1837 a petition to Congress asked for a light, saying that "many vessels have been wrecked on this Island and numerous lives have been lost."[^4] The Friends of the lighthouse say the movement went nowhere, but that in 1855 the Lighthouse Board recognized that there "was a need or a lighthouse on IAH."[^5] LighthouseFriends adds that the 1855 study led to a decision to put the light on Spoon Island instead.[^4] In 1906 a two-acre plot was bought from Charles Robinson, at the place that became known as Robinson Point.[^5]

## Building the light

Congress did not act until 1906. The nomination says Congress "did not authorize construction until June 23, 1906, when it appropriated $14,000 for the station."[^1] The Friends give the sum as $14,400, "to erect a fog signal station on IAH."[^5] LighthouseFriends says the Board had insisted the station could not be built for $14,000 and asked for another $400, which never came. A 1907 record, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says that "the entire amount of the appropriation was exhausted in the construction of the station."[^4]

The station was finished in 1907 and, the Friends say, formally inaugurated the following January.[^5] According to the nomination, the tower has a conical granite base, 24 feet (7.3 m) high, that supports a cylindrical brick shaft 16 feet (4.9 m) tall.[^1] The Society's record gives its height from base to lantern as 40 feet (12.2 m), and its focal height as 48 feet (14.6 m).[^6] The keeper's dwelling, the nomination says, is "the original 1907 dwelling," and the oil house and boathouse date from 1907 too.[^1][^6]

The original lens was a fourth-order Fresnel lens, according to the Society's record.[^6] LighthouseFriends names its maker as Henry-Lepaute.[^4] A notice in the *Ellsworth American* of 15 January 1908 says that a light of the fourth order was "established in the tower recently completed at Robinson point." The light shows "fixed white between NE. E. and ESE. and fixed red over the remainder of the arc," and the notice gives its range in clear weather as twelve and one-third miles.[^2] The fog bell followed a year later. LighthouseFriends says a "Bell struck by machinery [was] established" in 1908.[^4]

## The keepers

Elmer F. Holbrook was the first keeper, from 1907 to 1922, according to the Society's roster.[^6] LighthouseFriends gives his full name as Francis Elmer Holbrook and says he started out as a third assistant keeper on Matinicus Rock in 1898, before moving up and transferring to Isle au Haut in 1907.[^4] The Friends say that on Christmas Eve 1907 the keeper's twelve-year-old daughter, Esther, helped him light the lantern for the first time.[^5] LighthouseFriends, passing on local tradition, gives her age as eight and says the lighting was on Christmas Eve, though another source gives New Year's Day 1908.[^4] The notice in the *Ellsworth American* gives 30 December as the date the light was established.[^2]

By April 1922 the *Ellsworth American* reported that "Capt Holbrook is going to the hospital for surgical treatment," and called him "keeper of the light station at Isle au Haut."[^8] LighthouseFriends says he took a one-year leave in June 1922 and resigned a year later.[^4]

Harry Smith succeeded him, from 1922 to 1933.[^6] LighthouseFriends says he had been an assistant keeper at Rockland Breakwater, Egg Rock and Petit Manan, then head keeper at Boon Island from 1916, then in charge at Petit Manan before his transfer here in 1922. He remained until the light was automated on 29 June 1933, and then spent the rest of his career at Two Bush Island.[^4] The Society's roster lists only these two principal keepers.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A federal table of vessels aided in the fiscal year 1916 includes a line for "E. T. Holbrook, keeper, Isle au Haut Light Station, Me.," among the keepers who gave aid.[^7] The initials differ from the roster's, and the report does not say whether this is the same man. The line is read from a table in scanned text, so the match to the keeper is uncertain.

## After the keepers

The nomination says the light was automated in 1934, and the buildings, except the tower, were sold.[^1] The Society's record gives 1934 as the year of automation.[^6] LighthouseFriends dates it to 29 June 1933, and says the characteristic changed to flashing red and the fog bell was discontinued.[^4] The Friends say the tower was closed off with barriers and its fog bell taken down.[^5]

The keeper's house did not sell in the Depression. The Friends say there were no takers, and in 1936 Senator Margaret Chase Smith negotiated a sale of the keeper's house back to Charles Robinson, who had sold the land for the light.[^5] LighthouseFriends places Robinson's purchase after the August 1934 newspaper announcements that Maine lighthouses were being sold, and says he bought back the keeper's house and land with the senator's help.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the house then stayed in the Robinson family for three generations, over fifty years.[^4]

The Friends say the original Fresnel lens was replaced by a plastic one in 1955, and that a Coast Guard officer rescued the original and placed it in a Rockland museum.[^5] LighthouseFriends says a fifth-order lens was installed after automation, and that the lens on display at the Maine Lighthouse Museum in Rockland is that later lens. The two accounts do not agree on which lens that is.[^4]

In 1986 Jeff and Judi Burke bought the keeper's house and made it an inn.[^5] In 1987 the Coast Guard gave the Burkes a license to remove the barricades from the tower.[^5] In 1988 the tower and the house were registered as one unit, although they had separate owners.[^5] By 1993 a bulge had appeared on the northeast side of the upper brickwork of the tower.[^5] In 1998 the town voted 40–1 to accept the title to the light, and in 1999 a restoration contractor replaced rotten brick on the tower's northeast side.[^5][^4]

In 2012 Marshall Chapman bought the property. He reopened the inn in 2013, and closed it after the 2018 season. It was sold in 2023 for $1.3 million, LighthouseFriends says.[^4] The Friends say the keeper's house is now a private home.[^5] The Friends of the Isle au Haut Lighthouse was formed in 2016. Restoration work came in 2021, gallery repairs in 2024, and the access bridge was rebuilt in 2025.[^5]

## Today

The tower is owned by the Town of Isle au Haut, and the Society's record says the station is not open to the public.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the dwelling and tower closed.[^4] Visitors can reach the lighthouse by ferry from Stonington to Isle au Haut and then walk.[^4] Half of the island is in Acadia National Park.[^4]

The light is still an active aid to navigation, and its current optic is a 250 mm solar-powered unit, the Society's record says.[^6]
