The Port Mouton light stands on the northeast point of Spectacle Island, one of two small islands that are linked at low water, off the harbour of Port Mouton on Nova Scotia's South Shore.[^1][^4] A light has been shown there since 1 September 1873. The square wooden tower built in 1937 is the second on the site, and since late 2015 a local society has owned and cared for it.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

LighthouseFriends describes Port Mouton as a wide, moderately deep bay between Mouton Head and White Point. Mouton Island lies offshore and acts as a sort of breakwater for the harbour.[^1] The Spectacle Islands are named for their shape seen from above, which resembles a pair of spectacles.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the village took its name from a sheep (mouton is French for sheep) that leapt overboard and drowned there, excited by the sight of land, after Pierre Dugua, Sieur de Monts, and Samuel de Champlain landed on 13 May 1604.[^1]

The register of historic places explains why a light was wanted. It says the islands were "known to provide shelter to ships in foul weather," so that keeping a light on them was "vital."[^2] Since 1873 the light has served the local fishery, the register adds.[^2]

## Building the light

The Department of Marine's report for 1873, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, describes the new light as one that "shews a fixed red light, elevated forty-seven feet above high water." It stood on a square wooden tower, 6 m (20 ft) high, "erected by Mr. G. S. Parker for the contract price of $665."[^1] The light was put in operation on 1 September 1873, "under the temporary charge of Mr. Patrick Burgess, who receives $2 per day."[^1] Robert J. Smith was appointed the first permanent keeper on 11 November 1873, at $300 a year.[^1]

"The total cost of the lighthouse came to $1,375.25," including the catoptric illuminating apparatus.[^1] An iron floor and zinc lining protected the wooden, octagonal lantern room from the oil-burning lamps and reflectors.[^1] The keeper's dwelling stood a short way from the tower, "near to, but detached from, the light tower."[^1]

## The keepers

In 1877 a report says Smith had built an addition to the dwelling "at his own expense," because he had "only one room and one bed-room for a family of six."[^1] The report calls the addition "much required," though the keeper "had no right to build it without orders."[^1]

Smith resigned in 1890, and Edward McPherson replaced him.[^1] McPherson drowned on 5 May 1892, aged forty-six.[^1] His wife, Anna McPherson, took over the keeping and served until 1898, when J. Oscar Campbell took charge of the light.[^1] LighthouseFriends' keeper list gives "Mrs. E. McPherson" for those years, so the sources differ on her name.[^1]

After Campbell came a run of keepers named Cook: A.H. Cook (1912 to 1919), Harold J. Cook (1919 to 1926) and W.M. Cook (1926 to 1929). James O. Inness is listed from 1929 to at least 1937.[^1] The list has no names between Inness and Harvey L. Huskins, the final keeper, who served from 1954 to 1961.[^1] In an interview with Chris Mills, quoted by LighthouseFriends, Huskins recalled a foggy night when he answered a horn he took for a boat ashore. It was a train on the railway that ran across Summerville Beach. "I must have blew that horn three or four hours," he said.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 16 June 1882 lightning struck the lighthouse at 2 p.m., shattering the wooden lantern room and splitting the tower's south wall from top to bottom.[^1] Smith put up a temporary light until a new lantern room could be sent from Halifax. LighthouseFriends says it was fortunate that the oil, which was probably stored in the tower, did not catch fire.[^1]

Heavy seas carried away the wharf at the landing in 1891, and a new wharf with a slip and a boathouse was built at a more sheltered spot.[^1] The Department's report on life-saving stations lists a separate station at Port Mouton: "Port Mouton station. — Established 1889," with a coxswain paid $75 a year and "No. of crew, 7."[^5]

In 1906 the keeper was given a hand-operated foghorn, which he was to sound in response to vessels' signals during thick weather.[^1] NSLPS's list dates a hand fog horn at this light to 1930, so the two sources differ.[^3]

The Region of Queens municipality lists wrecks around Spectacle Island. Among them is the Clifford J. White, which the municipality says ran aground and sank off Spectacle Island Ledge in 1916 on a voyage from New York to Yarmouth. It also names the Ethel Emerson, which struck the island in 1884.[^6] These dates come from the municipality and are not confirmed against a primary record.

## After the keepers

In 1915 a fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed, and the light was changed from fixed red to fixed white.[^1] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society's list dates a "4th order dioptric lens, fixed white" to 1922.[^3] The two dates disagree, and this story does not choose between them. A fire destroyed the keeper's dwelling, and a new one was built in 1922.[^1]

The present tower was built in 1937 "to take over the duties of the first lighthouse on the site."[^2] LighthouseFriends gives its height as 8.5 m (28 ft), about 2.4 m (8 ft) taller than the original.[^1] Huskins was the final keeper on LighthouseFriends' list, which ends with him in 1961.[^1] NSLPS dates automation to "by 1993."[^3]

By the 1990s the tower was showing signs of deterioration.[^1] In 2004 residents learned that the Coast Guard might demolish it, and they rallied to save it. It was repaired extensively in 2005.[^1] Lead paint had contaminated the soil around the lighthouse. On 9 December 2011 a helicopter slung bags of contaminated soil off the island and brought back clean soil.[^1] The tower was declared a heritage lighthouse on 2 February 2015.[^1] The Spectacle Light Society gained ownership in late 2015, along with a $43,000 federal grant to care for the light. In 2016 the society installed a floating dock to improve access.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard list gives the light as long-flashing white, every twelve seconds ("LFl W 12s"), with "Flash 2 s; eclipse 10 s," from the northeast point of Spectacle Island.[^4] The Region of Queens calls it "this unmanned light," still operational as a navigational aid, and says it can be seen from Route 3 "between Summerville Beach and Hunts Point."[^6] The municipality places the light on the northwestern point of the island, where the Coast Guard list and NSLPS have the northeastern point.[^6][^3][^4]

The Nova Scotia list gives access as "By Boat" and directs readers to the Spectacle Light Society on Facebook for more information.[^3] LighthouseFriends' visitor note says the Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and that the grounds are open with the tower closed.[^1] That conflicts with LighthouseFriends' own history, which records the society's ownership from late 2015.[^1]
