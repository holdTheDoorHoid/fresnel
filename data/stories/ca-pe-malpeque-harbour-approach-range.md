The Malpeque Harbour Approach Range Lights marked the entrance to Malpeque Bay, on Prince Edward Island's north coast. They stood on Fish Island, the eastern end of Hog Island. LighthouseFriends also calls the island Bill Hook Island.[^1][^3] A light stood on the island from 1853. The metal rear tower of 1961 was discontinued in 2011 and was no longer on the island by 2020. The old wooden front tower was cut in two, moved to Cabot Beach Provincial Park and restored there.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Malpeque Bay is a large lagoon, separated from the Gulf of St. Lawrence by the barrier beaches of Hog Island.[^3] The Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society describes the harbour as the most important harbour of refuge on the Island's north coast, and says vessels needed a light to bring them safely through the shifting sand bars.[^2] A gale in 1851, which the sources call the Yankee Gale, struck the north coast and wrecked much of the New England fishing fleet working offshore.[^1][^2] The Society gives the loss as 160 lives and more than 80 vessels.[^2]

A few months later, Henry Palmer, the United States consular agent for the Island, wrote to the Lieutenant Governor that American shipowners would be willing to pay toward lights at Malpeque and Tracadie harbours.[^1] The colony charged American fishing vessels an anchorage duty that was said to be for the support of its lights.[^1] The Americans objected that their own payments should be enough to light the whole northern coast.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1853 a letter from Captain Bayfield of the Royal Navy, presented to the House of Assembly, recommended Bill Hook Island at the entrance to Richmond (Malpeque) Bay as the best site for two lights. James H. Beckwith was paid £37 under contract to put up the lighthouse there.[^1] Benjamin Thomson was paid £36 a year to keep the light from 16 June 1853. LighthouseFriends thinks it was lit early that summer.[^1]

Two years later, a petition to the House of Assembly complained of "the failure of the Light placed on Fish Island to answer the ends designed in its establishment." The petitioners also said it deceived mariners.[^1] In 1857 £100 was appropriated for a new light. Hired labour built it, and John Smallwood was the main workman, under Silas Barnard, the colony's Supervisor of Public Works. Barnard set the light going in August that year.[^1]

In 1874, a year after Prince Edward Island joined Confederation, the General Superintendent of Lighthouses found the light "a slight wooden structure not worthy of repair." He said a new light, dwelling and oil store were needed, and a beacon range light.[^1] Thomas Fahey of Alberton took the contract, for $1,987, in 1875. The new light and range went into service at the opening of navigation in 1877.[^1][^2] The lighthouse was a white square wooden building 14 m (46 ft) high, attached to a dwelling of a storey and a half. Its range light, an open-frame tower, stood 366 m (1,200 ft) away.[^1]

The range moved as the channel did. In 1887 it was shifted 6 m (20 ft) south to clear a bank on the north side of the channel. In 1894 it was moved again, to clear a sand spit off Darnley Point.[^1] In 1895 a red light on a mast marked the channel into Darnley Basin. It stayed until 1897, when the Darnley Basin range lights were established on the mainland.[^1]

The Department of Marine's annual reports show routine work in the years after. An oil shed was put up at the station in the year to June 1906, for $175.[^4] Repairs to the Fish Island tower followed in the year to June 1907.[^5] In the year to June 1909 the main light got a 35 mm Diamond vapour installation on an adjustable brass stand, in place of its old duplex lamp.[^6] The Department's 1912 list of stations on the Island includes the Fish island range.[^7]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends and the Lighthouse Society give nearly the same list of keepers, and the Department's 1910 table confirms one of the appointments.[^1][^2][^8]

Benjamin Thomson was the first keeper, from 1853 to 1857. William H. McKay replaced him a few months after the 1857 light was set going, and kept the light until 1859.[^1] John Craig followed. In 1861 he submitted a report listing fishing vessels whose captains had refused to pay the light dues that were used to support the colony's lights.[^1] Stephen McLellan, John Champion and Archibald J. McLellan kept the light in turn from 1867 to 1873.[^1]

Angus J. McLellan kept the light from 1873 to 1895. The Department's agent for the Island, William Mitchell, visited in 1878. In his annual report, Mitchell wrote that "Mr. Angus J. McLellan is keeper, who is neither energetic nor careful," and that the lights were not satisfactory.[^1]

Matilda McLellan is listed as keeper from 1895 to 1897.[^1][^2] She is the only woman named as keeper of this light in the sources read. William Sinclair kept it from 1897 to 1906.[^1][^2]

Patrick Gould's appointment is dated 7 December 1906 in the Department's table of light-keepers for 1910.[^8] LighthouseFriends and the Society both give him as keeper from 1906 to 1910.[^1][^2] After him came J. A. L. McLellan, from 1910 to 1913, and Peter Hickey, from 1913 to 1924.[^1][^2] J. Chappell followed from 1924 to 1950, and George Simmonds from 1950 to 1959, the last keeper on the list.[^1]

LighthouseFriends notes that the lights needed the constant attention of a dedicated keeper to be effective.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1961 a metal skeletal tower with a wooden upper portion went up as the rear range light. It was 15.2 m (50 ft) high.[^1][^3] In 1965 the front range was still an enclosed square wooden tower.[^1]

Shore erosion threatened the old front tower in the late 1980s. The Department of Transport considered burning it, but locals stepped in.[^1] The president of the Malpeque Historical Society and a local fisherman sawed the tower in two. In 1989 a Labrador helicopter from the search and rescue crew at CFB Summerside flew the two sections to Cabot Park, where the tower was reassembled.[^1][^3] Rowlett adds that the keeper's house was demolished.[^3] The Society says the Coast Guard had planned to demolish the tower after the erosion.[^2]

In the late 1990s the restored tower was part of the set of the CBC series *Emily of New Moon*, which was filmed in the park.[^1][^3]

The Coast Guard discontinued the metal rear tower in 2011.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends reports that by 2020 the tower was no longer on the island.[^1]

## Today

The old wooden front tower stands at Cabot Beach Provincial Park, off Route 20 north-west of Malpeque. The Malpeque Historical Society owns it, and the park manages the site. The site is open and the tower is closed.[^2][^3]

The Coast Guard's list of lights for Prince Edward Island, updated on 25 August 2026, still carries the Malpeque buoys. It lists no range light at the harbour entrance.[^9] The rear tower is gone from the island, and the front tower is kept as a historic light.[^1][^3]
