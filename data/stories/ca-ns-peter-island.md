Peter Island is a small island at the south entrance to the Grand Passage, the channel between Brier Island and Long Island, which run south from Digby Neck in Nova Scotia's Bay of Fundy.[^1][^2] A light has marked it since 1850. The octagonal wooden tower that replaced the first light in 1909 was decommissioned in 2014, and the light shown from the island today is a long green flash on a square skeleton tower that the Coast Guard set up in 2016.[^1][^2][^4]

## The place

The register of historic places says the area earned the reputation of the "Graveyard of the Fundy" through its "dangerous tidal currents and dense fog," and that the first light was lit "to provide safe navigation in and around the southern entrance to the Grand Passage."[^2] The 1885 *Sailing Directions* for the South-East Coast of Nova Scotia and Bay of Fundy, as LighthouseFriends quotes them, put the main difficulty of the passage in "the great velocity of the tidal streams through the channel."[^1]

The petitioners were at Westport, the village on Brier Island. LighthouseFriends reports that in 1843 Elisha Payson and other residents began asking the government of Nova Scotia for a light at the south entrance, and that a petition of 1846 recommended Peter Island, a small island just off Westport.[^1] In 1847 a committee of the House of Assembly proposed that New Brunswick be asked to contribute. The New Brunswick commissioners refused, saying their province had "done more to benefit navigation in Nova Scotia" than the reverse.[^1]

## Building the light

The Nova Scotia government allocated money in 1850 for five new lighthouses, and one of them was to mark the south entrance to Grand Passage.[^1] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society and the register both give 1850 as the year the light was first lit.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends dates the first keeper's hiring to 1851. John D. Suthern's salary was sixty pounds a year, with a fuel allowance of fifteen pounds.[^1]

The first light was shown from the windows of a small square house that served as both lighthouse and dwelling.[^1] An 1857 inspection by William Condon, Superintendent of the Board of Works, described "Two fixed horizontal lights in the windows of the eastern and western roofs," with two catoptric lamps in each window.[^1] Condon also found the dwelling too small for the family: "The keeper here is very much cramped for room for his family." The keeper offered to add a kitchen "if the cost of materials would be allowed him." LighthouseFriends says Suthern completed the addition the following year.[^1]

The 1885 *Sailing Directions* describe the building as "a square white building 15 feet high," with "two fixed white lights, placed horizontally, 24 feet apart," which in clear weather should be visible from a distance of 10 miles.[^1] The Department of Marine and Fisheries' report for the year ended 30 June 1901 says the two lanterns "have been reglazed with large plate glass, to remove interference with the light by sash bars."[^6]

According to LighthouseFriends, W. Brooks of Digby built the present wooden, octagonal tower in 1909, for $1,350.[^1] The Department's annual report, in its list of changes and improvements to existing aids, records the "Erection of a new 44-foot" octagonal wooden tower with an octagonal iron lantern.[^5] LighthouseFriends says a fifth-order Chance lens was mounted in the lantern room, and that the light burned petroleum vapour under an incandescent mantle.[^1] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society's list gives a different lens history: a fifth-order dioptric lens with a fixed white light from 1922 to 1941, and a fourth-order lens with a flashing yellow light from 1992.[^3] The sources do not reconcile the two.

## The keepers

John D. Suthern died in 1875, and his son Rupert took over as keeper.[^1] LighthouseFriends' list of head keepers carries on from there: Rupert Suthern to 1890, then Edward W. Suthern (1890 to 1929), Percy G. Welch from 1929 (the list gives "at least 1963"), Richard Thompson, and Pete Welch from 1972.[^1] Two men named Welch appear in that list, and the sources do not say whether they were related.[^1]

Pete Welch was the last keeper, from 1972 until the station was de-staffed in 1984.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Department's annual report, dated Ottawa, December 1900, lists a reef marker at "Peters Island Reef": "An iron spindle to mark the end of the reef," running north-east from Peters Island in Grand Passage, Digby County. It "was erected August 24, 1900," and stood 10 m (33 ft) from base to top.[^7]

A later report, printed in the 1905 Sessional Paper, from the Chief Engineer, says the spindle "has been bent down by floating ice," so that it "is not visible after the tide has made for an hour." It "will be repaired as soon as ice has ceased to run."[^8]

## After the keepers

The station was de-staffed in 1984, and the Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society lists the light as automated by the same year.[^1][^3] A mariner reported on 11 December 2014 that the light was not functioning.[^1] The Coast Guard set up two temporary green lights, because mould kept maintenance crews from reaching the lighthouse.[^1]

In September 2016 the Coast Guard poured a concrete foundation on the island and mounted a square skeleton tower on it.[^1][^4] The register puts it this way: the lighthouse was "decommissioned in 2014 and replaced by a skeletal tower displaying a flashing green light."[^2] The register gives the recognition date as 14 June 2021, under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act. LighthouseFriends gives December 2021 for the same designation, and this story follows the register.[^1][^2]

A group called SAIL², formed in 2015 to save both the Brier Island and Peter Island lighthouses, has raised money through auctions, bake sales and calendar sales.[^1]

In 2025 volunteers began a first phase of restoration, arriving from 30 August.[^1][^9] The project lead, John Schwinghamer, owner of Hammer and Sky Studios on Brier Island, said: "We had all the materials beforehand."[^9] Over three weeks the volunteers, drawn from six provinces, replaced decayed beams and posts, patched the outside, built a new deck around the dome, and painted the dome and door fire-engine red.[^9]

## Today

The register describes the island as "small, uninhabited" in one passage and, in another, as the site of "rough, open terrain of an inhabited island." It also says the island itself has been designated a bird sanctuary.[^2]

The island is reached by boat.[^3] LighthouseFriends' visitor note says the grounds are open and the tower closed. It points to a view of the light from South Point on Brier Island, at the end of the road from the Westport ferry dock.[^1] The Coast Guard list gives the light as a long green flash every six seconds ("LFl G 6s"), with "Flash 2 s; eclipse 4 s," from a square skeleton tower.[^4]

Sources disagree on who owns the lighthouse. The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society's list gives the Municipality of the District of Digby as owner.[^3] LighthouseFriends' visitor note names the Canadian Coast Guard as owner, but its history says the municipality has been working with the federal government on divestiture, a process that takes several years.[^1] A 2025 SaltWire report says the municipality took ownership of the lighthouse in 2023.[^9]
