New Point Comfort Light is a sandstone tower, 58 feet (17.7 m) tall, on a small island at the entrance to Mobjack Bay, on the western shore of Chesapeake Bay in Mathews County, Virginia.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends and the Mathews County Historical Society both give 17 January 1805 as the date it was first lit.[^1][^5] The Historical Society calls it the "tenth oldest American lighthouse still standing" in the country.[^5]

## The place

LighthouseFriends says the Chesapeake Bay carried more shipping than any other waters in North America during the 1700s and early 1800s.[^1] The state register says that "because of the threat to navigation caused by the point and its shoals," Congress appropriated funds in 1801 for a permanent lighthouse.[^2]

The island was low and broken.[^1] The surveyor John Patterson, whose report LighthouseFriends prints, described it as covered with drifted sand hills, and said it held about 100 acres.[^1] One Phillip Tabb owned the land and asked a high price for the few acres the government needed.[^1] Elzy Burroughs bought the whole island from Tabb and sold the two acres to the government.[^1] The Mathews County Historical Society says the island was originally more than 200 acres and that erosion has reduced it to less than a quarter of an acre.[^5] The 1973 nomination describes the island as about one-third of an acre, and says that when the light was built the point was still a peninsula joined to the mainland.[^3] LighthouseFriends prints a survey that describes an island separated from the mainland by a creek.[^1] The sources do not agree on the shape of the land, and this story does not choose between them.

## Building the light

The Virginia landmark nomination gives the act of Congress as 3 March 1801.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the appropriation was $5,000, and that the two builders invited to submit proposals would not take the job for that sum.[^1] Burroughs, who had built the towers at Smith Point and Old Point Comfort, was rewarded the contract after the commissioner, William Miller, negotiated an extra $3,500.[^1] The nomination gives the construction agreement as $8,750, which is not the sum of the $5,000 and $3,500 that LighthouseFriends reports.[^1][^3]

The contract called for an octagonal tower of hewn stone, with a brick dwelling and kitchen.[^1] The Historical Society says Burroughs built it of sandstone from the Aquia quarry, the same stone used for government buildings in Washington.[^5] LighthouseFriends says Burroughs completed the tower between March and November 1804.[^1] The dwelling was delayed when the subcontractor's workers ruined the bricks in firing them.[^1] To appease the government overseer, Burroughs volunteered to keep the light himself until the dwelling was finished.[^1]

The state register says the lighthouse "was completed in 1805."[^2] The nomination says the light "was completed by 1805" and was first put into use in 1806, the year Burroughs received his final payment.[^3] LighthouseFriends says Burroughs lit it for the first time on 17 January 1805, with oil and wicks, and the Historical Society gives the same date.[^1][^5] The original light used nine lamps with nine-inch reflectors.[^1]

Winslow Lewis revamped the lantern and added fourteen-inch reflectors in 1841.[^1] A Fresnel lens replaced the whole lighting apparatus in 1855.[^1] The Historical Society also dates the refitting with a Fresnel lens to 1855.[^1][^5]

## The keepers

Burroughs was the first keeper.[^5] The Historical Society says President Jefferson appointed him to that post, and the archive's roster begins with his stint from 1804 to 1814.[^5][^4] Robert Lithburn kept the light from 1814 to 1815, and James B.H. Johnson from 1815 to 1819.[^4]

Wesley F. Ripley kept the light twice, from 1881 to 1883 and again from 1906 to 1916.[^4] Oliver R. Hudgins served from 1888 to 1899.[^4] Edward A. Sibley, the last head keeper on the roster, served from 1916 to 1919.[^4]

James McHenry Farley was keeper from 1871 to 1873.[^4] The Historical Society describes him as a preacher, and as one of the few officially appointed African-American keepers known.[^5] LighthouseFriends prints a petition in which Farley asked the Lighthouse Board for an assistant, or for rations.[^1] He wrote that on many nights he stayed for hours in the lantern tower, keeping frost from the glass.[^1]

When the light was converted to acetylene in 1919, the keeper's post was done away with.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the job became an occasional lamplighter's task.[^1] The roster lists Henry L. Dow from 1919 to 1954 under the heading "Light Attendant."[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the War of 1812 the British occupied the point.[^5] The Historical Society says they held it for several weeks.[^5] LighthouseFriends says they burned the keeper's house and oil vault, shattered the glass in the lantern, and took out the window frames and the door.[^5][^1]

A Mathews County man wrote to the Norfolk customs collector after the British left.[^1] He warned that the water was already eating away at the base of the tower and would undermine it.[^1] In 1839 the keeper was given a boat, because he could no longer walk to the mainland at low tide.[^1]

Confederate soldiers extinguished the light in 1861.[^5][^1] After a general overhaul that included a new lens, the station was back in service by September 1865.[^1] In 1933 a hurricane and its tidal flooding cut a channel between the lighthouse and the rest of the island.[^1]

## After the keepers

Repairs continued through the Board's years.[^6] Its report for 1894 lists New Point Comfort, Va., among the stations where repairs were made that year.[^6] Its report for 1896 to 1897 says that "In August the station was thoroughly repaired."[^7]

In 1919 the light was converted to acetylene.[^1][^5] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1918 to 1923 lists a "change in illuminant" at New Point Comfort.[^8] A 1919 list of changes gives the station a change dated 15 September 1919.[^9]

The sources disagree on when the light was electrified.[^1][^5] LighthouseFriends dates the conversion to 1950.[^1] The Historical Society gives 1960.[^1][^5]

The tower was in use until 1963.[^2] The state register says a new light on piers to the south then took over.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the old light was replaced by a lighted buoy.[^1] The state register gives the date of its listing on the Virginia register as "06/20/1972," and of its listing on the National Register as "03/01/1973."[^2]

The Coast Guard transferred the lighthouse to Mathews County.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the deed to 1976, and the Historical Society to 1975.[^1][^5] The light was rededicated in August 1981 after an $80,000 restoration.[^1] In 2001 a preservation task force was set up.[^1] In 2010 the county received a $424,000 state transportation enhancement grant for a rock wall to protect the exposed tower.[^1] A new pier went in during 2016, and restoration began in 2020.[^1] The project cost $1.8 million, and the restoration concluded on 12 October 2021, when a ceremony was held to relight the lighthouse.[^1]

## Today

The county owns the lighthouse, and LighthouseFriends says the tower is closed.[^1] The light is visible from the boardwalk at the end of the Nature Conservancy's New Point Comfort Preserve.[^1] For a closer look, LighthouseFriends says, a boat is needed.[^1]

The state register says that changes in currents have separated the lighthouse from the mainland, "leaving it on a tiny island."[^2] LighthouseFriends describes the tower as standing on about a hundred yards of rocky rubble, with water all around.[^1] The Historical Society says the preservation task force is working with the Army Corps of Engineers.[^5] The sources do not describe public access to the island itself.
