The Cleveland East Entrance light stands at the outer end of the east breakwater that protects Cleveland's harbor on Lake Erie.[^1] Its 47-foot (14 m) steel tower shows a red light that flashes three seconds on and three seconds off.[^2] The light can be reached only by boat.[^1] The Commissioner's report and LighthouseFriends date its first light to 1915, the year the breakwater extension was completed; the Society's record gives 1918.[^4][^1][^8]

## The place

The city built breakwaters on both sides of the Cuyahoga River's entrance to protect the inner harbor and to make a larger outer harbor on the lake.[^1] The west breakwater was built between 1876 and 1883.[^1] Work on the east breakwater began in 1888 and left a 500-foot (152 m) gap for the harbor entrance.[^1] A river and harbor act of June 13, 1902 provided money to extend the east breakwater three miles (4.8 km) east to Gordon Park. By 1910, 10,200 feet (3.1 km) of the breakwater had been completed, the shore arm was removed by 1914, and the extension was finished in 1915.[^1] The Ohio Sea Grant guide describes the light as guarding the east entrance to the harbor.[^2]

A different light, the East Breakwater pierhead light, marks the end of the breakwater on the east side of the Cuyahoga entrance. It was placed in commission at the opening of navigation in 1911.[^3]

## Building the light

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1915 lists the "Cleveland East Breakwater East End Light, Ohio" among the construction completed that year.[^4] LighthouseFriends describes it as a group-flashing white light, shown from a skeletal steel tower about 40 feet (12 m) above the water.[^1] LighthouseFriends also gives the 1915 plan: the light and its fog signal were to be controlled from shore by an electric cable from the power station directly opposite.[^1] None of the cited sources says whether that plan was carried out.[^1][^2]

The electric change came in 1916. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for June 1916 lists "Cleveland East Entrance, Ohio, acetylene to electric incandescent" among the lights whose illuminant was changed.[^5] LighthouseFriends says that from May 1, 1916 the light was an occulting white electric light, lit for eight seconds and dark for two in each cycle.[^1] The Commissioner's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1916 lists a new fog signal at "Cleveland East Entrance, Ohio" among those established that year.[^6] LighthouseFriends says an electric siren was established at the light on May 15, 1916.[^1]

In 1918 a new steel, skeletal tower with an enclosure at its top went up at the east entrance, and an air diaphone was installed in the enclosure.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for September 3, 1918 reports that the fog signal at "Cleveland East Entrance, Ohio" had changed "from electric siren to an air diaphone."[^7] LighthouseFriends dates the diaphone's first operation to July 24, 1918, keeping the siren's four-second blast every twenty seconds, and gives $6,262 as the cost of the tower and diaphone together.[^1] The following year a new armored submarine cable was laid, at a cost LighthouseFriends gives as $6,473.[^1]

The sources disagree on when the station was established. The U.S. Lighthouse Society's place record gives 1918 as the year established, calls the station a minor aid to navigation, and marks it as not existing.[^8] The 1915 date comes from the Commissioner's report, from LighthouseFriends, and from the Ohio Sea Grant guide, which says the original structure was a skeletal lighthouse built in 1915.[^4][^1][^2] The year 1918 is when the new tower and diaphone went up.[^1] The Society's record does not say why it uses 1918.[^8] The Ohio Sea Grant guide also describes the present 47-foot steel tower as still an active navigational tool, which conflicts with the record's "Existing: No."[^2][^8]

## The keepers

The Society's roster for the place lists no people.[^9] None of the sources cited here names a keeper for this light, so this story has no keepers to name.

## Today

The present tower is a 47-foot (14 m) cylindrical steel tower, white with a red band, and it shows the red light described above.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says a modern D9 steel tower, like those on other Great Lakes pierhead lights, replaced the earlier light after several decades. It gives no date for the change.[^1]

The Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and the tower is closed.[^1] Because the breakwater is not attached to the shore, the light is accessible only by boat.[^1] LighthouseFriends directs visitors to Cleveland Lakefront State Park, where the light can be seen at the end of the offshore breakwater.[^1] The Ohio Sea Grant guide suggests viewing it from Cleveland Lakefront State Park's Gordon Park or the E. 55th Street Marina, and says it can only be seen from a distance.[^2]
