Chicago Harbor Light is a white cast-iron tower between two red-roofed buildings at the south end of the north breakwater, a short way east of Navy Pier. It was first lit in 1893 on a site just north of where it stands now and was taken apart and rebuilt on its present spot in 1918.[^2] The city's landmark listing calls it the only surviving lighthouse in Chicago.[^20]

## The place

Chicago's harbour was a problem before it was a port. The city's landmark report says a sand bar blocked large vessels from the Chicago River in the early 1830s, so schooners and large ships anchored off shore, "often as far as a mile away", and ferried cargo and passengers in by small boat.[^1] The report says the first lighthouse, built on the river's south bank in 1831, collapsed, and that a forty-foot (12 m) replacement went up on the same spot the following year.[^1] In 1859 a metal-skeleton tower was built on the North Pier, but by 1870, the report says, smoke from the harbour's industry and steamers had so thickened the air that this tower and its light were obscured.[^1] The report says a leading light was built in 1874 at Grosse Point, in Evanston, as further help to ships.[^1]

The light that stands today was planned for the outer breakwater. The Light-House Board's report for fiscal 1891 says the appropriation act required the new light to stand "on an independent crib site in 23 feet of water near the end of the outer breakwater, and 2 miles from shore", with a fog signal also.[^3]

## Building the light

Congress first voted $36,000 on 2 March 1889 and added $15,000 on 30 August 1890, making $51,000 in all.[^3] The foundation superstructure was put out to contract on 17 September 1891. Progress was slow while the Board waited for the steel casing that joined the timber crib to the stone pier; the work stopped at the end of November and resumed in May 1892.[^4] The stone foundation pier was finished on 2 September 1892. The ironwork for the tower had already been delivered to Chicago, but the Board bought it and held it until Congress voted another $15,500, which it did on 3 March 1893.[^5]

Erection of the tower began that March and was complete on 1 September 1893.[^6] The Board's report describes a tower fitted with a hot-water heater and radiators throughout, two cisterns in the basement, and two fog-signal houses on the pier, one on each side of the tower. Each house measured 11 by 24 feet (3.4 by 7.3 m), and the station had duplicate 10-inch steam whistles with their own engines and boilers.[^6] The report says the light was shown for the first time on the night of 9 November 1893, and the fog signal started the same night.[^6] The same report's table of new lights gives 10 November for both the third-order light and the whistle.[^7]

In 1917, after the north arm of the breakwater was extended, the lighthouse had to move.[^2] The act of 12 June 1917 appropriated $88,000 to move the light and fog signal to the south end of the north arm of the extended exterior breakwater and rebuild the station there, and to put new lights on the breakwater's south arm.[^8] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for fiscal 1920 describes how it was done. The reinforced-concrete superstructure of the new foundation pier was practically finished in December 1917. The old cast-iron tower was taken down and re-erected in the 1918 season by the Lighthouse Service's own working party, with a new steel fog-signal building and boathouse added. Interior lining and finish were completed in November 1919.[^9] The fog signal went into service at the new site on 8 May 1918 and the light on 1 August 1918.[^9] The landmark report gives the base as 44 by 65 feet (13.4 by 19.8 m) and 20 feet (6 m) high, and the focal plane as 82 feet (25 m) above mean lower water.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists the height of the light as 67.33 feet (20.5 m) above water, so the two sources differ.[^10]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive, drawing on the National Archives' registers of keepers, lists a head keeper and as many as five assistants in the station's early years. Thomas J. Bailey was head keeper from 1893 to 1906, and Alfred Curtis Erickson followed him until 1911.[^10] Thomas J. Armstrong held the post from 1918 to 1931, and the Lighthouse Service awarded the station its efficiency flag for 1924, the highest general efficiency in the twelfth district, with Armstrong named as keeper.[^10][^11][^23]

The Light-House Board's report for fiscal 1894 mentions one of the station's keepers without naming him. During the Columbian Exposition of 1893 he stood duty "in full uniform" at the lighthouse exhibit in the Government building. The Board wrote that visitors would remember his courtesy and "the intelligent answers he gave to their many questions".[^12]

The landmark report says that when the station was staffed each of its three keepers had a small room in the tower, reached by the spiral stair.[^2] For 1960 to 1967 the archive lists a long run of Coast Guard crewmen, with a few more names into the 1970s.[^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The annual reports count the hours the fog signal ran. The report for fiscal 1894 puts the two steam whistles at about 297 hours of running in the year, burning some 30 tons of coal and 3 cords of wood.[^13] By fiscal 1901 the figure had risen to some 732 hours, 78 tons of coal and 9 cords of wood.[^14]

The report for fiscal 1899 records a gale on 7 and 8 October (so in 1898) that carried away the station's iron ladder; a passing vessel also broke off the boat davit. Both were replaced.[^15]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin records several small rescues. On 26 September 1915 the keeper, Severin H. Danielsen, rescued five men who could not reach shore from the pier at the station because of the rising sea.[^16] On 15 April 1917 the keeper, Phil Sheridan, took a supply of gasoline to a launch in danger of drifting onto the stone breakwater. On 16 May the first assistant, Walter Donovan, and a laborer pulled another gasoline launch off the breakwater near the light.[^17]

## After the keepers

The station's fog signal was changed in 1925 to a Type-F diaphone. The landmark report says mariners were impressed by its penetrating sound, but that people living and working nearby were much less enthusiastic.[^2] The Coast Guard automated the station in 1979, according to the landmark report and the Chicago Sun-Times; a 1992 Chicago Tribune article says the keepers left in 1978.[^2][^18][^19]

The Commission on Chicago Landmarks designated the lighthouse a Chicago Landmark on 9 April 2003.[^20] LighthouseFriends reports that the Coast Guard deemed the lighthouse excess and offered it free to eligible bodies in 2005, and that the Secretary of the Interior started its transfer to the City of Chicago on 24 February 2009.[^21] A non-profit, Friends of the Chicago Harbor Lighthouse, is working to take it over and restore it; it said in April 2025 that it had delivered its application for ownership under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act to the National Park Service.[^22]

## Today

The light is an active aid to navigation maintained by the Coast Guard's Kenosha aids-to-navigation team. It shows a red flash every five seconds.[^2][^19][^22] The lighthouse sits on a breakwater and can be reached only by water. The tower is closed. LighthouseFriends reports that the City of Chicago owns it, and the Friends' newsletter of April 2025 says the transfer application was pending and that in-person tours were still planned for the future.[^21][^22] People can see it from Navy Pier and from harbour boat tours.[^21]
