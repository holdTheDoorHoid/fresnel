A white concrete lighthouse with a red roof stands at the seaward end of a third-of-a-mile breakwater, about 0.4 mile (0.6 km) northeast of Wisconsin Point, marking the way into the harbour of Superior, Wisconsin.[^1] The point has had a light of one kind or another since the 1890s, and the building that stands there now has been the harbour's main light since 1913.

## The place

Superior's harbour lies behind a barrier of sand about ten miles (16 km) long. A natural inlet through it, Superior Entry, let ships pass between Lake Superior and the sheltered water inside, and the boundary between Wisconsin and Minnesota runs through that inlet: Wisconsin Point on one side, Minnesota Point on the other.[^1] The first light was a stone tower on Minnesota Point, which the National Register nomination says was built in 1856 at a cost of $13,700; Lighthouse Friends dates the start of its operation to 1858.[^1][^2] In 1879 a wood-framed light was built at the head of the north pier. Pier work that began in 1881 forced it to be discontinued, the old Minnesota Point light was relit as a substitute, and that light was put out for good in 1885, when the north pier light came back into service.[^1]

The nomination says that navigation improvements in the 1880s left the south pier, on the Wisconsin side, longer than the north one, which led the Light-House Board to plan a new station there.[^1] In June 1893 the Board moved the pierhead beacon across the entry to the south pier and had an elevated walk built so the beacon could be reached in stormy weather.[^3] Its report called for a first-class fog signal, estimated at no more than $5,500, to guide ships "in thick or foggy weather."[^3] (The nomination puts the move of the tower in 1892; the Board's own report says 15 June 1893.[^1][^3]) Lighthouse Friends notes that more than 900 vessels arrived and cleared from the bay each year at the time, carrying cargoes worth over $28 million.[^2]

A fog signal house was built on the south pier in 1893.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends says a duplex with six rooms in each apartment was built for the two keepers now needed, while the Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 1894 says the keeper's dwelling was transferred from the Minnesota side and arranged so each of two keepers had six rooms.[^2][^14] The sources disagree about the signal: the nomination describes a pair of 6-inch steam whistles that began sounding on 27 August 1893, while Lighthouse Friends describes a single 6-inch whistle first sounded on 15 October. The Board's own list of new fog signals for that fiscal year gives a 6-inch steam whistle on 15 October 1893.[^1][^2][^14] Either way it was used hard. In 1895 the keepers burned 44 tons of coal and ran the whistles 895 consecutive hours, which the nomination calls a record for the station; Lighthouse Friends puts the average for the signal's first decade at 394 hours a year.[^1][^2]

## Building the light

A second light was wanted to line up with the pierhead light and guide ships in. A temporary post light was first shown on 30 November 1897 (Lighthouse Friends says a pole light was placed in 1898), and in 1901 a wooden tower from Devils Island, in the Apostle Islands, was moved to the south pier. It was first lit on 1 April 1902 with a fixed fourth-order lens.[^1][^2]

A storm on 27 and 28 November washed away the front light and badly damaged the fog signal house, and a temporary lantern was set on the roof of the repaired building. The sources disagree on the year: Lighthouse Friends gives 1905 and a Superior newspaper ties the loss of the earlier light to the Mataafa Storm of November 1905, while the nomination says 1903.[^2][^4][^1]

Meanwhile the Army Corps of Engineers was replacing the wooden piers with concrete ones and, from about 1910, adding two converging breakwaters. The lighthouse plan changed with them: instead of ranges on the new pier, a combined light and fog signal would go on the end of the south breakwater, with unattended acetylene beacons on the piers. Congress had appropriated $20,000 in 1906 and added $25,000 on 4 March 1911.[^1][^2] The Corps finished the breakwater and its concrete pier in July 1911, and contracts for the lighthouse were let in June 1912.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported in 1912 that the concrete for the main light's second story and tower had been placed.[^5]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported that the main light and fog signal station "was completed and placed into commission on June 30, 1913."[^6] The building is reinforced concrete, an oval two-storey house with an integral tower and a lantern with helical astragals, standing on the concrete pier. The focal plane is 70 feet (21 m) above the lake.[^1] The first story held machinery and the second held quarters, and the tower carried the fog trumpets.[^2] The original fourth-order Fresnel lens showed five seconds of light and five of dark, produced by a rotating screen.[^1] Lighthouse Friends adds that the screen was turned by a clockwork driven by a 75-pound weight that had to be wound every four hours.[^2] George Putnam's 1917 survey of American lighthouses named the new light as a recent example of rebuilding made necessary by harbour works.[^7]

A further light, Superior Harbor Basin Light No. 1, was established on 30 June 1915. The additional assistant keeper authorized for it needed another dwelling, and the Commissioner's report for the fiscal year ended June 1916 says a concrete and hollow-tile dwelling had been built and was occupied.[^6][^8][^2]

## The keepers

The keepers lived ashore. The duplex on Wisconsin Point remained their home, and the quarters in the lighthouse were used only in an emergency or bad weather. Keepers went out by boat, or on foot along the breakwater, which had a steel cable along its length for their safety.[^2]

The US Lighthouse Society's archive, which draws on the National Archives registers of keepers, lists Edwin C. Bishop as the first head keeper, 1913 to 1919, followed by George J. Hassett. John Woods held the post from at least 1924 to 1934, then Hans F. Christensen to 1939, and John W. Kirkendall from 1939 into at least 1950.[^9] David S. Simonson is listed from 1951 to 1965.[^9] Assistants changed far more often: the archive lists fourteen men as first assistant between 1913 and 1950, among them Roger W. Campbell, who held the post from 1913, and Angus Graham, who served 1937 to 1949, having come as a second assistant the year before.[^9] The nomination says the lighthouse was manned by Lighthouse Service personnel until the 1939 merger of the Service into the Coast Guard, and by Coast Guardsmen afterwards.[^1] A radiobeacon was added in 1938 as a further aid in poor visibility.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In February 1914 Campbell, then first assistant, walked out on the shore ice to hunt rabbits. About seven miles (11 km) from the entry he found the ice had broken away from the shore and was drifting him out onto the lake. He fired his rifle repeatedly, caught the attention of three men on shore, and they reached him in a rowboat as the floe began to break up.[^2]

On Monday, 28 November 1960, forty-foot waves hit the entrance. The Coast Guardsman on watch, Robert Dunno, wrote in the station logbook that heavy seas had broken a window of the radio beacon room. He and Fred Ketola kept the water down in the radio beacon and engine rooms so that the station could keep working. The waves also knocked a hole in the breakwater and cut the two men off from shore. The next morning the officer in charge, Simonson, crawled out over the ice-covered rocks from the shore end with a rope round his waist and a fourth crewman, Richard Bye, anchoring him, while Ketola crawled shoreward with Dunno as his anchor. Once the two lines were tied together the men could cross the gap on the lifeline for the rest of the shipping season.[^2]

## After the keepers

Resident crews left in 1970, when the light and fog signal were automated and the Fresnel lens was replaced by an aerobeacon. The sources name it differently: the nomination calls it a DCB-25, while Lighthouse Friends and the US Lighthouse Society archive say DCB-24.[^1][^2][^9] A VRB-25 beacon replaced it in 1997, still showing a green flash every five seconds.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends retells an account, from a 2010 Lighthouse Digest article by a Coast Guardsman based at Duluth, of the time shortly after automation. Sent more than once to chase a reported outage, he found the light flashing when he reached it on the first two visits; on a later one it was dark until it came back on while he stood in the lantern room, and he spoke aloud to any "old keeper" who might be listening and asked him to stop. By his account the light then stopped failing. It is one man's story, not a record.[^2]

On 1 May 2013 the General Services Administration declared the lighthouse excess to the Coast Guard's needs and offered it free to public bodies and non-profits. No suitable organization was found, and in 2019 it was sold at auction for $159,000 to a buyer from San Francisco who had never seen it.[^2] That sale did not hold: the Army Corps, which owns the breakwater, and the buyer could not agree about controlling access, and in June 2024 the GSA offered the lighthouse free again.[^10] In October 2024 a National Park Service architect told the Superior Telegram that letters of interest had been received from six organisations.[^11] The sources do not say how that round ended.

## Today

The tower is closed to the public.[^2][^9] It can be seen from Wisconsin Point, and the breakwater that leads to it belongs to the Army Corps of Engineers; the nomination describes the light as reachable on foot along the breakwater, but no source found says the public may enter the building or that the breakwater is open for visitors.[^1][^10] The Coast Guard still owns the navigation equipment and must be able to reach it, a green light that flashes every five seconds and a fog signal sounding once every 30 seconds, which the nomination says runs from May to October.[^1][^10]

In June 2026 news reports of a Coast Guard release said that someone had broken into the lighthouse on or around 13 May, cut the power and stolen its beacon, and that a $1,000 reward was offered for information.[^12][^13] InForum's account calls it the "privately owned" light.[^13] Which owner now holds the building, and whether the replacement light is in service, are not settled in the sources found.
