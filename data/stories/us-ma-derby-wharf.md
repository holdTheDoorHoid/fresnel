Derby Wharf Light is a small brick tower at the outer end of Derby Wharf in Salem, Massachusetts. It has stood there since 1871, marking the channel into Salem Harbor, and it is now part of Salem Maritime National Historic Site.[^4][^2]

## The place

The wharf was built by the Derby family of merchants. LighthouseFriends says Richard Derby began it in 1762, and that it did not reach its half-mile (800 m) length until 1806.[^2] Elias Hasket Derby sent ships to China and India during Salem's great age of sail, and the wharf was once lined with roughly twenty structures, including the family's counting house.[^2]

By the middle of the nineteenth century the overseas trade had shrunk, but local shipping still needed a marked channel.[^2] The Park Service quotes the Lighthouse Board's 1870 report: the new lights were to "mark the main channel leading into this anchorage, with the view to it becoming a harbor of refuge which may be safely entered at any time."[^4] The plan was for three lights. One was to stand at Hospital Point in Beverly. The lights at Fort Pickering and Derby Wharf were to function as range lights, marking the channel into Salem Harbor.[^2]

## Building the light

On 3 October 1870 the government bought a plot of 1,190½ square feet (about 111 m²) from the Derby Wharf Corporation, to build on.[^2] The form gives the cost as $3,000 and the date as 1870.[^1] Its significance statement, though, says the light was "Constructed in 1877." The sources disagree on when the tower was built.[^1]

The range went into operation on 17 January 1871, LighthouseFriends reports. At first the light was shown from an existing warehouse on the wharf, until the brick tower was finished that spring.[^2] The tower is "approximately twelve feet square."[^4] Its lantern held a fifth-order Fresnel lens that showed a red light, and the Lighthouse Society's record gives the same original optic.[^2][^3]

The lens changed more than once. LighthouseFriends says a sixth-order lens was requested in 1892, a fourth-order lens was fitted in 1904, and a sixth-order lens went in in 1910.[^2] The Park Service says that for many years the light had "one of only 17 sixth-order Fresnel lenses in the United States."[^4]

## The keepers

Charles C. Pettergill was the first keeper, from 1871 to 1873.[^2][^3]

Robert Peele, Jr., kept the light from 1873 to 1885.[^3]

John J. Lynch kept it for twenty years, from 1885 to 1905. LighthouseFriends calls his tenure by far the longest of the seven keepers.[^2][^3] Because the light was close to town and there was no room on the wharf, no keeper's dwelling was built. The keepers lived in their own homes and made regular treks out to the light.[^2]

Shepley Paul Sawyer (1905 to 1908), Charles L. Wales (1908 to 1911) and Harold C. Tedford (1911 to 1913) followed.[^3] The last keeper was William M. Osgood, from 1913 to 1917. LighthouseFriends says he walked from his nearby home to the light every morning and evening.[^2][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Board's annual report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 records that a schooner broke the railing around the lantern, and that the railing was repaired.[^5] LighthouseFriends dates the repair to 1892.[^2]

In 1914 a devastating fire struck Salem, LighthouseFriends says. Mrs. Osgood, while her husband was trying to save their own home, went out to start the light on Derby Wharf. She only just got out of the tower before smoke and flames engulfed the wharf, and afterwards Osgood needed a rowboat to reach the light.[^2]

## After the keepers

The change from oil to acetylene came in 1917, which LighthouseFriends says allowed the light to be automated.[^2] The Commissioner's annual report for fiscal 1918 lists Derby Wharf, in Salem Harbor, among the lights changed from oil-wick lamps to acetylene or other illuminants.[^6] The Bulletin index places the change in illuminant on page 292.[^7]

In 1922 the tower's color was changed from red to white, and in 1930 the light was electrified.[^2] In 1933 wire cloth was placed around the lantern glass to protect it. A photograph caption that LighthouseFriends quotes says spots on the tower show "where hoodlums have thrown stones."[^2]

The sources disagree about when the light joined the national historic site. The form says it "became part of the Salem Maritime Historic Site" in 1966.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it was not included in the site until 1979, after the Coast Guard decommissioned the light in 1977 and turned it over to the Park Service.[^2] The form records the tower as part of the Derby Waterfront local historic district in 1976.[^1]

The Friends of Salem Maritime restored the tower in 1983, and the beacon was relit on 4 October that year as a private aid to navigation, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The form records the 1983 relight as solar powered, with a six-second flash.[^1] The tower was renovated again in 1989 with an $8,000 grant from the Massachusetts Historical Commission.[^2]

## Today

The light stands at the end of Derby Wharf inside Salem Maritime National Historic Site. The Park Service's page and the Lighthouse Society's record both say the light station is not open to the public.[^4][^3] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^2] The sources disagree, so check the current position with the Park Service before visiting. LighthouseFriends gives directions: from Highway 1A, go east on Derby Street, and the light is at the end of the wharf, on the right.[^2]

The light is solar powered. It flashes red every six seconds, and the Park Service describes it as about 20 feet (6.1 m) high from the base to the top of the cupola.[^4] The form gives the tower as standing 25 feet (7.6 m) above high water.[^1]
