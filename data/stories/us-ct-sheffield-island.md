Sheffield Island Light stands at the southwest tip of Sheffield Island, one of the Norwalk Islands, about a mile off the Connecticut shore where Norwalk Harbor opens into Long Island Sound.[^1] The lighthouse there was finished in 1868: a stone dwelling with a cast-iron lantern tower rising from one gable.[^1][^3] The light was retired in 1902, sold in 1914, and bought in 1986 by the Norwalk Seaport Association, which now runs tours to the island.[^2][^5]

## The place

The nomination for the lighthouse describes the approach to the harbor as "a passage past numerous reefs, islands, and shallows leading to South Norwalk."[^1] A light on Sheffield Island was "established in 1826 to aid vessels in entering Norwalk harbor," the nomination says.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that agents of the Treasury, which then ran the lights, surveyed several sites in the Norwalk Islands and chose the western end of Sheffield Island.[^2]

The traffic it was meant for grew steadily. The nomination says the port peaked in the 1880s, "when nearly 2,500 steamers, schooners, barges and other large commercial vessels, almost seven per day, arrived or departed the port."[^1] In its report for 1891 and 1892 the Light-House Board proposed a small structure with a light and fog bell on the 4-foot spot off Norwalk Point, then marked by a buoy. The report says it was claimed that a light there "will render more safe the navigation at the entrances of the harbors of Sheffield and South Norwalk."[^8]

Gershom Smith, Robert Sheffield's son-in-law, owned the island when the government bought land for the light.[^7][^2] LighthouseFriends says Smith "sold three acres to the government to build the new tower."[^2]

## Building the light

The sources do not agree on when the first light was shown. The Service's place record gives 1828 as the year the station was established.[^3] New England Lighthouses says a lighthouse "was built" in 1827,[^4] and the nomination calls the earlier building an "1826 lighthouse."[^1] LighthouseFriends says a thirty-foot (9 m) stone tower was standing by mid-1827,[^2] and the nomination gives the earlier tower as 34 feet (10 m) high.[^1]

The first apparatus was ten lamps with parabolic reflectors, turned by clockwork so that they showed alternating red and white flashes.[^4] Its lens was replaced by a fourth-order Fresnel lens in 1857, according to the Service's record and New England Lighthouses.[^3][^4] LighthouseFriends gives 1858 for the same lens, installed in the old tower.[^2]

Mariners had long complained that the light was not bright enough and that the tower was too short for it to be seen well. LighthouseFriends says that in 1867 Congress appropriated funds for a new lighthouse.[^2] The new house was finished in 1868, with a lantern tower of cast-iron plates bolted together.[^1] The nomination says the tower rises to 46 feet (14 m), and the Service's record gives the same height.[^1][^3]

The sources disagree on the stone. LighthouseFriends says the house is granite,[^2] the nomination describes gneiss,[^1] and the Service's record gives limestone blocks for the keepers' quarters.[^3] The nomination also says the plans "simply re-used those for Block Island North Light with a different name and minor amendments pencilled in," and that the Sheffield house is "one of at least six nearly identical structures all built in the period 1867-1869."[^1]

The lens moved into the new tower.[^2] When it was working, its focal plane was 52 feet (16 m) above the sea and it could be seen for 12.5 nautical miles.[^1] In a list of lights where a mariner "may have to wait one or even two minutes before he sees the flash," the Board's 1899 report gives the Norwalk Islands light as fourth order, fixed white, with a red flash every minute.[^10]

## The keepers

Gershom Smith, the first keeper, lived in a separate stone dwelling with his wife and twelve children.[^7][^2] He also farmed, harvested oysters and kept animals.[^7] New England Lighthouses reports that Smith and his family lived on the island for five generations, and CT History says the family's cows often wandered between the islands at low tide, so that Smith rowed from one to another to milk them.[^4][^7]

LighthouseFriends describes an 1838 inspection of the lamps and reflectors. The flash interval was supposed to be two minutes and forty-five seconds, but Smith had cut it to sixty-six seconds after complaints that the light was hidden for too long between flashes.[^2]

The Service's record lists Smith's successor, Lewis Whitlock, from 1845 to 1861.[^3] The sources differ on how Smith left the post. LighthouseFriends says he was removed in 1845 and was not a supporter of President John Tyler; it says a keeper's job then was "subject to the political winds."[^2] Connecticut History says he manned the light until his death in 1845.[^7] LighthouseFriends also says that, according to the Norwalk Gazette, Whitlock's one qualification was that he supported Tyler. The paper called him a "good-natured, good-for-nothing creature."[^2]

An 1850 inspection, as New England Lighthouses quotes it, found the keeper alone and his house in disorder. "Keeper is alone, entirely so, and everything he has in the house is out of fix." The same report added: "Poor man, and miserable, and will continue so without a wife."[^4]

Noah Mosher, Sr., was keeper from 1861.[^3] New England Lighthouses quotes a July 1872 newspaper story on his death. It said he "was seated in a chair, spyglass in hand, watching several vessels which were passing the island" when he "suddenly fell backward and in an instant was dead."[^4] LighthouseFriends gives the death as his falling dead from his chair while watching passing vessels.[^2] His son, Noah Mosher, Jr., kept the light until 1878.[^3]

The Service's record then lists Frederick S. Comstock (1878 to 1885) and Neil Ward (1885 to 1886).[^3] Samuel A. Armour was keeper from 1886 until the light was retired in 1902.[^3] In August 1901 he became ill with typhoid fever "after drinking contaminated water from the station's cistern." New England Lighthouses says his wife kept the light until he recovered three months later.[^4] That source does not give her name, and this story does not supply one.

Armour also had a cow. The New York Herald of 17 December 1893, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, wrote that "Daisy follows the bent of her own free will," and that in such a humor she would take to the water and swim to the mainland, two miles away.[^2]

The Light-House Board's report for 1896 records the year's work at the station: some 75 running feet (23 m) of new picket fence built, and 200 feet (61 m) of old picket fence repaired.[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources give one wreck near the station, in 1899. The Waterbury Evening Democrat of 16 October 1899 printed a South Norwalk dispatch that said the steam propeller *Pottsville* was "wrecked and burned off Norwalk islands, near the mouth of the harbor, late Saturday night."[^12] Its captain and crew of nine escaped unharmed but lost their effects.[^12] The dispatch puts the wreck off the islands, not at the light, and does not mention the light.

LighthouseFriends reports a major storm in 1992 that flooded the basement with more than four feet of water and washed away ten feet of shoreline near the tower. New England Lighthouses dates the flooding to a tropical storm in the fall of 1993.[^2][^4] Hurricane Fran in 1996 caused more damage, and in 1997 gabions were installed and beach grass planted to slow the erosion.[^2][^4]

## After the keepers

The nomination places the discontinuance in the late 1890s, when the harbor was dredged and two new sites, Green's Ledge and Peck's Ledge, were established.[^1] The same nomination gives the lighthouse's operating span as 1868 to 1902,[^1] and LighthouseFriends gives 15 February 1902 as the day it was retired.[^2] The Board's report for 1901 records that on 30 January 1901 the fourth section of the foundation cylinder for the Greens Ledge light had been completed and filled with concrete.[^11] The nomination says the old dwelling was used for the keeper of Green's Ledge for twelve years.[^1]

In 1914 the Lighthouse Service declared the lighthouse surplus. LighthouseFriends says the highest sealed bid came from Thorsten O. Stabell of South Norwalk, who used it as a summer residence.[^2] New England Lighthouses describes a sale by auction for under $5,000, and says Stabell was captain of the Norwalk Yacht Club.[^4]

New England Lighthouses says a 1930s plan to make the island a resort, with a golf course and tennis courts, shut down in 1937 for lack of fresh water.[^4] Connecticut History gives storm damage as the reason a country club on the island closed in 1937.[^7]

The Stabell family owned the lighthouse until 1986,[^2] when the Norwalk Seaport Association bought it for $700,000. LighthouseFriends says $160,000 was paid when the sale closed, and the rest was due over three years.[^2] Volunteers did much of the refurbishment in the late 1980s.[^2] The lighthouse was listed on the National Register on 19 January 1989.[^5]

On 17 October 2011 the Norwalk Seaport Association lit the tower for the first time in more than a century.[^6] The light is solar-powered, installed with the help of NRG Energy, which also rebuilt the island's electrical system. The Association says it is not an aid to navigation, but it can be seen intermittently from the Connecticut side of Long Island Sound.[^6][^4] A state grant of $250,000 paid for restoration work in 2012, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2]

## Today

The Norwalk Seaport Association owns the lighthouse. Its ferry leaves from the dock at 90 Water Street in Norwalk,[^5] and the Association says the ferry is "the only public access to the Norwalk Islands."[^5] Its tour season runs from May to September, and over 7,000 visitors a year make the trip to tour the lighthouse.[^5]

The grounds are open, and the dwelling and tower are open in season, according to LighthouseFriends. Seasonal caretakers live on the island in summer.[^2] Much of the island is now a wildlife refuge, with public trails and observation platforms.[^2] The nomination recorded no optic in the tower in 1988.[^1]
