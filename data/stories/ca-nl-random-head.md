Random Head Light is a red-and-white checkered cast-iron tower on Motion Island, off the southeast tip of Random Island in Trinity Bay, Newfoundland. It was first lit in 1895, and from 1895 until 1952 the Cooper family kept it.[^1][^2][^4][^5]

## The place

Random Island lies on the northwest shore of Trinity Bay, and a causeway has linked it to Clarenville since 1952.[^1] The lighthouse stands on Motion Island near East Random Head. The Cooper family called the station "The Motion."[^1][^5][^6] Waves and wind batter the small island, and a boat can approach only in the calmest weather.[^4] The cliffs of Random Head rise 156 metres (512 feet) over the station.[^4]

The Canadian Register of Historic Places statement describes the tower's "high visibility to passing sea going vessels," and the Newfoundland Historic Trust's 2012 report calls the light significant for its visibility on the approach to Random by mariners.[^2][^8] For the wider system, which is not this station's own history, the same report says the lights of Newfoundland and Labrador were established co-operatively by Great Britain, Newfoundland and Canada, and that by 1938 there were 230 major shore-based, attended light stations along the coasts of Newfoundland and Labrador.[^7]

## Building the light

The tower was first meant for Heart's Content on the south side of Trinity Bay.[^1][^4] A cast-iron tower from the Victoria Works in St. John's was delivered to a site there in 1893. Work had begun in 1894 when an executive decree ordered the tower and its materials moved to the largest of the Motion Islands.[^1] Rowlett's directory says a crew was ready to install the light at Heart's Content in 1894 when the order came to build it at Random Head instead.[^6]

The tower and keeper's dwelling were erected on Motion Island in 1894. The light could not be lit until early 1895, because the optical apparatus was late in arriving.[^1] The Coast Guard text, as the society reprints it, says the tower was made by the Victoria Iron Works of St. John's to the specifications of J.T. Nevill, Inspector of Lighthouses for Newfoundland, and gives its height as 10.4 metres (34 feet), painted red and white in checks.[^4] The present Coast Guard list gives the tower as 10.2 metres.[^3]

The first light was a sixth-order lens showing an occulting white light at a focal plane of 38.4 metres.[^1] The heat from the light turned a screen around the lamp fifteen times a minute, giving alternating two-second periods of light and darkness.[^1] The keeper's dwelling stood 3.7 metres north of the tower. A concrete water tank was built into its basement, and a 100-metre tramway hauled supplies up from a landing on the west side.[^1]

Thomas Cooper's appointment letter, dated 15 April 1895, told him to light the lamp shortly before sunset and turn it up "as large as possible without smoke," to put it out at daylight, and to keep the station clean and painted. His pay was $400 a year, paid quarterly, with $160 more to hire an assistant of his choice.[^1][^5]

## The keepers

The first keeper's name appears in several forms. LighthouseFriends calls him Thomas Cooper, and its own account of the 1895 letter gives Thomas H. Cooper.[^1] The Lighthouse Digest article and the Coast Guard text give Thomas Henry Cooper.[^4][^5] The society's copy of the 1895 contract is titled with the name Henry Thomas Cooper.[^4] The first keeper's grandson, George's son, was also named Thomas Henry, so two keepers in the family share the name.[^1][^5]

Cooper moved his family from Inglewood Forest to the island with his second wife, Roxana Stowe.[^1][^5] The family included Georgianna Cooper, who was ten at the move and later became "an artist and writer of some note," according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] Cooper died in 1905 (LighthouseFriends' roster says 1904, but its text and the Digest give 1905), and his son George Havlock Cooper, from his first marriage, took over.[^1][^5] George's wife Fanny was a sister of Roxana.[^1]

George and Fanny brought their five children to the station from Inglewood.[^1][^5] Getting from the station to the base of Random Head meant a network of ladders: a vertical ladder to the top of Wedge Rock, a horizontal ladder with rope handrails, then a series of ladders up the headland to a trail.[^1] Though it required a two-hour journey, the family attended church in Deer Harbour when possible.[^1] In the early 1900s an 18-metre ladder was fixed to the cliff so keepers could cross from the island to a path on the cliff edge.[^4][^8] The Coopers relied on a supply ship that came twice a year with fuel, canned goods and barrels of food, and they kept a small garden, raised sheep and fished.[^1][^5]

Fanny died at the lighthouse in July 1932. By then two of the children had left: Sadie was working at Holloway Studio in St. John's, and Samuel Robert, known as Bert, had entered the ministry.[^1][^5] In 1931 the light had been changed to acetylene, so it no longer had to be lit and put out each day.[^1] George retired in 1932, by LighthouseFriends' account, and the rest of the family moved to Clarenville soon after.[^1][^5]

In his 1982 book, *Random Thoughts*, Rev. Bert Cooper recalled a stormy Christmas at the station, when the family gathered in the living room of the keeper's house while the waves crashed outside. The Digest article quotes the passage without dating the Christmas.[^5]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard text says the light became unwatched in 1931, when it was changed to a flashing white light on acetylene.[^4] LighthouseFriends and the Digest instead have a Cooper serving as caretaker until 1952, so the accounts differ on when the light stopped being watched.[^1][^5] George's son Thomas Henry, whom the Digest calls Tom, commuted to the lighthouse after the family moved away and kept the light until 1952, when he resigned and automation took over.[^1][^5] The Coast Guard text, as the society reprints it, calls the last full-time keeper Thomas Cooper, who resigned in 1952.[^4] John Watton looked after the light through the 1960s, when it was converted to battery power.[^1][^4]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the station. Its List of Lights gives the light as a white flash every five seconds, with a focal height of 38.4 metres and a range of 5 nautical miles. The list says the light works year round and operates around the clock.[^3] The older Coast Guard text, as reprinted by the society, gave the range as 11 miles.[^4]

The keeper's house and other station buildings have since been demolished, and only the tower remains.[^5][^6] The tower was refurbished in the late 1980s and restored in 2009.[^4][^6] In 1990 the tower was given Recognized Federal Heritage Building status.[^1]

The island can be reached only by helicopter or private boat in very calm weather. The society's reprint of the Coast Guard text says the headland is about 15 km (9.5 miles) from the end of the road at Hickman's Harbour.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower closed; Rowlett lists the site and the tower as closed, so the sources disagree on whether the grounds are open.[^1][^6] The statement of significance lists the red and white checkerboard paint scheme among the features that give the tower its character.[^2]
