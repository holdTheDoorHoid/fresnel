Mohawk Island Light, also called Gull Island Light, is a ruined stone lighthouse on a small island in Lake Erie, east of Port Maitland and near Dunnville, Ontario. John Brown, a Scottish stonemason, built it between 1846 and 1848, and it has been dark since 1969.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

## The place

The island lies about 1.5 to 2.2 km (1 to 1.4 miles) offshore from Port Maitland, which sits at the mouth of the Grand River. The sources do not agree on the distance. LighthouseFriends gives 2.2 km (1.4 miles), but in one passage places the island about five kilometres east of Port Maitland. The Lighthouse Directory gives about 1.5 km (1 mile).[^1][^2]

In 1844 the Board of Works of the Province of Canada asked for a first-class light there. The Board said Port Maitland was about to become a critical port, and that a long, dangerous reef ran out from the island, where a great deal of property had been lost.[^1] The light was put up to warn of the offshore shoal and to direct traffic to and from the southern end of the Welland Canal.[^3] The canal had opened in 1829 and was substantially rebuilt and improved in 1845.[^5]

## Building the light

A contract to build the tower went in 1846 to John Brown, a Scot who had worked on stone locks for the Erie Barge Canal and the Welland Canal.[^1] Brown quarried limestone at Queenston and shipped it through the Welland Canal to Port Maitland.[^1] Work began in the spring of 1847 and the tower was finished that year. The lighting apparatus arrived late from England, so the light was not lit until the spring of 1848.[^1]

The Canadian Register dates the building 1846 to 1848 and lists John B. McIntyre as architect or designer. The sources do not say what he contributed.[^4]

The tower was a stone cylinder about 18 m (59 ft) tall, according to the register, joined to a one-storey dwelling.[^4][^1] LighthouseFriends also gives the tower's height as about 18 m (60 ft). The dwelling had a kitchen, a sitting room, a single bedroom and a central hall that led to the tower.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory gives the tower's height as 24 m (80 ft).[^2]

The light's ten lamps sat in parabolic reflectors, and clockwork driven by a weight hung in the tower turned them. The keeper climbed six flights of wooden stairs to wind the weight.[^1] Sperm oil was the first fuel, but its price rose beyond what the catch could supply. The lamps were adapted to coal oil in 1864.[^1]

The pattern changed twice more. In 1889 the light became a group of three bright flashes. In May 1918 a double-flash reflector gave a flash, a six-second eclipse, a flash and an eighteen-second eclipse.[^1] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 the Department of Marine also ended the use of a hand fog horn at the station.[^7]

## The keepers

John Burgess, a farmer from Burgess Point, which is now Rock Point Provincial Park, was the first keeper.[^1] He retired in November 1872. His son, J. L. Burgess, took temporary charge until Richard H. Smithers took over the following spring.[^1]

Smithers kept the light for twenty-three years, until 1896, when his son Richard O. Smithers became keeper. LighthouseFriends says the younger Smithers served on the island for twenty-five years.[^1] The Department's keeper list for the year ended 30 June 1909 has "R. O. Smithers" against Mohawk Island with March 31, 1896 beside it.[^9] The list for the year ended 30 June 1906 also names Smithers, R. O. against the island.[^8]

Richard Foster took charge in 1921. He had moved to Dunnville in 1915, and LighthouseFriends says he enlisted in the 114th Haldimand Battalion and went overseas in the First World War. It adds that lightkeeping jobs were often given to veterans.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The station showed a flashing light in summer. From 1929 it also had an unwatched winter light, used through the cold months.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes Foster's log entry for 14 December 1932, made at 7:45 a.m.: "summer light put out and winter light left burning."[^1]

Foster usually stayed a day after the switch to make sure the winter light worked. LighthouseFriends suggests that he and his son James, who it says was twenty-five, probably closed up the station and set off in a small skiff for the trip of about 2.4 km (1.5 miles) to the mainland on 15 December.[^1]

A United Press dispatch from Dunnville, printed in the Washington *Evening Star* on 2 January 1933, says the two had been missing since 14 December. They had failed to return to their Dunnville home from the station at the close of navigation.[^6] LighthouseFriends dates their departure to 15 December, so the sources differ on when the two went missing. Two Dunnville men rowed out to the island, found the keeper's belongings gone and the station's boat missing, and reported the two men missing to the Ontario Provincial Police.[^1] The search was held up by ice. LighthouseFriends says piles of slush ice about 4.6 m (15 ft) high lined the shore and ice floes on the lake hampered the effort.[^1]

The sources differ on when and where the bodies were found. LighthouseFriends says both were found on 31 December, on the beach near Point Abino. Ovide Charette, a family friend, found the father lying in the water about 90 m (100 yards) from where the son had been found.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes a newspaper that places the son about 4.8 km (3 miles) east of Port Colborne.[^1] The dispatch says the son's body was found the night before, "half-buried in the sand, 100 yards from shore," about 3.2 km (2 miles) east of Port Colborne, and that the father was still being sought.[^6]

The two also disagree on their ages. LighthouseFriends says James was twenty-five and that his father began on the island at fifty. The dispatch calls James "21-year-old" and Richard Foster a "52-year-old" keeper.[^1][^6]

## After the keepers

After the deaths the station was made unstaffed. A caretaker still visited to look after the battery-powered light.[^1]

In 1969 vandals landed on the island, climbed the tower and smashed the beacon. The light was moved to a buoy offshore.[^1][^2] The Lighthouse Directory says the vandals broke into the lighthouse and smashed the lens.[^2]

Eight years later the Department of Transport announced plans to demolish the abandoned tower, citing liability concerns. Fishermen, boaters and lakeshore residents protested, pointing to the tower's historic value and its usefulness as a reference point for navigation.[^1]

In 1977 Mohawk Island National Wildlife Area was created, as the island had become a nesting site for terns and gulls.[^1]

The Mohawk Lighthouse Preservation Association was formed in 1990 to restore the tower. LighthouseFriends says little visible progress appears to have been made, and the Lighthouse Directory says there doesn't seem to be any recent preservation activity.[^1][^2] The Lighthouse Directory also says a fire gutted the buildings, leaving only shells. It gives no date for the fire.[^2]

## Today

The register recognised the lighthouse as a heritage lighthouse on 29 May 2015, under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act. It describes a ruin lacking some of its original features, including the lantern and roofs.[^4] A federal heritage statement calls it the only surviving stone lighttower with an integrated residence in Canada.[^5]

The island is a National Wildlife Area operated by Environment Canada. Visiting it is prohibited from 1 April to 31 August to protect nesting waterbirds, though the surrounding waters can be visited by boat.[^4]

LighthouseFriends names the end of Pyle Road as a place to get a good view of the tower, and says it can also be seen from Rock Point Provincial Park.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory places the view at the end of Pyle Road off Lakeshore Road, says the island is accessible only by boat, and says the tower is probably visible from Rock Point Provincial Park too.[^2]

The Lighthouse Directory rates the tower as gravely endangered.[^2] A beacon that was reportedly used at the light is on display at the Port Dover Harbour Museum.[^1]
