Menominee North Pier Light stands at the end of the north pier, where the Menominee River runs out into Green Bay on the Michigan side. A light has marked that pier since 1877. The tower is an octagonal cast-iron tower, painted red, which was moved twice along the pier and set on a concrete base in 1927.[^1][^7]

## The place

Lumber made the river important. Its mills shipped out through the mouth of the river, but the water at the entrance was only about five feet (1.5 m) deep, so for many years lumber had to be loaded by lighter onto vessels offshore.[^7] Two towns face each other across the river, Menominee in Michigan and Marinette in Wisconsin.[^7] Harbor improvements began at the mouth in 1871, and over the next few years two parallel piers, 400 feet (122 m) apart, were extended into Green Bay.[^7]

The traffic grew with the mills and, from 1894, with the railway car ferries that crossed Lake Michigan to Frankfort.[^7] The Board reported that a fog signal was needed here, and that 2,040 vessels had arrived and departed in the year ending 31 December 1893.[^3] LighthouseFriends gives the same 2,040 figure for 1893.[^7]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says a fixed red light of the fifth order was established at the end of the north pier on 1 January 1877, under a tower of octagonal cast iron.[^7] The first keeper, William A. Holmes, served for the first two seasons.[^7] The USLHS record gives 1877 as the year the station was established, but gives 1927 as the year the tower was established, and it lists the original optic as a fourth-order lens.[^1] The Board's 1892 report records the change from fifth to fourth order on 10 September 1891, so the archive's fourth-order original optic does not match that record.[^2]

As the pier grew, the light was moved with it. LighthouseFriends says the elevated walkway was extended in 1879, and that the tower was moved 305 feet lakeward in 1881 and another 345 feet in 1886.[^7] LighthouseFriends says that in 1881 the Board asked for $5,000 for a keeper's dwelling, and that the duplex for the station's two keepers was not funded until 1906.[^7]

On 10 September 1891 the light was changed from a fifth order to a fourth order. The Board's report records that change, and LighthouseFriends says the new fourth-order lens was an F. Barbier & Cie. Fresnel lens.[^2][^7]

The Board's 1895 report says a fog signal was needed here. On 11 June 1896 Congress provided $5,000 for a steam fog signal at the station.[^3][^4] LighthouseFriends says the ten-inch steam whistle was established on 4 September 1897.[^7] A concrete foundation 15 feet (4.6 m) square was put under the cast-iron tower in 1900.[^7]

In 1916 a steel range tower was placed on the pier about 248 yards (227 m) from the octagonal light. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists a "Menominee Pierhead Range Rear" among the lights established that year.[^5][^7] In 1927, while the Engineer Department was improving the piers, the old fog-signal building was taken away, the lighthouse was set on a concrete block, a diaphone fog signal was put in the tower, and the light was electrified.[^7] The National Register record gives 1877 and 1927 as the station's significant years.[^6]

## The keepers

The archive's roster has 52 entries for this station, one for each stint: sixteen head keepers, seven first assistants, thirteen laborers, and sixteen names under a "USCG" heading from 1959 to at least 1970.[^1]

After Holmes came a run of head keepers, most of them for a few years each. Hans J. Hanson kept the light from 1905 to 1923.[^1] Eugene V. Kimball came to the station as first assistant in 1897, to help with the extra work the steam fog whistle brought. LighthouseFriends says he was transferred to Chicago after three years, returned as head keeper in 1923, and stayed until 1938.[^1][^7] Henry A. Anderson was an assistant from 1918 and head keeper from 1938 to 1942.[^1] Jesse L. Bunker was an assistant from 1938 to 1946, and head keeper from 1946 to 1963.[^1]

The roster also lists thirteen laborers from 1900 onward, with only their names and years.[^1] For the people under its "USCG" heading, the archive gives no role beyond that heading.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 4 June 1884 a fire was discovered in the lighthouse's foundation crib. LighthouseFriends says it was quickly put out, before any serious damage was done.[^7]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1972, and the metal walkway was removed from the pier afterwards.[^1][^7] The Coast Guard declared the light excess in 2005, and it was transferred to the City of Menominee in 2008 through the federal program for historic lights.[^7] LighthouseFriends says the transfer deed required the city to maintain the tower, and that a June 2013 Eagle Herald article reported that the city council had failed to live up to that agreement.[^7] Thanks to an anonymous donor, the city began restoring the lighthouse in 2016: the exterior was repainted, and the interior was stripped to the plaster and refurbished.[^7] The fourth-order Fresnel lens from Menominee is on display in a museum beside Sand Point Lighthouse, in Escanaba.[^7]

## Today

The City of Menominee owns the tower. The pier can be walked to reach it, and the grounds are open.[^7] LighthouseFriends says the tower is open to visitors in season, with tours from Memorial Day to Labor Day, on Thursdays and Fridays from noon to 3 p.m., Saturdays from noon to 6 p.m., and Sundays from 1 to 4 p.m.[^7] Those hours are as LighthouseFriends printed them, so check with the city before you go.
