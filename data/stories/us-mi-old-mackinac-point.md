Old Mackinac Point Light is a brick tower joined to its keepers' house at the tip of Michigan's Lower Peninsula, in Mackinaw City, where the Straits of Mackinac narrow between Lake Michigan and Lake Huron.[^3][^12] Its fog signal began sounding on 5 November 1890, the light was first lit on 25 October 1892, and it was discontinued in 1957, after the Mackinac Bridge made it unnecessary. Mackinac State Historic Parks runs it today.[^1][^4][^12][^14]

## The place

In the summer of 1838 Lieutenant James T. Homans, sailing the Lakes to choose sites for newly authorised lights, recommended "a beacon-light, near the town of Mackinaw". He gave two reasons: the large amount of commerce through the straits, and the danger at the narrowest part of the strait, opposite the town. He preferred the western point of the harbour, because the land there was higher than on the east side.[^12]

The light Congress authorised was to stand on a new point. McGulpin Point, two miles west of Fort Michilmackinac, had had its own light since 18 June 1869.[^12] In 1888 the Lighthouse Board asked for $25,000 to move that light to Old Mackinac Point. The Board had objected to closing McGulpin Point's light at all, and it described the new point as "low and scraggy", only six to eight feet above the lake, while McGulpin Point stood 70 feet or more.[^12] The McGulpin light was kept going for several more years after the new light was authorised.[^12]

## Building the light

Congress authorised a light and a fog signal for the point on 2 March 1889, but the act provided only $5,500 for the fog signal.[^1][^2] The land was purchased and its deed recorded in June 1890.[^1] Work on the fog signal ran from 1 July to 9 October 1890, and the ten-inch steam whistles, installed in duplicate, were first operated on 5 November 1890.[^1][^12]

Congress appropriated $20,000 for the light on 3 March 1891.[^2] The metalwork was contracted on 10 October 1891.[^12] No bids had come in for the tower and dwellings, so the work was readvertised, and on 19 March 1892 six bids were received. The lowest, $13,722 from John P. Schmitt of Detroit, was accepted.[^3][^12] Erection began in late May 1892.[^3]

The Board's 1892 description gives a cylindrical brick shaft on a base of limestone ashlar, 13 feet 4 inches (4.1 m) across outside, 45 feet (14 m) to the gallery and 50 feet (15 m) to the focal plane. The tower forms the northwest corner of the keepers' dwellings, which are two houses under one roof. The fog-signal building, 22 by 40 feet (7 by 12 m), stands just east of them.[^3]

The Board's report for fiscal 1893 says the keeper's dwelling was completed on 27 October 1892 and that the station "was lighted for the first time on the night of October 25, 1892."[^4] The Board's list of new lights gives 26 October.[^5] This story uses the 25th, the night the report describes. The lantern held a fourth-order Fresnel lens, which LighthouseFriends says flashed red every ten seconds.[^12]

The Board found the fog signal too close to the dwelling and wanted it moved fifty feet (15 m) east. The ground it needed belonged to Mackinaw City, which planned to use it as a public park and would not give it up. Condemnation proceedings began in 1899.[^12] The Board's report that year notes that "a sketch was made showing additional land required for light-house purposes at this station."[^16] Congress provided $400 in compensation for the land on 3 March 1905, a new brick fog-signal building was begun in May 1906, and its boilers and whistles were operating in 1907.[^12]

## The keepers

The station had a head keeper and an assistant from 1890 until 1909, when a second assistant was added.[^12][^13]

George W. Marshall, the first head keeper, came from Waugoshance Light after eight years there and kept this light until he retired in 1919. He was a Civil War veteran and one of five sons of William Marshall, a longtime ordnance sergeant at Fort Mackinac, "who became lighthouse keepers."[^12][^13]

His brother Charles became first assistant in 1900. He had become stranded in a boatswain's chair while painting the St. Helena Island light, and after about two years here his health gave way; he was hospitalised at the Northern Michigan Asylum in Traverse City.[^12][^13] George E. Leggatt, who had been first assistant here, took Charles's place as keeper at St. Helena Island.[^12]

George and Margaret Marshall had no children of their own. After Charles was hospitalised and his wife had died, they adopted some of his children.[^12] Their adopted son James, whom they had taken in during the 1880s, was keeper at White Shoal Light, and in 1919 he took charge here when George retired.[^12][^11] The Commissioner of Lighthouses listed "J. M. Marshall, keeper" at White Shoal in its 1915 report.[^11] A stroke forced James to retire in 1940.[^12] LighthouseFriends counts the Marshall family as having had charge of the light for fifty years in all.[^12]

Henrik G. Olsen, first assistant here from 1933 to 1941, was head keeper from 1941 to 1951.[^12][^13] John P. Campbell, first assistant from 1944 to 1951, succeeded him as head keeper. LighthouseFriends has Campbell "in charge of the station until 1957", but the archive roster carries his head-keeper stint to 1958.[^12][^13] He was then transferred to Point Betsie Light, where he served until his death in 1963.[^12]

William Barnum was first assistant from 1911 to 1919.[^13] The Commissioner's 1915 report lists "W. Barnum, first assistant keeper" at Old Mackinac Point in its table headed "Saving of Life and Property by Vessels or Employees of the Lighthouse Service."[^11] The same entry names him with J. M. Marshall and J. H. Sullivan, third assistant keeper at White Shoal, and credits the three with saving the launch Ida L. during a gale.[^11]

The rules of the period are a different matter. The 1881 *Instructions to Light-Keepers* were general rules, not this station's record, and they said that "watches must be kept at all stations where there is an assistant."[^15]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources on this station record no wreck at the light. What they record is fog and ice.

The fog signal's yearly running hours give a rough measure of foggy weather at the straits. In the fiscal year 1894 it ran "some 396 hours" and used 43 cords of wood.[^6] In 1895 it ran "some 600 hours", using about four tons of coal and 33 cords of wood,[^7] and in 1896 "some 669 hours", with about 65 cords.[^8]

Ice damaged the station. The Board's report for fiscal 1895 says the landing crib "was carried away by ice," and that its rebuilding was completed in the early part of July.[^7] In the winters of 1898–99 and 1899–1900 the light and fog signal ran until "the ice in the straits was solid."[^9][^10] In 1900 the Board added that the steam ferry between Mackinaw City and St. Ignace had made a channel, and so the light and signal were "no longer needed."[^10]

## After the keepers

The light was discontinued in 1957, after the Mackinac Bridge was completed.[^12] In 1960 the Mackinac Island State Park Commission bought the lighthouse and made it part of the state park. After about $70,000 of restoration it opened in 1972. It closed in 1990, when budgets and attendance fell, and reopened in 2004 as a "restoration in progress."[^12] A fundraising effort launched in 1996 aimed to raise $2.2 million to restore it and reopen it to the public.[^12]

LighthouseFriends also says the signal changed from flashing red to flashing white on 1 September 1913, and that electricity replaced oil vapour as the illuminant in 1929.[^12] Neither date is yet confirmed by an official report.

## Today

Mackinac State Historic Parks runs the lighthouse. The tower is open to visitors in season, and the Parks' seven-day pass covers it with the other historic sites.[^14][^12] A fourth-order Fresnel lens from the station is on display inside.[^12] In 2007, 32,933 people visited, many of them drawn by the chance to climb the tower for its view of the straits.[^12] Check the park's current hours and tour rules before you go.
