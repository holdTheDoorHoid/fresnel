Bird Island Light stands on a small island at the mouth of Sippican Harbor, off the town of Marion, Massachusetts. A tower was first lit there in 1819. The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1933 as the year the light was discontinued, and since 1997 it has shone again as a solar light on an island the town owns.[^1][^2][^3][^10]

## The place

The island is about three acres, covered in low scrub and rock, half a mile (800 m) off the eastern tip of Sippican Neck.[^1][^2] The National Register form places it "on the east side of the mouth of Sippican Harbor, opposite the town of Marion," and says the island "serves as a tern nesting ground."[^1]

Winter limited the light's usefulness. The form says its role "gradually diminished because Sippican Harbor was frozen over and therefore during much of the winter."[^1] Its chronology has George and Betty Blankinship selling the land to the United States on 5 March 1819, and Massachusetts ceding jurisdiction over it on 19 June that year.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated money for the light in March 1819, LighthouseFriends reports, and the rubblestone tower, joined to a stone dwelling by a covered walkway, was activated on 1 September.[^2] The National Register form dates the tower to 1819, but its significance statement says the light was "Constructed in 1817." The sources do not reconcile the two years.[^1]

In his report of 1 November 1838, Naval Lieutenant Edward W. Carpender described a revolving white light of ten lamps and reflectors. He recommended a fixed red light of six lamps without reflectors, "so as to permit the light of all the lamps to be seen from every point of the compass."[^2] In 1842 I. W. P. Lewis visited and found the light still revolving, contrary to Carpender's recommendation.[^2]

LighthouseFriends and the Lighthouse Society's record both give a fourth-order Fresnel lens, installed in 1856.[^2][^3] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 mentions a lens-lantern light "shown in place of fifth-order light" from 28 to 30 September 1898.[^5] The sources disagree on the order of the optic.

A new lantern room went up in 1863.[^2] The Board's annual reports covered the fiscal year to 30 June, and they record routine repairs. For the year to 30 June 1895 the Board reported: "Some 150 feet of close board fence were rebuilt."[^4] For the year to 30 June 1899 it reported: "The old lantern and deck were replaced by new ones."[^6] For the year to 30 June 1900 the boat-house was enlarged and rebuilt, with 286 running feet (87 m) of close board fence built.[^7] LighthouseFriends adds that by 1890 the old stone keeper's house had been demolished and a frame dwelling built on its foundation, and that a bell tower with a fog bell, struck by machinery, followed in 1902.[^2] The Lighthouse Society's record calls the 1890 dwelling stone, so the sources disagree on its material.[^3]

## The keepers

William S. Moore was the first keeper, from 1819 to 1834.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he was a veteran of the War of 1812. It recounts several legends about him and calls one of them "a popular, but unsubstantiated story." The legends are not repeated here.[^2]

John Clark kept the light twice, from 1834 to 1849 and again from 1853 to 1860.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he earned $400 a year. At Lewis's request in 1842 he reported, "There is a well here, but no water," and wrote: "I am therefore obliged to boat my water from the mainland, over two miles distant."[^2]

Peter Murray kept the light from 1891 to 1895. LighthouseFriends tells of a winter when his eleven-month-old son fell ill with pneumonia. Lacking the means to leave the island, Murray put out the tower light, "praying to attract attention." Help came too late, and the family buried the child on the mainland.[^2]

Zimri T. Robinson, known as "Uncle Toby," kept the light from 1895 to 1912. He petitioned to move to Ned's Point, because his advanced age made it difficult for him to row out to the island. The petition was granted in 1912, and the move cut his salary by $50 a year.[^2][^3]

Maurice A. Babcock kept the light from 1919 to 1926, and George T. Gustavus from 1926 until the light was discontinued in 1933.[^3] LighthouseFriends calls Gustavus the light's last keeper.[^2] Between 1867 and 1882 the station had six stints as first assistant, one of them, William A. Simmons, held twice.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In November 1866 the sea swept over the island, filling the well and the cellar and damaging the seawall, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] On 8 September 1869 a storm "of unprecedented severity" swept the island again. It reduced the station "from a condition of perfect order to that of a perfect wreck," carried away the outbuildings, tipped the boathouse, and destroyed 280 feet (85 m) of seawall.[^2] The storm prompted a new seawall, which LighthouseFriends describes as "stout five-foot-thick and seven-foot-tall."[^2]

On 12 September 1923 Keeper Babcock rescued a young man whose small boat had capsized near the station.[^8] LighthouseFriends adds that the man had been clinging to the upset vessel for three or four hours. Babcock took him to the station and then went back to save the boat.[^2]

On 29 March 1930 Keeper Gustavus towed a disabled motorboat safely into Marion Harbor.[^9]

The hurricane of 1938 washed away the outbuildings, according to the National Register form.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the storm surge destroyed every other structure on the island.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Society's record and LighthouseFriends both give 1933 as the year the light was discontinued, its work taken over by lighted buoys.[^3][^2]

The sources do not agree on what happened to the island next. The National Register form says the government sold it to the town in 1939, after it was declared surplus.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the government offered the reservation at auction in 1940, that the highest of seven bids, $654, came from a Bar Harbor, Maine, man, and that Augustus Fiske bought it a month later. It says the town bought the island from Fiske's heirs in 1966 for $2,500.[^2]

The town and the Sippican Historical Society restored the light in 1976, with local contributions of $13,000. An automatic beacon was installed and the tower painted.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the light was relit briefly that year.[^2] The form says the tower was vandalized in 1981 and restored again by the Historical Society.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the Bird Island Preservation Society was formed in 1994, and that the light was relit on 4 July 1997 as a solar-powered automatic light.[^2]

The island's protection needed rebuilding too. The Corps of Engineers' 2017 article says construction on the island started in December 2015 and built a new 1,100-foot (335 m) boulder revetment.[^10] LighthouseFriends dates the Corps' project to 2016.[^2] The article describes the old rock wall as "in poor condition," and says that coastal storms had been wearing away the vegetation and sand that the terns need for nesting.[^10]

## Today

The Town of Marion owns the island and the light.[^2][^3][^10] The tower is closed to visitors, and the island is off limits during the tern nesting season, from mid-May to mid-August, LighthouseFriends reports.[^2] Keep off the island in those months, while the terns are nesting.[^1][^2]

The Lighthouse Society's record lists the light as a private aid to navigation.[^3] It flashes every six seconds, running on solar power.[^1][^2]

The island lies offshore, and LighthouseFriends says the light is best viewed from a boat. From land, distant views are possible from the end of Point Road, at the Kitanset Golf Club.[^2]
