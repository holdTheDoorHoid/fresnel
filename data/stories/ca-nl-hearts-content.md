Heart's Content is a harbour town on the eastern side of Trinity Bay in Newfoundland and Labrador, and its red-and-white cast-iron lighthouse has stood at the harbour entrance since 1901.[^5][^2] The town is better known for the telegraph cable that came ashore there in 1866, and the register says the light was essential to the cable ships that called at the harbour.[^1]

## The place

Heart's Content lies halfway up the Bay de Verde Peninsula, at the entrance to a harbour that the register calls "one of the best in the province," because it is very deep.[^5][^1] Lighthouse Friends describes the harbour as well protected, bounded by Norther Point and Souther Point.[^2] In 1871, Lovell's Newfoundland Directory called the place "the most important [fishing] station on the south side of Trinity Bay," as Lighthouse Friends quotes it.[^2]

Lighthouse Friends quotes a pamphlet by Henry Field, son of the American financier Cyrus W. Field, which says the place "was never heard of outside of Newfoundland till 1864".[^2] In that year Mr. Field, sailing up Trinity Bay in a surveying steamer, "fixed upon this secluded spot" as the place to bring the cable ashore.[^2] A note on the same page, quoting a display at the telegraph station museum, gives 27 July 1866 as the day a cable was hauled ashore there.[^2] The register says the community was chosen in 1866 as the eastern end of the transatlantic telegraph cable, which brought employment and services to the village.[^1]

None of the sources names a particular rock or shoal that the light was built to mark. The register says the cable maintenance ships depended on it for a safe arrival and departure.[^1] The Newfoundland 3D Lighthouse Gallery calls it "a navigational aid to the many cable repair ships that would visit the harbour."[^4]

## Building the light

In 1893, Lighthouse Inspector John T. Nevill noted that surveys of sites on the south side of Trinity Bay had been made, and that Heart's Content had been chosen.[^2] A road was built from the harbour to Southern Head, and a cast-iron tower made at the Victoria Works in St. John's was landed at Heart's Content, ready to be put up in the spring of 1894.[^2]

Work on the tower began in 1894, but it was stopped by executive order before it got far.[^2] The tower and its materials were sent to Motion Island, off Random Head, where a cylindrical tower and dwelling were finished that year.[^2][^4] The Random Head light did not show until the following year, because its optical apparatus was delayed.[^2]

Heart's Content had to wait until 1901 for its own light.[^2] The register gives that as the build date and calls the cast-iron tower "inexpensive to build and easy to erect."[^5][^1] Lighthouse Friends describes a circular iron tower, painted red and white in a spiral, with a small dwelling joined to its southern side by a covered passage.[^2] The register gives the tower's height as 8.7 m (28.5 ft), and the Coast Guard list gives the same figure.[^5][^3] The register compares its gallery to the enclosed lookouts at the tops of ships' masts.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends says the tower first showed a fixed red light, with a focal plane of 25.5 m.[^2] The sources disagree about the light today. The Coast Guard list gives a white light that flashes every four seconds, with a focal height of 25.2 m.[^3] The Newfoundland 3D Lighthouse Gallery describes an occulting white light every six seconds, at 25 m, reaching out to 8 nautical miles.[^4] This story follows the official list.[^3]

## The keepers

Barzillia Budden was the first keeper of the light, from 1901 to 1905, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^2] Robert Piercey followed, listed from 1906 until at least 1912.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives John Warren's service as running from the 1910s to the 1940s, Hubert Warren's from the 1940s to 1958, and Selena Warren's as beginning in 1958, with no end date.[^2] The sources do not say how the three Warrens were related, or whether they were.

## After the keepers

The light's source was changed to electricity in 1931, after which a caretaker was contracted to look after it.[^2] The telegraph station remained in operation until 1965.[^2] In 1990 the tower was made a Recognized Federal Heritage Building, and the register records it as a heritage lighthouse recognized on 30 July 2015.[^2][^5]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^2] From the north end of town, a road runs west along the harbour for 1.3 km (0.8 miles) to the lighthouse, Lighthouse Friends says.[^2]

The register says the town's local economy "depends heavily on tourism," and that the lighthouse is "one of the most visited attractions in town."[^1] The register says the site "provides a view of all of Trinity Bay" and is "a popular spot for sighting whales and seabirds."[^1] The Coast Guard list shows the light operating all year round.[^3]
