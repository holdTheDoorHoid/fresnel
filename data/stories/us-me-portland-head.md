Portland Head Light stands on a rocky headland at the mouth of the main ship channel into Portland Harbor, in Cape Elizabeth, Maine. Its stone tower was first lit on 10 January 1791, and it was the first lighthouse the new federal government finished.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Portland, called Falmouth until 1786, was the sixth busiest port in the United States by the 1790s. In 1784, 74 merchants petitioned the Massachusetts government (Maine was then part of Massachusetts) for a light on the headland. The deaths of two people in a shipwreck at Bangs (now Cushing) Island in 1787 are given as the spur for the appropriation of 150 pounds in June 1789, and work began that August.[^4]

Money ran short. By the time Congress took over the country's lighthouses the Massachusetts project was unfinished, and in August 1790 the Treasury Secretary, Alexander Hamilton, allotted up to $1,500 to complete it.[^5][^3]

## Building the light

The masons were two local men, John Nichols and Jonathan Bryant (the National Register nomination's typescript is badly scanned and may print the first name as "Michols").[^2] They built the tower of rubblestone, taken from nearby fields and the shore.[^2] The plan was a 58-foot (18 m) tower, but the builders were told to make it 72 feet (22 m) so the light would not be hidden from the south; Bryant resigned over the change and Nichols finished the work.[^4][^5] The nomination gives the finished height as 72 feet with a 15-foot (4.6 m) lantern.[^2] Putnam's 1917 history of the service says instead that the original tower "is stated to have been thirty feet in height"; the construction accounts and the nomination say 72 feet, so the sources disagree.[^3][^2][^4]

The light first shone on 10 January 1791, from whale-oil lamps.[^2][^4] Lighthouse Friends counts sixteen whale-oil lamps at the first lighting.[^5] In 1910 keeper Joseph Strout, speaking of an earlier keeper, told the *Lewiston Journal* that Captain Joshua Freeman had kept the light "in the days when they burned whale oil and had sixteen lamps."[^4]

The tower has been altered many times. In 1813 the contractor Winslow Lewis, who judged the upper part poorly built, took the top off the tower (twenty feet in the National Register nomination, about twenty-five in New England Lighthouses) and installed a lamp-and-reflector system of his own design for $2,100.[^2][^4][^5] A fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the reflectors in 1855, when the tower was also lined with brick and given a cast-iron stairway.[^1][^4][^5] After the steamer *Bohemian* was lost near Portland in 1864 with some 40 immigrants aboard, the Lighthouse Board raised the tower twenty feet and installed a second-order lens; Putnam gives the result as an 80-foot tower in 1865.[^4][^5][^3] In 1883 the Lighthouse Board removed the roughly twenty-foot brick addition and put a fourth-order lens in a new lantern, having decided that Halfway Rock Light (1871) had lessened the station's importance; less than two years later it reversed itself, raised the light twenty feet again and, from 15 January 1885, showed a second-order fixed white light.[^4][^5] The National Register nomination tells it as a removal of 21 feet in 1883, followed by a rebuilding in brick "due to public reaction."[^2]

In 1900 the Light-House Board reported that the "rubble tower, built in 1790, was extensively repointed, and many of the stones were removed and replaced."[^6]

## The keepers

The first keeper was Captain Joseph Greenleaf, a Revolutionary War veteran appointed in Washington's presidency. At first he was paid nothing: his pay was the house and the right to fish and farm. By November 1791 he was saying he could not afford to stay. In a letter of June 1792 he described the previous winter, when ice on the lantern glass grew so thick he had to melt it off. A salary of $160 a year began in 1793.[^4][^5] Greenleaf died in October 1795 while in his boat on the Fore River; the *Eastern Argus* wrote that he had "faithfully discharged his duty to the satisfaction of those who occupy their business on great waters."[^4]

A blacksmith, Barzillai Delano, who had lobbied for the post, took over in 1796. The roster at the US Lighthouse Society archive lists "Dave Duncan (1796-1796)" before him; the New England Lighthouses history names a short-lived Reuben Freeman instead.[^1][^4][^5] Delano asked in 1809 for a covered way between house and tower, because the path was steep, rocky and often iced over or washed by the sea. It came in 1816, when Henry Dyer built a new stone keeper's house for $1,175. In 1812 a petition of 22 signatures raised Delano's pay from $225 to $300.[^4][^5] He died in 1820, and his son James Delano was keeper from 1854 to 1861.[^1][^4]

Joshua Freeman, keeper from 1820 to 1840, was remembered for hospitality. He sold drinks to visiting fishermen at three cents a glass, and was said to sit with a coil of rope beside him in case a wreck came suddenly.[^4][^5] An *Eastern Argus* writer of 1825 recommended the trip out to the light: "I know of no excursion as pleasant as a jaunt to the Light House. There our friend Freeman is always at home, and ready to serve you."[^4]

John F. Watts, keeper from 1849 to 1853, complained to an inspector that nobody had told him how to tend the light, so that he had paid a man out of his own pocket to teach him over two days.[^4][^5]

The family most tied to the place is the Strouts. Captain Joshua Freeman Strout, born in Cape Elizabeth and named for Freeman, whom his mother had served as housekeeper, came ashore after a fall from a ship's masthead and became head keeper in 1869 at $620 a year. His wife Mary was paid $480 as first assistant until 1877, when their son Joseph took the post. The couple raised eleven children at the station.[^4][^5][^1] In an interview of 1898 Joshua said he had once gone seventeen years without time off, and two years without getting as far as Portland.[^4][^5] He retired in 1904 and Joseph became head keeper until 1928, so that the family's service at Portland Head covered about 59 years.[^1][^4]

Storm days gave the household its stories. A parrot warned of fog from its perch; one source names the bird Mickey, another Billy and gives the words "Joe, let's start the horn. It's foggy!"[^4][^5]

After the Strouts came John Cameron (1928-29) and Frank O. Hilt, head keeper from 1929 to 1944.[^1] Hilt, from St. George, Maine, had served on Matinicus Rock, and built a giant checkerboard near the tower base.[^4] Robert Thayer Sterling, an assistant from 1928 and head keeper from 1944, wrote *Lighthouses of the Maine Coast and the Men Who Keep Them* (1935) and called Portland Head the most desirable of all light stations for a keeper.[^1][^4][^5] He retired in 1946, the last civilian keeper before the Coast Guard.[^4][^5] The archive lists Coast Guard head keepers from William L. Lockhart (1946-1950) to Davis Simpson (1986-1989).[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Fog was the station's other work. The Light-House Board reported that the second-class Daboll trumpet ran 939 hours and burned about 11 tons of coal in the year reported in 1894, and 1,013 hours and about 14 tons in the year reported in 1895.[^7][^8]

On 8 September 1869 a hurricane knocked the fog bell into a ravine; Joshua Strout, newly keeper, was nearly killed. A new bell tower followed the next year, and the bell was later replaced by a trumpet.[^4] Lighthouse Friends gives the damage as the loss of the bell tower.[^5]

The gale of Wednesday 28 December 1887 was, Captain Strout told the *Portland Daily Press*, the worst he had ever known at the station. The paper described seas breaking on the outer rock, rushing into the chasm between it and the tower and sending spray over the lantern, "and sometimes sheets of solid water" onto the roof of the keeper's house. The one-storey brick engine house and fog-signal station, 25 by 25 feet, was shifted more than eighteen inches on its foundation by one wave and its plaster cracked; the fog whistle and engines were tested the next morning and found all right.[^11] Lighthouse Friends says instead that the building was "shattered" and replaced; the newspaper, printed two days after the gale, reports a shifted building that was tested and working, not a destroyed one.[^5][^11]

Late on Christmas Eve 1886 the British bark *Annie C. Maguire* struck the ledges at Portland Head. The Strouts got a ladder across the rocks to the vessel and everyone aboard, including the captain's wife, came ashore. Lighthouse Friends counts fourteen people, including a boy, and says Mary Strout tore blankets into strips and soaked them in kerosene for torches; Joseph Strout later remembered that the family had killed eight chickens for Christmas and fed the starved crew a chicken pie.[^4][^5] The *Portland Daily Press* of 29 December 1886 reported the wreck sold at auction to a Thomas Towle, for a price its scanned text prints as "8175", probably $175; New England Lighthouses says a storm destroyed the vessel on New Year's Day 1887, after everything of value had been removed.[^12][^4] A rock near the tower still carries the painted record of the wreck, which later keepers kept up.[^4][^5]

Gun practice at neighbouring Fort Williams did harm too. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of September 1916 reported that the keepers' buildings were "directly in range of some of the guns at Fort Williams" and had lost windows, finish and roof, with chimneys broken in the attic of the double dwelling; casings and roof openings stopped the trouble.[^9][^3] The light was darkened from June 1942 to June 1945 as a wartime precaution.[^5]

## After the keepers

Portland Head was automated in 1989.[^1] On 7 August 1989 the Coast Guard held a ceremony marking the 200th anniversary of the Lighthouse Service and the removal of the keepers.[^4] The Museum at Portland Head Light opened in the former keeper's house in 1992, and in October 1993 the property was deeded to the Town of Cape Elizabeth.[^4]

## Today

The light is an active aid to navigation and the town of Cape Elizabeth is the owner.[^1] The grounds are in Fort Williams Park, a public park open from sunrise to sunset, and the museum and gift shop keep seasonal hours from Memorial Day to Indigenous Peoples Day. The tower itself is open to the public only one day a year, Maine Open Lighthouse Day.[^10] The tower's old second-order lens is displayed in the museum.[^4] The station is on the National Register of Historic Places (listed 1973).[^2][^1]
