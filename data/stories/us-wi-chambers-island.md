Chambers Island Light stands at the northwest tip of Chambers Island, roughly seven miles (11 km) offshore from Fish Creek, in Green Bay.[^1] It was first lit in October 1868 to mark the west passage into the bay.[^1][^2] The light was automated in 1955 and lost its lantern in 1958. The land has been a town park since 1976, when the Town of Gibraltar received the deed.[^1]

## The place

Chambers Island is the largest island in Green Bay.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it was named in 1816 after Colonel John Chambers, second-in-command to Colonel John Miller, when Miller sailed past on his way to build Fort Howard at the mouth of the Fox River.[^1]

In 1837 G. J. Pendergrast, reporting to the Board of Navy Commissioners after a tour of the Great Lakes, wrote that the island "is exactly in the track of vessels," with shoals and reefs scattered around it. LighthouseFriends prints his report.[^1] More than thirty years passed before the light was built. In 1865 a Lighthouse Board committee examined Green Bay's commerce, and Congress appropriated $25,000 on 20 July 1866 for a light on Chambers Island, range lights at the Fox River entrance, and a beacon on Peshtigo Reef.[^1] Colonel W. F. Raynolds chose a peninsula on the northwest side of the island, and Lewis and Anna Williams deeded thirty acres of it to the federal government for $250.[^1]

The National Register nomination, quoting Hjalmar Holand's history of Door County, says the light was erected in 1868 "to mark the west passage of Green Bay."[^2]

## Building the light

A construction crew from Detroit landed in early June 1868, with cut stone, brick, lime and lumber, carried on the steamer Rocket.[^1] Chambers Island and Eagle Bluff were built the same year. LighthouseFriends describes both dwellings and their kitchens as one-and-a-half-story cream-colored Milwaukee brick. The nomination calls the Chambers brick buff-colored, and says the Chambers dwelling "appears to have been built from the same plans used for the Eagle Bluff Lighthouse."[^1][^2] The towers differ. LighthouseFriends describes a square base with an octagonal upper section, while the nomination describes a three-story octagonal tower built into the northeast corner of the building.[^1][^2]

A decagonal lantern housed a revolving fourth-order Fresnel lens that flashed white every sixty seconds.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the tower as 44 feet 9 inches (13.6 m) from its base to the ventilator ball, with the focal plane at 68 feet (20.7 m).[^1] The nomination gives the focal plane as "approximately 638 feet M.S.L.," a different measure, and the sources are not reconciled.[^2]

Improvements followed through the 1890s. The Board's report for the fiscal year 1893 says the landing wharf and the boatways were repaired and a new barn was built.[^3] In the fiscal years 1896 and 1897 the boathouse was moved 75 feet (23 m) nearer the lake and rebuilt, and new boatways were constructed.[^4] LighthouseFriends dates a brick oil house and a summer kitchen to 1898.[^1]

The light's character changed in 1899. The Board's report for the fiscal year 1899 records apparatus, including a mercury float, shipped to the station.[^5] The report for the fiscal year 1900 says the characteristic "was changed on July 15, 1899, by reducing the interval between the flashes from one minute to thirty seconds," and describes the new light as "fixed white," varied by flashes.[^6] LighthouseFriends gives the same thirty-second flash.[^1] The same report's list of changes, however, gives 13 July for the change.[^6]

## The keepers

Lewis S. Williams was 48 when he became the first keeper, LighthouseFriends says, and he had been living on the island since the early 1850s. He and his wife had eleven children, one of them born after he was appointed.[^1] He made the first lighting on 1 October 1868, less than four months after the building crew arrived.[^1][^7] He kept a large strawberry patch near the lighthouse. LighthouseFriends reports that in 1875 a party of twelve residents of Menominee were given strawberry pie and strawberries and cream by the keeper.[^1] Williams retired in 1889 after twenty-one years.[^1][^7]

Later keepers included Peter Knudsen (1889–1891), Charles E. Young (1891–1895), Soren Christianson (1895–1900), Joseph Napiezinski (1900–1906), and Jens J. Rollefson (1906–1925).[^7] LighthouseFriends says that only Rollefson came close to Williams's length of service.[^1] The assistants included George W. Drew (1903–1909), Samuel O. Hanson, Edward R. Ledwell and Theodore Jacobson, who was assistant from 1925 to 1941.[^7]

Claude F. Chapman was head keeper from 1925 to 1933, and Jacobson was his assistant when the keepers made the winter trip described below.[^1][^9] Alfred L. Cornell was transferred from Green Island when that light was automated in 1933. He retired on 1 December 1941, and Jacobson, his assistant, replaced him.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' record of assistance for the fiscal year 1916 names "J. J. Rollefson, keeper; E. R. Ledwell, assistant keeper" at Chambers Island Light Station. It credits them with giving food and clothing to three shipwrecked sailors whose motor boat was destroyed by fire, and with taking the men to Marinette, Wisconsin.[^8] LighthouseFriends tells the same rescue.[^1]

In the winter of 1931, the Bulletin reports, the acetylene winter light at Chambers Island was reported not to be burning. The keepers "journeyed 30 miles by automobile," then "traversed 7 miles of ice on skiis" and tied themselves to one another with a long rope in case either went through.[^9] The Bulletin's 1930 to 1935 index lists "skiis needed to reach Chambers Island" at page 56.[^10]

In January 1875 one of the Williams sons set out across the ice to Eagle Harbor with a companion and a horse. They led the horse over a crack, which they found had been much wider earlier, but the horse broke through the ice, and the men walked the rest of the way.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1917 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin listed Chambers Island among the lights changed from oil to incandescent oil vapor.[^11] A marker radiobeacon was established on the island in 1947.[^1]

In a cost-saving move in 1955, the Coast Guard automated the light and removed the landing pier and boatways.[^1] The nomination says the Coast Guard vacated the lighthouse and leased it for an insignificant sum to an adjacent property owner, who occasionally used it as a summer retreat.[^2] The nomination also says that in the late 1950s a forest fire "swept" this end of the island, destroying earlier nearby frame buildings.[^2]

The lantern room was removed from the tower in 1958, and LighthouseFriends reports that the lens was purportedly sold at a surplus auction.[^1] A short skeletal tower carried a beacon above the tower until the Coast Guard erected a 97-foot (29.6 m) skeletal tower near the oil house in 1961.[^1] On 10 March 1976 the station's land, except the small parcel under the skeletal tower, was deeded to the Town of Gibraltar for park purposes. The park opened the following year.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is in the Town of Gibraltar's park. The town says visitors can use the public dock and walk about a mile and a half to the lighthouse, and that the lighthouse is open for public tours when a volunteer is on site.[^12] LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds are open and the dwelling and tower are closed.[^1] The skeletal beacon stands on its own parcel, not the town's.[^1] The nomination describes an automated light that continues the service, and the lighthouse is listed on the National Register.[^2][^12]
