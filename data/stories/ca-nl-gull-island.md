Gull Island Light, in Newfoundland and Labrador, stands on a barren island about 8 km (5 mi) off Cape John, the northern tip of the Baie Verte Peninsula, where ships make for Notre Dame Bay.[^1][^2] A cast-iron tower was lit there in 1884. Seventeen years earlier the brigantine *Queen of Swansea* had been wrecked on the same island, and eleven of the people who reached it died there.[^1][^5]

## The place

The heritage statement calls Gull Island "a remote, barren and rocky island off Newfoundland's north coast."[^2] Lighthouse Friends says it was a menace to vessels entering Notre Dame Bay.[^1] The heritage statement ties the light to "the development of safe navigation in pre-Confederation Newfoundland," and says it came as the Grand Banks fishery was growing.[^2]

The ship that wrecked there had sailed from St. John's with mail and passengers for Tilt Cove, a mining settlement on Notre Dame Bay.[^1]

## Building the light

The Newfoundland Board of Works erected the light in 1884.[^3] The heritage statement says it was one of perhaps 38 cast-iron structures built in Newfoundland before reinforced concrete arrived, and that at least 26 survive. It was designed and cast in Britain by a firm the statement does not name.[^3] The register says the tower was "shipped from England and assembled on-site."[^2]

The tower is a cast-iron cylinder, 11.6 m (38 ft) high, with a gallery on iron brackets and a lantern of two tiers of triangular glazing.[^3] The light shows a white flash every ten seconds, from a focal height of 160 m (525 ft), with a nominal range of 17 nautical miles.[^4] A fog alarm building with a keeper's dwelling was added in 1916. In 1960 and 1961 two new residences were built for the keepers, and two diesel generators supplied the station.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends says Captain Mark Rowsell, whose men had found the remains of the *Queen*'s people in April 1868, was appointed the first lightkeeper in 1884.[^1]

The later keepers, in the order Lighthouse Friends gives them, were William Purchase, George Morey, George Ridout (1905 to 1920), Norman Welshman, Ephraim Whalen (at least 1922) and Percy Winsor. Most of the names carry no dates in that list.[^1]

Until the late 1970s three families lived on the island for nine months of the year. Their school-age children boarded with friends or relatives on the mainland.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The *Queen of Swansea* sailed from St. John's on 6 December 1867. Lighthouse Friends says a gale drove her several miles out to sea, and that after six days she grounded in a narrow gulch on the island, in a snow squall.[^1]

The captain's journal, as an 1868 letter printed it, says the ship made the island "on the 11th, at 4 o'clock p.m." The same letter gives the day she was lost: she "was actually lost on Cape John, Gull Island, on December 12." Early reports had placed her near Twillingate.[^5]

All fifteen people aboard came off the ship, Lighthouse Friends says. Three crew and a passenger went back to the vessel for food, according to Lighthouse Friends; the 1868 letter says four of the crew did.[^1][^5] While they were aboard the rope parted, a wave carried the ship out to sea, and the men and the ship were never heard of again.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends says eleven were left on the island.[^1] The 1868 letter says that men who came ashore later found "the bodies of nine men and two women."[^5] The same vessel, "with about sixty" men aboard, went back the next day to take the bodies to Tilt Cove for burial. The letter says some were frozen together so that they had to be "separated with pries and crowbars."[^5]

The letter says the captain and Mr. Dowsley had kept a journal "up to the 24th December, twelve days after they were wrecked." It adds that Dowsley wrote he did not expect his body would ever be found.[^5] Lighthouse Friends calls him Dr. Felix Dowsley, appointed to the medical staff at Tilt Cove.[^1] The 1868 letter calls him Mr. Dowsley.[^5]

The letter also says that a man ashore "saw a light several times on the island" and told others, "but they would not believe" him.[^5]

Lighthouse Friends says a monument was "erected in the cemetery at Tilt Cove."[^1]

## After the keepers

Rowlett's Lighthouse Directory says the historic cast-iron tower has been inactive since 2014, and that the active light "has been moved to a 5 m (17 ft) skeletal tower." It adds that ownership was being transferred in 2014, without saying to whom.[^6]

The Coast Guard's list still gives the light, marked "Seasonal."[^4] Lighthouse Friends says the light was left running unattended during the harsh winter months.[^1] The tower was classified a Recognized Federal Heritage Building in 1989, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse. Lighthouse Friends says the grounds and tower are closed, and that the light is best seen by boat or from the air.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory says the site and tower are closed, and that the island is reached only by helicopter or by boat in heavy seas.[^6]

This is not the Gull Island Light at Cape Freels, in Bonavista Bay, which the Coast Guard lists separately.[^7]
