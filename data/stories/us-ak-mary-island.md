Mary Island Light stands on Cross Point at the north end of Mary Island, in the Revillagigedo Channel of southeast Alaska, just inside the border with Canada. The first light here, a wooden octagon lit in 1903, was one of the stations built to guide Klondike-era traffic up the Inside Passage; the concrete light and fog-signal building that replaced it in 1936 to 1937 is the only station building left.[^1]

## The place

The island sits where ships coming north from British Columbia turn through the Revillagigedo Channel toward Tongass Narrows and Behm Canal, which the National Register nomination calls "a turning point."[^1] The nomination calls it the first location identified for a full light station since the initial survey of the late 1860s. The Light-House Board began pressing Congress for money for a light and fog signal on the island in 1890 and was ignored.[^1]

A customs house opened at the north end of the island, in 1892 according to the nomination (LighthouseFriends gives 1891).[^1][^6] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 already used it to press its case, and the report for 1893 repeated the passage: the customs house meant that "many vessels are obliged to make this a place of call," and a small, inexpensive light was proposed, for which an estimate of $800 appears in the Board's estimate tables.[^4][^3] Congress did not act.[^1] The gold rushes that began with the Klondike strikes of 1896 and 1897 changed that. In 1900 Congress authorized money for study and construction of primary light stations, the chief inspector and chief engineer of the Thirteenth District ranked Mary Island fourth of fifteen sites, and President McKinley signed the order reserving the land on 4 January 1901.[^1]

## Building the light

Plans were drawn in the engineering office of the Thirteenth Light-House District at Portland, Oregon, under W. C. Langfist, and went to the Board in December 1901.[^1] Work started in the summer of 1902 and went slowly. On 6 September 1902 the Ketchikan *Mining Journal* reported, as the nomination quotes it, that work was "proceeding slowly, owing to the difficulty experienced in landing material," and that the department had ordered a change of site after the contractor had already landed most of his material at the first point.[^1] The nomination says the first plan put the light farther north and that the prominent Cross Point was chosen shortly after work began.[^1] LighthouseFriends names the contractors as R. M. Henningsen and Thorvald Olsen.[^6]

The station was an eight-sided, one-story frame building, 32 by 32 feet (10 by 10 m), with an octagonal shingled tower rising through its middle and a 12-foot (3.7 m) brass Daboll trumpet projecting from the east face as the fog signal. The lantern held a fourth-order Fresnel lens made in 1902 by Chance Brothers of England, showing a fixed light (given as "fixed white" in the Bulletin of 1918) from 67 feet 6 inches (20.6 m) above mean tide, with a 12-degree red sector to the north to cover the shore, Point Winslow and the Twin Islands.[^1][^10] A Skagway paper of 25 June 1903 carried the Light-House Board's notice that the station would be in operation "next month."[^5] The nomination says the light began in mid-July; LighthouseFriends gives 15 July 1903.[^1][^6]

Later changes are less certain. The nomination says generators and batteries were installed in 1927, raising the light from 600 to 6,000 candlepower, and that the light was an occulting white by 1933.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1918 had already listed Mary Island among lights changed from "fixed white, oil" to "flashing white, acetylene," and LighthouseFriends dates electrification and a flasher to 1926.[^10][^6] The sources do not agree on the sequence. A radiobeacon was added in 1931, the fourth in the Lighthouse Service by the nomination's count, and the Bulletin for 1932 lists Mary Island among the latest additions on the Pacific coast.[^1][^12]

The concrete station followed in 1936 to 1937 (LighthouseFriends says 1936 to 1938).[^6] Edward W. Laird designed it under Assistant District Superintendent Dwight W. Chase in the district office at Ketchikan, the fifth of six Art Deco and Moderne light and fog-signal buildings raised in Alaska between 1931 and 1940. Work began in September 1936 under foreman M. Harris, with the Lighthouse Service as its own contractor and labor hired in Ketchikan.[^1] The tower stands about 52 feet (16 m) above grade in front of a 36 by 20 foot (11 by 6 m) fog-signal building, and the old lantern was moved to the top of it.[^1] The old tower was demolished to make room.[^1]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends names David Crosby as the first head keeper, with Carl Peterson as his assistant; the U.S. Lighthouse Society archive lists Crosby from 1903 to 1905 and Carl E. Peterson in 1903.[^6][^2] Sylvenus F. Shepard took over in 1905 and stayed until 1922.[^2] Shepard's report on the 1907 garden, as LighthouseFriends prints it, said that "Lettuce and radishes could be grown here by the ton without trouble."[^6] In 1917 the Lighthouse Service listed Mary Island, with Shepard as keeper, among the stations that won efficiency flags for the 1916 fiscal year, a flag the station would keep until the end of December 1917.[^9]

The deadliest event the sources record began on 30 December 1920. The assistant keeper Herbert C. Scott and Leslie Williams of Ketchikan died of exposure on the island, and Charles Chapman of Ketchikan, who had gone with them, reached the station "in a nearly exhausted condition."[^7] The Bulletin said the men were trying to help a boat that had been wrecked near the station. The Juneau *Alaska Daily Empire* described the three as having come over from Ketchikan for an outing and some days of hunting, and said that Scott, aged 62, who had gone to Ketchikan in 1880, was frozen to death while Williams fell over a precipice; the paper put Scott's death on 2 January.[^8] The two accounts do not match on why the men were out or on the date of the deaths. The Lighthouse Service drew a lesson from it: keepers in remote places "should familiarize themselves with the physical characteristics of the country" around their stations.[^7]

Glenn E. Maddox came as assistant keeper in 1924 and was head keeper in 1930 and 1931.[^2] The Bulletin reported that in March 1929 he used an amateur radio set at the lighthouse to get a distress call to the cable office at Ketchikan, after the keepers sighted the boat *Fairbanks*, which had burned out a main bearing off Tree Point on the early morning of 7 March and was a practically helpless vessel; a boat sent out from the station could not reach her. A vessel then left and picked her up after she had drifted 18 hours.[^11] Loran S. O'Connor, assistant from 1929, was head keeper from 1931 to 1944.[^2] The Coast Guard took over the Lighthouse Service in 1939.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The nomination found that no disastrous wrecks happened within range of the station; the shipwreck inventory shows mostly gas launches in the neighborhood, with losses to the south around Duke Island and to the north near Point Alava and Hogg Rocks.[^1] The deaths of 1920 and the *Fairbanks* rescue are the events the sources tie most closely to the station.

## After the keepers

By 1960 only one keeper's house was still in direct use, and the second was demolished soon after. LighthouseFriends instead says one dwelling stood empty until 1964, when it was sold to a logger and towed away on a float.[^1][^6] The station was automated in 1969 with four Coast Guard crewmen on the island at the time.[^1] The nomination says the remaining buildings were removed in 1974, with a contract that called for burning them and putting the debris in the house basements; LighthouseFriends says the last dwelling was moved to Ketchikan in 1970.[^1][^6]

What became of the Fresnel lens is unclear. The nomination reports that it "reportedly remained as part of the automated light system" and that its whereabouts were not known; LighthouseFriends says a Fresnel lens from the station was on display in a shipwreck exhibit at the Juneau-Douglas Museum in 2005.[^1][^6] By 1995 a solar-powered light had been mounted on the outside of the tower, leaving the old lantern room to protect a battery.[^1] In May 1997 a former coastguardsman was awarded a lease on the lighthouse, planning a bed and breakfast, but the plan did not go ahead.[^6] The station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2005.[^13]

## Today

The tower is still an active aid to navigation, and the U.S. Coast Guard owned it when the station was nominated for the National Register.[^2][^1] It is not open to the public.[^2] The island is remote and no keeper's quarters remain.[^2] LighthouseFriends lists two air-tour operators and a cruise out of Ketchikan that offer a distant view of the light.[^6]
