Cobb Point Bar Light stood from 1889 on a screwpile foundation at the western side of the entrance to the Wicomico River, where that river meets the Potomac in Maryland.[^1][^2] Its square wooden keepers' house was severely damaged by fire in December 1939 and was dismantled in 1940. A small automatic light stands on the old foundation today.[^1][^2]

## The place

The Board's reports of the early 1890s spell the station's name "Cob Point Bar," and the archive and LighthouseFriends use "Cobb." The station marked the mouth of the Wicomico, where bars projecting from both sides nearly closed the river, so that sailing vessels rarely tried to enter or leave at night.[^9][^2]

The Board's report for 1885, as LighthouseFriends reproduces it, says that three steamboat lines ran into the Wicomico from the Potomac, carrying oysters, tobacco and other goods.[^2] It adds that as many as 350 vessels had anchored at one time inside the bar, and that the river mouth was so nearly closed by the bars on either side that a vessel avoiding one was in danger of being stranded on the other.[^2] In 1883 the steamer Sue went out and ran upon the bar, after a mistake of one minute in the time of running, LighthouseFriends reports.[^2]

## Building the light

In 1885 the Board asked for $15,000 for a lighthouse at the point, according to LighthouseFriends. In 1887 it repeated the recommendation at $25,000.[^2] Congress appropriated $15,000 on 2 October 1888, according to the Board's report as LighthouseFriends quotes it.[^2] The two requests disagree in amount, and the appropriation is the figure this story follows.

The light was to be built on the same plan as the Tangier Sound light, and the metal work was due at Baltimore by August 1889.[^2] The superstructure was framed at the Lazaretto depot in August. Erection began on 5 November, and by the end of the month it was complete except for painting.[^2]

The new structure was a square wooden dwelling on five iron screw piles, with a fixed white light of the fourth order. The light was first shown on the night of 25 December 1889. When required, a fog bell struck by machinery sounded every fifteen seconds.[^2]

In 1893 the Board made arrangements for a red sector in the light, to mark, in conjunction with one in the Lower Cedar Point light, the fifteen- and seventeen-foot (4.6 and 5.2 m) lumps in the Kettle Bottom Shoals. The sector was shown from 15 August 1893.[^9][^3] In April 1894 an additional red sector was established to cut two buoys marking part of the Dukehart Channel, LighthouseFriends reports.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive lists 42 stints at the station between 1890 and 1940.[^1] James L. Weems, Sr., was the first head keeper, from 1890 to 1891.[^1] Ole O. Johnsen served as head keeper in three stints, from 1910 to 1920, 1921 to 1928 and 1929 to 1939, with other keepers in charge briefly in between.[^1] Robert Kuhn was an assistant from 1912 to 1915 and again from 1920 to 1921.[^1]

Alaska D. Jennett was first assistant from 1930 to 1940, and it was his property that was lost in the fire of 1939, LighthouseFriends says. The archive spells his name Jennett, and LighthouseFriends spells it Jennette.[^1][^2] James W. Wilson was the last head keeper, from 1939 to 1940.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 13 April 1914 the Service's bulletin records that keeper O. O. Johnsen and assistant Robert Kuhn assisted two men who had a disabled lumber-laden power boat near the light, and gave them shelter.[^4]

In September 1917 Ole O. Johnson, keeper, and George H. Cullison assisted five people in a disabled motor boat, LighthouseFriends reports.[^2]

Ice was a hazard here.[^5] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1920, says that 800 tons of riprap were deposited on the southeast side of the station, "forming an ice breaker and protecting same from ice fields carried against it by the ebb tide of the Potomac River."[^5]

In fiscal 1921 repairs were made to the station's metal substructure, the Commissioner's report says.[^6]

On 26 June 1927, during a heavy gale, Keeper O. Johnson (the archive's Ole O. Johnsen) and assistant W. A. Gibbs rescued five people adrift in a small skiff. The Service's 1927 bulletin says the keeper sailed about a mile from the station to reach them.[^7]

In October 1928 assistant keeper Thomas J. Steinhise helped the boat Demo, which had gone aground near the station with passengers on board, LighthouseFriends reports.[^2]

Fire struck the station on 7 December 1939. A Coast Guard board of investigation found the cause unknown, and imputed no fault or negligence to Alaska D. Jennett, who had claimed for personal property lost in the fire. LighthouseFriends says the superstructure was severely damaged.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Service placed an automatic light on the screwpile foundation in 1940, after the fire.[^2] The archive records the disposition as: "House Dismantled 1940, Foundation Serves Small Automatic Light."[^1]

The Bulletin's index for 1924 to 1929 lists the station as "used in aerial navigation."[^8]

## Today

The keepers' house is gone, and the archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that a modern light is shown at the location today.[^2] No source describes a visit.
