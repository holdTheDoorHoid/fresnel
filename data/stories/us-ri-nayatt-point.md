Nayatt Point Light stood on the western tip of Nayatt Point, on the Barrington shore at the mouth of the Providence River. From 1828 to 1868 it guided ships up the river past the shoals. Its keeper's house, the oldest surviving one in Rhode Island, has been a private home since 1890.[^1]

## The place

The light was built on the east side of the entrance to the Providence River.[^1] The National Register nomination says it was meant to "warn whaling ships, fishing boats and coastal freighters en route to Providence of sandbars and rocks between Nayatt Point and Conimicut Point, directly across the river."[^1] The point and the reef off Conimicut face each other across a narrow passage, and the light marked that passage.[^1][^3]

Congress paid for the first light. LighthouseFriends says Congress appropriated $3,500 for it in May 1828, and that a deed to the site was recorded that June. The deed, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, conveyed the land "to John Quincy Adams, President of the United States, for the use of the United States."[^3]

## Building the light

The first light was a "freestanding octagonal tower," and a small keeper's house was built nearby the same year.[^1] A Navy officer's report of 1838, as quoted by LighthouseFriends, described the tower as built of brick and 23 feet (7 m) high. It said that "Vessels arriving at or departing from Providence river are benefited by this light."[^3]

The first contract came with a catch. The law then required the government to take the lowest bid. A Treasury letter, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, put the point plainly: "As there is no option left us by law but to accept the lowest offer."[^3] LighthouseFriends says the first tower began to fall apart as soon as it was finished.[^3]

The second tower came in 1856. The dwelling was enlarged with a brick wing, and "a second tower, attached to the new addition," replaced the first.[^1] Congress paid $6,500 that August for the new tower, its lighting apparatus and the site. LighthouseFriends says the new light began showing on 25 December 1856.[^3] The nomination describes the present tower as a "three-story square brick tower," and says it "retains most of its original features."[^1]

The archive's station record gives the original optic as "6 14 inch reflectors, FOURTH ORDER, FRESNEL," with the light 31 feet (9.4 m) above the water.[^2] It dates the original lens to 1863.[^2] LighthouseFriends says a fourth-order Fresnel lens was used in the new lighthouse of 1856.[^3] The two sources disagree on when the fourth-order lens was installed.

## The keepers

The archive's roster names eight principal keepers between 1828 and 1868, beginning with Daniel Wightman (1828 to 1845) and ending with Davis Perry (1865 to 1868).[^2]

In August 1868, about two months before the light went out, a church sewing circle held an outing at Nayatt. Most of the guests came from Providence by steamer, and some of them "visited Nayatt Light." The *Pawtucket Gazette and Chronicle* says that "the gentleman in charge kindly exhibiting the lamp to all who visited it during the day."[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1855 a winter storm damaged the station. LighthouseFriends says a strong winter storm nearly destroyed the seawall that protected the lighthouse, that the foundation was undermined, and that the tower cracked from top to bottom.[^3] A survey that followed found the old tower not worth the cost of repair, which led to the 1856 replacement.[^3]

In March 1875 ice destroyed the keeper's house at the Conimicut beacon, across the river.[^1][^3] The nomination says that the dwelling there "was destroyed by an ice floe." LighthouseFriends describes a heavy field of floating ice coming down the river and demolishing the dwelling.[^3]

## After the keepers

The station went out of service on 1 November 1868. The nomination says it "was replaced by an offshore beacon east of Conimicut Point at the west side of the river's entrance."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the Fresnel lens moved to the new Conimicut Point light that same day.[^3]

The old keeper's house was not left empty. The nomination says that the dwelling "was kept as quarters for the keeper who rowed a mile across the river to the new light."[^1] By 1870 "the dwelling at Nayatt Point was in poor condition," and the Lighthouse Board proposed selling it.[^1] It was repaired and enlarged in 1875, after the ice destroyed the Conimicut dwelling.[^1][^3]

The government sold the property in 1890. The nomination says it was "sold for $4,000 at public auction," and LighthouseFriends names the buyer as Charles H. Merriman.[^1][^3] The two sources agree on the sale but differ on the Conimicut tower. The nomination puts the new tower and quarters there at 1890, while LighthouseFriends gives 1883 for the completion of the iron tower.[^1][^3]

## Today

The nomination, dated December 1987, says the house is "now used as a private residence."[^1] It lists the ownership as private and the accessibility as "yes, restricted."[^1] The archive's record says "Open to Public: No."[^2] The lens and lamp were removed when the light was deactivated, and the tower keeps most of its original features.[^1]

The house is not open to visitors, and the sources give no visiting arrangement. The owners are private people, and this story does not name them.[^1][^2]
