Bodie Island Light is a black-and-white banded brick tower on the northern end of North Carolina's Outer Banks, the third lighthouse to carry the Bodie Island name. It first showed its light on 1 October 1872 and still holds its original first-order Fresnel lens.[^1]

## The place

The Outer Banks are a thin line of barrier islands between the Atlantic and the Albemarle, Croatan and Pamlico Sounds. The National Register nomination describes them as flat and short of landmarks, with shoals and the meeting of the Gulf Stream and the Labrador Current offshore, and notes an estimate that more than 2,200 vessels were lost in the vicinity of Cape Hatteras.[^2] Hatteras was lit in 1803, but the stretch between Cape Hatteras and Cape Henry stayed dark, and one observer reported that Bodie Island was "literally covered with wrecks."[^2]

In 1837 Lieutenant Napoleon L. Coste of the revenue cutter *Campbell*, surveying the coast for the government, wrote of Bodie Island that "More vessels are lost there than on any part of our coast."[^2] Congress that year appropriated $5,000 for a light on Pea Island; Captain Charles Skinner, sent to look at the site, argued for Bodie Island instead, because it lay nearer the ocean and building materials would be easier to land there.[^3] Buying the land took years. In January 1843 the Collector at Washington, North Carolina, Thomas H. Blount, wrote to a congressman that fifteen wrecks had been in sight at one place the previous summer, and that a brig bound for Norfolk had been wrecked within the month.[^3] The Attorney General ruled the deed valid in 1846.[^3]

## Building the light

**The first tower (1847).** Congress added $12,000 in 1847, and the lowest bidder, Francis A. Gibbons of Baltimore, won the contract at $8,750.[^4] The tower was finished in September 1847 and fitted with fourteen Argand lamps and fourteen 21-inch reflectors. Samuel Tillitt was appointed keeper at $400 a year. Lighting was delayed for want of lamp equipment such as tube glass and wicks, and the nomination says only that the light was first shown between 22 January and 13 March 1848.[^4] Within about two years the tower was found to lean a foot out of plumb "canted to eastward"; repairs were estimated at $1,490.[^5] Lighthouse Friends, a modern history site, blames the choice of an unsupported foundation and a supervisor with no lighthouse experience.[^6] A fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed in 1854.[^5]

**The second tower (1859).** By 1858 the Lighthouse Board judged the first tower beyond repair. The replacement, an 80-foot (24 m) brick tower on 70 driven piles with a third-order lens, was lit on 1 July 1859 and the old tower was taken down.[^7] The work had its own troubles: a vessel carrying brick was lost, and a storm in October closed Oregon Inlet so that materials had to be lightered in from Hatteras Inlet.[^7] During the Civil War, Confederate forces used the tower as a lookout and for storing guns, and before leaving the Outer Banks in 1861 they blew it up. The lens was saved and went to the Lighthouse Inspector's depot on Staten Island.[^8]

**The third tower (1872).** By 1870 Oregon Inlet had moved to within 400 yards of the old site, so the Board chose a new one north of the inlet: fifteen acres, bought from John B. Etheridge and his wife on 13 June 1871 for $150.[^9] In June 1871 Peter Hains, the Fifth District's lighthouse engineer, told construction superintendent Dexter Stetson, who had worked on the Cape Hatteras tower, to take down the temporary buildings at Hatteras for use as storehouses and quarters, to build a temporary wharf into Roanoke Sound, and to "push the work along with the greatest practical dispatch."[^10] The Board built the station itself rather than contracting it. Borings showed layers of sand and shell, so the builders dug a pit seven feet (2.1 m) deep, kept it pumped dry, and laid a wood grillage topped with granite blocks.[^11] (Lighthouse Friends describes the foundation as timber pilings; the nomination, which quotes the engineers' records, describes a grillage and says piling was judged unnecessary.[^6][^11]) The ironwork came from the West Point Foundry in New York, and a first-order lens was bought from Barbier & Fenestre of Paris.[^12][^13] The station cost $140,000 across three appropriations, more than five times the previous one.[^11]

On 30 September 1872 Hains reported that the lantern was finished and the lens up, and that "The black and white bands show well."[^12] The Notice to Mariners gave a fixed white light, visible 18.5 nautical miles from a vessel's deck, a focal plane 156 feet (48 m) above the sea, and a tower painted in alternate white and black zones, each about 22 feet (6.7 m) high.[^14] The nomination calls the tower 164 feet tall;[^15] Lighthouse Friends agrees with 164 feet,[^6] but the *Outer Banks Voice* in 2013 called it a 156-foot (48 m) tower, the same figure the Notice gave for the focal plane above the sea.[^26][^14]

## The keepers

The first keeper of the third tower was William F. Hatsell, who served 1872 to 1878; Peter G. Gallop followed and stayed until 1906.[^17] Rebecca Hatsell appears in the archive's roster as second assistant in 1872 and 1873 and as third assistant in 1873 and 1874.[^17] The nomination says the third-assistant post, held by the keeper's wife, was abolished in 1874.[^18] For the first assistant of 1872 the sources differ: the archive roster, and the roster printed by Lighthouse Friends, give Peter Johnston, while the narrative on the Lighthouse Friends page names Isaac Meekins; both name William E. Etheridge as second assistant.[^6][^17] Later principal keepers were Ephraim Meekins Jr. (1906 to 1919) and Lloyd V. Gaskill (1919 to 1940), whom Lighthouse Friends calls Lloyd Vernon Gaskill.[^17][^6]

Isolation was a recurring problem; the Park Service says it remained the keepers' difficulty. In 1900 the Lighthouse Board reported that there was only one dwelling for the keeper and his two assistants, so "it is impossible for them to have their families with them", and said this did not help the keepers to be content or to take interest in the station; it renewed the request the next year.[^19] The Park Service says the closest school was at Manteo, across the water on Roanoke Island, so keepers' wives and children lived away except in the summer.[^16] The Board asked for $7,500 for a second dwelling.[^19] Lighthouse Friends reports that money was finally appropriated in 1907 but the work was abandoned when construction costs at the remote site exceeded the sum.[^6]

In 1928 Keeper Gaskill described his work for a government questionnaire. He lit the lamp in the tower every other evening and raised the curtains so the light would be visible to ships; the assistant did the same on the alternate evening. He then watched the light at intervals until sunrise, put it out, refilled the kerosene tanks and cleaned the lens. He kept a motor boat for fetching supplies and mail from a post office seven miles away across the sound.[^20] His assistant, William H. Etheridge, reported similar duties.[^20] The second-assistant post was done away with in 1922.[^18]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Eighteen days after the first lighting, a flock of geese struck the lantern, breaking three panes and damaging the lens; a wire screen was fitted soon after.[^21] In December 1877 the district engineer found vertical cracks on the second to seventh landings, which he put down to lightning. The spiral stair doubled as the lightning conductor, and a keeper standing on a landing in a storm had received a shock that left his lower body numb "for some little time". Lightning struck the tower again in April 1884, and the Board then ordered a cable run inside the tower to a buried iron plate.[^21]

Lighthouse Friends says that at least six vessels went aground nearby between July 1871 and the lighthouse's completion, with a loss it puts at $133,000.[^6] The 1915 Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports that on 22 April Keeper Ephraim Meekins Jr. found the mail launch *Swastika* with its machinery disabled and towed it to its destination, using the boat of Second Assistant William H. Etheridge. The launch had seven passengers aboard, no food supply, and was 24 hours overdue.[^22] In 1898 a telephone was installed as part of a national-defence programme linking five lighthouses to the lines of the life-saving stations, with any "untoward activity" off the coast to be reported at once.[^18]

## After the keepers

The light was electrified by a generator in 1932, and the nomination says Gaskill, the last keeper, transferred to another station when the light was automated in 1940, after which the nearby Nags Head lifeboat station looked after it.[^23] The Cape Hatteras National Seashore had been created in 1937. In 1953 the Coast Guard declared 56.37 acres excess, and the National Park Service took the property effective 15 October 1953, except a 100-foot (30 m) square plot under the tower. Commercial power replaced the generator the same year.[^23] The Park Service turned the keepers' quarters into a visitor centre and museum. From May 1983 visitors could enter the tower to look up, and in 1984 the Coast Guard fitted a chain-link fence at the foot of the stairs to keep people from climbing. The Outer Banks Lighthouse Society began helping to interpret the station in 1994, and the tower itself passed from the Coast Guard to the Park Service on 13 July 2000.[^24]

In August 2004 two large pieces of cast iron fell from the gallery, and the Park Service fenced the tower off, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^25] Restoration began in January 2010; that August "significant structural integrity issues" were found in the struts supporting the balcony, and with too little money the project was suspended and the scaffolding taken down in March 2011.[^25] Work resumed in March 2012. The Fresnel lens was relit on 18 April 2013 and the tower opened for climbing the next day.[^25][^26] The light had been dark since 28 September 2009, according to the *Outer Banks Voice*, which counted 214 steps to the top.[^26]

## Today

The Bodie Island Light Station is part of Cape Hatteras National Seashore, off Highway 12 near Nags Head, North Carolina,[^1] and the tower is a Park Service property; the Park Service describes it as still a functioning aid to navigation.[^16] Climbing is offered in the summer months, with tickets bought online and safety restrictions applying. The double keepers' quarters, used as the visitor centre, is closed through mid-summer 2027 because of damage from a January 2025 fire, but the outdoor areas stay open and the park store is open for the summer season.[^16] The original first-order Fresnel lens is still in the tower.[^1][^25]
