Cape Croker Light is a concrete lighthouse on a point south-east of Cape Croker, a headland on the Bruce Peninsula shore of Georgian Bay in Ontario.[^1][^4][^10] Cape Croker is part of the Chippewas of Nawash First Nation community.[^2][^4] A light and fog alarm went into operation on the point in July 1902, and the octagonal tower that stands there now was built in 1909.[^3][^10]

## The place

Cape Croker is a prominent headland roughly midway between Cabot Head and Owen Sound, and mariners were advised to give it a berth of at least 366 m (400 yards).[^4] The 1902 report puts the station not on the cape but on "the outer extreme of the point one and a half miles southeastwardly from Cape Croker", about 2.4 km south-east of the cape.[^3] LighthouseFriends places it differently, on "the northeast extremity of the cape".[^4] The Coast Guard's list agrees with the 1902 report that the light stands on a point south-east of the cape.[^1]

A tourism page gives an earlier light on the site: a wooden structure built in 1898 at the northern entrance to Colpoy's Bay, whose light was turned by weights.[^2] The earliest department report on the station cited here is for 1902. It says the light and alarm were "put in operation" that July and mentions no earlier light, so the 1898 date is unconfirmed.[^3]

## Building the light

The 1902 station was a wooden building with a square lantern, standing 20 m (65 ft) back from the water, with the keeper's dwelling 13 m (43 ft) behind it. Its light was an occulting white light, showing for 25 seconds and hidden for 5, from a seventh-order dioptric apparatus, and it was meant to be visible ten miles (16 km) from all points of approach by water.[^3]

The light and the fog alarm were electric, powered by a plant in the building.[^4] The department's engineer wrote that "the plant has failed to give satisfaction, the sound produced being comparatively feeble", and noted that a similar alarm had been in use at Victoria Harbour, British Columbia. The machinery cost $3,358.78.[^3][^4]

The buildings were put up by a contractor from Owen Sound for $3,559, according to LighthouseFriends.[^4] LighthouseFriends also says that in 1903 the light was changed to show a fixed light with a short eclipse, and that a compressed-air siren replaced the electric horn.[^4]

A separate fog alarm building, 12 m (40 ft) back from the water and south of the lighthouse, went into operation on 20 July 1905 with a diaphone that gave one five-second blast a minute in fog. The buildings cost $4,225.56 and the fog plant $10,001.85.[^6] In 1908 a small coal dock and a concrete-lined well were added.[^9]

The concrete tower was built in 1909.[^4][^10] The department's engineer for that year noted that Mr. W. C. Surtees was superintending "reinforced concrete lighthouse tower at Cape Croker, Ont.".[^10] LighthouseFriends gives the contract to the Forest City Paving and Construction Company of London, Ontario, for $1,820, and describes an octagonal tower with a third-order Fresnel lens showing two flashes every five seconds.[^4] A photo caption tagged to the Bruce County Museum says the lens was made by the Louis Sautter Company of Paris.[^12]

## The keepers

The first keeper was Richard Chapman.[^4] The 1902 report says that in August the electric machinery "failed to work satisfactorily", and that since then only a fixed white light had been shown.[^3] LighthouseFriends adds that Chapman was forced to exhibit a fixed white oil light for a time.[^4]

The department's keeper lists name "Chapman, Richard" for the Cape Croker station in 1903, and again in 1906 and 1907.[^5][^7][^8]

LighthouseFriends names the later keepers as William J. Chapman (1910 to 1940), Captain J. D. Chapman (1940), Wesley Morrison (1940 to 1942) and Norman Whetton (1942 to 1965). It says Whetton spent two years building a road, because the station had been isolated from the rest of the Bruce Peninsula. Once the road was finished, Whetton and his wife would drive to the station each April and depart each December, when the station was closed for the season.[^4] Frank Rourke (1965 to 1968), John Adams (1968 to 1979) and Fredric Jerome Proulx (1980 to 1986) followed. Proulx signed the logbook for the last time in 1986, and the lighthouse was left unmanned.[^2][^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 12 September 1905 the screw tug A Seaman, of Toronto, was burned near Cape Croker. The report records no casualties.[^7] On the night of 30 July 1907 the tug Esperanza, of Toronto, caught fire near the cape; the report gives the cause as unknown and records no casualties.[^9]

Among these losses, the steamer J. H. Jones is the only one with deaths reported. On 22 November 1906 she left Owen Sound in a heavy gale, bound for Lion's Head. She was last seen off Cape Croker at the close of darkness that evening. The department supposed she had foundered there, although her two lifeboats and other wreckage had come ashore at Christian Island. It reported that "about twenty-two persons" were lost.[^8]

The Yale Expositor, a Michigan newspaper, ran the wreck on 7 December 1906 under the headline "Harvest of Death", with the subhead "Twenty-six Persons Perish". It gave the crew as 13 and "at least 16 passengers" believed drowned, said 26 persons were "known to have been lost", and reported that two bodies and wreckage had been picked up on the north shore of the Christian Islands, about 64 km (40 miles) east of Cape Croker.[^11] The department's figure and the newspaper's do not agree, and no source settles which is right.

## After the keepers

An oil plant replaced the steam fog alarm in 1927.[^4] In 1975 the two-storey keeper's residence and the fog alarm building were pulled down, leaving the concrete tower as the station's only structure.[^4]

In 2007 the Coast Guard removed the third-order lens after repeated vandalism.[^4] In 2009 it went to the Bruce County Museum and Cultural Centre in Southampton under a long-term lease.[^4][^12]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light.[^4] Its list gives a white octagonal tower with a red upper portion, 15.9 m (52 ft) high, with the light 18.7 m above the water. The light shows white in equal light and dark periods of four seconds (Iso W 4s), has a nominal range of 13 nautical miles, is seasonal, and operates for 24 hours.[^1]

LighthouseFriends reports the grounds open and the tower closed.[^4] A tourism page says the lighthouse is not open for tours but can be viewed and photographed.[^2]

The station is at Cape Croker, which LighthouseFriends places within the Chippewas of Nawash First Nation community.[^4] Anyone planning a visit should ask the First Nation first.
