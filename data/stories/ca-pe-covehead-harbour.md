Covehead Harbour Lighthouse is a small white wooden tower with red trim, set in Prince Edward Island National Park above the Gulf of St. Lawrence. Before it came lanterns hoisted on masts, in use from 1879 and easy to move as the sands shifted.[^4][^1]

## The place

Covehead Harbour is in Queens County, on the Gulf shore. In 1879, two masts were erected at the entrance to serve as range lights, one a front light and the other a rear light, each with its own keeper.[^1] The Department of Marine and Fisheries described the arrangement in its 1893 report, as quoted by Lighthouse Friends. The front light stood "close to the edge of the sand beach", its lantern hoisted up a 5 m (17 ft) mast, and the back light stood 69 m (225 ft) to the south-west, on an 8 m (27 ft) mast. Both showed a fixed white light visible over three miles. The report added a warning about the harbour: "The position of the light is liable to be moved to suit the changes in the channel, and only 3 feet can be depended on at low water on the bar."[^1]

## Building the light

The sites of the lights changed more than once. According to Lighthouse Friends, the back range light, formerly at Black Point, was moved to the beach in 1894 after fishermen petitioned for the change, and a new block was built for the front light.[^1] In 1917 a set of movable range lights was built under the supervision of G. L. Gaudin.[^1] The Department of Marine's annual report for 1918 lists at Covehead the "Construction of two moveable range lights and boxes for lanterns."[^3]

The present tower came later. The Prince Edward Island register and Tourism PEI both record that the lighthouse was built in 1975, replacing a tower of 1967.[^4][^8] A Canadian Coast Guard list gives the light a focal height of 11.0 m and a range of 11 nautical miles, flashing white every 5 seconds, on a tower 8.2 m high.[^5] Lighthouse Friends gives the tower height as 8.2 m (26.9 ft) and the focal plane as 10 m.[^1] The tower is square and tapered, clad in clapboard and painted white with red trim.[^4] Its lantern is said to hold a revolving bull's-eye Fresnel lens, reported to be over 100 years old. Lighthouse Friends adds that the lens was reportedly moved from Dalhousie, New Brunswick.[^1][^4]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists the range-light keepers, citing the Department of Marine and Fisheries' annual reports. In 1879 John McCabe kept the front light and Ernest McMillan the rear light, and McMillan stayed on the rear light until 1894. On the front light, James D. McMillan served from 1880 and Donald McMillan from 1883; Donald then kept both lights from 1895 to 1900.[^1] After him the list gives John A. Kielly, James McCabe, George Herbert Kielly, W. J. Thomas McCabe and Lloyd Bell, who kept the lights from 1936 until 1959.[^1] One sentence on the same page dates Bell's service from 1926 instead.[^1]

The dates do not agree everywhere. Lighthouse Friends places John A. Kielly's tenure at 1900 to 1911, but the Department of Marine's tables of lightkeepers give him an appointment date of 27 November 1890, at "Cove Head, Inner Light" in the report labelled 1902, and show him paid $100 a year as keeper of the "Cove Head Range" in the report labelled 1910.[^6][^7] The reports spell the harbour "Cove Head" and "Covehead" in different years.

The end of the keeper's post is recorded plainly. When the range lights were electrified in 1959 a keeper was no longer needed, so Bell was hired instead as caretaker at $100 a year, in place of the $550 he had earned before.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A plaque on the present tower remembers the Gale of 1851, called the Yankee Gale. Lighthouse Friends quotes the plaque: schooners had sailed into shallow water after mackerel and cod, and that evening a northeast gale rose after darkness found them becalmed close to land. The plaque ends: "Many men were buried here in Prince Edward Island." The heritage register gives the date as 3 October 1851 and the toll as 74 ships and 150 lives.[^1][^4] The gale predates every light on this site; the plaque is a memorial mounted on the tower, not a record of a wreck the light answered.

## After the keepers

Electric lights and a caretaker replaced the keeper in 1959.[^1] The lighthouse was renovated in 1994 and covered with pressure-treated cedar siding, and its fog horn, maintained by the local harbour authority, faces north.[^1] The tower was recognized nationally under the federal Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act in 2015.[^4]

In March 2011, Pat and Keith Notman of Stanhope heard a Parks Canada presentation on the Heritage Lighthouse Act, which allows lighthouses to be transferred to communities. They formed Friends of Covehead Lighthouse and gathered the signatures needed for a petition to the Department of Fisheries and Oceans before the May 2012 deadline.[^1] Lighthouse Friends reports that the group was told the lighthouse was eligible for transfer and had until May 2015 to prepare a business plan. The sources cited here do not say whether the transfer went ahead.

## Today

The tower stands in the national park, close to the Gulf Shore Parkway, and the heritage register calls it the central tourist attraction for visitors to the east side of the park.[^2] Tourism PEI says it is not open to the public, and Lighthouse Friends lists it as owned by the Canadian Coast Guard with the grounds open and the tower closed, so it is seen from outside.[^8][^1] The Coast Guard list shows a seasonal light that operates around the clock, with a fog horn sounding a 3-second blast followed by 27 seconds of silence.[^5]
