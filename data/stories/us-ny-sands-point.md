Sands Point Light stands at the tip of the Sands Point peninsula on Long Island's North Shore, in Nassau County, New York. Its light was first shown on 9 September 1809, and its first keeper was Noah Mason, the Revolutionary War veteran who had built the tower.[^2] The federal government sold the station in January 1924. Today the lighthouse is privately owned and closed to visitors, and the only view of it is from the water.[^8][^2][^4][^1]

## The place

The point takes its name from the Sands family. LighthouseFriends says they bought 500 acres at the tip of Long Island's Cow Neck Peninsula in 1695.[^1] The light was meant to warn ships off the Execution Rocks, which lie 1,500 yards (1,370 m) north of the point.[^1]

Congress acted on a bill for a light here on 26 January 1806. The bill had been pushed by Samuel L. Mitchill, a senator who lived on Cow Neck.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends gives the appropriation as $6,000, and says the light was known locally as Mitchill's.[^1] A shorter Cow Neck society note says it is sometimes called the Mitchell Lighthouse, after the man who fought for it.[^3]

The site was about five acres, bought from Benjamin Hewlett.[^2][^8] The sources give different dates for the sale. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin says the United States bought the site in July 1808.[^8] LighthouseFriends says New York bought the land in 1808 for $512.50 and gave it to the federal government.[^1] The Cow Neck society quotes a 1927 article in the Port Washington News that gives 15 January 1808 for a purchase by Governor Tompkins.[^2]

## Building the light

Collector of the port David Gelston solicited proposals for the work. The Cow Neck society says he awarded the contract to a team from New London, Mason and Way.[^2] The Keeper's Log article of 1989 says Mason's low bid was $8,500, which does not match the $6,000 appropriation.[^5][^1]

The sources do not agree on the tower's height. LighthouseFriends describes a forty-foot (12 m) octagonal tower of coursed brownstone.[^1] The Cow Neck society says it would stand about fifty feet (15 m) tall and sixty-five feet (20 m) above the water.[^2] The federal place record gives the light as 64.5 feet (19.7 m) above high water.[^4]

The light was first shown on 9 September 1809, and Samuel L. Mitchill was there to light the first lamp at the opening ceremony.[^2] As of 1838 it used eleven lamps and nine-inch reflectors, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that an 1838 report by Lt. Bache described the keeper's house as having twenty-three rooms.[^1]

The sources disagree on the order of the lens. LighthouseFriends says a fifth-order Fresnel lens was installed in 1856.[^1] The Light-House Board's 1895 report describes a fourth-order light, and the federal place record gives a fourth-order Fresnel optic.[^7][^4] A new lantern room went on the tower in 1863.[^1]

Congress allocated $9,400 in 1867 to renovate the tower and replace the keeper's house.[^1] The Bulletin says the tower and dwelling were both remodeled in 1868–69. The house was rebuilt in brick and joined to the tower by a passageway.[^8] The Light-House Board's report for 1891 to 1892 records that the light was changed from flashing to fixed white on 31 May 1892.[^6]

## The keepers

Noah Mason was born in Dighton, Massachusetts, in 1757, according to the Cow Neck society. LighthouseFriends gives 1755.[^2][^1] He enlisted in 1775 and was discharged in 1778.[^2] When Gelston recommended him for the post, President Madison endorsed the choice on 5 September 1809, and the endorsement reads, in part, "I approve Noah Mason for keeper of the Light house at Sand's Point."[^2] In 1816 he earned $250 a year as keeper.[^2] In 1834 the governor made him wreck master for the town.[^2]

Mason died on 27 February 1841 and was keeper at his death. A notice in the Sag Harbor Corrector said he had served as keeper for thirty-two years.[^2] His wife, Lucretia, is listed for 1841 alone, and then Daniel H. Caulkins took over.[^4]

Benjamin Downing (1851 to 1857), John H. Chapman (1857 to 1861), Edward P. Downing (1861 to 1864) and Willet Hicks (1864 to 1866) followed.[^4] John W. Seaman was appointed on 12 March 1866.[^1] After fifteen years at the station, an inspector found the dwelling full of boarders, which the regulations did not allow, and asked for Seaman's dismissal. LighthouseFriends says he kept his place and remained until his death in 1895.[^1] The roster gives Seaman a short stint from January to July 1895.[^4]

Charles F. Jayne is listed for the summer of 1895.[^4] Cornelius Douglass is listed as keeper from 1895 to 1916, and LighthouseFriends says his tenure of twenty-two years ended in 1917.[^4][^1] LighthouseFriends also says that in 1913 Douglass was recognized for helping a woman who had been shot on the lighthouse beach.[^1] Thomas J. Murray was transferred from Esopus Meadows Lighthouse to take charge of Sands Point, and the roster lists him for 1917 to 1922.[^1][^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends says that on 10 September 1814 a British frigate, the Acosta, fought American gunboats just offshore from the tower, and that Mason watched from the tower.[^1] The Cow Neck society prints a memoir by Mitchill that places the gunboats off Cow Bay in September 1813.[^2] The two sources give different years for the same engagement.

In 1836 the schooner Washington Packet was lost off the point. Mason was part of the salvage, and his receipt, dated 12 November 1836, was for $25.25 in net proceeds from the sale of the wreck.[^2]

LighthouseFriends quotes an 1841 inspection by I.W.P. Lewis, who found that a stone sea wall built the year before had partly collapsed.[^1] The station has been damaged over the years by erosion and storms, LighthouseFriends says.[^1]

The Light-House Board's 1895 report says the light was discontinued on 31 October 1894 "as being no longer needed." It was reestablished on 21 January 1895 "at the urgent and persistent request of mariners."[^7]

## After the keepers

In 1917 a wealthy New York socialite bought the land beside the lighthouse and built a large and expensive mansion there.[^1] LighthouseFriends says she persuaded the Bureau of Lighthouses to bar visitors to the keeper on summer weekends, and that many people think her interference was one of the main reasons the station was closed.[^1]

On 15 December 1922 the light moved to a steel skeleton tower roughly 800 feet (240 m) offshore, on a reef, and was automated.[^1][^8] The Bulletin says the new light was unwatched.[^8]

The Bulletin reports that the sale of the station took place on 31 January 1924, and that the property had cost the United States about $512 and was sold for $100,000.[^8] LighthouseFriends dates Mrs. O. H. P. Belmont's purchase, for the same $100,000, to 31 January 1923.[^1]

In 1927 Belmont sold the mansion and the lighthouse to William Randolph Hearst for $400,000.[^1] The Hearsts gave up the property to a New York bank in 1940 to satisfy a mortgage, and a realtor later divided the estate into one-acre lots.[^1] The Village of Sands Point designated the lighthouse a village landmark in 1992.[^2] On 23 October 2015 the village landmark commission celebrated a rededication after extensive renovations.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse and the keeper's residence, which was added in 1868, are in private hands.[^2] The federal record lists the station as not open to the public.[^4] For visitors, the only view of the lighthouse is from the water.[^1] The steel tower offshore has carried the light since 1922.[^1][^8]
