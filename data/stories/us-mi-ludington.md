Ludington North Breakwater Light stands at the outer end of the north breakwater at Ludington, Mason County, Michigan, where the harbor opens onto Lake Michigan.[^1][^2] Its steel tower has stood there since 1924.[^2] A light has marked the harbor since 1870, when a wooden beacon went up on the south pier.[^3][^1]

## The place

Ludington grew up at the mouth of the Pere Marquette River.[^1] LighthouseFriends says James Ludington lent money in 1854 for a sawmill on the river, and took the mill over when the borrower, George W. Ford, defaulted in 1859. The village was chartered as the City of Ludington on 22 March 1873, and many of its streets are named for the family.[^1]

In its 1915 report the Commissioner of Lighthouses described the city's commerce as including "important car ferry lines across Lake Michigan," a reason it gave for improving the aids.[^8] The same report said that the fog-signal station at the end of the south pier "subjects vessels to danger of striking the breakwater."[^8]

## Building the light

The Board's report for 1891 says a light had been kept on the south pier since 1870, and that the extension of the pier was then progressing rapidly.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the Board asked for $6,000 in 1869 for a sixth-order beacon and a keeper's dwelling. Congress provided the funds on 15 July 1870, and a pierhead light was built that year. Before title to a plot for the dwelling could be obtained, LighthouseFriends says, the balance of the money went back to the Treasury.[^1] The first tower was a wooden structure, thirty feet (9 m) tall, painted white, with a fifth-order Fresnel lens showing a fixed red light.[^1]

William Gerard was the first keeper.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he lived in an old house while the Board made annual requests for funds for a proper dwelling.[^1][^2]

The light has moved several times as the pier grew. LighthouseFriends says a December 1876 gale washed the pierhead crib and light away, that the tower was rebuilt in 1877, and that it was moved 140 feet (43 m) to the end of the extended pier in 1880.[^1] The Board's report for 1891 gives the next move: after the south pier was extended in the autumn of 1890, the pierhead light was moved 750 feet (230 m) lakeward, to the third crib from the end.[^3] An elevated walk of 754 feet (230 m) was built on the south pier in 1892.[^3]

The fog signal came later. The Board's 1891 report says "A steam fog signal has long been needed here," and that the work had to wait until the pier was finished.[^3] An appropriation of $5,500 was made by the act approved 18 August 1894, and a ten-inch steam whistle began working on 31 January 1895.[^4] The Board's report for 1895 says the whistle ran for some 306 hours that first season.[^4] The report for 1896 and 1897 gives 607 hours, and says the whistle consumed about 71 tons of coal and five cords of wood.[^5]

In 1899 the Board reported that the beacon had been moved close to the signal house, with a covered way built between them, and that the whistle had been moved too.[^6] The Board asked for $4,500 for a keeper's dwelling in its 1894 report.[^14] A contract for the dwelling was made in 1900.[^7] LighthouseFriends says work began in May 1900 and was completed later that year. The Board's 1901 report records the dwelling as completed.[^1][^13]

Larger changes came in the twentieth century. LighthouseFriends says Congress authorized $839,000 for arrowhead breakwaters in 1907, and that the timber breakwaters were finished in 1914.[^1] In 1915 the Commissioner's report proposed a main light at the outer end of the north breakwater, with fog-signal apparatus, and the discontinuance of the old steam fog signal in its wooden structure on the south pier.[^8] The 1924 steel tower followed. The USLHS record gives 1924 as the year the tower was established.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the light and fog signal went into service on 30 December 1924. The tower was originally fitted with a fourth-order lens and a diaphone fog signal worked from shore.[^1]

## The keepers

The roster and LighthouseFriends name a long line of keepers. Fred Samuelson had the longest tenure.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he helped build a steel tower on a crib inside the breakwater at Chicago in 1893, joined the Lighthouse Service in 1894, and was head keeper at North Manitou Island from 1898 before coming to Ludington in 1909. He retired on 30 June 1937. For three months in 1934 he was in charge of the lighthouse exhibit at the Century of Progress in Chicago.[^1][^2]

Lemuel Marvin served as first assistant in 1895 only.[^2] John L. Paetschow was first assistant from 1920 to 1937, then head keeper from 1937 to 1941.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends quotes an 1890 history of Oceana County about a November 1872 gale.[^1] The schooners Souvenir and Minnie Corlett left harbor bound for Chicago, and the wind turned northwest in the night.[^1] The next morning the Souvenir was found near the Claybanks south of Ludington, on the outer bar, with one man on deck at the wheel.[^1] According to the account, Girard went out alone in a small boat and reached the vessel about noon. He found the last crew member alive but unconscious, and the account says he breathed only a few moments longer.[^1] LighthouseFriends spells the keeper's name Girard in this account and Gerard in its keeper list, and the roster has Gerard.[^1][^2]

On 10 February 1922 the Pere Marquette Car Ferry No. 18 struck the north breakwater, destroying the superstructure of the timber breakwater for a length of twenty feet (6 m) or more.[^9] The Commissioner's report says, "This collision occurred in a dense fog," and that a fog signal on the outer end of the breakwater would in all probability have prevented it.[^9] LighthouseFriends notes that the light and fog signal on the south pier were then 1,500 feet (460 m) inside the entrance.[^1]

## After the keepers

The lens served until 1995, when LighthouseFriends says it was replaced by a modern beacon.[^1] The lens went on loan to White Pine Village, and after a restoration in 2016 it went to the Port of Ludington Maritime Museum, which opened in 2017.[^1]

The light was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 6 September 2005.[^10] That year the Coast Guard deemed the light excess and offered it at no cost under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000. LighthouseFriends says the National Park Service transferred it to the City of Ludington in May 2006.[^1]

The sources name different groups as operators. LighthouseFriends says the city signed an agreement with the Sable Points Lighthouse Keepers Association, which would maintain the lighthouse and open it to the public.[^1] Lakeshore Keepers says it has operated the light since 2006, in partnership with the city.[^12]

The Light List for 2025 gives the characteristic as isophase green every six seconds, with the horn sounding two blasts every thirty seconds.[^11]

## Today

The light belongs to the City of Ludington.[^1] Lakeshore Keepers says visitors reach it by walking the half-mile (0.8 km) pier from Stearns Park Beach. The gift shop is on the first floor, and tower climbs are sold there.[^12] The light closes periodically in high winds, when waves break over the pier.[^12] It is closed for the 2026 season and will reopen on 18 May 2027.[^12] The Lighthouse Society's record still lists the station as not open to the public.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes the grounds as open and the tower open in summer. The operator's 2026 notice of closure is the more recent word.[^1][^12]
