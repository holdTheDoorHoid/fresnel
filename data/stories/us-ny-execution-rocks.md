Execution Rocks is a reef at the western end of Long Island Sound, off Sands Point, and a granite tower has stood on its largest exposed rock since the light was first shown in 1850.[^1] Its name is a grim one, and where it came from has never been settled.

## The place

The reef lies about 1,650 yards (1,500 m) northwest of Sands Point.[^1] Two explanations of the name are told. One says the British carried condemned men to the rocks in colonial times, chained them there at low tide and left the rising water to finish the sentence; Lighthouse Friends notes that this folklore "has never been proven true".[^1] The other comes from the early settlers of Manhasset Neck, then called Cows Neck, who held that the reef destroyed many ships bound for Manhasset Bay.[^1]

A light already stood close by. Sands Point Lighthouse, built in 1809, was reported to be ineffective in heavy fog or storm.[^1] In 1837 Congress appropriated $5,000 for a revolving or double light on the south side of the rocks, opposite Sands Point. An inspector found the sum too small for a lighthouse and suggested a light-boat, but it was too small for that as well.[^1] A decade later, on 3 March 1847, Congress appropriated $25,000 for a lighthouse directly on the rocks, judged cheaper in the long run than a lightship.[^1]

## Building the light

The design went to Alexander Parris, who had drawn a similar tower for Saddleback Ledge in Maine.[^1] Parris chose the largest exposed rock on the reef. Local mariners wanted another site, and an independent body sent by the Lighthouse Board recommended a third; Parris answered that building there would cost four to five times as much, and the tower went where he had first proposed.[^1] The contract went to the lowest bidder, Thomas Butler, who by Lighthouse Friends' account was not up to the job: subcontractors did most of the work and the tower was finished almost a year late.[^1] It was lit in 1850 under Daniel H. Caulkins, who kept his old post at Sands Point and was given two assistants for the new light.[^1][^2]

The first lighting apparatus was fifteen lamps in 21-inch (53 cm) reflectors, with red shades so the light could be told from Sands Point's white one.[^1] A fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced it in 1856; the Lighthouse Society's archive and Lighthouse Friends agree on the year.[^1][^2] The tower is described as granite with a brick lining.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives its height as 58 feet (18 m), tapering from 26 feet (8 m) at the base to 13 feet (4 m) at the lantern; the Society's archive instead gives 55 feet (17 m) as the height of the light above mean high water.[^1][^2]

Lighthouse Friends reports that the new tower leaked badly at first, making living conditions miserable, and that there was some apprehension about its safety. Stones set east of it as a breakwater were soon washed to the west side of the ledge, and that Congress appropriated $5,250 on 18 August 1856 to secure the foundation, with protective work done the following year.[^1] In its report covering 1896 the Light-House Board recorded some 600 tons of riprap stone delivered "for the protection of the site".[^11]

Living quarters came late. Lighthouse Friends says the early keepers lived in the tower itself, and that a granite dwelling on a stone pier, linked to the west side of the tower, was begun in 1867 after Congress allocated $19,000 on 2 March of that year.[^1] The Society's archive dates the keeper's quarters to 1867.[^2] By 1894 that building had "settled dangerously"; the Board's report says the southwest wall was taken down, the stone beneath it replaced, the gaps filled with concrete and the wall rebuilt.[^8]

The station also grew a voice. Lighthouse Friends traces a succession of fog signals, from a Daboll trumpet driven by caloric engines when the dwelling was added to oil-engine sirens in 1904.[^1] The Board's volume for 1891-92 records a new fog-signal building with a coal shed and a cistern in the base of the tower that was partly completed.[^18] The automatic steam siren, in duplicate, ran 319 hours in the year covered by the 1894 report.[^9] In 1895 the fog signal was out of action from 9 to 28 March; the report gives no cause.[^10]

Two changes to the light itself are recorded. A red sector had been added to the white light to keep vessels clear of the south end of Hart Island, but the Board judged that, with the light about 2½ miles from the island, it "fails of its object in thick weather".[^11] On 15 May 1899 the colour of the tower was changed, the Board's note for this station saying no more; Lighthouse Friends identifies it as the brown band the tower still wears.[^12][^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1928 announced that radiobeacons would be placed in commission on 15 May at Execution Rocks and at Little Gull Island, near the two ends of the Sound, and Lighthouse Friends dates the one here to May 1928.[^13][^1]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society's archive lists the station's principal keepers from Caulkins in 1850 to John Kurian, who is shown leaving in 1979.[^2] Lighthouse Friends repeats a story that keepers here served for as long as they were willing and that a transfer request was always granted, and adds that only one head keeper stayed more than a decade.[^1] The archive's roster shows several principal keepers within a single year in 1863, in 1890-91 and in 1923.[^2]

The roster shows longer stints than that: William H. Tooker is listed as principal keeper from 1892 to 1903, and Leonard J. Clark from 1925 to 1954; his own archive record gives his retirement as 31 March 1954.[^2][^17] The archive's dates for George E. Sheffield, shown as principal keeper from 1945 to 1948 inside Clark's span, do not fit with that, and one of them must be wrong.[^2]

Women appear on the assistant rolls. The archive lists Alicia Odell as second assistant from 1859 to 1863, Clarissa Miller as first assistant from 1867 to 1869, Jane Williams from 1869 to 1871, Ellen Fraser from 1871 to 1873 and Mary L. Sherman as second assistant from 1873 to 1882.[^2] Each shares a surname with the principal keeper listed for the same years, but the archive does not say how they were related.[^2]

From about 1960 the archive's roster lists Coast Guard crew members in place of civilian keepers.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In a heavy blow in December 1857, Lighthouse Friends says, Keeper Joseph Brundage saw a man in a boat drifting down the Sound before the wind and, with a Hell Gate pilot, Captain John Marshall, towed it to the lighthouse, where its drunken occupant came round with difficulty.[^1]

Ice cut the station off in February 1905. Lighthouse Friends writes that when the Sound filled with ice floes, Keeper Willis Green could no longer row ashore for the family's provisions, and that after a day without food he fought through the floes to land, taking two hours.[^1]

The Lighthouse Service's Bulletin gives a plainer rescue. On 17 August 1913 Keeper George L. Costello and second assistant Frederick C. Lovatt took a party from a disabled motor boat to the station and then towed the boat to Huckleberry Island.[^3]

On 8 December 1918 a fire of undetermined cause destroyed the engine house and made the engine useless; it also damaged the oil house, chipped the stonework on the north and east sides of the tower, burned out windows and scorched the woodwork in the dwelling's lower hallway.[^4] About 2,000 gallons of oil were in the oil house and about half burned.[^4] The New York City fireboat *Cornelius W. Lawrence*, which arrived about 2 p.m., soon had it under control, with Navy scout patrol boats also helping; the Bureau gave the keeper and second assistant credit for their quick efforts.[^4] The Bureau put the damage at $7,675 on original cost, or $13,500 to replace at the prices of the day.[^4][^5] Lighthouse Friends adds that the keeper had been running the fog signal since 7 a.m. and found the engine room alight after noon, and that a second, smaller fire followed in 1921.[^1]

Congress voted $10,000 on 19 July 1919 to restore the station.[^20] Work began in June 1920 and ended in June 1921 at a cost of $9,860.18, with a new fog-signal building, three fuel-oil tanks and new duplicated siren machinery.[^6] A 1922 bulletin reports riprap round the station 65 per cent complete.[^19]

In the year to June 1921 the keepers sheltered a man whose motor boat was drifting toward the channel, and first assistant Walter A. Storey and second assistant Leonard Hainsworth rescued two men whose canoe had capsized in a windstorm and gave them food and clothing at the station.[^7]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Society's archive gives 1979 as the year the station was automated.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the last keepers left on 5 December 1979 when a modern flashing optic was installed.[^1] The same archive records that a concrete oil house and a radio beacon were destroyed in a storm in 1992.[^2] The station was added to the National Register of Historic Places on 23 February 2007, with Parris named as architect.[^14]

The Coast Guard declared the station excess in May 2007, according to Lighthouse Friends, and offered it under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act; the nonprofit Historically Significant Structures was the only applicant and, that source says, received the deed in January 2009.[^1] The group's own blog says the island was donated in 2010.[^15] The two accounts differ, and neither gives a document.

## Today

The light remains an active aid to navigation, and the Society's archive lists its optic as a solar-powered APRB-251; the same record marks the station as not open to the public.[^2] The island belongs to Historically Significant Structures, which has run boat tours and overnight stays; its blog says the island was "previously closed to the public" and is now open to tours and stays.[^15] Its website later said that tower climbs and overnight stays would not be scheduled while the pandemic continued, and that its small access boats could not allow distancing.[^16] Visitors reach the island by small boat and board it by ladder, so anyone interested should ask the owner about current tours instead of trying to land.[^16]
