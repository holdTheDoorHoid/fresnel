The Craighill Channel Upper Range Front Light stands within 350 feet (107 m) of North Point, near Fort Howard, on the north side of the mouth of the Patapsco River in Baltimore County, Maryland.[^1] It is an octagonal brick tower on the former stone foundation of the North Point Lighthouse of 1822.[^1] The Coast Guard owns it, and access is by boat.[^1]

## The place

The front light and its rear light, 1.3 miles (2.1 km) to the northwest, guided ships into a cut-off channel that ran into the Patapsco River inside Seven Foot Knoll Light.[^1] The cut-off saved several miles on the way to Baltimore.[^1]

The nomination calls the station the Cut-off Channel Range Front Light Station, and also lists New Cut-off Channel, Craighill Channel Upper Front Range, Fort Howard and North Point Lighthouse as names for it.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's archive calls it Craighill Channel Upper Front.[^2]

It is not the Craighill Channel front light at Sparrows Point, which is a separate station.[^3] The Board's 1900 repair list names the two separately, as "Craighill Channel, front" and "Cutoff Channel, front."[^4]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $25,000 on 3 March 1885 for range lights to be erected at the "new cut-off from Craighill Channel, approaches to Baltimore."[^1] The first plan was to reuse the rear tower of the old North Point range as the front beacon.[^1] A closer look showed that tower was unsuitable, so a new octagonal brick tower was planned for the old stone foundation.[^1]

Work on the front beacon and its dwelling began in September 1885.[^1] Crews traveled between Baltimore and the site on the steam launch Nettle.[^1] Both beacons were first lighted on 15 January 1886, though the dwellings were not yet finished.[^1] All the work was completed by the end of June.[^1]

The heights in the sources disagree.[^1] The nomination cites the Board's reports for 1885 and 1886 for a front range 25 feet (7.6 m) above the water.[^1] Its own 2002 description gives the light 15 feet (4.6 m) above the water and the tower 22 feet (6.7 m) tall.[^1] It also reports that the 1896 Light List gave the tower as 18 feet (5.5 m), the 1994 list as 22 feet, and Robert de Gast's book on the Chesapeake lights as 27 feet (8.2 m).[^1]

One report says the tower was torn down in the late 1930s and a new one built in 1938.[^1] The nomination thinks the present tower is the original 1886 one.[^1] It notes that Linda Turbyville disputes the 1938 date, and its own boundary note still refers to "the 1938 New Cut-off Front Range tower."[^1]

## The keepers

The archive lists three head keepers for 1886 alone: George W. Brock, George F. Culleton and John B. Lewis.[^2] Charles Robinson followed for 1886 to 1888, and then Frederick Burmeister served from 1888 to 1914.[^2]

Burmeister's earlier service appears in the archive's record for the lower front at Sparrows Point, where he is listed as first assistant from 1883 to 1886 and head keeper from 1886 to 1888.[^3] The archive links both entries to one man.[^3]

The keeper's access to the light was disputed.[^1] In 1888 the owner of the adjoining land would not let the keeper cross his property.[^1] The gate to that land was closed and moved to the waterfront, where the government held a right of way.[^1] In 1890 the dwelling's roof and one boat davit were repaired, a new boat hoist was installed, and a 450-foot (137 m) picket fence was built.[^1] The nomination, quoting the Board's reports for 1888 and 1890, says the station was described as "in excellent condition."[^1]

The archive also lists Isaac J. Allen as first assistant in 1894.[^2] It lists Frederick W.M. Burmeister as first assistant from 1895 to 1914, and Frederick Burmeister as head keeper for the same years.[^2] The archive keeps these under separate person records, so the two entries need checking against register images before they are read as two men.[^2]

In 1914 the archive lists Frederick Burmeister as "Custodian" until 1917.[^2] Those are the last entries on the station's roster.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A keeper's quarters was built on shore in 1885.[^1] On 28 August 1893 a severe storm carried away the timber-and-stone bridge that joined the beacon, about 850 feet (260 m) offshore, to land.[^1] The keeper's quarters on shore were abandoned.[^1]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 says the beacon was fitted for the keeper's occupancy rather than rebuilding the bridge.[^5] The work included a suitable boat for the keeper, a boat landing and davits.[^5] These changes were made in October, with some minor repairs.[^5]

According to the nomination, the lamp came out of the tower onto an exterior iron platform so that the keeper's quarters would fit, and the work was finished in October 1893.[^1]

The two sources disagree about the oil house.[^1][^5] The Board's report describes a sheet-iron oil house with room for 1,000 gallons of mineral oil, bought and delivered to the lighthouse depot, to be erected on the station.[^5] The nomination describes an iron oil house for 55 five-gallon cans, erected in 1894.[^1] The sources do not reconcile the two.[^1][^5]

## After the keepers

According to the nomination, electricity reached the light on 28 November 1929, and it has had no keeper since.[^1] The Bulletin's account of the year's electrification program says that in November the front light was electrified, with current from the lighting system at Fort Howard carried through a submarine cable 650 feet (200 m) long.[^6] The electric equipment was similar to the rear light's, and the front light gave a red light of 20,000 candlepower.[^6] The installation cost $375.[^6]

The nomination treats the period of significance as running from completion of construction until automation, which it dates to 1886 to 1929.[^1]

The archive gives a different date: its record puts automation in 1914, and its roster continues with the "Custodian" entries to 1917.[^2] The sources used here do not explain either date.[^1][^6]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns and manages the station, and access is by boat.[^1] The archive lists it as not open to the public.[^2]

The nomination says the old "locomotive" type light has been replaced with a DCB 24 and that the light characteristic is fixed red.[^1] The nomination and the 1930 Bulletin both describe a red light, so the characteristic should be checked against a current Light List before it is relied on.[^1][^6]

The tower is painted red with a white band one-third of its height, and the range light is mounted on the outside wall just above the decorated brick belt between the two stories.[^1] It is closed to visitors.[^2] Do not climb the tower or land on the pier.
