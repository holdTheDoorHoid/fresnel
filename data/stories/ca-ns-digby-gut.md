Digby Gut Lighthouse is a square skeleton tower with a green-and-white daymark, on the eastern side of the entrance to Digby Gut, the narrow channel between Annapolis Basin and the Bay of Fundy in Nova Scotia.[^3][^1] The station began in 1915 as a fog bell in a two-storey house, got its light in 1963, and has shown a green light on a steel tower since 2014.[^1][^3]

## The place

Digby Gut is a narrow channel joining Annapolis Basin to the Bay of Fundy.[^1] Lighthouse Friends describes strong tidal currents, rocky ledges, frequent fog and unpredictable winds that make the passage hard to navigate.[^1] The Coast Guard's list places Prim Point on the western point of the entrance to Annapolis Basin, and this station on the eastern side of the entrance to Digby Gut.[^3]

The two Lighthouse Friends pages give different years for the first light at Prim Point: 1817 on the Digby Gut page, and 1804 on the Victoria Beach page.[^1][^6]

## Building the light

A separate light at Victoria Beach, a community on the northern side of Digby Gut, was put in operation on 8 July 1901.[^6] The Department's report, printed in 1902, describes it as on the eastern side of Digby Gut.[^5]

The Department's report for the twelve months ending 31 March 1916 lists an "Establishment of a mechanical fog bell" at Digby Gut, with M. J. Egan as contractor or foreman.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the bell house was built by day labour under M.J. Egan's direction in 1915, at a cost of about $1,800.[^1] It was a two-storey house, with the bell suspended in a gable above the porch.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends says John McGrath was hired to tend the bell for around $185 a year. It names him as keeper from 1915 to at least 1923, and names Howard McGrath as well, without dates.[^1] No other source read names keepers at this station.

## After the keepers

The bell house had no light of its own until 1963, when a light finally joined the bell.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the light was shown for years from a pole beside a white shed with a red roof. In the shed a fog detector sent out a beam of light and measured how much of it came back off the fog, to decide whether the foghorn needed to sound.[^1] A SaltWire photo caption says the station "didn’t receive a light until 1963."[^4]

In 2014 the Coast Guard changed the station. A flashing green light on a skeletal steel tower replaced the fixed white light, and the fog signal was discontinued.[^1] The Coast Guard's list gives the new light as a green flash of 2 seconds in every 6 seconds, at 20 m (66 ft) above water.[^3]

That September Coast Guard helicopters did the work. Pieces of the new tower were lifted by helicopter across the gut from Point Prim to Victoria Beach, and the remains of the old bell house were dropped at Point Prim.[^4] The white shed was dismantled. The new skeletal tower was set on an existing concrete foundation.[^1]

## Today

The light belongs to the Canadian Coast Guard.[^1] The grounds and tower are closed.[^1] The road to the bank is a right-of-way through private property, and Lighthouse Friends says permission should be sought.[^1] Visitors should not go down the bank to the light without that permission. It can be seen from the Saint John–Digby ferry.[^1]
