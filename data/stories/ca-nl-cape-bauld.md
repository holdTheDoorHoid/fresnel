Cape Bauld Lighthouse stands on the northern tip of Quirpon Island, at the northern entrance to the Strait of Belle Isle. Its light and fog signal went into operation on 15 August 1884, and the tower standing now is a concrete one put up in 1961.[^1]

## The place

The heritage statement describes a landfall light, with a range of 17 nautical miles, that serves ocean-going and coastal ships at the north entrance to the strait. The site is a steep, rocky, barren point, reachable only by boat.[^2]

In 1860 John Page, chief engineer of public works for the Province of Canada, listed Cape Bauld among four sites along the strait where a light would help. Lighthouse Friends says Page put the greatest need at Point Ferolle and Cape Norman, because the light at the southwest tip of Belle Isle already gave many of the same advantages. Cape Norman got its light in 1871, but mariners still thought Cape Bauld needed a light and a fog alarm to make the approach from the south safer.[^1]

The heritage statement says the Dominion government built the first tower here "soon after Confederation," when the waters around the site were not yet Canadian.[^2] It gives no date for that work. Lighthouse Friends dates the funding to 1880 and the lighting to 1884. The sources do not reconcile the two accounts.[^1]

## Building the light

A sum of $5,000 was set aside in 1880 for a lighthouse on Quirpon Island. In 1881 John A. Askwith took the contract for $4,775.[^1] The work ran late. The light and fog signal went into operation on 15 August 1884, and the total cost came to $8,675.71.[^1]

The first tower was wooden: square, about 18 m (60 ft) high, and attached to the keeper's dwelling. Its catoptric apparatus gave red and white flashes in turn, forty-five seconds apart, from a focal plane 43 m (141 ft) above high water.[^1]

The Department of Marine's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 records a water tank and a chimney cap for the dwelling, sent to the station by supply steamer.[^3] Its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1905 says the fog alarm was repaired, with its roof and sides reshingled and a coal shed built. It adds that modern diaphone machinery was installed, and that the new alarm would start operating the following season.[^4] On 1 November 1906 the fog horn was replaced by a diaphone, operated by compressed air, according to the report for the nine months to 31 March 1907.[^5] The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1906 says a tower similar to one fitted at Prescott for Cape Norman was then being prepared for Cape Bauld.[^6]

The report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1908 says the old wooden tower "is being replaced by a circular cast-iron tower, reinforced by a concrete covering with flying buttresses." The tower was prepared in the Department's workshops at Prescott and was being erected on a concrete foundation.[^7] The next report says the new tower was completed and its illuminating apparatus placed on it. The light was a second-order dioptric double flash, burning petroleum vapour under an incandescent mantle.[^8]

Lighthouse Friends says this tower was one of nine flying-buttress lighthouses put up between 1907 and 1912, a period when William P. Anderson was the Department's chief engineer. It gives the lens a turn every fifteen seconds, with a double flash.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the keeper's dwelling stayed in use until 1922, when a one-and-a-half-storey duplex was built.[^1] The Department's report for the fiscal year 1921-22 records a road bridge and culverts replaced, a barn reshingled, and the tower replastered and its lantern reglazed.[^9]

The heritage statement counts the present light as the site's second replacement.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the buttressed tower had begun to deteriorate after about fifty years. Instead of repairing it, a new octagonal concrete tower went up in 1961, with a new dwelling for the head keeper.[^1] The heritage statement places the concrete tower in a standard design that the Department of Transport developed and refined over the 20th century.[^2]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends names five keepers: Alexander St. Laurent (1882 to 1892), Edouard Fontaine (1892 to 1905), Edmond Fontaine (1905 to at least 1937), Edward P. Fontaine (1944 to 1963) and Cyril John Campbell (1963 to 1996).[^1]

St. Laurent drew an annual salary of $800 and had to hire a qualified assistant. Lighthouse Friends places his appointment with the 1884 opening, while its list starts in 1882.[^1] In 1898 Keeper Edouard Fontaine had a large new bridge built on the island, with local help and material sent from Quebec City.[^1]

The Department's keepers' table for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1908 places Edmond Fontaine on the same line as "Cap Bauld." That table was read from scanned text, which makes it less certain than the printed sources.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1889 Captain Williams of the SS Oregon complained to the Department of Marine that on 7 or 8 August he had been two miles off Cape Bauld in fog so dense he could not see the light, while the fog signal stayed silent.[^1] William H. Smith, who visited on the fall supply trip to investigate, read the station logbook. It showed no fog on the 7th or 8th, and fog on the morning of the 9th, when the horn sounded for five hours until rain cleared it. Local fishermen told Smith the alarm was promptly sounded whenever fog was about. Smith cleared Keeper St. Laurent and concluded that the captain must have met a dense fog bank and wrongly thought himself near land.[^1]

In May 1901 the Department's ice report, compiled from reports by its agents in the strait, says the ice seemed very light to the south of Cape Bauld. A steamer that came in on the 26th to land a crew reported little ice outside to the south, but a great deal along the shore, where every bay was blocked.[^10] The same report says, as earlier reports had, that wind and weather at Cape Bauld varied little from those at Belle Isle, 14 miles away.[^10]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends dates the change from a steam fog signal to an oil plant to 1916.[^1] The Department's report for the fiscal year 1920-21 records the steam fog-alarm plant at Cape Bauld replaced by an oil plant that year, with minor repairs.[^11] The two dates do not agree.

In 1924 an automatic radio fog-signal beacon was established at Cape Bauld. Lighthouse Friends says it had a range of about 25 miles and kept running in thick or foggy weather.[^1] The Department's report for the fiscal year 1923-24 had said a beacon station was proposed there for the next year.[^12]

The station got commercial electricity in 1966. In 1971 an electronic fog signal replaced the oil plant.[^1]

In 1998 the government offered the keeper's dwellings on Quirpon Island for sale. Linkum Tours, a company formed to run them, opened the Quirpon Lighthouse Inn to the public in July 1999.[^1] The keeper's duplex became a provincial Registered Heritage Structure in 2004, and in 2007 the lighthouse gained Recognized Federal Heritage Building status.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse. The keeper's dwellings are run by Linkum Tours as Quirpon Lighthouse Inn; guests reach them by boat from Quirpon and stay overnight.[^1] The heritage statement says the site is reachable only by boat.[^2] The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1]

The Coast Guard list gives a white flash every 15 seconds, a focal height of 53.9 m (177 ft) and a range of 17 nautical miles. It describes a red, hexagonal tower 14.6 m (48 ft) high.[^13] The heritage statement describes an octagonal base and lantern, so the two descriptions of the shape do not match.[^2] The 1909 report described a double flash; the current list gives a single flash. The sources do not say when the pattern changed.[^8][^13]
