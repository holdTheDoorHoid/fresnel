Concord Point Light stands at Havre de Grace in Harford County, Maryland, where the Susquehanna River meets Chesapeake Bay. Its conical granite tower dates from 1827. For most of its working life its keepers were members of one family, the O'Neills, and the Coast Guard discontinued the light as an active aid in 1975.[^1][^2]

## The place

The 1975 nomination says Maryland's General Assembly authorized the light in 1826 "because of the numerous vessels lost in this area."[^1] The nomination says the name apparently dates to the seventeenth century, when the area was known as Point Concord. The official name, it says, has always been Havre de Grace, and local people still call the light the Concord Lighthouse.[^1]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says the town commissioners deeded the 484-square-foot (about 45 m²) tract to the federal government in 1826, and that Congress provided $2,500 for the lighthouse on 18 May 1826, adding $1,500 on 2 March 1827.[^2] The nomination says the complex authorized in 1826 had a lighthouse, a keeper's quarters and an outhouse, and that the lighthouse was built in 1827 by John Donohoo, who was responsible for many of the early lighthouses on the Chesapeake Bay.[^1] LighthouseFriends spells the name Donahoo and says he built the tower of Port Deposit granite, barged down the Susquehanna, between May and November 1827. The keeper's house, about 200 feet (61 m) away, went up in the same season.[^2]

The height is hard to pin down. The scanned copy of the nomination gives a figure that is difficult to read, and the Archive's place record gives 30 feet (9 m) from base to the center of the lantern. LighthouseFriends also says thirty feet.[^1][^8][^2] The nomination puts the focal plane 32 feet (10 m) above the base.[^1]

The lantern first held nine lamps, each with a 16-inch (41 cm) reflector.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a steamer's lens replaced them in 1855, and that a sixth-order Fresnel lens came in 1869.[^2] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1892 records that on 28 October 1891 the sixth-order lens was replaced by one of the fifth order.[^3]

## The keepers

John O'Neill, an Irishman, was the first keeper. The nomination says the city gave him the post "in recognition of his heroism in defending Havre de Grace from the British during the War of 1812."[^1] LighthouseFriends describes the defense: O'Neill, a militia lieutenant with some four dozen men, held a battery of cannon on the point. When the British opened fire his men quickly abandoned their posts, and he kept one gun firing alone. He later described the experience, as LighthouseFriends quotes him: "The grape shot flew thick about me." He was eventually captured by the British. LighthouseFriends says his fifteen-year-old daughter reportedly rowed out in a skiff to plead for him, and that Admiral Cockburn, impressed by her courage, released him.[^2]

O'Neill kept the light until his death in 1838.[^2][^8] LighthouseFriends says Thomas Courtney and John Blaney then each served two terms, until Thomas Sutor took charge in 1853.[^2][^8] John O'Neill, Jr., succeeded Sutor in 1861 and died in 1863. His widow, Esther, took charge of the light, and the roster gives her name as keeper from 1863 to 1881.[^2][^8] LighthouseFriends says she was helped for part of those eighteen years by her son-in-law, Gabriel Evans.[^2]

Her son Henry E. O'Neill became keeper in 1881.[^2] The Archive roster ends his keepership in 1918.[^8] LighthouseFriends says he stayed in the keeper's house after he was due to retire, until his death in 1919.[^2] In 1918 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that he had written to the Secretary of Commerce to say he had bought $300 of Liberty Bonds with the savings he had put aside for his and his wife's funeral expenses. The Bulletin gives his age as 77 and his service as 40 years. The Secretary sent the letter to the President, who replied.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year 1894 records that "Severe storms in October, accompanied by unprecedentedly high water, caused considerable damage to the coal house, fences, board walks, and the like." The repairs were made and the debris cleared.[^4] The fiscal year ran from July 1893 to June 1894, so the storms were in October 1893. No wreck or rescue tied to this light appears in the sources.

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the light was switched over to electrical operation in November 1918, that Henry O'Neill was due to retire the following month, and that he was allowed to stay in the house as a custodian.[^2] The Bulletin's list of changes for 1919 instead says the light went from oil to acetylene, and from fixed red to flashing white.[^6] The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1919 lists Havre de Grace among lights changed from fixed to flashing.[^7] LighthouseFriends gives a different sequence: fixed white, then fixed red from 1879, then fixed green from 1931.[^2] The nomination describes the fixed green light as the last one used.[^1] The sources do not agree on what the light used and showed, or when those things changed.

The Archive gives 1920 as the year the light was automated.[^8] LighthouseFriends says the keeper's dwelling was sold to Michael Fahey in 1920 for $4,000 and used as a rooming house, restaurant and bar.[^2]

The nomination says the Coast Guard discontinued the light as an active light on 17 November 1975.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the Fresnel lens disappeared from the tower shortly afterwards.[^2]

## Today

A group of citizens formed the non-profit Friends of Concord Point Lighthouse in 1979.[^2] The tower was restored in 1981, and in 1983 a fifth-order Fresnel lens, on loan from the Coast Guard, was mounted in the lantern. The state acquired the keeper's house in 1988 and handed it to the city, and the Friends restored its interior between 2002 and 2004.[^2] The Friends' website lists a Keeper's House Museum & Gift Shop.[^9]

The lighthouse is owned by the City of Havre de Grace. LighthouseFriends gives opening times of Saturdays from 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. and Sundays from 1 to 5 p.m., April through October, and says special tours may be scheduled by telephone.[^2] In 2023 the fifth-order lens was taken down and put on display in the museum, and a 300 mm beacon loaned by the Coast Guard replaced it in the lantern.[^2] No source cited here says whether that beacon shows a light today.
