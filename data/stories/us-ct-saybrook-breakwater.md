Saybrook Breakwater Light stands at the end of the west breakwater at the mouth of the Connecticut River, off Old Saybrook.[^1][^2] It is a cast-iron tower on a cast-iron caisson, first shown on 15 June 1886 to mark a sand bar at the river's entrance.[^1][^2]

## The place

Congress approved a buoy to mark the bar at the river's mouth on 3 March 1831.[^1] After the Civil War, the National Register form says, buoys were no longer enough: "Such an aid to navigation was inadequate for the needs of the increased maritime traffic in Long Island Sound after the Civil War."[^1] The river mouth was dredged in the 1870s, and a granite breakwater was built out from each side, LighthouseFriends reports.[^2]

The older light was at Lynde Point, on the west side of the entrance. The National Register form says it was a land-based masonry tower established in 1802, one and a half miles (2.4 km) from the breakwater.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives 1803 for the first light at Lynde Point.[^2]

## Building the light

Money came in stages. LighthouseFriends says Congress appropriated $20,000 on 7 August 1882, half of the Lighthouse Board's rough estimate, and that the Board placed a temporary stake light on the breakwater while it awaited more money.[^2] The National Register form, by contrast, records an inadequate appropriation in 1883, after which more was requested and approved.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives a further $18,000 on 4 July 1884.[^2]

The form credits Major George H. Elliot, engineer-secretary of the Lighthouse Board, with designing a tubular cast-iron foundation to be prefabricated in curved sections and assembled near the site.[^1] The tower was fabricated by the G. W. and F. Smith Iron Company of Boston, whose name is inscribed on a plate above the entrance.[^1][^2] The caisson is 30 feet (9.1 m) across and 32 feet (9.8 m) high. It was lowered through 17 feet (5.2 m) of water onto a sand and gravel bottom, and the subfoundation was finished in 1885.[^1]

The light was first exhibited on 15 June 1886, when the form says the pier, superstructure and lantern were complete.[^1][^2] The form gives the tower as 48 feet (14.6 m) high, and LighthouseFriends as 49 feet (14.9 m).[^1][^2] The first lens was a fifth-order one. A fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced it on 1 January 1890.[^1][^2]

The fog-signal equipment was supplied in 1889. The form says the light then took over the fog warning from Lynde Point Lighthouse, one and a half miles (2.4 km) away.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the station's fog bell was struck every twenty seconds until a diaphragm foghorn replaced it around 1937.[^2] The tower and caisson were painted brown until 1892, when the tower was painted white.[^2]

The Board added to the station in its early years. Its report for 1891 records a landing stage and gangway, and 500 tons of riprap stone placed to protect the pier.[^4] The volume labelled 1895 records a small oil house and new landing steps, 500 tons of riprap placed around the pier, and a contract for 500 tons more.[^5] The Board's report for 1899 records that the fourth-order lens of 360 degrees was replaced by one of 270 degrees, and that the striking machine was replaced.[^6]

## The keepers

Frank W. Parmele was the first head keeper, from 1886 to 1890.[^3][^2] The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists 21 head keepers from then to 1959. In between there were assistants, and from 1943 the roster also lists Coast Guard personnel.[^3]

In 1905 keeper John Dahlman, who had decided to go duck hunting, was placing his shotgun down in his boat when it went off and cut his left arm from wrist to elbow.[^2] His wife sounded the fog bell as a distress signal, and the keeper at Lynde Point summoned medical help, LighthouseFriends reports.[^2]

Joseph F. Woods was head keeper here from 1912 to 1918.[^3] The archive's person record for him also lists head keeper at New London Harbor until 1912, and a short spell as head keeper at Cold Spring Harbor in 1912 between the two.[^10]

In 1917 Woods asked for an assistant. He pointed out that the railroad roundhouse in the village had three watchmen for each twenty-four hours "to cover about the same duties that I have to cover alone," and wrote that "the only relief I get is when my wife begs of me to rest while she stands watch."[^2] His request was not granted. He was assigned to a new station, and a few years later an assistant's post was added at Saybrook.[^2]

Alfred L. Bennett was assistant keeper from 1937 to 1938. LighthouseFriends says he was assigned to help with the extra work that the new fog signal caused, under the head keeper, Sidney Z. Gross.[^3][^2] Gross kept the light from 1935 to 1940.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 22 August 1923, the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports, keeper Elwood L. Butler picked up a man from the yacht Mohican, who had been driven into shoal water where the yacht could not reach him.[^7]

The hurricane of 21 September 1938 struck the station hard.[^8] Its keeper, Sidney Z. Gross, furnished an account of the storm at the station, which the Lighthouse Service Bulletin printed and LighthouseFriends prints.[^8][^9][^2] By 2 p.m. a light southeast breeze had sprung up from a perfect calm. By 3 p.m. it was blowing a gale, and Gross could not step out of the engine-room door, the only door through which the building could be entered or left.[^2] At 3:30 the water almost reached the platform, and the gasoline drum and the bridge to the breakwater were carried away.[^2] At 4 p.m. the platform around the tower was torn from its fastenings and carried away with the 12-foot (3.7 m) rowboat.[^2][^9] Dozens of rocks, weighing several tons, were moved, some as much as 60 feet (18 m).[^2][^9]

By 5 p.m. the water stood almost level with the engine-room floor, and the waves smashed the south window.[^2] Seawater poured in, and the fog-signal machinery was put out of commission.[^2] At 6 p.m. water came through a second-story window, and the battery house was broken up.[^2][^9] When daylight came, Gross wrote, there was nothing around the tower but the battery house, which was "badly out of shape."[^2][^9] He had earlier tried an oil vapor lamp, but its mantles collapsed in the vibration. He then fitted the fourth-order oil wick lamp and stayed up with it through the night.[^2][^9]

The Bulletin's editors wrote that the experience of the keeper and assistant keeper at Saybrook was typical of that of other keepers at exposed stations within the storm's path.[^8]

On 14 May 1939 a man fishing from the breakwater fell into the water.[^2] Gross took him into the kitchen, removed his wet clothes, gave him dry underwear and a flannel shirt, and kept him by the stove with hot coffee for about three hours.[^2] The man wrote to the district superintendent praising the keeper's "ability and integrity."[^2]

## After the keepers

A 17-wick oil lamp was converted to incandescent oil vapor in 1917.[^1] The first-story gallery was roofed over with asbestos building lumber in 1927.[^1] Since 1958 the fog signal has been operated by remote control.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the light was automated in 1959, and that the Coast Guard began occupying the station only in bad weather, leaving personnel at Lynde Point to guard the river mouth the rest of the time.[^2] The National Register form dates automation to 1968.[^1]

The light was listed on the National Register in 1990.[^2] A $64,000 project in 1996 repainted the tower, installed new handrails, and removed a generator and a 500-gallon fuel tank used for backup power in blackouts.[^2]

In June 2008 a notice offered the light, at no cost, to eligible public and nonprofit bodies, and said it could be reached only by boat.[^2] No transfer followed. The General Services Administration held an online auction in 2013, and the winning bid of $340,000 did not lead to a lease, because the state legislature did not pass a bill in 2014 authorizing bottom-land leases.[^2] The light went to auction again in 2015. The high bid of $290,000 came from a private buyer.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that the buyer put the light on the market in 2016, and that a New York newspaper reported in 2017 a plan to turn the tower into a clubhouse.[^2]

## Today

The light is privately owned, and LighthouseFriends reports that the tower is closed.[^2] It can be reached only by boat. A distant view from land is possible from Fenwick, from Agawam Avenue.[^2] The light shows a flashing green light.[^2] Do not land on the breakwater or the light, or try to climb it.
