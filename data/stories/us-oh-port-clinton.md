Port Clinton Light is a restored wooden pier light that now stands in Waterworks Park, on the Lake Erie shore of Ottawa County, Ohio, where the Portage River meets the lake.[^1][^11] It is the town's second light.[^2] A stone tower went up at the harbor mouth in 1833, and the wooden tower in the park was first exhibited on 15 July 1896, at the end of the west pier.[^2][^4]

## The place

By the early 1830s Port Clinton had become a fishing center, with fish sheds along the Portage River estuary.[^1] The harbor was unimproved, with shifting sandbars, and the conservancy's history says that whether the port needed a light was argued for years.[^2]

The argument outlived the first tower. In 1838 Lieutenant Charles T. Platt inspected the light and found it in good order, apart from chimneys that were too short. He thought it could be discontinued, as LighthouseFriends quotes his report, "without the slightest detriment to the commerce of the lake."[^1] Five years later Stephen Pleasonton, who then ran the country's lights, also recommended discontinuing it, because "not more than one or two vessels enter the port in the course of a year."[^1]

## Building the light

The government bought the acreage on the east side of the river mouth from Ezekiel and Charlotte Haines in October 1832. Two months later Levi Johnson, a Cleveland contractor, was commissioned to build the light.[^1] The tower that resulted was split stone, forty feet (12 m) tall, with a stone dwelling beside it.[^2][^8]

The number of lamps is one place where the sources disagree. LighthouseFriends describes eight lamps with reflectors, the same number Platt's inspection describes.[^1] The conservancy says the lantern count moved from eleven to four, then to five and eight, as politicians argued over the light's cost and value.[^2] Both sources agree that a sixth-order Fresnel lens was installed in 1855.[^1][^2]

In the 1890s Congress returned to the harbor mouth. The Light-House Board's report for 1893 says that the "reestablishment of a light, at a cost not to exceed $1,500," had been "authorized by the act approved February 15, 1893," but that no money had yet been appropriated.[^5] LighthouseFriends dates the act to 1895 instead.[^1] The new light went on piles at the outer end of the west pier, a square, pyramidal wooden tower with an octagonal lantern and a balcony.[^1][^3] Its lens lantern had ruby chimneys to give the red light.[^3] The Board's report for 1896 says the old light "was discontinued some 26 years" before, and that it had been reestablished.[^3] The Board's next report says the new light was first exhibited on 15 July 1896.[^4]

## The keepers

Austin Smith, a Connecticut sea captain, became the first keeper. LighthouseFriends says he took the post six months after work on the lighthouse began.[^1] His granddaughter, Juliet Smith, was the first child born in the keeper's dwelling.[^2] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's digital archive lists Smith as principal keeper from 1833 to 1848.[^6]

After Smith came a run of short tenures: William B. Craighill (1848–1849), Benjamin J. Orcutt (1849–1853), George O. Momeny (1853–1859) and A. Borden (1859).[^6] Momeny had been appointed on the recommendation of General J.A. Jones and others of the Democratic Party, according to LighthouseFriends, and he resigned after six years.[^1]

The Board discontinued the light in 1859 and reactivated it on 1 August 1864, with Leander S. Porter as keeper.[^1][^6] The light's final discontinuance came in 1870, when the lantern room was removed from the stone tower.[^1] The historical marker at the site gives the reason as a poor location and a lack of river traffic.[^8] The conservancy's history gives an earlier end date, 1856.[^2] The Board's report for 1896 says the old light had been discontinued some 26 years before it was reestablished, which points to about 1870.[^3]

Residents objected. An article in the Ottawa Union in November 1870, as LighthouseFriends describes it, argued that seven steamers, besides scows and schooners, worked the river day and night, and it proposed that the lighthouse be given to the town, which would use entrance and clearance fees to maintain it.[^1]

While the light was dark, a custodian lived in the vacated dwelling and drew a nominal salary of one dollar a year to keep the place in repair.[^1] Captain William Duff and his wife lived in the house while their own was built, and their son Alfred was born there.[^1] The Board's report for 1897 says the new keeper was allowed to take over the old dwelling, which "was occupied heretofore by the custodian of public property at this station during the period of discontinuance of the light."[^4]

Robert P. Waterfield (1896–1900) was the first keeper of the new light. LighthouseFriends says he was the son-in-law of Peter and Mary Hineline, the last caretakers of the old dwelling.[^1][^7] The conservancy calls him the last keeper of the stone light, but the U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster for the old station does not show him, and that roster ends with Porter in 1870.[^2][^6] William H. Egather and Daniel Finn follow, each listed for 1900 alone, before George H. Pope (1900–1911).[^7] LighthouseFriends says Pope kept up his trips out to the light even in winter, when the lake and river froze, and that he served until he died in 1911.[^1]

David C. Sutherland (1912–1926) came next. LighthouseFriends says that in March 1926 he moved to Toledo to look after the Manhattan Range lights.[^1][^7] His son Wallace tended the light for a few months, until electricity replaced the oil burners and the light was automated.[^1] Clarence Perry, whom the society's roster lists as a lamp lighter from at least 1939, later spent many years as the light's custodian.[^1][^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources name no dated storm, ice event or wreck tied to this light. The one rescue they describe is from 1836. In that year, LighthouseFriends reports, Smith took in a group of shipwrecked travelers who were bound for Chicago, and they stayed on and made their homes in Port Clinton.[^1]

The stone tower came down in August 1899. LighthouseFriends says the stone went by the government steamer Warrington to the Detroit River, where it protected the beach in front of Grassy Island light.[^1] The Elmore Independent, as LighthouseFriends describes it, regretted the loss of a tower that was reportedly as sound in 1899 as the day it was built.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1899 the Board called the keeper's dwelling "unsightly, uncomfortable, and unhealthful" and asked for $3,000 to build a suitable one.[^9] The Board renewed the request in 1900.[^10] The old dwelling came down in 1901, and a two-story frame house went up in its place.[^1] The conservancy dates the demolition to the spring of 1901.[^2]

The keeper had to row across the river to reach the light, because the dwelling stood on the east shore.[^1] A boathouse was built for that trip. The conservancy dates it to 1902.[^2] An iron oil house was placed on the pier in 1905.[^1]

The light was converted to electricity in 1926 and kept operating until 1952, according to the marker.[^8] In 1927 the government sold the lot and the keeper's house to a local physician, in May, the conservancy says.[^2] The house later became a restaurant, and a basement fire closed it in 2009.[^1] The conservancy says the site now holds a bank branch.[^2]

In November 1952 Dave Jeremy had the wooden tower taken off the breakwater and moved to his marina on the Portage River.[^1] A skeletal tower with a small house at its base took its place, and it carried a 375 mm lens.[^1]

The tower stayed at the marina for about sixty years, according to the conservancy.[^2] In 2010 the owner began to explore transferring the lighthouse to city ownership, and the conservancy says that idea led to its own formation the next year.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the owners donated the lighthouse to the city in 2011 so that it could be refurbished.[^1] The conservancy's home page gives 2012 as the year of the donation.[^11] LighthouseFriends dates the signing over of ownership to the conservancy to a ceremony on 14 April 2016, after a compromise in July 2015 over a clause in the city's contract.[^1] The sources disagree on when the gift was made. The conservancy's home page gives 2012, while LighthouseFriends gives 2011 for the donation and 2016 for the signed transfer.

The restoration was finished in 2014, the conservancy says.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the announcement that the work was complete to August of that year.[^1] On the morning of 16 August 2016 a crane set the restored tower on a barge, and that carried it down the Portage River to the north end of Waterworks Park.[^1] On 11 September 2016 a replica fifth-order Fresnel lens, crafted for the conservancy by Artworks Florida, was installed in the lantern room.[^1] The Archive's record gives the current optic as a "6th Order Replica," which disagrees with the fifth order.[^7] A replica of the boathouse went up beside the tower in 2018.[^1]

## Today

The tower stands north of Derby Pond in Waterworks Park.[^1][^11] LighthouseFriends gives the conservancy as the owner, and the conservancy describes the tower as placed on its waterfront.[^1][^11] The park has parking, a paved walkway around Derby Pond, fishing access, a public beach and restrooms.[^11]

The conservancy runs tours on Saturdays from noon to 5 p.m. from Memorial Day through Labor Day, for a $3 fee.[^11] The published tour season ends at Labor Day. A Lighthouse Passport can be stamped at the gift shop when it is open, and the Port Clinton Chamber of Commerce can stamp it on weekdays.[^11]

Nothing of the 1833 stone tower survives.[^1] The marker says the keeper's dwelling and boathouse have not survived, although a replica boathouse was placed beside the tower in 2018.[^8][^1]
