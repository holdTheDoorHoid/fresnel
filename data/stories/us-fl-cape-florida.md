Cape Florida Light stands at the southern tip of Key Biscayne, the nearest land to the Gulf Stream.[^1] Its brick tower was lit in 1825, and it is the oldest standing structure in Miami-Dade County.[^6] In July 1836 a band of Seminole Indians set it on fire with its assistant keeper and his helper inside.[^1][^2] The tower was rebuilt, lost its lamp in the Civil War, and went dark in 1878, when a light on Fowey Rocks replaced it.[^1][^2] It was relit in 1978 and now stands in Bill Baggs Cape Florida State Park.[^2][^6]

## The place

The nomination calls the Gulf Stream "the world's mightiest current," and says Cape Florida is the nearest land to it.[^1] Beginning in the late 1600s, the nomination says, "pirates, who used the Key as their base, began taking advantage of the Gulf Stream traffic to make their fortunes."[^1] The light's job was to mark the point. "With its completion in 1825, the light marked the southeastern tip of Key Biscayne, indicated the location of the dangerous Florida Reef and led mariners to the safe anchorage provided in the lee of the Key."[^1]

In May 1822 Congress appropriated $8,000 for a light on Cape Florida, to be built once the land had been ceded to the federal government.[^2] It added $2,790.85 in the spring of 1824.[^2]

## Building the light

The sources do not agree on who built the tower, or what became of its contractor.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says that in July 1824 the contract for Cape Florida and two other lights, at Key West and the Dry Tortugas, went to Samuel B. Lincoln of Boston. Lincoln sailed for Florida that August with his plans and materials, and neither he nor his crew was seen again. Noah Humphreys then took over the work.[^2] The nomination names the contractor as Samuel Lincoln, who "contracted to build a tower of solid brick, sixty-five feet high." It says Humphreys was appointed to inspect the tower and that he certified it on 1 December 1825, "attesting to the completeness of the work."[^1]

The nomination says the walls were not what the contract called for.[^1] "The walls were found to be actually hollow and the contractor saved the cost of about half the required brick," it says, and the destruction of the lighthouse "uncovered a fraud perpetuated on the government."[^1]

LighthouseFriends dates the first light to 17 December 1825, with fifteen lamps.[^2] The Society's record gives seventeen Argand lamps in 21-inch reflectors.[^9]

Rebuilding was slow. Congress appropriated $10,000 in 1837 to rebuild the tower.[^2] The nomination says the work "was not actually accomplished until 1846 because of hostile Indians in the area."[^1] LighthouseFriends gives a $13,000 appropriation of 10 August 1846 and a finish in 1847.[^2] Rowlett's directory lists the second tower as 1846.[^8] The nomination says the tower was raised to 95 feet (29 m) in 1855.[^1] LighthouseFriends adds that a new second-order Fresnel lens went in that year, as a result of the recommendation of Lieutenant George G. Meade, who later commanded the Army of the Potomac at Gettysburg.[^2]

## The keepers

John Dubose was the first keeper.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he was appointed to the post in 1825 and moved his wife, five children and two people LighthouseFriends describes as former slaves of his wife's brother to Key Biscayne, where they became the first American family to take up permanent residence.[^2] Dubose wrote to Stephen Pleasonton, pleading for a boat, about the station's isolation: "the situation of this light is far different from that of any other on the American coast."[^2]

The Society's roster shows the head keepers after him. Reason Duke served from 1846 to 1852, and Temple Pent held the post twice, in 1852 to 1853 and again from 1866 to 1868.[^9] The Frow family appears on the roster for most of the 1860s and 1870s: Simeon Frow was head keeper from 1859 to 1861 and first assistant from 1868 to 1878. John W. Frow was second assistant from 1867 to 1868 and then head keeper until the light went out. Charles and Joseph Frow were second assistants.[^9]

The assistant keeper in 1836 was John W. B. Thompson, who is on the roster for that year.[^9] He and a helper, a man LighthouseFriends names as Aaron Carter, were the only people at the light when the attack came.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The attack came on 23 July 1836.[^2][^4] Thompson wrote an account of it that the Charleston Courier printed, and that the Richmond Palladium reprinted that November.[^3][^4] "As many misrepresentations have been made concerning the burning of the Lighthouse at Cape Florida," he began.[^3] He was crossing from the kitchen to the dwelling when he saw the Seminoles, and he ran for the tower.[^4] He wrote that "the flames spread fast, being fed with yellow pine wood."[^3][^4] The nomination says the fire "was fed by 225 gallons of stored oil" kept for the light.[^1] Carter was killed.[^2]

Thompson fought from the windows and then from the top of the house, and ended up lying on the narrow platform outside the lantern.[^4] He wrote that "I took the determination to jump off," but that "something dictated to me to return and lie down again." He then threw a keg of gunpowder down the scuttle. The blast threw down the stairs and "all the wooden work near the top of the house," and he was left with "six rifle balls, three in each foot."[^4] The attackers thought he was dead and left.[^4]

Thompson was rescued the next morning.[^4] He wrote that boats from the United States schooner Motto, Captain Armstrong commanding, with a detachment of seamen and marines under Lieutenant Lloyd of the sloop-of-war Concord, had come to the landing that afternoon and retaken his sloop. They told him they "heard my explosion twelve miles off, and ran down to my assistance, but did not expect to find me alive."[^4] Those boats went back to the Motto for the night. The next morning three boats landed, one of them under Captain Cole of the schooner Pee Dee. They had made a kite during the night, to get a line to him, but without effect, and then "fired twine from their muskets." Thompson caught the twine, hauled up a heavier line, and two men were hoisted up to him, "who soon landed me on terra firma."[^4]

The light was damaged again during the Civil War.[^2] In August 1861, LighthouseFriends says, a trio of southern sympathizers removed the lamp and smashed the prisms of the lens, so the lighthouse could not help Union sailors.[^2] The nomination describes the damage as the work of "a band of lawless persons (presumably Confederates)."[^1] The two sources disagree on the repair. LighthouseFriends says the light was back in service on 15 April 1866, while the nomination says the lighting apparatus "was destroyed and not put back into operation until 1867."[^2][^1]

## After the keepers

The light went out on 15 June 1878, when the Fowey Rocks light began to shine.[^2] The nomination says the light "went out of active service" when the Fowey Rock light went into operation.[^1] Rowlett's directory describes the station as inactive from 1878 to 1978, and then again from 1990 to 1996.[^8]

The Light-House Board's report for 1901 has an entry on a tender which "assisted in a survey of the old Cape Florida Reservation."[^5]

LighthouseFriends says the State of Florida purchased the tower and the tip of the island in 1966, after the federal government twice rejected it as a historic landmark, and that the park opened in January 1967 under the name of Bill Baggs, a news editor who led the campaign to save it.[^2] The light was relit on 4 July 1978.[^2] LighthouseFriends ties a later outage to Hurricane Andrew, which damaged the tower in August 1992 and put it out until the state restored it in time for Miami's centennial in 1996.[^2] Rowlett's dates for the dark years differ from that account.[^8] By the 1920s the coastline had eroded so far that the water stood ten feet from the tower, which had been 100 feet from shore. Tropical storms had also destroyed the keeper's house and cookhouse.[^2]

## Today

The tower is 95 feet (29 m) tall.[^6][^8] Guided tours run at 10 a.m. and 1 p.m. Thursday through Monday.[^7][^8] The park's site says the climb is 95 steps, and VisitFlorida says 109.[^6][^7]

The lighthouse is owned by the State of Florida and stands inside the state park.[^2][^8] The Society's record lists the light as an active aid to navigation and as a private aid.[^9]

Rowlett's directory says the keeper's house and other station buildings are replicas built in 1970.[^8] The Society's record lists a replica summer kitchen, outhouse and cistern, also from 1970.[^9] LighthouseFriends places the rebuilding of the keeper's dwelling, outhouse, cookhouse and cistern after the 1978 relighting.[^2]
