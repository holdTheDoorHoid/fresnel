Fort Louisa Augusta light stood on a promontory at the north end of Christiansted harbor, on St. Croix in the U.S. Virgin Islands.[^1] The keeper's house that once carried its lantern stands, according to Rowlett's directory, as a damaged, unroofed ruin beside an active range light.[^1]

## The place

The light was set on a promontory at the north end of Christiansted harbor.[^1] In October 1918 the Lighthouse Service's inspector recorded the site as 1.14 acres, with the note "Being purchased from Municipality of St. Croix, VI later date." The tower stood about 100 yards (91 m) from the nearest high-water mark.[^2] The March 1918 inspection gives the means of reaching the light as a boat, one mile from Christiansted.[^5]

The islands had been the Danish West Indies until 1917.[^3] The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1917 says that the lighthouse work in St. Thomas, St. Croix and St. John, "formerly constituting the Danish West Indies," was placed under the Lighthouse Service by executive order dated July 20, 1917.[^3] The fiscal 1918 report says the care and maintenance of the aids were formally transferred by the military governor on August 16, 1917.[^4] The 1918 Commissioner's table and the 1924 Bulletin spell the name "Louisa"; Rowlett's directory and the Society's place record use "Louise."[^6][^9][^1][^11]

## Building the light

Rowlett's Lighthouse Directory gives the station as established in 1857 and dates the present one-story concrete keeper's house to 1919.[^1] The inspector who visited in March 1918 wrote "Not known" against the question of when the station was first built.[^5] In March 1918 the light was an oil lamp: three 1.5-inch flat-wick burners with no lens, in a home-made burner that the report dates "About 1898," hung in a lantern on the gable of the dwelling roof.[^5]

By October 1918 the light was a lens lantern. The inspector recorded "Characteristic changed October 4, 1918," and gave the light as fixed red, from a lens lantern with a fifth-order improved lamp and two red screens, in a small wooden tower on the gable of a one-story dwelling.[^2] The fiscal 1918 Commissioner's table of lights established that year includes "Fort Louisa Augusta, St. Croix." The table's note records that the Virgin Islands stations had been transferred to the Lighthouse Service by executive order of July 20, 1917; the table does not say what was established at this station.[^6]

## The keepers

The March 1918 inspection records a keeper who had been at the station for 17 years, and says the structure was already old when he took charge.[^5] The October report lists one "laborer in charge" for the station.[^2]

The Society's roster names Philip A. Molley as light attendant, "at least 1921-".[^7] In August 1924 a notice in the St. Croix Avis, dated at the station on August 25, was signed "R. A. Edwards, Keeper Fort Louisa Augusta." The notice, issued by order of the superintendent of lighthouses, said that persons of "objectionable character" using the lighthouse property as a bathing beach and for picnics would be put off, and that violators would be prosecuted.[^8] The sources do not show how the service of Molley and Edwards overlapped.[^7][^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On August 2, 1924, a hurricane visited St. Croix.[^9] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports "slight damage" at Fort Louisa Augusta: a few galvanized roof sheets were loosened, the house leaked, and the top of the bridge was broken up.[^9] During the night the lamp began to shake violently and had to be taken down.[^9] The Bulletin says the keeper took it down with the aid of another man.[^9]

## After the keepers

Rowlett's directory describes the station as "Inactive since 1931."[^1] The Society's light lists do not agree. They give the station a flashing white light in 1927 and 1935, a "Fl W 4s" light in 1943, and, in 2004, a "Q W" (quick white) light under Coast Guard number 3-32870.[^10] The lists describe a "Lantern on white house" in 1920, a "Lantern on white house with red roof" in 1927, and a "White skeleton tower" by 1935.[^10]

Rowlett's directory gives the active range light the same Coast Guard number, 3-32870. It describes the light as quick-flashing green, on a 17-foot (5 m) square skeletal tower beside the house, with a focal plane of 48 feet (14.5 m).[^1]

## Today

Rowlett's directory says the house is unroofed and heavily damaged, "probably caused by Hurricane Maria in September 2017," and that "battered ruins of this lighthouse still stand."[^1] The directory says the site is reached by road, and that the McCourts report it is not easy to find, so local assistance is suggested.[^1]

Rowlett's directory lists the site as open, the tower closed, and the owner as the U.S. Coast Guard.[^1] The Society's place record marks the station as not existing and not an active aid, which disagrees with the directory's active range light.[^11]
