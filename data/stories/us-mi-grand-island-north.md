Grand Island North Light is a brick light station on a cliff at the north tip of Grand Island, Lake Superior, near Munising, Michigan.[^1] Its tower dates from 1867, and the light was discontinued in 1961.[^2] A private owner has held the station since 1972.[^1]

## The place

Congress appropriated $5,000 on 3 March 1853 for a lighthouse on Grand Island. The site chosen was atop the cliffs at the northern end of the island.[^1] The light was meant for the vessels that began sailing the south shore of Lake Superior after the Soo Locks opened in 1855.[^1]

The reservation later shrank. The Lighthouse Board reported that "certain lots of land no longer required for light-house purposes, were sold at auction" at Munising on 10 June 1901.[^3]

## Building the light

The first light was lit in 1856 with a fourth-order Fresnel lens. Its focal plane was 204 feet (62 m) above the lake, according to LighthouseFriends; the Society's record gives 205 feet.[^1][^2] By 1865 the station was in poor condition, LighthouseFriends reports, because of inferior materials used in its first construction, and the Lighthouse Board had asked for money so that it would need no repairs for years.[^1] Congress provided $17,000 in July 1866 to rebuild it.[^1]

In 1867 the old tower and dwelling were torn down. The new tower was brick, nine feet (2.7 m) square, and attached to the west gable of a one-and-a-half-storey brick dwelling. The fourth-order lens went into its lantern.[^1] The height from the base of the structure to the centre of the lantern is 73 feet (22 m).[^2]

A stairway and tramway ran 229 feet (70 m) from the cliff top down to a boathouse on the lake. It was rebuilt in 1885.[^1]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's digital archive lists 43 keeper and assistant entries for the station, from 1856 to 1926.[^2] The first principal keeper, Reuben Smith, served from 1856 to 1860.[^2]

William Cameron was principal keeper from 1869 to 1885.[^2] LighthouseFriends, which draws on the station's log, describes him as a Canadian who had been educated at Toronto and who married a Chippewa woman, Sophie Nolan. That family detail comes from LighthouseFriends alone.[^1]

George Genry was principal keeper from 1893 to 1908, and Alf Evensen from 1908 to 1925.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In late November 1872 the keeper's log, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, records a gale that drove the seas up the stairway and tore away 43 feet (13 m) of it.[^1]

In June 1908 the *Detroit Times* reported from Munising that the body of the assistant keeper of the north light on Grand Island had been found in a sailboat between Munising and Grand Marais, and that keeper George Genry (spelled Genery in the newspapers) was missing. "It is believed that both men were murdered and robbed," the paper said.[^4] The paper said a report named the assistant as E. S. Morrison of Detroit, but that the identification was not conclusive.[^4] The archive lists an Edward S. Morrison as a first assistant for 1908 only.[^2]

On 13 July the paper reported that Genry's body had been found on the beach about twenty miles (32 km) from Munising.[^5] The paper said the finding "clears up the mystery" of his disappearance, that his death was "undoubtedly due to accidental drowning", and that a coroner's jury had decided Morrison died of exposure.[^5] A report on 14 July described the body as "badly decomposed" and said there were "no marks of violence".[^6] LighthouseFriends gives the official cause of Morrison's death as exhaustion from exposure in an open boat, and says the circumstances of both deaths were never explained.[^1] That differs from the July newspaper reports, which said the Genry finding cleared up the mystery.[^5]

## After the keepers

The Board's 1899 list of lights gives the Grand Island light as "fourth order, fixed white, varied by a white flash every ninety seconds".[^7]

In 1915 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists a change at "Grand Island, Lake Superior, Mich." from oil to incandescent oil vapour.[^8] The list does not say which Grand Island light it means. This story assumes it is the north light; the Grand Island Harbor light is a separate station.[^7][^8]

On 4 October 1926 the characteristic was changed to a one-and-a-half-second white flash every fifteen seconds, and the station became unwatched.[^1]

In 1961 the brick tower was abandoned, and a battery-powered light on a twelve-foot (3.7 m) metal post nearby took over.[^1][^2] The original light was discontinued that year.[^2]

In 1972 a private owner bought the station and restored the lighthouse and its outbuildings.[^1]

## Today

The brick tower and dwelling are a private summer home, and the station is not open to the public.[^2][^1] Visitors should not go onto the grounds or the cliff.

The station is recorded on the National Register of Historic Places under reference 85002149, but the Park Service's online copy of the nomination has not been digitised.[^9] Cleveland-Cliffs sold the island to the Forest Service in 1990, and Congress made it a national recreation area.[^1]
