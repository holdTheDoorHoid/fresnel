Green Island is a bare rock in Chatham Sound, about 40 km northwest of Prince Rupert and 5 km from the Alaska border, and its lighthouse is the northernmost in British Columbia.[^1] Parks Canada calls it the first notable landmark seen as marine traffic enters Canada.[^1]

## The place

Green Island lies in Chatham Sound on the track of vessels using the Inside Passage. The Klondike Gold Rush of 1897 brought a surge of traffic to this coast, and Parks Canada's statement of significance names the steamer *Mexico* and the collier *Bristol* among the wrecks that led to the light's establishment in 1906.[^1] The *Bristol*'s loss is the best documented. The Department of Marine's wreck list records that on 2 January 1902 the steamer, of Victoria and 1,983 tons gross, was carrying coal from Ladysmith, on Vancouver Island, to Alaska when, in a southeast gale and "dirty weather," she stranded on a reef at 11 p.m. She slid off the next morning and sank in 34 fathoms, and seven of the crew, including the master, pilot and chief engineer, were drowned.[^2] The report names the reef "Grey Island," off North Dundas Island; Lighthouse Friends and the CoastView site place the same wreck on Green Island; Lighthouse Friends' own account of a 1929 incident also mentions a Gray Island close to the station. Which island the *Bristol* struck is not settled here.[^2][^3][^4]

## Building the light

In its report for the year ended 30 June 1905 the Department of Marine described a combined tower and dwelling being established on the southwesterly point of the island: a framed wooden building with shingled sides, a 10-foot iron lantern, and a height of 44 feet from base to the top of the lantern's ventilator.[^5] The same report describes the apparatus as a single-flashing third-order lens of eight panels, one revolution taking 45 seconds.[^6] The following year's report says the lighthouse had been completed at a total cost of $8,775.20.[^7] Lighthouse Friends adds that the lens was made by Barbier, Benard & Turenne of Paris, that the work was done by day labour under George Forrest, and that the Victoria *Daily Colonist* announced on 2 September 1905 that building would begin within ten days.[^3]

The wooden light was replaced by the present octagonal concrete tower. Parks Canada gives the tower's construction as 1956, while Lighthouse Friends says "around 1957."[^1][^3] The tower is 10.5 m (35 ft) high. Two keepers' dwellings dating from 1960 and several outbuildings, including sheds from 1945, belong to the heritage designation.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists William A. Bosdet as first head keeper, serving about a year from 1906, followed by Stephen Baker, Alexander Dingwell (1910 to 1918) and John Thomas Moran (1919 to 1936); it counts the station's keeper turnover as higher than average.[^3] Its references list departmental reports and a *Daily Colonist* article of 21 October 1909. In autumn 1909 the supply steamer *Quadra* was close to a month overdue. Baker's family are said to have lived on cornstarch and bread, and when the light's oil ran out the household lamps were drained to keep it burning one more night.[^3] Of the Dingwell years, the same source relates a family story (its references include an email from Georgina Dingwell) of a child who cut his head on the rocks and was taken by his father in a rowing boat across Hecate Strait to the hospital at Port Simpson.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 24 April 1929 Keeper Moran watched a dinghy overturn between the station and the vessel *Grier Starrett*. He rowed out and pulled the engineer, William Cummings, from the overturned dinghy into his rowboat, but could not find the captain, Harold Dahl. Moran's report to his superior ended with a protest against the practice of letting fishing boats leave port with unseaworthy dinghies, which he called "simply death-traps."[^3] This account is Lighthouse Friends' retelling of the report; the original was not read.

Lighthouse Friends also records that in spring 1963 keeper Milton John Wolski and his assistant were blown out to sea in a small boat in a wind of 60 mph and beached it some miles from Ketchikan, Alaska.[^3]

## After the keepers

Fog signals came gradually. A hand foghorn was supplied in 1919, an acetylene gun fired once a minute replaced it in 1938, and a diaphone fog alarm followed in 1949.[^3] The station has kept resident keepers: Lighthouse Friends lists Serge Pare as head keeper from 1996 and Barney Hynes from 2016, the latter to at least 2024.[^3] In July 2024 the Canadian Coast Guard ended keeper functions at Carmanah Point and Pachena Point, and said 25 other light stations on the British Columbia coast would keep operating as staffed stations; the release does not name them.[^8] Parks Canada recognised the lighthouse as a heritage lighthouse on 29 May 2015, and its statement of significance says the light helps reinforce Canadian sovereignty by keeping a human presence on a remote shore.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard's List of Lights gives a white flash every 5 seconds from 19.2 m above the water, with a nominal range of 17 nautical miles;[^9] Lighthouse Friends says the Coast Guard owns the lighthouse.[^3] It stands on an island reached by boat. Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower closed, and that it can be seen from cruise ships and ferries bound to or from Ketchikan.[^3]
