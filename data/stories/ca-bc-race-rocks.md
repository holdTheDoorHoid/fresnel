Race Rocks is a small group of islets just over one nautical mile off the southern tip of Vancouver Island, where the tide runs past at up to eight knots. Its stone tower was first lit in the last days of 1860, and it has stood on the rocks ever since. Resident keepers tended the light until 1997, when it was automated.[^1][^2]

## The place

The name describes the water. The tide makes a race around the rocks, and the Juan de Fuca Strait narrows here to about 12 nautical miles between the Canadian and American shores.[^2] Ships transiting the strait for Victoria, Vancouver, Seattle and the inside passage relied on the light as an aid to navigation.[^2]

The need for a light had become obvious to the British Admiralty in the early 1850s.[^2] Rear-Admiral Robert Baynes, in a letter that went with Captain George Richards's report, called it "a great want" for vessels coming to Vancouver Island, and wrote that Victoria harbour was "almost impossible after dark" to make.[^2] In October 1858 Captain Richards of HMS *Plumper* recommended a light there, at the request of Governor James Douglas.[^1] The colony also had a border to assert. The gold rush on the Fraser River had brought thousands of Americans north.[^2]

## Building the light

According to the *British Colonist* of 12 August 1859, as the Race Rocks Ecological Reserve's history quotes it, HMS *Plumper* left Esquimalt to choose a site on the outer island of Great Race Rock, on its southern side, about 8 m (25 ft) above the water.[^2] The money came from the Imperial Treasury, about £7,000. The two colonies were expected to repay £1,750 each.[^1] Joseph Pemberton, the colony's surveyor, oversaw the work, and the tower contract went to John Morris.[^1]

The materials are a matter of dispute. Richards had suggested that stone for the tower could be quarried on the island.[^1] The racerocks history says the Admiralty chose Scottish granite, cut and numbered in Scotland and shipped out as ballast. Timber derricks and scaffolding raised the stones through 1860.[^2] By April 1860 Pemberton reported that a third of the tower had been dressed.[^1]

The lens was a second-order Fresnel, which came out from England aboard the *Grecian* in August 1860 and was set atop the finished tower that autumn.[^1] A notice reprinted in a Honolulu newspaper in March 1861 said the tower was complete and the light, flashing every ten seconds, would be shown "in the course of a few weeks".[^7] Lighthouse Friends gives 26 December 1860 as the day the light was first exhibited.[^1] The racerocks timeline calls 25 December the official operating date.[^2]

The tower is cylindrical, with black and white bands. Its focal height is 36 m (118 ft), and the List of Lights gives the tower as 24.4 m high.[^4][^5] Keeper George Davies painted the bands.[^2] Mariners were told the tower would carry the bands after 1 October 1864.[^1] The keeper's dwelling was of rubble stone quarried from the rock. In 1873 it was plastered outside and given new windows, doors and a steeper roof.[^1] A wooden tower was put up in 1867 to hold a fog bell, but the surf often drowned the bell out.[^1]

Fog signals came later, and the two histories disagree on the first steam signal. Lighthouse Friends says a steam fog alarm began operating in August 1881, after an appropriation of $3,500 was made that year.[^1] The racerocks history says that in 1892 the Department of Marine and Fisheries installed a steam plant and two compressed-air fog horns.[^2] The Department's annual report for the fiscal year 1909–10 lists a 3-inch duplicate diaphone plant for Race Rocks, costing $6,800, among that year's changes to existing aids.[^10] The lantern was replaced in 1925.[^4][^1]

## The keepers

The sources disagree on who kept the light first. The Lighthouse Friends history says George Davies was the first keeper at the new Fisgard light, at Esquimalt, and then moved to Race Rocks to take charge of the first lighting there.[^1] The racerocks keeper list starts with a keeper named Roberts for February 1860 to 1861, and then gives George Nicholas Davies from 9 February 1861.[^3] Davies kept the light until December 1866, when he died on the station. His funeral was held in Victoria three days later.[^1][^2][^3]

His widow, Rosina, stayed on.[^3] She had the flag lowered to half-mast while her husband was ill. For nine days, the Lighthouse Friends history says, no passing vessel noticed the signal.[^1] The racerocks list gives her as keeper from 1866 to 1879 after her husband's death. Her name appears as keeper, and the list gives no other role for her.[^3]

Thomas Argyle followed, as chief keeper.[^3] The two histories disagree on when he started. The Lighthouse Friends history gives 1866, and racerocks gives 1867. Both say he left in 1888.[^1][^3] His wife, Ellen, is listed as an assistant keeper from 1867 to 1871.[^3]

The two histories also disagree on his pay cut. Lighthouse Friends says that in 1880 the Department cut his salary by 80 percent, to $125, and told him to pay his own assistants and provisions out of it.[^1] The racerocks history places the cut to $125 after the Dominion took over the lights in 1871, when he was expected to pay for his own assistants and all supplies.[^2]

Albert Argyle, the keeper's son, took charge in 1888. The Lighthouse Friends history says that a few months later, in a political move, he was replaced as keeper.[^1] The keeper list ends his stint on 1 January 1889.[^3] Frederick Mercer Eastwood then kept the light from 1891 to 1919. The Department's keeper tables for the fiscal years 1907–08 and 1911–12 both name him.[^8][^9] James Thomas Forsyth followed, from 1919 until he died in office in December 1932.[^3]

Andrew Ritchie and Mary Ritchie are listed together from 1933 to 1940. The list does not say what role Mary held.[^3] Arthur Anderson left the service in January 1950, when he drowned on the way back to the station.[^1][^3] A note on the keeper list says that before 1961 the senior keeper hired his own assistant, and that "The government then began a system of examining all applicants."[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The bark *Nanette*, a British vessel carrying sawmill machinery and Hudson's Bay Company goods, was wrecked on the rocks three days before the official operating date.[^2][^6] The *Washington Standard* of Olympia, quoting the Victoria *Colonist*, gave the wreck as happening on the 24th.[^6] Lighthouse Friends dates it to 23 December and says the crew reached the island, where the lighthouse workmen cared for them.[^1] The dates differ.[^1][^2][^6]

The two histories give different values for the cargo: $165,000 in one, and an estimated $200,000 in the other.[^1][^2] The Lighthouse Friends history says the wreck was sold at auction for $650, after locals had taken much of the cargo.[^1] The racerocks history says salvage ended in 1861, when the wreck was swept into 15 fathoms of water in foul weather.[^2]

Lighthouse Friends dates a boat accident to Christmas Eve 1865; the racerocks history says Christmas Day.[^1][^2] A boat carrying Rosina Davies's brother, his wife and three friends overturned near the station, which had no boat. The keepers could only watch as their visitors drifted away.[^1]

In September 1877 Argyle's assistant, Henry Montrose, pulled two men from the water. They were deserters from HMS *Shah*, who had been six hours on the water trying to cross the strait on two logs.[^1] Argyle advised them to go back to their ship. He was later fined $100 for helping two seamen desert, and on appeal a jury found him not guilty.[^1]

In July 1888 Thomas Argyle Jr., returning from a sealing trip, rowed for the station in a westerly gale. His small boat was found the next morning with one body in it.[^1]

In May 1929 the survey ship *Lillooet* investigated the "silent zone" that keepers and captains had reported around the rocks. The investigation found that the sound was deflected by the horns' position. The horns were moved to a separate tower.[^2]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the stone keeper's house was torn down in 1972, despite pleas to preserve it.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory gives the same year for the demolition.[^4] The racerocks history's caption, however, places the destruction of the stone house in the early 1960s.[^2] The station's buildings from 1951 and 1966 remained in use, and since 1997 they have served as an ecological research station.[^4]

Automation came in 1997. The keeper list records the station as automated that year.[^3] Pearson College now leases the station, and its ecoguardians have been in residence there since 1997.[^3][^4]

The tower was restored and repainted in 2009, in time for its 150th anniversary in 2010.[^4][^1]

## Today

The Lighthouse Directory names the Canadian Coast Guard as owner.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says the lighthouse is owned by Pearson College.[^1] Pearson College leases the station, and the Race Rocks Ecological Reserve manages the site.[^4]

The light shows a white flash every ten seconds from a focal height of 36 m (118 ft), with a range of 19 nautical miles. A fog horn sounds from the station.[^5]

The island can be reached only by boat. The site and tower are closed, and landing on the island needs special permission.[^4] Do not try to land on the rocks or climb the tower.
