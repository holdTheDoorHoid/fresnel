Four Mile Crib Light is a navigation light on a water-intake crib in Cook County, Illinois, about 2.9 nautical miles (3.3 miles) east of Monroe Harbor in Chicago. The crib is a stone-and-brick structure, and a tunnel runs from it to two pumping stations on shore.[^1][^2]

## The place

Chicago's residents once drew their drinking water from the Chicago River, which by 1860 was badly fouled by sewage and slaughterhouse waste.[^1] The city's first water crib, the Two-Mile Crib, stood out in Lake Michigan, and its tunnel was completed on 30 November 1866.[^1] Four Mile, the city's second crib, was set farther out still, to keep clear of the pollution the Chicago River carried into the lake.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the city was responsible for the light on the Two-Mile Crib. It quotes the Light-House Board's 1877 call for a proper light on that crib, and its 1881 agreement to supply a third-order lens once the city built a tower.[^1]

## Building the light

The sources do not agree on when the crib was built. Preservation Chicago says it was designed by the architect D.H. Hayes, begun in 1887 and completed in 1894.[^2] LighthouseFriends says it was completed in 1891, and the US Lighthouse Society's archive gives 1891 as the year the station was established.[^1][^4] The preservation group's website says the crib went into service in 1892 and describes its original condition as completed in 1894.[^3]

The costs disagree as well. LighthouseFriends gives $472,890.93 for the stone and brick crib alone and $1,526,143.68 for the whole system, tunnels included.[^1] Preservation Chicago gives $429,000 in 1894 dollars for the crib and tunnel.[^2]

In 1898 a steam heating plant was installed. It kept the well room at 70 degrees and, by LighthouseFriends's account, meant no extra keepers were needed in winter to manage ice.[^1]

The light stood on a red skeletal iron tower with a silver lantern, which showed a white flash every three seconds from a focal height of 66 feet (20 m).[^1] No source gives a date for the tower.

LighthouseFriends reports that in 1932 the Bureau of Lighthouses recorded a submarine cable from the Chicago Harbor light that powered the Four Mile light, and a two-room addition on the crib.[^1] The Lighthouse Service's 1934 Bulletin, describing its cable-laying work, lists two cables for Four Mile Crib, laid at the same time.[^5]

## After the keepers

Preservation Chicago says that for many years the city ran the cribs round the clock with isolated crews who lived and worked on them, and that the cribs still in use are now staffed remotely.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the last crew of keepers was taken off duty in 1990, and that all the intake cribs received security systems in 2002.[^1]

Preservation Chicago says Four Mile had been on standby service for years before the city planned to demolish it under a four-year capital programme announced in 2015.[^2] The preservation group says the city's 2013–2017 capital plan allocated $11,400,000 to demolish Four Mile and the Wilson Avenue crib, and that the work was put out for bid in 2016.[^3] The group says Four Mile "has been spared from intended destruction, at least for now."[^3]

## Today

Four Mile Crib belongs to the Chicago Department of Water Management, and LighthouseFriends reports that its tower is closed.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^4] Vessels may not enter the buoyed zone that surrounds each crib, and LighthouseFriends says the best views of the light are from a boat or an aircraft.[^1]

The preservation group proposes to stabilise the crib first, then open it to the public. Its website describes self-guided and docent-led walks, but those are plans, not a visitor programme that exists now.[^3][^6]

The group's website carries a 2021 copyright notice, and it does not say what the city has decided since.[^3]
