Ashland Harbor Breakwater Light stands on a concrete pier at the outer end of a rock breakwater in Chequamegon Bay, in western Lake Superior, off the city of Ashland in Ashland County, Wisconsin.[^1] Its 58-foot (17.7 m) reinforced-concrete tower was lit in October 1915, and the men who kept it lived on shore, a little over two miles away.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The Corps of Engineers began a riprap breakwater at Ashland in 1889 to protect the port's facilities and the vessels in its harbor.[^1] The breakwater caused a problem of its own. Its length and its nearness to the shipping channel made it a hazard to navigation, so in 1911 a fixed light mounted on a pole went up at its end.[^1] Congress appropriated money for a permanent lighthouse in 1913. LighthouseFriends gives the sum as $25,000, granted on 22 October 1913.[^1][^6]

## Building the light

The Eleventh Lighthouse District's depot at Detroit drew the plans, for one integrated structure: a reinforced-concrete pier with the tower built on it.[^1] The pier was begun in 1914, on a pierhead crib set out in 1911 that had already settled.[^1][^6] LighthouseFriends says that 350 tons of rock were placed on the crib to speed its settling, and that by the spring of 1913 it had sunk 3.16 feet.[^6] The rock was removed, a concrete cap was poured, and the superstructure was completed in 1915.[^1][^6]

The beacon was officially established on 15 October 1915.[^1] The Commissioner of Lighthouses reported that "This light and fog-signal station was established October 15, 1915," for the purpose of marking "the outer end of the breakwater at Ashland."[^2] The 1916 report describes the tower as "hexagonal in plan and pyramidal in shape."[^3]

Power came from shore. Current came from the city to a control station on the boathouse site, where it was "transformed from 2,200 volts into two currents of 220 volts and 110 volts," according to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the submarine cable ran from the control station to the eastern end of the breakwater and then along it to the lighthouse.[^6] The 1916 report places the dwelling, the boathouse and the control station "on shore in the city of Ashland," "slightly over 2 miles from the outer end of the breakwater." It describes the boathouse as built "on a submarine site on cribwork."[^3] The total cost came to $24,943.80, LighthouseFriends says.[^6] Its electric siren fog signal sounded a four-second blast every twenty seconds, and a sign flasher in the control station gave the light two seconds of light and then one second of darkness.[^6] The nomination describes the light today as one that "flashes once every 6 seconds."[^1] The two descriptions do not match, and this account does not reconcile them.

## The keepers

The archive's roster names seven people for the station between 1915 and 1962.[^7] The first keeper, James H. Bergan, served from 1915 until his death. LighthouseFriends says he died of a heart attack on 23 September 1922.[^6][^7] His wife and family tended the light until Roger Campbell, the replacement keeper, arrived.[^6] The archive lists a Cecelia Bergan for 1922 alone and does not say who she was.[^6][^7]

Roger W. Campbell followed in 1922 and stayed to 1925.[^7] Frank H. Mersy was on the roster from 1925 to 1947. LighthouseFriends says Mersy came to Ashland by transfer in 1925.[^6][^7] Earl G. Seseman, whom LighthouseFriends describes as the station's last keeper, held the post from 1947 until the light was automated.[^6][^7]

Keepers at Ashland lived ashore. The nomination says the light was run from 1915 to 1961 by "keepers who resided onshore in Ashland," and that the lighthouse "contained minimal living quarters providing accommodations for personnel there on duty."[^1] LighthouseFriends says living quarters were provided in the tower's second and third stories, for use in an emergency, and that a derrick on the pier was there for lifting the keeper's boat.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 28 September 1924, Keeper Campbell helped the occupants of the disabled motorboat U 297. The boat had "stalled in the bay during the night in a northeast storm" and partly filled with water.[^5] LighthouseFriends gives the date as 29 September.[^6]

In 1925 the light was out of service for a time after damage to the submarine cable.[^6] LighthouseFriends says new cable was laid, and that the light came back on 15 June of that year.[^6]

According to LighthouseFriends, which names no newspaper or report for the account, Mersy rowed out in November 1926 to service the light and was caught at the tower by a sudden storm.[^6] After three days his supplies ran out. When he tried the two-mile crossing by boat, heavy ice barred the way near shore. Men on skis reached him, threw him a rope and towed his boat to safety.[^6]

## After the keepers

The nomination says the light "was automated in 1961," and that its original fourth-order Fresnel lens "was removed in 1967."[^1] LighthouseFriends puts automation in 1962. It says the lens was taken out of the lantern room and replaced by a modern optic in 1980.[^6] The archive's place record gives "Year Automated: 1980."[^7] The sources disagree on the year, and this account does not settle it.

The nomination records that the fog signal and its air compressor were taken away, without giving a date.[^1] It also says it "appears" that vandals removed the watch room's "brass-framed port-lights" in 1983.[^1] The Fresnel lens went to a Coast Guard station for survey, and its "present whereabouts is unknown."[^1] The nomination also describes "the attachment of a solar array to the lantern gallery guardrail."[^1]

## Today

The National Register nomination describes the light as "Owned by the U.S. Coast Guard, this automated lighthouse," and says the breakwater belongs to the Corps of Engineers.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that a 2014 measure, enacted in the 2015 National Defense Authorization Act, transferred the light to the National Park Service, and that a centennial celebration marked the transfer in July 2015.[^6] The U.S. Lighthouse Society Digital Archive's place record gives the owner as "National Park Service - Apostle Islands NL."[^7]

The light is reached by boat. The nomination says "Access to the lighthouse is by boat," and LighthouseFriends notes that the tower is closed.[^1][^6] LighthouseFriends says the keeper's dwelling and boathouse still stand on the shore nearby.[^6]
