The Peche Island Rear Range Light was the rear light of a pair of range lights that marked a twenty-foot ship channel off Peche Island, a Canadian island where the Detroit River meets Lake St. Clair.[^1][^3] In 1983 a modern structure replaced the rear light. The old tower was restored and moved to the riverfront at Marine City, Michigan, on the St. Clair River.[^1]

## The place

Peche Island, called Isle aux Peches (French for Fisheries Island) and for a time Peach Island, is a Canadian island at the meeting of the Detroit River and Lake St. Clair.[^1] The Life-Saving Service's 1884 report has a table of places where vessels stranded during the previous ten years, and it lists "Peche Island, Lake Saint Clair, Canada."[^2]

The channel was a federal project. In July 1892 Congress passed an act for a ship channel with a minimum depth of twenty feet in the connecting waters of the Great Lakes. The work was carried out over the next five years, and one of its improvements was a twenty-foot channel through Grosse Point Flats in Lake St. Clair.[^1]

## Building the light

Pile clusters marking the edge of that channel were carried away in January 1898. The new range lights were set in shoal water north of the island to mark the channel, and were established on 15 April 1898.[^1][^3] The front light was a mast on a pile cluster in nineteen feet of water. The rear light stood in eight feet of water, 4,650 feet (1,417 m) southwest of it.[^1]

The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 says the lights were carried away by ice in the spring of 1899 and were "reestablished on April 26, 1899," after the piles were finished on 20 April.[^3] LighthouseFriends puts the reestablishment two days after the piles were driven, which would be about 22 April.[^1] The two accounts differ by about four days.

Congress appropriated $18,000 for stronger lights on 30 June 1906.[^1] The permanent lights went into operation on 15 June 1908.[^1] A description of the new towers by the Lake Carriers' Association in 1908, as LighthouseFriends prints it, gives the rear light as 57½ feet (17.5 m) above the water and as "a fixed red reflector light."[^1]

In 1914 the front light was converted to acetylene, and the range was automated. LighthouseFriends says the station's two keepers were then assigned elsewhere.[^1] In 1926 the cribs were rebuilt in reinforced concrete after they had greatly deteriorated.[^1]

## The keepers

John F. Kerby was the first head keeper, LighthouseFriends says, and he served from 1898 to at least 1912.[^1][^7] The roster lists "John F. Kerby (1898-at least 1912)" and, after him, William H. Gill, head keeper at least from 1913 to 1914, who had been a first assistant before that.[^7] LighthouseFriends says Kerby had six different assistants in his fourteen years.[^1] The roster names them. William Schweikart served from 1898 to 1905, William C. Fisher from 1905 to 1908, and Edward B. Gates from 1908 to 1910.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Ice carried the range away in several springs.[^1] In the spring of 1899 it did so, and the report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 records that a tug carried the front light away on 27 July 1899. The light was re-established on 4 August 1899 "at the expense of the owners of" the tug.[^4]

The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 says that both clusters were carried away in the spring of 1901 and were "reestablished on April 29, 1901."[^5]

On the evening of 5 November 1927 the front range tower was "destroyed by fire and explosion," according to the Lighthouse Service Bulletin's account, which describes an explosion that "lifted the tower about 20 or 30 feet in the air."[^6] The Bulletin and LighthouseFriends differ on who responded. The Bulletin names the city fire tug and the lighthouse tender *Thistle*. LighthouseFriends names the fireboat *James R. Elliott*.[^6][^1] LighthouseFriends says a tugboat captain reported the light ablaze after seeing two men leave its crib in a rowboat.[^1]

The Bulletin said that in such a tower the tank compartment and the light compartment should be "isolated from each other and separately ventilated."[^6]

## After the keepers

After the fire, the front crib was given a square, pyramidal tower in place of the conical one that was destroyed.[^1] The range lights were electrified in 1940.[^1]

By 1980 the rear light was badly listing, and in 1983 it was replaced by a modern structure.[^1] A Port Huron bank acquired the old tower from a contractor that had been hired to scrap it. The bank restored it and placed it on the riverfront in Marine City, where it was dedicated on 21 August 1983.[^1]

In 2013 Marine City failed to win a matching grant from the state's lighthouse assistance program.[^1] In 2014 the tower was repainted and given new windows and lighting, at a total cost of about $35,000.[^1]

## Today

The tower stands on the Marine City riverfront. LighthouseFriends says the city owns it, and that the tower is closed.[^1] LighthouseFriends also reports that skeleton towers displaying fixed white lights serve the range today.[^1]
