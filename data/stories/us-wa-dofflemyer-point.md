Dofflemyer Point Light stands on a sandy point at the east entrance to Budd Inlet, the southernmost inlet of Puget Sound, about nine miles north of Olympia, Washington.[^2] Its eight-sided concrete tower, 30 feet (9.1 m) high, was built in 1933 and 1934.[^3][^1] The first light on the point was a lamp hung on a pole over a wharf.[^3]

## The place

The point was named Brown's Point by the United States Exploring Expedition in 1841, after James B. Brown, a carpenter's mate on the expedition.[^3] The nomination says the point was known earlier for "house-pits," the dwelling sites of Puget Sound Salish people.[^3]

Isaac and Susan Dofflemyer settled there.[^3] The nomination says they came by ox team from Iowa to Portland, Oregon, in 1850.[^3] They located in Thurston County in 1851 or 1852, and took out a 320 Donation Land Claim at the point.[^3] LighthouseFriends instead dates a claim for 316 acres to 27 September 1865.[^1] The two accounts do not match, and this story does not choose between them.

The light was there to serve the traffic into Olympia.[^3] The nomination says the light "guides shipping traffic through a relatively narrow entrance to Budd Inlet."[^3] The City of Olympia built a mile-long dock to deep water in 1887.[^3] Between 1928 and 1930, LighthouseFriends says, 198 million board feet of lumber were shipped out of Budd Inlet, passing Dofflemyer Point.[^1]

## Building the light

The Light List records the aid to navigation as established in 1887.[^3] The nomination says it was "a light suspended on a pole on a wharf at the point." It was listed at 20 feet (6.1 m) above high water, with a visibility the nomination gives as six miles and 40 candlepower.[^3] A rope and pulley raised and lowered the lamp under a small wooden overhang.[^3] LighthouseFriends gives the date as 13 December 1887, when a lens lantern on a twelve-foot (3.7 m) stake was set up at the point.[^1] The two accounts differ on the structure: the nomination describes a light on a pole on a wharf, and LighthouseFriends describes a lens lantern on a stake.[^1][^3]

The supply boat *The Heather* brought oil, wicks and other stores once a year in the early years.[^3]

The present tower replaced the pole light.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the light was upgraded to this tower in 1934.[^1] The nomination says the tower was built under the Office of Superintendent of Lighthouses in Portland, Oregon, and was designed in 1933 by a designer it gives only as "P.H.P." It names a local contractor, Rufus Kindle, who "actually constructed the facility," and dates its replacement of the pole light to "1934 or 1935."[^3]

## The keepers

Dofflemyer Point was never given a formal keeper.[^1] LighthouseFriends says local residents were contracted to care for the light and activate the fog signal.[^1] The names below come from the nomination and from LighthouseFriends.

The nomination names Leonard Sperring as "the first recorded keeper."[^3] It says Sperring retired in 1912, and that "there is no record of when Mr. Sperring began his work or who may have preceded him."[^3] Edward R. Robinson took over in 1912 and retired in 1942.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he lived beside the tower and calls him a caretaker.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that in 1942 his son Robert, a next-door neighbor, took over the work.[^1] The nomination says Robert E. Robinson was keeper until 1961.[^1][^3]

The nomination says Nellie Vassell and Madeline Campbell followed the Robinsons and "looked after the light and foghorn" until automation in 1976.[^3] LighthouseFriends describes Campbell's work in detail. When fog was expected overnight, she set an alarm to wake herself and check conditions.[^1] When she went on vacation, a local resident had to serve as a "fog horn sitter."[^1] LighthouseFriends says she looked after the tower until the fog signal was automated in 1987.[^1]

## After the keepers

The nomination says the light was automated when the 1934 tower was built, and that the fog horn, which was manually operated, was automated in 1976.[^3] LighthouseFriends dates the light's automation to the 1960s, and says the fog signal still needed manual activation until 1987.[^1] The two accounts differ on both dates.

In 1962 the lighting apparatus was increased to 2,500 candlepower.[^3] In 1972 the glass lamp was replaced with a plastic lens, and the light was changed to direct current.[^3] On 1 May 1995 the light was listed on the Washington Heritage Register and on the National Register of Historic Places, within the group "Maritime-Related Resources of Budd Inlet."[^1][^4] Today Coast Guard personnel aboard a buoy tender maintain the fog signal.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light and has an easement to reach it across private property.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the grounds and tower are closed, and that the lighthouse is surrounded by private property.[^1] A photograph can be taken from the marina.[^1] The nomination calls the light "the only lighthouse-type major aid to navigation below the Narrows on Puget Sound."[^3]
