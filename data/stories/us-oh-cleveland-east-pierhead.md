The Cleveland East Pierhead Light stands at the end of the east breakwater, where the Cuyahoga River meets Lake Erie. LighthouseFriends describes the present white tower with its black lantern room as standing there since 1911, and it is one of the two pierhead lights that mark the river entrance.[^1]

## The place

Cleveland's harbor entrance is guarded by two breakwaters.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the eastern breakwater was begun in 1888. Sailing through the gap between the two in rough seas was, it says, "akin to threading a needle with a shaky hand."[^1]

## Building the light

The eastern breakwater first carried a temporary light. LighthouseFriends dates a temporary mast light there to 8 September 1888.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for 1891 to 1892 lists "a lens lantern on and near the west end of East Breakwater at Cleveland, Ohio," as one of two lights that are "temporary and unlisted."[^3]

The same report describes a boathouse built in front of the beacon on the East Pier, "projecting 4 feet over the pier on the lake face."[^3] The sources do not say how the East Pier beacon, the breakwater's temporary lights and the later pierhead tower relate to one another.

A separate Lighthouse Society record gives 1831 as the establishment year of a "Cleveland East Pierhead Beacon" that no longer exists.[^6] The sources do not connect that beacon to the present tower.

On the completion of the concrete pier, the East Pier light was moved to the outer end of the new pier, "about 121 feet southward of its former position," according to the Board's report for 1896 to 1897.[^5] LighthouseFriends dates a formal tower on the eastern breakwater to 1897, with a five-day lens-lantern first lit on 20 August that year.[^1]

The present tower came with a later appropriation. LighthouseFriends says the Board asked for $12,000 for a pierhead light on the new east breakwater, that Congress approved the funds on 27 May 1908, and that work on preparing the pierheads began in 1909.[^1] The light that followed was a cylindrical iron tower 25 feet (7.6 m) tall, with a fifth-order acetylene flashing light when navigation opened in 1911. It was changed to a fourth-order light in 1912.[^1]

LighthouseFriends also describes a second change on the same breakwater. On 27 June 1911 the light at the west end of the east breakwater was shown from a 25-foot (7.6 m) square steel tower that replaced a taller tower on wooden legs. The sources do not say how that light relates to the pierhead tower.[^1]

## The keepers

The Life-Saving Service's report says that during the 1884 call-out Keeper Charles C. Goodwin hailed "Customs Inspector Bates and Lightkeeper Reed, who were on the pier" and asked them to send out a tug.[^2] LighthouseFriends calls him "Assistant Lighthouse Keeper Reed."[^1] No other keeper of this light is named in the sources read.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the night of 11 November 1884, "during the prevalence of a violent northwesterly gale," the crew of the Cleveland life-saving station saw a vessel scudding before the wind for the harbor. It was the schooner-rigged barge John T. Johnson, which was compelled to anchor off the end of the east pier.[^2] The sea ran so high, the report says, that "at times the light house on the end of the east pier was completely buried in a smother of foam."[^2] The Life-Saving Service's report calls the rescue "one of the most thrilling episodes in the annals of the life-saving station at Cleveland, Ohio."[^2]

The rescue crew first rowed out in the surfboat. When the tugs would not go out, they left a surfman on the barge and rowed back for the breeches buoy. On the return the surfboat capsized, and onlookers on the pier threw the men planks and ropes.[^2] A breeches buoy later carried everyone from the barge to the pier, and LighthouseFriends says the whole life-saving crew received the gold life-saving medal for the night.[^1]

One of the crew, Frederick T. Hatch, later became a lighthouse keeper at Cleveland, according to LighthouseFriends. It does not name the light.[^1]

In a collision on the night of 19 November 1892, the steamer Detroit hit a schooner and was forced against the east pier.[^4] The Board reported that the steamer's guards, striking the foundation sills of the pierhead beacon, injured them and "put the beacon itself in peril." The foundation was repaired.[^4]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the pierhead light was temporarily removed in 2005 so the Army Corps of Engineers could repair the pier.[^1] In May 2007 the Coast Guard, which had deemed the light excess, offered it at no cost to eligible entities, including federal, state and local agencies, non-profit corporations and educational organizations. No qualified owner was found, and an online auction closed on 2 December 2008 with the lighthouse selling for $10,000.[^1]

A 2009 magazine report, which LighthouseFriends cites, named the buyer as Artisan Restoration Group, a New York City-based restoration company. The reported plan was to install a composting toilet and rent the light and its pier to boaters.[^1]

## Today

LighthouseFriends reports that the lighthouse is privately owned and that its grounds and tower are closed.[^1] The sources read give no account after 2009 of the light's condition or the owner's plans.
