Courtenay Bay Breakwater Light stands at the end of a long breakwater on the east side of Saint John Harbour, New Brunswick. It has shown a light since 1927, but the concrete tower it now uses was built in 1944 as part of a wartime gun battery, and it was made to look like a lighthouse.[^1][^2][^4]

## The place

The breakwater runs out to the approach of Courtenay Bay, and the light was placed there to mark that approach. LighthouseFriends says the 1927 light was set up "to show the entrance to Courtenay Bay."[^1]

The breakwater was a public works project.[^5] LighthouseFriends dates the start of the work to the early twentieth century and says a 760 m (2,500 ft) extension was completed in 1919.[^1] The Department of Marine and Fisheries' report for the fiscal year 1913–14 lists, among its important notices to mariners that year, a description of the improvements the Department of Public Works was making in Courtenay Bay: a dredged channel, a breakwater and a dry dock.[^5] In the report for the fiscal year 1923–24, the Department noted that Public Works had placed pile dolphins to mark the limit of the dredging near the main channel.[^6]

## Building the light

In 1927, LighthouseFriends says, an unwatched white light was established at the outer end of the breakwater. A year later it was moved 46 m (150 ft) shoreward and re-erected on the breakwater.[^1] Rowlett's directory says the original light stood on a small wooden tower.[^2] The sources do not say when that tower was built or what lens it carried.

The war changed the site. A defence plan of 1936–37 called for light quick-firing guns on the breakwater, to support the main batteries on Partridge Island and others at Fort Dufferin. The guns were not mounted until October 1939, after war had been declared, when the local military authority, using its own initiative and funds, put a First World War 18-pounder gun on the breakwater and rigged up automotive spotlights.[^4] In 1942, heavy concrete structures went up near the tip to hold a magazine and a gun platform. In June 1944 the 18-pounder was replaced by a semi-automatic twin 6-pounder, and a steel-reinforced concrete fire control post was designed and built to look like a lighthouse.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the guns were meant to fire on torpedo boats that a larger enemy vessel might have sent out, and to cover a small number of landing sites.[^1]

Rowlett's directory describes the present tower as a faux lighthouse built in 1944 as part of that artillery installation. It says the light was added sometime after the war ended in 1945.[^2] LighthouseFriends does not give a date for the move from the wooden tower to the battery tower.[^1]

## After the keepers

The light was unwatched from its first lighting, so it never had keepers to withdraw.[^1] After the war the battery was taken out of service and mostly dismantled in 1946.[^1][^4]

In April 2018 the Canadian Coast Guard began work on aluminum siding and a lantern room for the concrete tower. It also planned an upgrade to the light itself, to increase its brilliance by thirty to forty percent.[^1] Rowlett's directory describes the 2018 work as adding the lantern, the aluminum panels and a more powerful light.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the lantern room came from a decommissioned lighthouse.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light.[^1] Its grounds and tower are closed, and the site is accessible only by boat.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends advises visitors to view it from the water, with distant views also possible from the Saint John waterfront.[^1]

The sources disagree about the light's signal and tower. The current Canadian Coast Guard list gives an isophase red light every two seconds, from a focal height of 13.0 m (43 ft), visible 8 nautical miles, on a white hexagonal tower with a red top.[^3] Rowlett's directory gives a red flash every four seconds, a focal plane of 13.5 m (44 ft), and an octagonal concrete tower.[^2] Neither source explains the difference.
