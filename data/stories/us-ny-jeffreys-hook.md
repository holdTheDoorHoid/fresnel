Jeffrey's Hook Light is the small red cast-iron tower that stands under the George Washington Bridge in Fort Washington Park.[^1] It is the only lighthouse on the island of Manhattan.[^1] The light began in 1889 as a post with lanterns on a point of the Hudson shore.[^1] The tower itself was first built at Sandy Hook, New Jersey, in 1880, and was moved here in 1921.[^1][^2] The light was shown until 1947, the Coast Guard decommissioned it in 1948, and the City of New York has owned the site since 1951.[^1][^2]

## The place

A small point runs out from the Manhattan shore into the Hudson below Washington Heights.[^1] The designation report says the name appears to date from the Dutch colonial era, and that "hook" comes from the Dutch word *hoek*, meaning an angle and also a small spit of land.[^1] In 1776, in conjunction with the construction of Fort Washington, a battery with a single gun was built at the tip of the point.[^1]

In 1895 the Light-House Board described the point: "The point extends well out into the river, with deep water close to its outer end." The usual route of steamers, it added, "is close to the point."[^3] The city took in the point as part of Fort Washington Park, which was established in 1896.[^1]

## Building the light

The first light was a post in the river.[^1] The designation report describes "a wood post from which hung two oil lanterns," showing "fixed red lights of ten candlepower."[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's guide dates the post light's establishment to 1 November 1889.[^2]

The Light-House Board wanted more. Its 1895 report asked for "A larger light and a fog bell here," and estimated $3,000 for a site on land.[^3] The designation report says the Board made several proposals through 1907, "although they were never funded."[^1] After the Board obtained permission from the city's parks department to use a site, its estimate fell to "just $1,400." In 1905 it raised the figure to $3,400 to include a keeper's dwelling.[^2] The 1900 report says permission had been obtained "to occupy the necessary site for a fog-signal and a more powerful light."[^4] The U.S. Lighthouse Society record lists no keeper's quarters on the point.[^8]

The work came in 1921. In 1913 the Commissioner of Lighthouses proposed "Hudson River Aids to Navigation" as a high-priority special project, and the project was funded in 1916.[^1] The designation report says that in 1918 the Bureau of Lighthouses applied to the parks department for a permit, and that the 1918 agreement required the removal of the lighthouse and restoration of the site once it was no longer in use.[^1] The tower that came was a cast-iron one that had stood at Sandy Hook as the North Hook Beacon.[^1] The designation report says the Sandy Hook light was improved in 1880 with a cast-iron tower, and that the tower was dismantled in 1917 and replaced with a skeleton tower, after the beacon was found to be in the direct firing line of Fort Hancock's new gun battery.[^1]

The parts were reconditioned at the Tompkinsville depot on Staten Island before the tower went up on the point.[^1] On 10 October 1921 the light at Jeffrey's Hook was changed to "a one-hundred candlepower, fifth-order acetylene red lamp, flashing every three seconds," with its focal plane "sixty-one feet above the water" (about 18.6 m).[^1] The fog signal followed two weeks later. It was a bell that sounded "one stroke every fifteen seconds."[^1]

Other sources describe the 1921 light differently. The 1921 Bulletin's list of lights whose illuminant was changed gives the Jeffreys Hook entry as "fixed red to flashing white."[^6] The Society's guide describes a light that flashed white.[^2] The same Bulletin's list of fog signals says the bell was "operated by machinery."[^6] Earlier in the same volume it reported that "Work on the last aid in the system, Jeffrys Hook, is now in progress."[^5]

## The keepers

The 1921 light "was tended by a part-time keeper."[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster names William Knapp as principal keeper, "at least 1926-".[^8] The designation report gives a different name for the late 1920s. Its note 16 says that "In the late 1920s this position was held by Bill Collins, a building superintendent in Washington Heights," and that he made two trips a day down through Fort Washington Park to check that all was in order.[^1] The report takes this from a newspaper research file. The sources do not say how the two accounts fit together.

The roster also lists a lamplighter, John P. Gundelsheimer, "at least 1939-".[^8] The designation report notes that the Coast Guard had taken over responsibility for navigational aids in 1939.[^1] Two laborers appear on the roster as well, Thomas J. Roache ("at least 1909-") and Henry J. Whitely ("1911-").[^8] Both spans begin before the 1921 tower.[^8] The roster gives only the heading "Laborer" for them, with no duties.

## After the keepers

On 21 September 1927 ground was broken for the bridge.[^7] The Bulletin reported that "One of the piers of this bridge will occupy the site of Jeffreys Hook Light and Fog Signal."[^7] The George Washington Bridge (built 1927 to 1931) went up "almost directly above the lighthouse," and the designation report says the light's prominence as an aid to navigation was "diminished" by it. An aeronautical beacon was placed on the bridge in 1935.[^1] The light "remained in use, with signals unchanged, through 1947."[^1] The Society's guide says the Coast Guard decommissioned the lighthouse in 1948 and put out its lamp.[^2]

The Coast Guard then planned to auction the tower, according to the Society's guide.[^2] In July 1951 the lighthouse was offered for sale as excess property.[^1] The designation report says the 1942 children's book *The Little Red Lighthouse and the Great Gray Bridge*, by Hildegarde H. Swift with illustrations by Lynd Ward, had made the light widely known, and that the threatened loss prompted a public outpouring of affection and support from its readers and their allies.[^1] The report adds that "A four-year-old boy offered to buy the lighthouse himself," and that editorials in the *New York Times* and the *New York Herald Tribune* decried the loss.[^1] It says Parks Commissioner Robert Moses asked that the lighthouse be given to the city, and that on 23 July 1951 "it was transferred to the jurisdiction of the Department of Parks of the City of New York."[^1][^2]

The light was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1979, "as part of a thematic nomination of Hudson River lighthouses."[^1] The city's parks department carried out a restoration from 1982 to 1986, which included a new concrete cap for the foundation and newly made doors.[^1] The Society's guide puts a $209,000 renovation in 1986, when the concrete foundation was rebuilt and new steel doors were installed.[^2] The city designated it a landmark in 1991 (Designation List 237, LP-1654).[^1] The guide says the lighthouse got a fresh coat of red paint in 2000 and was reactivated on 19 September 2002.[^2]

## Today

The Society's guide gives the owner as the city's Parks Department.[^2] On 29 September 2026 the Historic House Trust of New York City announced a restoration that begins with an initial $13 million from the City of New York, and said that design work was under way.[^9] The Trust says Urban Park Rangers lead tours through the year, and that visitors to the annual festival can climb to the lantern room.[^9]

The sources disagree about access. The Society's place record says the light is not open to the public.[^8] The Society's teaching guide says the interior opens on select summer days.[^2] The 2026 release describes festival visitors climbing to the lantern room.[^9] Check current hours with the parks department before a visit; the restoration design is still in progress.
