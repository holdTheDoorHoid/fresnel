Caribou Island Light stands on a small island at the eastern end of Lake Superior, about 64 km (forty miles) from the Canadian mainland, and is best seen from a boat or from the air.[^2] Its white hexagonal concrete tower, with six flying buttresses and a red lantern, was built in 1911 and first showed its light in 1912.[^1][^2][^6] A wooden tower of 1886 stood on the site before it.[^2]

## The place

Caribou Island is about 4.8 km (three miles) long and 2.4 km (a mile and a half) wide, and dangerous reefs extend from its perimeter.[^2] Lighthouse Friends calls it the most remote island in Lake Superior.[^2]

The light was not put on the island itself. In 1884 Parliament provided money for a lighthouse and fog trumpet to mark Caribou Island, and a contract for $6,475 went that year to John George and David Currie of Port Elgin.[^2] Rather than build on the island, the authorities chose a five-acre (2 ha) islet 1.6 km (a mile) west of its southern tip, which later came to be known as Lighthouse Island.[^2]

## Building the light

The light went into operation on 26 August 1886. The Department's description, as Lighthouse Friends prints it, gives an octagonal wooden tower 24 m (80 ft) from base to vane, painted white with a red lantern. A square wooden fog-alarm building stood 30 m (100 ft) south of it, and its compressed-air horn sounded a five-second blast every thirty seconds. The total cost of the construction, including two foghorn machines, was $11,635.02.[^2]

In 1910 a five-inch duplicate diaphone plant was installed in the fog-alarm building.[^2] The Department's report for that year lists among its illustrations "Caribou Island, Lake Superior, reinforced concrete lighthouse under construction."[^3] The next year's list of works gives "Erection of an 80-foot reinforced concrete lighthouse tower" for Caribou island, and the report's inspection narrative says that "The new tower on Caribou island was also inspected."[^4][^5]

William P. Anderson, chief engineer of the Department of Marine, championed reinforced concrete and designed nine such towers with flying buttresses. Six still stand, and Caribou Island's is among the tallest, Lighthouse Friends says.[^2] The Canadian Register of Historic Places describes the buttresses as designed to give stability "with a minimum of material", and calls the tower's form "characteristic of the last three flying buttress lighthouses built by the Department of Marine."[^1]

The lens was a second-order Fresnel lens made by Chance Brothers of England. It turned on a weight that dropped down the tower's centre on a cable, and the keeper had to crank the weight up every six hours. The turntable floated in mercury.[^2]

The 1912 light changed the pattern. Lighthouse Friends describes it as a second-order dioptric light showing a group of three flashes every ten seconds, replacing the old revolving white light.[^2] The Coast Guard's list today gives a single flash every ten seconds, with a focal height of 36.9 m and a nominal range of 9 nautical miles.[^6] The sources do not agree on the pattern, and this story does not settle it. They also disagree on height: Lighthouse Friends gives the 1912 tower as about 32 m (104 ft) from base to vane, while the Coast Guard list gives the structure as 35.1 m.[^2][^6]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends names R. May as the first keeper, who served only a few months.[^2] Charles James Pim was appointed in April 1887. He was born in 1854, a son of early settlers of Sault Ste. Marie, and he stayed until 1898. He then became clerk for Sault Ste. Marie, a position he held until his death in 1914.[^2]

The Department's list of lightkeepers records the appointment of Wilbrod Demers on 10 May 1899, at "Caribou Island, Lake Superior".[^7] Lighthouse Friends, which spells him Wilbrod O. Demers, has him at the station until 1906.[^2]

Antoine Boucher followed from 1907 to 1912, then George W. Johnston from 1912 to 1921 and J. George Penfold from 1921 to 1922. John W. Kennedy served from 1922 to 1928 and Charles N. McDonald from 1928 to 1935, as Lighthouse Friends lists them.[^2] Arthur W. Hurley kept the light from 1935 to 1962; Lighthouse Friends says he "served as keeper for nearly thirty years."[^2] Alfred Thibeault served from 1962 to 1964 and George Rutherford from 1964. Reg Dawson was keeper in 1968.[^2]

In 1915 the government told its keepers it would no longer carry them to and from the lights. Each station was to be issued a "twenty-eight-foot sailboat" instead, and Johnston fitted his with a kerosene engine, a small cabin and a coal heater.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In October 1904 the steamers Midland Queen and Ottawa collided off Caribou island during a dense fog, according to the Department's casualty list, which dates the collision 14 October 1904.[^8] A Superior, Wisconsin, paper had reported it on 8 October. Its account speaks of "a heavy fog on Lake Superior" and says the Ottawa "was slightly damaged."[^9] The newspaper printed the story six days before the Department's date, so either the date or the account is wrong, or there were two collisions. The sources do not show which.

In December 1904 there was much anxiety at Sault Ste. Marie for Demers and his helper, Fred Pelletier. Lighthouse Friends says the tug Reid had called at the island at the end of the shipping season to take the men off, but could not land in heavy seas, and that a payment to a wrecking company later appeared in the Auditor General's report.[^2] A Sault Ste. Marie dispatch in the Minneapolis Journal of 27 December reported that the tugs Reid and Boynton "have returned from Caribou island" with the two men, and that "They had not suffered from exposure."[^10]

After the season closed on 15 December 1919, Johnston and his assistant waited ten days before conditions let them sail for the mainland. Poor weather drove them to Quebec Harbour on Michipicoten Island, and they were "trapped in ice in Michipicoten Bay" before they reached Michipicoten Harbour on New Year's Day.[^2]

Lighthouse Friends says that after losing at least one keeper, Ottawa in 1921 assigned the C.G.S. Lambton to carry keepers to and from the lights.[^2] The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1923 records that the ship "Left Sault Ste. Marie on April 18 with all lightkeepers for the east end of lake Superior on board, and was lost with all hands off Caribou island in a severe storm on April 19."[^11] The report gives those dates without a year, but its fiscal year ran from July 1922 to June 1923, which puts them in April 1923. Lighthouse Friends dates the loss to 1922, and says the ship went down "taking with it George Penfold, keeper of Caribou Island, and his assistant."[^2] The sources disagree on the year, and this story does not settle it.

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1923 volume records officers of the Lighthouse Service crossing from Passage Island to Whitefish Point on the night of 7 August, who saw the Caribou Island (Canadian) light at 67 statute miles. The Bulletin adds that "The weather on both of these nights was clear and cold."[^12]

## After the keepers

In 1944 a radiobeacon was set up at Caribou Island, synchronised with the foghorn, and a flashing electric winter light was placed on the gallery.[^2] Five radar reflectors went up in 1948 and served until 1955.[^2] In the early 1960s diesel generators were installed, an electric motor turned the lens, and a 400-watt mercury lamp became the light source.[^2]

The Register records the tower's recognition by the Government of Canada on 30 September 1991, as a Classified Federal Heritage Building, and describes its "fully automated red lantern."[^1]

In 1992 the light station was solarized, the lens was removed from service, and the lens now stands on display at the Canadian Coast Guard base in Parry Sound.[^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the station. Lighthouse Friends reports that the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^2] The Coast Guard list marks the light as seasonal, on a small island southwest of Caribou Island.[^6] Heritage Guide's copy of the Register notes that being listed does not make a place open to visitors, and the designation covers only the footprint of the building.[^1]
