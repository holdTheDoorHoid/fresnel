Pointe-au-Père, which English-speaking mariners knew as Father Point, is the place on the south shore of the St. Lawrence where ocean-going ships exchanged their river pilots.[^3] The concrete tower with a red lantern that stands there now, held up by eight buttresses, is the third lighthouse on the site, and the station around it has also served as a fog-signal testing ground, a wireless station, a tidal-gauge station and a pilot station.[^1][^4][^5]

## The place

The point takes its name from a Jesuit, Father Henri Nouvel, who arrived in Canada in 1662 and said the first Mass on this stretch of the south shore on 8 December 1663.[^2] When the first lighthouse went up in 1859, the Montreal Ocean Steamship Company already kept a telegraph and pilot station on the point.[^1] The pilots themselves officially set up quarters at Pointe-au-Père in 1905, after a wharf built between 1902 and 1905 allowed the government to move the pilot station over from Île du Bic, answering shipowners' persistent requests. Pilots worked from here until the station was transferred to Les Escoumins in 1959.[^3]

## Building the light

The first lighthouse was a wooden octagonal tower built in 1859 at that steamship company station. Lighthouse Friends gives 1 September 1859 as the day it went into operation, with a fixed red light, and a cost, including a signal gun for fog, of $1,453.61.[^1] A fire destroyed it on 13 April 1867, and it was replaced by a square three-storey house carrying a lantern, which showed a fixed white light.[^1]

The present tower dates from 1909. The Department of Marine and Fisheries' annual report describes it as a reinforced-concrete structure, octagonal in plan, "reinforced by eight flying buttresses" and topped by a circular metal lantern, with the light 27.7 m (91 ft) above high water.[^7] The same report gives the tower's height as 29.6 m (97 ft) from base to the vane on the lantern, while Lighthouse Friends gives 33 m (108 ft); the sources differ and the difference is not explained.[^7][^1] The Department's report for the same year names the engineer H. de Miffonis as building concrete towers at Father Point, Cap Anguille in Newfoundland and Estevan Point in British Columbia, and Lighthouse Friends credits the buttressed design to Henri de Miffonis.[^8][^1] The federal heritage statement instead credits the design to the Department's chief engineer, Lieutenant-Colonel William P. Anderson, and calls the tower the third on the site and the most significant element of a modernization begun in 1903 at 14 lightstations along the St. Lawrence.[^5] Lighthouse Friends reports that the lantern holds a third-order Fresnel lens by Barbier, Bénard and Turenne, and that the light was electrified in 1940.[^1]

Father Point also became a place where fog signals were tried out. Parks Canada says the first was a cannon the keeper loaded and fired every half hour. Explosive bomb signals followed (1894 to 1903), then a Scotch siren in 1903, a diaphone in 1904, and in 1972 an electronic signal that served until the station's navigational aid centre closed in 1997.[^4] The Department's reports confirm the middle steps: in the spring of 1903 the explosive signal was "replaced by a first class siren, operated by compressed air," and the next report says the siren "was changed to a diaphone operated bv compressed air."[^9][^10] On 28 July 1902 the illuminant in the lighthouse was changed from petroleum to acetylene, a change the Department expected to make the light whiter and more powerful.[^11] The wooden fog-alarm building is, according to the heritage statement, the oldest building on the site.[^6]

A semicircular rail behind the fog shed let the horn be turned to suit the wind.[^4]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists the keepers as David Lawson (1859 to 1876), John McWilliams (1876 to 1911, shown there in two stints), John Cahill (1912 to 1920), Thomas Matthew Wyatt (1920 to 1936), Charles Augustus Lavoie (1936 to 1964), Roger St. Pierre Lavoie (1964 to 1972) and Armand Lafrance from 1972.[^1] McWilliams took the post in 1876 on the death of Lawson, and both men also worked as telegraph operators.[^1] Parks Canada notes that in the early twentieth century the station's crew grew to include the keeper, an assistant, a fog-alarm engineer and pilots.[^3] The keeper also took the tide measurements: a Tidal and Current Survey station was set up here in 1894, and the Hydrographic Service carried on until 1985.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1906 a Marconi wireless station opened at Pointe-au-Père, which Parks Canada says replaced flag signals for the keeper.[^3] In the early hours of 29 May 1914 it received the distress calls of the liner *Empress of Ireland*, which sank in 14 minutes off Sainte-Luce after a collision with the *Storstad* in fog; 1,012 people died.[^3][^1] The Department's annual report published in 1916 puts the sinking four miles below Father Point, and elsewhere gives the loss of life as 1,042, not the 1,012 given by Parks Canada and Lighthouse Friends.[^12][^3][^1] Lighthouse Friends adds that the station staff alerted rescuers in the area.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1975 the official light was moved to a 35 m (115 ft) skeleton tower just northeast of the 1909 tower, which was then deactivated; the 1867 tower, which had served as a residence and pilot office, was demolished in 1966.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates the end of resident lightkeeping to 1988 and says the skeleton-tower light was decommissioned a decade later.[^1] Sources differ on designation: Lighthouse Friends says the site was declared a National Historic Site in 1974, the federal heritage statement says 1976.[^1][^5] Parks Canada took ownership in 1977, and the tower was restored in 1980.[^1][^5]

## Today

Parks Canada runs the site as the Pointe-au-Père Lighthouse National Historic Site, at 1034 rue du Phare. Visitors can climb the tower in season, and the keeper's house holds an exhibit; the lighthouse, both keepers' houses and the foghorn building are closed for the season at the time of writing.[^13] Lighthouse Friends says an *Empress of Ireland* pavilion opened near the lighthouse in 2000.[^1]
