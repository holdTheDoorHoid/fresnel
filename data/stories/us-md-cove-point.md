Cove Point Light is a white brick tower on a low, sandy point on the western shore of the Chesapeake Bay, in Calvert County, Maryland, four miles north of the mouth of the Patuxent River.[^1] First lit in December 1828, the Maryland Historical Trust reports it as the oldest extant lighthouse in southern Maryland, and the county's museum calls it the oldest continuously operating light on the bay.[^2][^3]

## The place

Mariners had asked for a light to help them into the Patuxent. In 1825 Congress voted $6,000 for one at Cedar Point, about six miles south of Cove Point; by 1828 a light on Cove Point was judged more useful, and a new appropriation of $5,685 was made for it instead.[^4][^1] Robert Mills's *American Pharos* of 1832, as the Calvert Marine Museum quotes it, said the light was meant "to lead vessels clear of the long low point on which it stands," with seven fathoms of water close by, and to guide vessels clear of Cedar Point and into the Patuxent.[^3]

The government bought land from Dorcas Gray Bourne on 12 June 1828 for $300: one parcel of two acres and seventy-two perches for the lighthouse, and another of two acres for a garden, which was sold in 1926.[^1] The National Register nomination of 1972 gives the nominated property as four acres.[^5]

## Building the light

The contract went to John Donaho (also spelled Donahoo) of Havre de Grace, who had built twelve of the first seventeen lighthouses in Maryland.[^1] The specification called for a tower forty feet high and a one-and-a-half-storey brick dwelling twenty by thirty-four feet, with a kitchen wing and a cistern fed from the roof.[^1] Later sources give the tower's height differently: LighthouseFriends says thirty-six feet, while the National Register nomination and the Maryland Historical Trust's survey say 51 feet (15.5 m) above the ground, conical, of brick under white stucco, with walls more than thirty inches thick at the base.[^4][^5][^2]

A separate 1828 contract, with James Geddes of Baltimore, supplied the lighting apparatus for $550: thirteen lamps, each backed by a sixteen-inch parabolic reflector.[^1] The Maryland Historical Trust's narrative instead says the light first used eleven lamps with eleven 18-inch reflectors.[^2] Eleven men applied to be keeper. James Somerville was chosen at $350 a year, and the light entered service in December 1828.[^1]

A fifth-order Fresnel lens replaced the reflectors in 1855, and a larger fourth-order lens followed in 1857.[^1] In 1928 the light was electrified, with a 150-watt bulb in place of the kerosene lamp, and the 1857 lens gave way to the present six-panel fourth-order lens, made by Barbier and Benard of France in 1897 (the Maryland Historical Trust's narrative dates the fourth-order lens to 1897 and the full electrification to 1907);[^2] the character became a flash every ten seconds, and candle power rose from 2,900 to 35,000.[^1] The clockwork that turns the lens was not electrified at the same time. It hung from a wire rope on thirty pounds of weight in a wooden shaft and had to be wound every seven hours, and Dodds notes that both the clockwork and the shaft are still in place.[^1]

The fog signal came later than the light. Money for one was appropriated in 1837; by 1858 a new bell and striking machinery were in a wooden frame, and the bell tower had to be replaced in 1868, 1881 and 1887, according to Richard Dodds of the Calvert Marine Museum.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for fiscal 1892 records that the foundation of the fog-bell tower, whose piers the sea had already reached, was moved back 16 feet.[^6] A wooden building of 1901 held an oil engine and air compressor for a Daboll trumpet, and the old bell was hung on its roof as a backup, where it still hangs.[^1] The Maryland Historical Trust dates the building to 1901; the US Lighthouse Society archive gives 1904.[^2][^7]

## The keepers

The roster of principal keepers begins with Somerville (1828 to 1834) and Walter Wyville (1834 to 1849).[^7] Dodds cites a letter from "keeper Wyville" to the Lighthouse Board in 1831, reporting that the bay was beginning to encroach on the lighthouse; the archive's roster, and Dodds's own list of keepers, give Wyville's term from 1834, so the dates do not line up.[^1][^7] Sarah Thomas was principal keeper from 1857 to 1859, following George Thomas (1855 to 1857).[^7] An assistant keeper was first appointed in 1864, thirty-six years after the station opened; the archive lists John H. Hagelin as first assistant from 1864 to 1866, and Mary Hagelin in the same post from 1866 to 1867, while Charles M. Hagelin was principal keeper from 1862 to 1868.[^1][^7] The sources do not say how these three were related.

Benjamin N. Gray was first assistant in 1882 and 1883 and principal keeper from 1890 to 1907; Clinton B. Gray, first assistant in 1903 and 1904, was principal keeper from 1917 to 1931, and Paul B. Gray appears as assistant in 1905 and from 1922 to 1925.[^7] Dodds singles out the Gray family as having a close association with the station.[^1] Richard S. Daniels, principal keeper from 1907, died at the station on 15 March 1917 at seventy-two, while talking with the captain of the lighthouse tender *Maple*; LighthouseFriends adds that he was an uncle of Josephus Daniels, the Secretary of the Navy.[^4][^7]

The station was good duty, Dodds writes, because keepers lived there with their families, unlike the keepers of offshore lights, but the grounds and buildings added work.[^1] James T. Somers, the last civilian keeper (1943 to 1958 in the archive's roster), told the *Baltimore Sun* in 1953 that he preferred lights surrounded by water, where there was only the living quarters and the light to keep, since at Cove Point he also had to look after eleven acres of ground.[^1][^7] Dodds says the surviving station logbooks show a day governed by strict routine and endless upkeep, with provisions from the lighthouse tender and from the general stores at Solomons.[^1] During the Second World War a Coast Guardsman was added as a third keeper; with three men, each worked eight hours on and eight off for forty-eight hours, then had twenty-four hours off.[^1]

In the early twentieth century Cove Point was also one of three vessel-reporting stations on the Chesapeake. In 1899 the Baltimore Chamber of Commerce successfully petitioned the Lighthouse Board for a telephone there, and keepers noted the names of inbound and outbound vessels by day and passed them to the Baltimore Maritime Exchange; a large spyglass on a tripod, often seen on the keeper's porch in photographs, was supplied for the purpose.[^1]

The keeper's dwelling grew with the staff. It stayed as built until 1883, when the roof was taken off, the walls raised and a new tin roof laid, making it a two-storey house; in 1925 it was rebuilt to two and a half storeys with a large dormer, and became a duplex, one half for each family.[^1] Indoor plumbing arrived in 1932.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends calls shoreline erosion a persistent problem at the station.[^4] Dodds finds the earliest mention of it in the 1831 letter noted above, and in 1841 Winslow Lewis contracted to build a raised wooden breakwater, fifty-five by ninety feet, all round the keeper's house and tower; two years later he added seven groins, and four large groins replaced them in 1850.[^1] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 describes a "substantial wooden shore protection" built in November "to arrest the further advance of the sea upon the light-house site": four-inch sheet piling twelve feet long, backed by walings bolted to piles, on a line 340 feet long.[^6] Because that report covers July 1891 to June 1892, the work was done in November 1891; Dodds and LighthouseFriends date the bulkhead to 1892.[^6][^1][^4] A concrete wall with riprap jetties in timber cribs was finished late in 1912 at a cost of $3,900, and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1914 reported that "The results have been satisfactory." The concrete seawall was rebuilt in 1993.[^8][^1]

On 31 August 1886 the keeper noted a slight shock and tremor lasting a second and a half, the effect of the Charleston earthquake.[^1] On 18 June 1908 the assistant keeper, Grover C. Riggs, saved a man who had lost his oars in a small boat after trying to swim ashore drunk.[^1] That December the *Calvert Journal* reported the schooner *Quick Time* sunk off Cove Point; Captain Dorman and his crew, it said, "suffered a great deal from the exposure and narrowly escaped being drowned," and were "hospitably cared for by Mr. Daniels, the lighthouse keeper at Cove Point," as Dodds quotes the paper.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported in 1928 that on 1 July a laborer-leader from the Lazaretto depot rescued the wife and daughter of the station's assistant keeper, who were in danger of drowning near the light station.[^9] The Bulletin for January 1931 commended assistant keeper Herman F. Metivier for helping the captain and crew of the launch *Panama*, which was in danger of sinking in a heavy sea on 30 November.[^10]

The great storm of August 1933 struck from the south with little warning. The Lighthouse Service called it an Atlantic coast storm of 22 August, of hurricane force, and reported the Cove Point radiobeacon put out of commission by water; Dodds gives the date as 23 August.[^11][^1] According to the station logbook, as Dodds reports it, two windows were knocked out of the fog signal building, the electric plant was put out of commission, the keeper's basement was flooded to the windows, four shade trees were uprooted, and the grounds were covered by two and a half feet of water and sand.[^1] Dodds adds that the base of the tower was flooded but the light kept operating, even at the height of the storm.[^1] Horace C. Groom's son, also Horace, remembered in 1997 a thirty-two-foot boat belonging to his brother, kept at the station, carried into the trees, and the assistant keeper's goats and chickens surviving.[^1] Esther Metivier, daughter of the assistant keeper Herman Metivier, recalled, in an account LighthouseFriends prints, that the water seemed to put the house in the middle of the bay, and that her father, tying a rowboat to the front porch, chose to ride out the storm.[^4]

About ten years later the beaches just south of the lighthouse were used for large practice landings, in preparation for amphibious assaults in the European and Pacific theaters of the war.[^1]

## After the keepers

Somers retired in 1958, and Coast Guard personnel took over the station until it was automated.[^1][^7] The radio beacon, established on 30 June 1928, was disestablished on 31 December 1984.[^1] The Coast Guard received orders to automate in March 1986, and the light was officially automated on 16 August 1986, with an automatic lamp changer in the lantern and a computer to monitor it; it is now controlled from Baltimore.[^3] The station was added to the National Register of Historic Places on 11 April 1973.[^5][^2]

Ownership passed from the Coast Guard to Calvert County in September 2000, according to the Calvert Marine Museum; LighthouseFriends says October.[^3][^4] The museum began offering tours in 2002.[^3] The museum society later raised $600,000 to renovate the keeper's house as two vacation rentals, with work running from June 2011 to a year later.[^4]

## Today

The tower is still an active aid to navigation; the land belongs to the Board of Commissioners of Calvert County and the Calvert Marine Museum manages it.[^2][^3] The grounds and visitor center are open to the public on Wednesdays, Saturdays and Sundays from 1 p.m. to 4 p.m., May through September, weather and conditions permitting; the museum describes visitors exploring the base of the tower, and does not say the tower can be climbed; LighthouseFriends lists the tower as closed.[^3][^4] The keeper's house is rented as a vacation home.[^3] The Maryland Historical Trust notes that Calvert County has identified the property as one at risk from flooding and erosion.[^2]
