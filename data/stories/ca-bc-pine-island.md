Pine Island Lighthouse stands on a small island at the entrance to Queen Charlotte Strait, where ships leave the shelter of the strait for the open water of Queen Charlotte Sound.[^5][^6] The Canadian Coast Guard station has shown a light since 1907. Keepers and their families lived on the island from the first years, and it is still a staffed station.[^5][^8]

## The place

Ships heading north along the Inside Passage near Port Hardy must leave Queen Charlotte Strait for the sound, and Pine Island lies at that transition. It is often buffeted by waves from the open ocean.[^5] An encyclopedia of BC place names describes the station as one of the province's most isolated and exposed lighthouse stations.[^9] The island can be reached only by boat.[^6]

## Building the light

Work began in 1906, and continued into 1908.[^5] The department's annual report for the year ended 30 June 1906 says: "A new lighthouse and fog alarm are being erected."[^1]

The report for the nine months to 31 March 1907 describes the finished station. "The lighthouse stands 100 feet back from the extremity of the west point of the island and consists of a square wooden tower," 13 m (43 ft) from base to lantern ventilator, set at the corner of a square wooden dwelling and topped by an iron lantern. "The light is fixed white dioptric of the fifth order, and is visible fourteen miles over an arch of 232 degrees." The fog alarm was to be a diaphone, sounding one blast of seven seconds every two minutes. The work was done by day labour under the foremanship of Thomas Blair, at a total cost of $21,071.29.[^2]

The diaphone was installed in the year to 31 March 1908, when the report notes that "the diaphone plant and machinery purchased last year from the Canadian Fog Signal Company, of Toronto, was installed."[^3]

The original wooden tower was not the last. LighthouseFriends describes the tower standing in 1967 as a square, pyramidal, skeletal structure that had replaced the original years earlier, but it does not date the change.[^5] The Lighthouse Directory says the 1907 tower was replaced by a 10.5 m (35 ft) square steel tower with a lantern and skeletal bracing, which was repaired after the 1967 storm.[^6] In 2001 a cylindrical white tower replaced the one that had survived the wave.[^5][^6] The Coast Guard's list describes the present tower as square.[^7] The sources do not agree on its shape.

## The keepers

Arthur Broughton Gurney was the first keeper, appointed on the recommendation of William Sloan, his Member of Parliament, and in post from 1907 to 1912.[^5] Within months he was asking for more money. He wrote to Sloan: "I find it is quite out of the question for me to live on $700 per annum." When no raise came, he wrote directly to the minister in Ottawa, which angered the Marine Agent in Victoria.[^5] In 1912 he expected a transfer to Sheringham Point, but that keepership went to someone else. The family was offered Ballenas Island instead.[^5]

Donald McPhee, a bachelor, replaced the Gurneys and advertised in Vancouver for a housekeeper. A widow answered, with her two teenagers, Violet and Percival Pike. McPhee married her, and in 1919 the couple moved to Estevan Point.[^5] Percival Pike, with his new wife Mary, took over the station and stayed until 1933.[^5]

LighthouseFriends records that Mary Pike was taken ill on the station in February 1920 and gave birth there that night. She and the infant were taken to hospital at Alert Bay.[^5] Later a second baby died of pneumonia, which LighthouseFriends says was caught on the boat ride out to the island.[^5] LighthouseFriends also says Mary was known to row twenty-two miles to Port Hardy when the sea was calm, and that a steamship master who passed often said she "would run that light."[^5]

Rex Pendril Brown was keeper from 1957 to 1967.[^5] In a Christmas Eve 1966 article, which LighthouseFriends prints, Brown explained that he would spend the holiday at the station with his wife and two children.[^5] LighthouseFriends notes that in winter no visitors could come to the station, because weather made landings risky if not impossible.[^5] The station's keeper list runs through Meredith Dickman, who held the post from 2020 to at least 2024.[^5] The Senate committee's appendix table, drawn from Coast Guard data of April 2010, lists Pine Island as remote and not automated, with two lightkeeping positions and permanent residents.[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 24 May 1907 the steamer *Thistle*, the yacht of the Lieutenant Governor, was on a cruise when her port bunkers burst into flame. She burned, then sank seven miles northwest of Pine Island lighthouse. The department's casualty list records that "All passengers and crew were saved by the lifeboats." It does not mention the light or its keepers.[^4]

One evening in February 1967, with gale-force winds blowing, Keeper Brown and his wife went to bed. Around 11:30 p.m. they heard chains clanking against the station's fuel tanks and went out with the assistant keeper, J. P. Lewis. The sea had already broken a window in the engine room. Shortly afterwards, LighthouseFriends says, "a fifty-foot wave crashed onto the island's rocky shore." It destroyed the fog signal building, pushed the engine house up to the Browns' front step, washed away two fuel tanks of 2,000 gallons each, and dislodged the radiobeacon antenna. LighthouseFriends says the tower "was only slightly damaged." The families fled into the woods and spent the night by a fire. A helicopter came the next day, and the tower was relit the following night with an incandescent oil vapour lamp.[^5] A Victoria paper headlined the story "Monstrous Wave Spared Home Only," as LighthouseFriends cites it.[^10]

The accounts disagree on how much survived. The Lighthouse Directory says the station "was heavily damaged" in that storm and the tower was repaired.[^6] The encyclopedia preview says the station "was completely destroyed".[^9] LighthouseFriends gives the fullest account, and in it the tower was only slightly damaged. The other two sources do not say what was lost, so the extent of the loss is not settled here.

## Today

Pine Island Light is an active Coast Guard light. The Coast Guard's list gives its characteristic as a red flash every ten seconds, from 24.6 m (81 ft) above the water, with a nominal range of 16 nautical miles.[^7] It is a staffed station where keepers live, and LighthouseFriends lists keepers to at least 2024.[^5][^8] The lantern, lens and fog horns of the earlier light are on display at the Campbell River Maritime Heritage Centre, on a replica lighthouse.[^5][^6] LighthouseFriends notes that pilots now board arriving vessels at the island.[^5] The island is reached only by boat.[^6]

LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower closed, and that the light should be visible from the ferries that serve the Discovery Coast and Inside Passage from Port Hardy.[^5] The Lighthouse Directory says the site and the tower are both closed.[^6] The station is a working Coast Guard post where keepers live, so visitors should not land there without an invitation from the Coast Guard.[^5][^6]
