Boon Island is a bare granite ledge about six and a half miles off York, Maine, rising only some fourteen feet (4 m) above the sea at its highest point, and on it stands the tallest lighthouse in Maine.[^1][^2] Keepers lived on it from 1811, and an early note sent to the Treasury about the post remarked that all the fresh water, wood and necessaries for a family had to be carried out to the island.[^1]

## The place

Where the island got its name is not known.[^2] Some writers link it to wrecks, but Lighthousefriends points out that the name appears in records as early as 1630, before either of the two best-known ones.[^1] One is the *Increase*, wrecked in April 1682 according to the Historic American Buildings Survey (the York museum says summer), whose four survivors, the museum says, lit a fire that brought rescue.[^2][^3] The other is the British ship *Nottingham Galley*, lost on 11 December 1710, whose survivors lived on the ledge for more than three weeks.[^2] More than a century passed before a lighthouse was built. In 1797 General Benjamin Lincoln, the local lighthouse superintendent, met the Boston Marine Society about an unlighted beacon for fishermen and coasters; a wooden tower was finished in 1799 and, according to the York museum's account, destroyed by a great storm in 1804, then replaced by a stone day beacon in the summer of 1805.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress authorised a lighthouse on 2 March 1811, on condition that Massachusetts give up the island, which it had already ceded on 26 February.[^3] The appropriation was $3,000, of which $2,590.69 was spent in 1812 and $409.31 in 1813.[^3] The sources do not agree on when the first light shone. The Historic American Buildings Survey calls the site a light station "since 1812"; the US Lighthouse Society's keeper roster begins in 1811; the National Register nomination says the station was established during the War of 1812; and the York museum says the tower was finished by the winter of 1811 and showed a fixed light 32 feet (10 m) above the water.[^3][^4][^5][^2]

The first tower did not last in its original form. Congress voted $4,000 on 3 March 1831 "for increasing the height" of the light, and $3,406.65 was spent that year.[^3] Lighthousefriends says a new 49-foot (15 m) granite tower was built by Colonel Seward Merrill for that sum, while the National Register nomination says the original tower was destroyed in 1831; the sources do not settle whether it was raised or replaced.[^1][^5] An 1843 report, as Lighthousefriends quotes it, found the new tower "laid up in bad lime mortar" and leaky.[^1]

The present tower came out of the 1850s. The architect Alexander Parris examined the old light in 1852 and advised a new tower; Congress appropriated $25,000 on 31 August 1852, spent in 1854, and $19,973 more on 3 August 1854 for the lighting apparatus and for finishing the tower and buildings, spent in 1855.[^3] Joseph W. Coburn of Chelsea, Massachusetts, supplied the granite for $22,000 under a contract dated 10 December 1853.[^3] The HABS record thinks the designer was Lieutenant W. B. Franklin of the Topographical Engineers; the National Register form names the Army Corps of Engineers.[^3][^5] The shaft is granite ashlar lined with some 140,000 bricks, and it was first lit on 1 January 1855 with a second-order Fresnel lens.[^3][^2]

Its height depends on who is measuring. The HABS record gives 118 feet (36 m), with 133 feet (41 m) above high water, while the National Register nomination says "nearly 123 feet" (37 m).[^3][^5]

## The keepers

Boon Island struggled to keep a keeper. The first man offered the job refused it, and David Oliver, the second, left after a salary dispute to go to sea; he had written that his needs came to nearly $500 a year.[^1] Thomas Hanna took the post in 1812 for $300 a year and after a year on the island was threatening to leave unless he got what had been promised; the note forwarding his request to the Treasury Secretary admitted that "All the fresh water, wood and necessaries for a family must be carried on to the Island."[^1][^4] Eliphalet Grover then served from 1816 until 1839, and his logbook records a night on 31 October 1829 when the family was driven into the tower and the island was "all under water for 4 hours."[^4][^1] Local rivals campaigned against him, and on his dismissal in 1839 he wrote that he had been turned off "Without Cause."[^1]

Captain Nathaniel Baker was keeper from 1846; the York museum says he rescued the crew of the schooner *Caroline*, wrecked on the island that year, and was dismissed in 1849 all the same.[^4][^2] When the big tower opened, the station got its first assistants. The archive's roster lists Christopher C. Littlefield as first assistant in 1854, and Charles H. Tobey and Samuel Tobey in 1855 under head keeper Josiah Tobey.[^4]

William C. Williams stayed longest. He came as second assistant in 1885, became head keeper in 1888 and held the post to 1911; his son Charles S. Williams was first assistant from 1897 to 1905.[^4][^1] The Light-House Board's 1888 report, as Lighthousefriends quotes it, said the stone dwelling "designed for two keepers is occupied by three, two of whom have families."[^1] The Board then asked for a third dwelling in report after report. In 1893 it renewed a request the Board had made in its "last two annual reports", explaining that the second assistant had to board with the keeper's or the first assistant's family, which was "quite unfavorable to the retention" of a good man; it estimated $3,400.[^6] The 1901 report renewed it once more, after ten earlier reports.[^7] A single-family house was finally built in 1905.[^3]

Daily life was hard to keep supplied. Howard Gray, an assistant under Keeper Hutchins (1924 to 1933), recalled mail and groceries coming "only once a month, and it cost $10 for this service."[^1] A letter printed in 1932 listed the trades a keeper had to know, among them doctor, painter, steeplejack, boatman and cook.[^1] Harold I. Hutchins was head keeper from 1924 to 1933.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sea has repeatedly come over the island. The log entry for 26 January 1839 has the Grovers retreating to the tower as the sea broke into the porch and forced the dwelling door.[^1] After a severe storm in the season of 1887 the tower was braced: six sets of wrought-iron stay rods were fitted in 1888 to check its vibration, and the keepers' dwelling was gutted and rebuilt with a frame upper storey in 1889.[^5][^3] A brick-and-stone oil house was built in 1887-89.[^3]

On the night of about 20 December 1892 the 90-ton schooner *Gold Hunter*, of Clementsport, Nova Scotia, bound for Boston with wood, struck Boon Island ledge in what the Portland *Daily Press* called a hurricane. Her crew took to the boats, reached the island, and stayed with Keeper Williams until the lighthouse tender *Lilac* took them to Portsmouth on 27 December.[^8] Lighthousefriends adds that six men were saved and that the temperature was four degrees below zero.[^1]

The gale that began on 31 January 1898, which Williams described in a letter to the Portsmouth *Herald*, brought winds of nearly 100 miles an hour; two water tanks of about four tons each were carried 75 feet across the island and wrecked.[^1] In the Lighthouse Service Bulletin, Keeper Mitchell Blackwood and Second Assistant Charles A. Radley are credited with rescuing two men whose disabled power boat was being driven to sea on 5 December, and bringing them to the station.[^9] The Bulletin's volume is for 1914; the year of the rescue is not clear from the page.

A bell struck by hand was the only fog signal. In October 1897 Captain R. D. Evans, then a member of the Light-House Board, told his colleagues that he had arrived off Boon Island in fog and had not heard it. In 1901 the Board asked for $10,000 for a better signal, noting that the Boston-Bangor steamers ran from Seguin for Boon Island and "are all afraid" of Pollocks Rock to the southwest.[^7]

## After the keepers

Keepers on the archive's roster from the 1940s on include Coast Guard personnel, and the list ends in 1978.[^4] That February's blizzard flooded the keepers' house and scattered boulders; Coast Guard keepers Kirby Eldridge and Leo Berry took refuge in the tower, were taken off by helicopter the next day, and damage was put at $100,000.[^2][^4] The station was automated soon after, and Lighthousefriends says the damaged dwelling was burned in 1981.[^5][^1] The Boon Island Light Station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 14 March 1988; by then only the first-storey granite walls of the keepers' house survived.[^5] The second-order lens was removed in 1993 and given to the Kittery Historical and Naval Museum.[^1]

## Today

The light still shows a flashing white signal every five seconds, automated, and the island is private. The Coast Guard offered the tower under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, and after no qualified owner came forward it was sold at auction in 2014 for $78,000 to a Portland developer, who sold it within months to Boon Island LLC.[^1] The tower and island are closed to the public; the lighthouse is best seen from a boat, and a distant view is possible from Cape Neddick Light.[^1] Please do not land on the island.
