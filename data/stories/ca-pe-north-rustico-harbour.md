North Rustico Harbour Light is a small wooden tower with the keeper's house still attached, on the north side of the harbour entrance on Prince Edward Island's Gulf of St. Lawrence shore. The building has been moved several times, was tipped over by a gale in 1899, and was threatened with being lost in the 1970s, when local residents pressed to keep it.

## The place

North Rustico faces the Gulf of St. Lawrence on the island's north shore and was settled around 1790 by Acadians. Lighthouse Friends describes it as a village long tied to the sea, where fishing still drives the local economy.[^1] Before there was a lighthouse, mariners were guided into the harbour by a pair of range lights. Records show a payment in 1868 for two lanterns and another for the posts to hang them on. By 1874 the General Superintendent of Lighthouses had seen these small lanterns, raised on masts about 82 metres (270 ft) apart, and decided a tower was needed.[^1]

## Building the light

The federal Department of Marine and Fisheries designed the lighthouse in 1876, and the heritage register says it was meant to guard the area's fledgling fishing industry.[^2] Lighthouse Friends reports that P. Carroll of Miramichi built it that summer under a $1,700 contract, while the Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society says the builder is unknown.[^1][^3] Because lamps were lacking, the lights were not shown until spring 1877: a white light in the lantern and a red light in a window 4.9 m (16 ft) lower, visible only when a vessel was in line with the channel.[^1]

## The keepers

Thomas G. Pursey was the first keeper and served until 1892; in 1878 the Department's agent called him "very trustworthy".[^1] The Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society lists the keepers of the tower as Pursey (1876 to 1892), followed by Joseph Dourant (1892 to 1897) and Joseph N. Pino (1897 to 1912).[^3] The Department of Marine's keepers' table for 1910 shows Pino at North Rustico, appointed February 6, 1897.[^4] Levi Blacquiere is listed as keeper from 1912 to 1925 (the Society prints "L. Blacquiere").[^3] Pino was the keeper in charge when the tower capsized in 1899, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] George Pineau, the last resident keeper, served from 1925 until the light was automated in 1960.[^2] The Society and Lighthouse Friends also name two men who held the post in the years of the range lanterns: William H. MacNeill (1868 to 1869) and Thomas Walsh, listed in 1874.[^1][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sea kept coming for the building. In the winter of 1883-84 it was moved 155 m (510 ft) from the middle of the beach to the north side of the harbour entrance, and the lower red light was replaced by a red light on a mast in front, making the tower the back light of a range. A gale in 1887 undermined the front light's foundation and left it leaning.[^1] In 1895 the tower was set on a hemlock timber crib and the shore protected with piling, at a cost of $527, but a heavy northeast gale on January 12, 1899, undermined the lighthouse and capsized it.[^1] It was moved back, repaired, and used afterwards as a coastal light, with a mast light taking over as the rear range light.[^1] The heritage record says a severe winter storm in 1899 almost destroyed it, and that it was moved in 1884, 1899 and 1914; Lighthouse Friends does not mention a move in 1914.[^2][^1][^3] A Department of Marine report published in 1923 lists repairs to the main light, the breakwater and the outer range light at North Rustico.[^5]

## After the keepers

After automation the attached house was leased as a summer residence from 1968 to about 1973. A new modern light went up in 1973 and the old tower was declared surplus, the Society dating that to 1974-75. Residents who feared it would be destroyed or sold rallied to keep it, and by 1976, in time for the building's centennial, the light was back in the old tower.[^2][^1][^3] The Society adds that the interior was stripped, and that surplus lighthouse equipment was stored there for some years.[^3] It is one of only four lighthouses left on Prince Edward Island with an attached keeper's dwelling; the others are New London, Wood Islands and the Blockhouse Light near Rocky Point.[^2]

## Today

Lighthouse Friends says the light is still active, with five seconds of light every ten seconds, and the Society lists the Government of Canada as owner. The Society prints a federal heritage building number for it (FHBRO 90-089), and on 3 October 2012 it was recognized as a provincial heritage place.[^3][^1] In 2012 the Friends of North Shore Communities petitioned to take over the lighthouse under the federal heritage lighthouse programme; neither source says how that ended.[^1][^3] The Society gives directions along Harbourview Drive but does not say whether the interior is open, so check locally before visiting.[^3]
