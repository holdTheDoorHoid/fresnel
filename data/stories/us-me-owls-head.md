Owls Head Light stands on a cliff on the south side of the entrance to Rockland Harbor, on the west shore of Penobscot Bay.[^1][^3] The first light was shown in September 1825.[^1] The brick tower there now dates from 1852, the light was automated in 1989, and the American Lighthouse Foundation runs climbing tours in season.[^3][^4]

## The place

The nomination describes a high, wave-swept headland, pine trees and grassy terraces above the bay, and a cliff whose rock formation is said to look like an owl's head, with two hollows for eyes.[^1]

After the Revolution, the nomination says, the Thomaston area built up a lime and shipping business. Schooners from Owls Head made voyages to Europe and came back with cargoes of salt, and many vessels passing the headland were wrecked in fog and storm before they made port.[^1] Agitation for a light started, and when the steamer *Maine* began making regular stops at Owls Head in 1823, plans were developed for a light on the headland.[^1]

## Building the light

Articles of agreement dated 18 April 1825 between the superintendent of lighthouses in Maine and the builders Jeremiah Berry and Green & Foster set the sum of $1,888 for a lighthouse and a dwelling on Owls Head.[^1] The house was 34 feet by 20 (10 by 6 m), one story with two rooms and a central chimney.[^1] An ell, a second story and a piazza were added about 1854.[^1][^3]

The nomination's physical description calls the light tower brick and dates it to 1826.[^1] Its historical narrative describes the tower built on the highest peak of the promontory as granite.[^1] In September 1825 the keeper, Isaac Stearns, lit the light for the first time.[^1]

The US Lighthouse Society's record and the American Lighthouse Foundation give 1852 for the present tower. It is brick on a granite foundation, and its light stands 100 feet (30.5 m) above mean high water.[^3][^4] The Society's record gives a fourth-order Fresnel lens installed in 1856, and the nomination describes the same order of lens in the lantern today.[^3][^1] The Foundation gives the tower's height as 26 feet (8 m) in one place and 30 feet (9 m) in another.[^4]

The Board tested the station's fog signal. Its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 records tests at Owls Head on 7 and 8 September 1893, and again on 13 and 14 June 1894. The station's regular 1,000-pound bell was sounded. In 1893 a 960-pound bell and the tender *Myrtle*'s 6-inch whistle were also tested.[^5]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists 31 stints at Owls Head, from Isaac Stearns in 1825 to Malcolm Rouse in 1989.[^3] Stearns kept the light until 1838.[^3]

William Masters was keeper from 1838 to 1841 and again from 1845 to 1849.[^3] The nomination gives two starts for Masters: it says he succeeded Stearns in 1838, and it also says he became keeper in 1845. The roster lists Penley Haines from 1841 to 1845.[^1][^3] The nomination says Masters resigned in August 1849 in favor of Henry Achorn; the roster spells the name Achora.[^1][^3]

The nomination dates the arrival of Joseph G. Haddocks to 1873, and it says Keeper Maddocks left in 1896. The roster lists Joseph G. Maddocks from 1872 to 1895.[^1][^3] The sources disagree on the name and the dates, and this story does not choose between them. The nomination says Haddocks was a shipbuilder before he was wounded in the arm at Gettysburg, and that he married Clara Emery of Owls Head.[^1]

Llewellyn S. Norwood was keeper from 1895 to 1911.[^3] The *Ellsworth American*'s notice of 19 April 1911 calls him "lightkeeper at Owl's Head" and says he died at his home there on 13 April.[^7] A notice reprinted from the *Rockland Courier-Gazette* on 26 April says he died on 13 April, of heart disease, after his health had been failing rapidly all winter.[^6] The two notices also disagree on his years in the service: one says "for the last twenty years," and the other's headline says "Fifteen Years' Service."[^6][^7]

The nomination calls Augustus B. Hamor the last regular keeper of the old Lighthouse Service at Owls Head. He entered the service in 1913, served 17 years at Egg Rock near Bar Harbor, and was then transferred here. The roster lists him from 1930 to 1945.[^1][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The nomination, which takes its narrative from Edgar Rowe Snow's *The Lighthouses of New England*, records several wrecks. On 21 March 1847 the schooner *Hero* was smashed against the rock shore at Owls Head during an equinoctial gale. On 24 January 1850 the schooner *Dove* went ashore at Ash Point and was a total loss.[^1]

The nomination's most detailed account concerns the gale of 22 December 1850. It says five vessels were thrown ashore between Owls Head and Spruce Head, and spray froze on them.[^1] A Haverhill schooner had three people aboard.[^1] Two of them lost consciousness under the ice on her deck, and the deckhand chopped his way out and walked to a road, where Keeper Masters, driving a pung, found him.[^1] The rescuers went back to the schooner and tried to revive the other two, who were treated in a house and in time recovered. The nomination says the couple married the following June.[^1] The account also names Masters as keeper in December 1850, although the same nomination says he had resigned in 1849.[^1]

The nomination also says there were more than eleven shipwrecks around Owls Head before Keeper Maddocks left in 1896.[^1]

The *Lighthouse Service Bulletin*'s 1925 volume lists Owls Head among the lights whose illuminant was changed, from incandescent oil vapor to electric incandescent.[^8]

## After the keepers

The nomination says electricity from the Maine Central line now supplies the light.[^1] The light was automated in 1989.[^3][^4] The National Park Service's record gives 18 January 1978 as the publication date of the station's National Register listing.[^2]

## Today

The American Lighthouse Foundation says the Coast Guard owns the light and licenses it to the foundation. The US Lighthouse Society's record lists the foundation as owner.[^4][^3] The foundation's headquarters have been at the station since 2013, with an interpretive center on the first floor of the keeper's house. In season, volunteer docents lead climbing tours to the top of the tower from Memorial Day to Columbus Day.[^4] The town of Owls Head has a park called Lighthouse Park on the land beside the station.[^1]
