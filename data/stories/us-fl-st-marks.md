St. Marks Light stands in the Big Bend of Florida's Gulf coast, about 25 miles south of Tallahassee, where the St. Marks River reaches the bay.[^2][^5] The tower in use today was completed in 1842 and is the third lighthouse at the station.[^2][^5] The Fish and Wildlife Service calls it the oldest lighthouse on the Gulf coast.[^5]

## The place

LighthouseFriends says an early Spanish settlement stood at the river's mouth, that Andrew Jackson took St. Marks from the Spanish in 1818, and that Florida passed to the United States three years later.[^2] In 1828 the territorial governor, William P. Duval, encouraged the territorial delegate Joseph M. White to petition for a light, and an act of 23 May 1828 provided $6,000 for one.[^2] LighthouseFriends says planters financed the Tallahassee–St. Marks Railroad in 1837 to carry their cotton to the port, and calls it Florida's first railroad.[^2]

## Building the light

The contract went to Winslow Lewis, and under his direction Benjamin Beal and Jairus Thayer built the first tower for $11,765.[^2] The nomination calls Lewis the government's "lighthouse builder."[^1] The customs collector would not accept the tower, because it was built with hollow walls.[^2] LighthouseFriends says Calvin Knowlton, Lewis's partner, supervised a solid replacement, which the collector accepted in January 1830.[^2]

The light had fifteen lamps in fifteen-inch reflectors and showed a fixed white light 73 feet (22 m) above the water.[^2] The Society's record gives fourteen-inch reflectors, and dates the establishment of the station to 1831.[^9]

The nomination reports that the limestone was taken from the nearby ruins of the old fort, or from a quarry near it across the Wakulla River.[^1] By 1842 erosion was threatening the tower, and a new one was built farther inland.[^2] LighthouseFriends says it rests on a base of limestone from the old fort.[^2] The Fish and Wildlife Service says the current tower was completed in 1842.[^5] The Board's report for 1895 records that a new cistern was put up at the station.[^8]

## The keepers

Samuel Crosby was the first keeper, from 1830 to 1839.[^9][^2] When the Second Seminole War broke out, LighthouseFriends says, Crosby asked for soldiers to be posted near the station, and then for an escape boat. Neither request was granted. No attack came while he was there.[^2]

The roster shows many short tenures in the 1840s and 1850s.[^9] Ann Dudley is listed as principal keeper from 1850 to 1854, the only woman on the roster before the Fines.[^9] The roster does not say how she came to the post or whether she was related to Needham Dudley, who had held it from 1844 to 1850.[^9]

David M. Kennedy was head keeper briefly before the Civil War.[^9] LighthouseFriends says he returned to the post in 1867, and the roster lists him as head keeper from then until 1879.[^2][^9] The position of assistant went to Kennedy's seventeen-year-old son James, who served as an assistant from 1867 to 1875.[^2][^9] LighthouseFriends says the family's new dwelling was not finished until 1871.[^2]

Charles Fine was head keeper from 1892 until he died in 1904.[^2][^9] His widow, Sarah J. Fine, succeeded him and served from 1904 to 1910, and again from 1911 to 1916, with Joseph M. Ladd as keeper between.[^2][^9] Their youngest child, Lela, grew up at the lighthouse and married John Young Gresham, who became keeper in 1918 and served until 1949.[^2][^9] LighthouseFriends says his thirty-one years were longer than any other keeper's at St. Marks.[^2] In January 1926 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded him as "keeper of St. Marks Lighthouse, Fla." and reported that on 5 January he "towed a disabled motor boat to St. Marks." On 12 January, it said, he conveyed two men from a hunting party who were lost to St. Marks.[^4]

The Greshams raised eight children at the station.[^2] LighthouseFriends, drawing on a memoir by one of the daughters, says the living room was made into a classroom, and that a private teacher lived with the family during the summer months.[^2] Alton T. Gresham, a son of John Young Gresham, served from 1957 to 1959, and Gerald R. King from 1959 to 1961.[^9][^2] LighthouseFriends reports that King's young son drowned in a slough near the yard, not long after King took charge.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The masonry tower stood up to the hurricane of September 1843, which destroyed the nearby town of Port Leon and damaged St. Marks farther upriver.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the sturdy construction saved Keeper John Hungerford and his family, and that a breakwater was built afterward.[^2] Another hurricane struck in 1851, and the tower stood again.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the dwelling was damaged and replaced in 1854, and the nomination dates the breakwater and the new keeper's house to 1853.[^2][^1] In September 1873 a hurricane flooded the dwelling, and the Kennedy family took refuge in the tower.[^2]

The Civil War reached the station directly. Union ships bombarded the light on 15 June 1862, and a year later Union sailors burned the wooden steps in the tower.[^2] The nomination says Confederate Army pickets were posted "in or near the lighthouse" on a fairly regular basis during the coastal raids.[^1] On 4 March 1865 a Union landing began at the lighthouse. The nomination says it was "a landing of one thousand Federal troops," and a prelude "to the Battle of Natural Bridge near Tallahassee on March 6, 1865."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the retreating Confederates had burned the dwelling and set charges in holes they had drilled in the tower, and that one blast tore an eight-foot (2.4 m) hole in it.[^2]

The tower was repaired between September and December 1866, and a new fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed in the lantern room.[^2] The light went back into operation on 7 January 1867.[^2][^9]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard took over the nation's lighthouses in 1939, and the Greshams continued to serve.[^2] According to LighthouseFriends, the area around the lighthouse was incorporated into St. Marks National Wildlife Refuge during John Young Gresham's service.[^2] The Friends group says the light was automated in 1960.[^6]

The Commissioner's report for 1922 lists, under a $100,000 appropriation of 28 March 1918 for the St. Marks Light Station, "Rebuilt 325 feet wharf," along with rebuilt walks and repairs to the outbuildings and dwelling.[^7] The Coast Guard spent $150,000 on repairs and stabilization in 2000.[^2]

Congress passed an act in June 2006 that moved the lighthouse and about eight acres from the Coast Guard to the Fish and Wildlife Service.[^2] The transfer was not completed until 10 October 2013, because lead-contaminated soil had to be removed first.[^2] A ceremony followed on 28 March 2014.[^2]

The Fish and Wildlife Service took the lens down in November 2014 so the lantern room could be restored.[^2] LighthouseFriends says volunteers at Ponce de Leon Inlet cleaned it, and it was back in the refuge's visitor center by the following March.[^2]

Florida's 2016 General Appropriations Act allocated $550,000 for the lighthouse: $50,000 for restoring the lantern room and $500,000 for preserving the lighthouse itself.[^2] Phase II, which focused on the keeper's dwelling, was finished in early 2018, and the lighthouse opened to the public on 29 September 2018.[^2][^5]

## Today

The keeper's quarters are open on Friday, Saturday and Sunday, from 11 a.m. to 3 p.m., and the refuge is open in daylight with an entrance fee.[^5] The Friends group says climbing the tower is not permitted.[^6] A replica fourth-order lens was installed on 12 July 2019, and the light was relit on 31 October 2019, on the refuge's birthday.[^2] Rowlett's directory gives the light's flash as white every 2.5 seconds, from a focal plane 82 feet (25 m) above the water.[^10]
