St. Simons Island Light stands at the southern tip of St. Simons Island, Georgia, where the sea route into the port of Brunswick meets the land. Its brick tower and two-story keepers' duplex date from 1872. They replaced a tabby tower of about 1810 that Confederate soldiers blew up in 1862.[^1]

## The place

The light marks the entrance to St. Simons Sound and the way into Brunswick harbour, one of four major historic ocean harbours in Georgia (the others the National Register nomination names are Savannah, Darien and St. Marys). The nomination says that since 1872 this light has been the principal visual aid guiding vessels in and out of the port.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that Congress first voted money for a lighthouse here in 1804, that John Couper, who owned a plantation at the south end of the island, sold the government four acres for a dollar, and that he was keen to have a light for the trade of the Brunswick area. That first appropriation lapsed in 1806, and Congress voted a larger sum in 1807.[^3]

## Building the light

The nomination says the first lighthouse on the site was completed about 1810, by a contractor named James Gould, whom President James Madison then appointed its first keeper.[^1] LighthouseFriends adds that Gould, newly arrived from New England, answered a newspaper advertisement and proposed building in tabby, a mixture of lime, sand, water and oyster shell, using the ruins of Fort Frederica as his source. It gives a 75-foot (23 m) tower, finished in late 1810 at a cost of $13,775, lit by oil lamps hung on chains.[^3] The sources do not agree on the date of the first tower: the nomination says "circa 1810", the U.S. Lighthouse Society archive gives 1811 as the year established, and LighthouseFriends says the tower was finished in late 1810.[^1][^2][^3] LighthouseFriends reports that the tower's eight-inch reflectors were judged too small in 1838, that nine lamps with fourteen-inch reflectors were installed in 1847, and that a third-order Fresnel lens was fitted in 1856.[^3]

In the Civil War, according to the nomination, Confederate soldiers blew up the station in 1862 to keep Federal ships from using it.[^1] The keeper roster has no entries between 1861 and 1872.[^2]

The new tower and dwelling were designed in 1867 and built between 1868 and 1872, and the light was first exhibited on the night of 1 September 1872.[^1] LighthouseFriends names the Georgia architect Charles B. Cluskey as author of the plans and gives $45,000 as the appropriation of 2 March 1867. It also says the work was slow and unhealthy: the first contractor fell ill and died during a summer of sickness at the site, one of his two bondsmen took over and also died there, and the surviving bondsman finished the job after the tower had reached 51 feet (16 m) in November 1871.[^3] The result is a 104-foot (32 m) brick tower and a two-story brick duplex that the nomination calls eclectic, with Gothic Revival and Italianate details.[^1][^3] The lens was a third-order Fresnel by L. Sautter; LighthouseFriends describes a fixed light with four revolving panels that gave a flash once a minute, at first alternating red and white.[^3] A brick oil house, 9 by 11 feet, was added later: the nomination says possibly as early as 1889, and a Light-House Board report of the early 1890s records a fireproof brick oil house of those dimensions, holding 450 five-gallon cans, built here.[^1][^10]

## The range beacon

Congress did not fund a second light to make a range with the main tower until 1893, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] The Light-House Board reported that the site was obtained through condemnation proceedings begun in 1893, finishing in March 1895, and that a front beacon showing a reflector light was lit in May 1895. It said the beacon, with the main light, formed a range "to guide through the best water on any one straight line across the bar".[^4] The structure did not stay put. The Board recorded it carried away by the hurricane of 29 September 1896 and replaced in October,[^5] and again carried away on 2 October 1898, with a temporary light shown on 11 October and the rebuilt structure lit on 27 October.[^6] In September 1900, the Board reported, high tides had so undermined the beacon's foundation, and the range was so short, that it could not be moved back along the range line.[^7] LighthouseFriends says a pile foundation was built in 1900 and a raised plank walk to the beacon in 1904.[^3]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society archive, working from the registers of lighthouse keepers, lists James Gould as principal keeper from 1810 to 1837, followed by Lachlan McIntosh (1837-1852), Alexander D. McIntosh (1852-1855) and John F. Carmon (1855-1861).[^2] LighthouseFriends says Gould was paid $1,700 in 1830 to build a keeper's dwelling, and that the new light of 1872 was the first to have an assistant: Bradford B. Brunt, its first principal keeper, was given one.[^3] The archive lists Brunt for 1872 to 1874 with Hosea Sherman as first assistant.[^2]

Frederick Osborne took over in 1874.[^2] In 1880 he was shot by his assistant, John Stevens, and died. The U.S. Lighthouse Society archive records that he "Died on March 10, 1880 from a gunshot wound received from Assistant John Stevens."[^8] A newspaper note of 12 March 1880, as quoted on a modern blog, said that "Mr. Osburn, keeper of the lighthouse on St. Simons' Island, was seriously shot by Mr. John Stephens, his assistant, for talking in a disrespectful manner to his wife."[^9] Accounts disagree on the cause: LighthouseFriends says it was reported either that Osborne spoke improperly to Stevens's wife or that Stevens made advances to Osborne's. The same two sources say Stevens was tried and acquitted.[^3][^9] The roster shows Stevens as first assistant from 1875 to 1880.[^2] A ghost story grew from the killing. LighthouseFriends reports that Carl Svendsen, keeper from 1907 to 1935, and his wife repeatedly heard footsteps in the tower that sent their dog into a frenzy; this is local lore, told here as lore.[^3]

After George W. Asbell (1880-1883) came Isaac L. Peckham, principal keeper from 1883 to 1892, with Marianne I. Peckham listed as first assistant for the same years. Joseph Champagne followed (1892-1907), with Abbie M. Champagne as first assistant for the same span.[^2] The roster does not say how the pairs were related. Carl Olaf Svendsen served 1907 to 1935, then Arthur F. Hodge (1935-1945) and David C. O'Hagan (1945-1950).[^2] Between 1907 and 1934 the post of first assistant has eighteen separate stints on the roster, some lasting only a few months.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends reports that the keepers complained repeatedly of the island's unhealthy air, and that the Lighthouse Board drained the ponds near the tower in 1875 and had eucalyptus seed planted, in the belief that the trees would neutralise the gases thought to cause malaria.[^3] It also tells of the Charleston earthquake on 31 August 1886, when Keeper Peckham, resting in the watchroom, reported a noise "like that made by a horse running over a hard road" and one broken red flash panel; and of lightning that struck the dwelling in May 1887, after which two lightning conductors were added.[^3]

## After the keepers

The sources differ on when the station lost its resident keepers. LighthouseFriends says it was automated in 1950; the archive's roster ends at 1950; the National Register update says the facility became fully automated in 1959.[^2][^3][^1] The nomination says the light was converted to electricity in 1934.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that the passage joining tower and dwelling was removed soon after automation and that the dwelling stood empty for some years until Glynn County leased it in 1972 as a museum and visitor centre.[^3] The keepers' building was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1972, and a Historic Preservation Fund grant helped restore the building in 1979.[^1] Ownership passed to the Coastal Georgia Historical Society on 26 May 2004 under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, and in 2012 a $250,000 federal grant, with the society's own funds, paid for a $1.3 million restoration of the dwelling, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3]

## Today

The National Register update says the lighthouse is still owned and operated by the U.S. Coast Guard.[^1] The keepers' building is a museum run by the Coastal Georgia Historical Society, and the National Register update says the county owns the keepers' building and the society operates it.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that the tower has been open to climbers since 1984, by a 129-step spiral stair, and gives the address as 101 12th Street on the island's southern tip.[^3] Hours and climbing access change, so check with the society before you go.
