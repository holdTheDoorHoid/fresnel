Mendota Light stands at the entrance to Lac La Belle, on the Keweenaw Peninsula in Keweenaw County, Michigan, where a ship canal runs from Bete Grise Bay into the lake.[^1][^10] Its square brick tower, with a fourth-order Fresnel lens, has stood there since 1895, when the government rebuilt a light that had been discontinued in 1870.[^1][^2] The station is privately owned now, and a private owner relit the light in 1998.[^10][^11]

## The place

The canal is the reason for the light. A private company, the Lac La Belle Harbor Improvement Company, was formed in 1865 to cut it, and Congress gave the company 100,000 acres in 1866 to pay for the work.[^10] LighthouseFriends says the canal was about 4,000 feet long and was finished inside its two-year deadline.[^10] On 2 March 1867 Congress appropriated $14,000 for a pierhead light to mark the canal.[^10][^11] The light took its name from a mining operation on the north shore of Lac La Belle.[^10]

The bay mattered more as the lake traffic grew.[^2] By 1892 the Light-House Board was writing that westbound vessels tried in the fall to use the point "as a harbor of refuge in which to await fair weather before attempting to make the passage around Keweenaw Point," and that at night the bay "is now almost inaccessible." A light near the head of the bay, the Board wrote, "will be of marked advantage in guiding vessels after dark to a safe anchorage."[^2]

## Building the light

The government secured a deed to a site of about two acres on 9 May 1868.[^10] LighthouseFriends says the contractor agreed to build the station for $11,220 and finish it by 1 November 1869. The owner's history gives the completion as November 1870, so the sources disagree on the date and this story cannot settle it.[^10][^11] The first tower was a wooden pierhead tower, and the first dwelling was brick.[^10][^4]

The light was lit in 1870 and kept for about four and a half months by William Kirby.[^10] The Board then ordered it discontinued after the season.[^10] Its 1892 report gives the reason: the light was discontinued "because of the failure of the local business," and the light tower "was moved to and used at Marquette Breakwater." The keeper's dwelling stayed at Mendota.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the tender Haze took the wooden tower down in the summer of 1871, and that it was set up at Marquette four years later.[^10]

In 1892 the Board asked for $7,500 to reestablish the light.[^2] Congress authorized the work on 15 February 1893 but made no appropriation that year.[^3] In 1894 the Board reported that the old brick house had been "ruined by inroads of the storms of the past winter." It estimated $2,500 to replace the dwelling and recommended an appropriation of $10,000, instead of $7,500, for the reestablishment of the light.[^4] The 1895 report then records that an appropriation of $7,500 was made "by the act approved March 2, 1895," for this station, and that plans and estimates for a keeper's dwelling and a light had been made.[^5] The $10,000 requested in 1894 and the $7,500 appropriated in 1895 do not match, and neither report explains the difference.[^4][^5] The owner's history repeats the $10,000 figure.[^11]

Materials came by the tender Amaranth in September 1895, and the dwelling and outbuildings were finished in November.[^6] The Board's report lists "a fourth-order light, November 30, 1895," as newly established.[^6] LighthouseFriends describes its first characteristic as a flashing white light with a white flash every 45 seconds.[^10] The archive records the tower as brick and square, and 36.7 feet (11.2 m) from the base to the center of the lantern. LighthouseFriends gives "just over forty-two feet" (about 13 m) for the tower.[^1][^10] On 31 October 1896 the Board changed the characteristic to a fixed white light.[^7]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's digital archive lists five people for Mendota.[^1] The first two, Henry A. Keichli (1869 to 1870) and William Kirby (1870), belong to the first light. LighthouseFriends names only Kirby for 1870, so the two accounts do not agree on Keichli.[^1][^10]

The longest service was that of William G. Jilbert, who kept the light from 1895 until his death in 1928.[^1][^10] LighthouseFriends says he and his wife Harriette raised eight children at the station, and that one of the children drowned in the canal at eighteen months old.[^10]

George W. Smith is listed as keeper from 1930 to at least 1931.[^1] The archive also lists a lamp lighter, Vivian R. Nortdstrom (its spelling), from at least 1939. The archive dates automation to 1933, and the sources do not say what the lamp lighter's post involved.[^1]

The station's keepers kept journals. Michigan Technological University holds journals for 1895 to 1930. The finding aid describes daily entries on "lighthouse duties and upkeep," with "some weather information," housekeeping, visitors and trips for provisions.[^12]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The storms of the winter of 1893 to 1894 ruined the old brick house, according to the Board's 1894 report.[^4]

On 28 April 1918 William G. Jilbert, keeper of the Mendota light, gave "valuable assistance to three fishermen" whose gasoline launch had swamped in heavy seas near the station, the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported.[^8]

In a letter dated 31 December 1926, the Coast Guard district commander thanked the keepers at Copper Harbor and at Mendota. While the crew of the Eagle Harbor Coast Guard station was helping two steamers, the Mendota keepers gave food and shelter to shipwrecked sailors. The Bulletin printed the letter, which does not name either keeper.[^9]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the light was converted to electric incandescent in 1929.[^10] In 1933 an unmanned acetylene light on a square, open-framework tower took the place of the brick lighthouse.[^10] The archive and the Michigan Tech finding aid both give 1933 as the year the light was automated.[^1][^12]

The sources also disagree on when it was decommissioned. The Michigan Tech finding aid and the archive give 1956.[^12][^1] Terry Pepper's keepers page gives 1960 as the year the station was discontinued.[^13] In 1960 a project to make the bay a harbor of refuge for pleasure boats dredged the canal and built new piers at its mouth, at a cost LighthouseFriends gives as $450,000. The 1933 tower was discontinued in the same project.[^10]

The Michigan Tech finding aid says the house was a private residence until 1996, when it was offered for sale and sold to a buyer willing to conserve it.[^12] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse was bought at auction by Heimo "Paddy" Jaaskelainen, who looked after it until his death in 1996.[^10][^11] The sources do not say whether this and the finding aid's 1996 sale are the same transaction. Gary Kohs, who first saw the light on a motorcycle ride in September 1997, became its owner in 1998.[^10][^14] Kohs located and restored the fourth-order lens and relit the light on 5 July 1998 at 9:45 p.m.[^10][^11] LighthouseFriends counts "roughly 1,000 onlookers" at the relighting, and the owner's website says approximately 3,500. LighthouseFriends also describes car horns and fireworks as the switch was thrown.[^10][^11] Kohs died in 2017, and the station was put on the market in June 2020 and sold that August.[^10]

## Today

Mendota Light is privately owned. The Michigan Tech finding aid describes it as an active private aid to navigation, and says it can be reached only by boat, because no road or private drive leads to it.[^12] LighthouseFriends says the grounds, dwelling and tower are closed to visitors. Its directions end at a road from which the light can be seen across the water.[^10]

The restored fourth-order lens is in place and in use.[^12] The owner's website says the restored outbuildings can be seen on a tour, and it lists weddings held at the station.[^14] Ask the owner before going there. Do not go onto the grounds or climb the tower without permission.
