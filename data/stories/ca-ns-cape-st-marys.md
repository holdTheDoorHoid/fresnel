Cape St. Marys Lighthouse stands on a clifftop on the east side of the entrance to St. Mary's Bay, in the Municipality of Clare on Nova Scotia's Acadian shore.[^1][^3][^4] The first light was shown there in 1868. The tower has been rebuilt since, and by Lighthouse Friends' count a father and son from one family kept the light for sixty-two years.[^1][^2]

## The place

The light sits 31.8 m above high water, on the edge of rocky cliffs in open country.[^1] It served shipping on its way to the Bay of Fundy and into St. Mary's Bay, including vessels bound for Grand Passage and Petit Passage.[^1][^2] The heritage register adds that the light served the lobster and groundfish fleets as well as shipping.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends says inhabitants of Yarmouth and Digby County petitioned the province for a light at Cape St. Mary in 1858, and that another petition followed in 1859. It was 1866 before a Nova Scotia committee recommended one.[^2]

## Building the light

On 26 June 1867 the provincial Board of Works awarded a contract of $2,800 to Dexter and Robichau to build the light. Lighthouse Friends says the work was not finished until Nova Scotia had become part of Canada, which it did that summer.[^2] The first tower was a wooden octagon, 13.1 m (43 ft) high.[^2] The society's entry gives its height as about 14 m (46 ft).[^3] The keeper's house beside it was 10 m by 6.7 m (33 by 22 ft).[^2] The light used ten lamps with 12-inch reflectors, in two faces of five, turned once a minute by clockwork to show alternating red and white flashes.[^2] The register says the first two lighthouses were staffed and had detached dwellings, and that the present one is the third on the site.[^1]

The society's list puts a second tower somewhere between 1883 and 1922, and gives no firmer date.[^3]

A fog signal came later. Lighthouse Friends dates a diaphone building to 1915.[^2] The society gives 1914.[^3] The Department's list for the year ended 31 March 1914 does not mark the station with a fog signal.[^8] The list for the year ended 31 March 1915 adds a second line for the station, "St. Mary, Cape, F. A", where other fog stations read "light and fog-alarm."[^7]

The present tower is a square reinforced-concrete tower set into the corner of a concrete-block fog-alarm building. It is 8.7 m high, with a red octagonal lantern.[^1] The register says its features largely follow Department of Transport drawings of 1968.[^1] Lighthouse Friends dates the tower to 1969, and the society to 1965.[^2][^3]

## The keepers

Mathurin Robichau, the first keeper, had been a sea captain and had sat in the Nova Scotia assembly for Clare, according to Lighthouse Friends. He gave up politics a year after Confederation and took the light.[^2] His salary was $500 a year.[^2] His son Benjamin, one of seven children, began helping him young and took over the light. Lighthouse Friends counts the service of father and son together at sixty-two years.[^2] It lists Benjamin as head keeper from 1886 to 1931.[^2]

The name is spelled three ways in the sources. A keepers' table for the year ended 30 June 1906 has "Robicheau, B. H." at Cape St. Mary.[^5] A table for the year ended 31 March 1910 has "Benj. H. Robichaud" there.[^6] Lighthouse Friends uses "Robichau," and the spelling here follows it.[^2]

Lighthouse Friends lists Benjamin's successors as B. A. Melanson in 1931, then Marco Frontain from 1931 to 1957. It prints a photograph of Frontain in the lighthouse garden in 1950.[^2] William P. Spinney followed from 1959 to 1972.[^2] Between 1868 and 1988 twelve keepers served at the station, and one of them was a woman, Mary Goodwin. Lighthouse Friends lists Mary Louise Goodwin as head keeper in 1987.[^2]

## After the keepers

The light was automated on 31 March 1989, when the keepers left.[^2] The keeper's dwelling was moved in 2003 to Middle West Pubnico, where it waited years for restoration. In October 2009 it started its journey to a new home on Canoe Island, off Abbott's Harbour.[^2]

On 10 January 2017 the Municipality of Clare took possession of the light and 2.1 acres of land, under a federal divestiture process.[^2] The society lists the owner as the Municipality of the District of Clare.[^3] Lighthouse Friends still gives the Canadian Coast Guard as owner.[^2] The following summer the municipality made the site a park with a parking lot, interpretive panels, benches and picnic tables. The tower was covered in cedar shingles and painted.[^2] The register notes that the local government planned a four-season webcam and a monument to lost members of the fishery.[^1]

## Today

The park is open, and the tower is closed to visitors.[^2] The Coast Guard's list gives the light a white flash every five seconds, a focal height of 31.8 m and a range of 15 nautical miles. It operates around the clock, and the list calls it a "Private aid."[^4] The register says visitors come to watch the sunsets or to go bird-watching.[^1] The tower is a working aid to navigation, and nothing in the sources suggests it is open to climb.
