Discovery Island Light stands at Sea Bird Point, the eastern end of Discovery Island, two and a half miles off the southeast tip of Vancouver Island, where Haro Strait meets the Strait of Juan de Fuca.[^1][^7] It first showed a light in April 1886.[^1] Its keepers left in the 1990s, and the Canadian Coast Guard owns the light today.[^1]

## The place

Captain Henry Kellett named the island in 1846 after HMS *Discovery*, the lead ship of George Vancouver's expedition.[^1] An 1869 edition of the Coast Pilot said a lighthouse was needed on the island to mark the southwest side of the entrance to Haro Strait, a shipping lane that leads to the Inside Passage.[^1]

The point takes its name from the paddle steamer *Sea Bird*. On 7 September 1858 she caught fire near the island, bound from Victoria to the Fraser River with gold rush passengers, and her captain deliberately grounded her on the island's eastern point.[^1] She was eventually freed, and the name has stayed with the point ever since.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1885 a contract for $2,300 went to Alex Mennie, of Port Moody, for a wooden lighthouse on Sea Bird Point.[^1] It was a square pyramidal tower with a metal lantern, joined to a one-and-a-half-storey keeper's dwelling. The tower stood 12.5 m (41 ft) to the vane, and the fifth-order light showed from a focal plane of 27.7 m (91 ft).[^1]

George Brown was placed in temporary charge in October 1885, and the light was first exhibited on 8 April 1886.[^1] The Department of Marine's list of stations gives the Discovery Island appointment as 14 June 1886, at a salary of $900.[^2]

A steam fog alarm was tendered for in 1889 and began sounding on 1 July 1890. It gave eight-second blasts with a minute of silence between them. Its building stood about 91 m (300 ft) southeast of the tower, with the horn 14 m (45 ft) above the water.[^1]

In 1906 the old sixth-order light was replaced by a fourth-order lens. The Department reported that it was "showing over 360 degrees of the horizon with occulting screen operated by clock-work."[^3] The Chief Engineer's report, published in 1908, lists the retubing of the Discovery Island boiler at a cost of $105.[^4]

## The keepers

Richard Brinn was the first keeper, from 1886 until he died in office on 23 September 1901.[^1] His daughter, Mary Ann Croft, had been caring for the light during the final years of his life. Two days after his death she wrote to the Member of Parliament who would nominate a successor, saying that the island was like home to her and that her family depended entirely on the station.[^1]

The Department's list gives Mary Ann Croft's appointment as 1 April 1902, at a salary of $960.[^5] LighthouseFriends gives 9 April 1902, and calls her Canada's first female lightkeeper.[^1] Her work was physically hard. She stoked the steam fog signal, trimmed wicks, cleaned the lens and painted the buildings, while caring for her two daughters.[^1] In March 1911 she asked for her salary to be raised to $1,200, to match the keeper at Trial Island, and LighthouseFriends says she was soon paid it.[^1] In 1919 she wrote to her Member of Parliament about a pension after twenty-three years in the service. LighthouseFriends says the reply bore bad news: superannuation for keepers had been withdrawn in 1882, so she worked on until 31 August 1932.[^1]

LighthouseFriends lists the keepers after her, from Arnold Moran in 1932 to Patrick Mickey, whose term ends in 1997.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the night of 30 October 1892 the steamer *Premier* passed the island at about midnight in fog, carrying the marine agent James Gaudin.[^1] The station's fog signal was not sounding.[^1] Gaudin wrote a sharp letter to Keeper Brinn, and told him to sound the horn whenever he could not make out the lime kiln on San Juan Island across Haro Strait.[^1]

## After the keepers

In the 1970s a new, slender tower replaced the second light on the island. That light was a lantern on the roof of a two-storey keepers' dwelling. The dwelling, without its lantern, went on housing keepers until the station was de-staffed in January 1997.[^1]

A lantern room from an earlier light on the island has been restored. It can be seen on the grounds of the Canadian Coast Guard base on Huron Street in Victoria.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard List of Lights gives the light a single white flash every five seconds, a focal height of 28.3 metres, and a range of 15 nautical miles.[^6]

Most of Discovery Island is a provincial park.[^7] LighthouseFriends describes the island as a marine park open to visitors, with the grounds open and the tower closed, and says the light is best seen by boat.[^1] The Chance Heritage Trust notes that boats have no sheltered landing, and that most visitors arrive by sea kayak.[^7] Do not climb the tower, and do not try to land where there is no landing.
