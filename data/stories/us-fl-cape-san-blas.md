Cape San Blas Light stood for more than a century on a sandy point at the southern tip of Florida's St. Joseph peninsula, where a shoal ran five or six miles out from the cape. Four towers were built there between 1848 and 1885. The last of them, an iron skeleton tower, was laid on its side and carried about twelve miles (19 km) to a park in Port St. Joe in 2014, and it stands there now.[^1]

## The place

Ships on the northern Gulf coast of Florida had to get past the shoal that ran out from the cape, and the light was meant to mark it.[^1] The nomination gives the distance from Port St. Joe as 12.3 miles (20 km) in one section and about 14 miles (23 km) in another.[^1]

Local people kept asking for a light.[^1] A plan for a new tower at the cape took shape in 1842, but Captain L. Rousseau of the revenue cutter *Woodbury* called a light there "a useless expense", and the work waited nine years.[^1] In 1847 Congress appropriated $8,000 for a tower at the cape's elbow. Permission was given on condition that the old St. Joseph Bay light be taken down and its materials reused.[^1]

## Building the light

Edward Bowden of Franklin County won the contract for an 85-foot (26 m) tower.[^1][^10] Winslow Lewis, a Boston contractor, supplied the revolving lamp apparatus, which had ten lamps and fifteen-inch reflectors.[^1] The nomination dates completion to April 1848. The Florida Lighthouse Association describes that first tower as 65 feet (20 m) and brick, so the two sources disagree about its height.[^8]

In 1852 Congress appropriated $12,000 for a second brick tower. It was not lit until November 1855, when Keeper Joseph Ridler was transferred from Dog Island to keep it.[^1] In March 1857 Congress allocated $20,000 for a third brick tower. It was first lit on 1 May 1859 with a third-order Fresnel lens, and its light flashed every ninety seconds, at a focal plane of 96 feet (29 m).[^1]

The Lighthouse Board had recommended an iron skeleton tower in 1881, and Congress granted $35,000 for one in 1883.[^1] The tower was made in the North and shipped south. The ship went down on the way along Florida's west coast, but in shallow water, so most of the material could be salvaged.[^1] The Florida Lighthouse Association places the sinking off Sanibel Island.[^8]

The nomination's history says work on the tower and two keepers' dwellings began in September 1884 and was finished the following February, apart from the lens and lamp.[^1] The third-order lens was first lit on 30 June 1885. It showed alternating red and white flashes thirty seconds apart.[^1][^8] The tower had four iron legs braced together. Its central stair column stopped twenty feet (6 m) above the ground, so the door was reached by an outside metal stair. The nomination says this was meant to keep the entrance above any water that came onto the station.[^1]

The record disagrees about the dwellings. The nomination's history says the work on them was completed in February 1885. Its summary, however, dates the two wooden keepers' houses to 1905, and it notes a 1904 appropriation for new dwellings.[^1]

The sea kept coming. The tower had been set 1,500 feet (460 m) back from the shore. By 1887, 300 feet (91 m) of that buffer was gone, and by 1890 only 144 feet (44 m) of sand separated the tower from the breaking waves.[^1]

After the 1894 gale, Congress provided $20,000 for moving the tower. The first choice of site, on the inside of the cape, was dropped because the title to the land was hard to get, and the Board chose Black's Island in St. Joseph Bay. Work began in February 1896 and stopped on 30 April, when the money ran out.[^1][^3] A further $4,500 was appropriated in 1897 for "completing the removal".[^11] In 1899 the Board recommended rebuilding the light on the shell ridge, at a cost "not exceeding $15,000".[^4] The 1900 report gives $15,000 as appropriated "for completing the removal ... to a new and safe site".[^5] The nomination tells it differently. It says the beach then built up around the tower, the Board decided to leave the light where it was, and in 1904 the Board was allowed to use $7,000 of the $20,000 for two new keepers' dwellings.[^1] The two accounts disagree, and the record does not show which decision came last.

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists 84 entries for the station, from 1848 to 1962, under the headings its sources used.[^2] The first principal keeper it lists is Francis Avrion (1848 to 1849), followed by Rufus Ballard (1849 to 1851).[^2] The roster lists no principal keeper between 1861 and 1866.[^2]

Joseph Ridler, who came from Dog Island, was in charge when the second tower was lit.[^1][^2] During the Civil War the keeper's dwelling was destroyed, and the Confederate lighthouse superintendent had the lens taken out before Union forces could claim it.[^1] Congress did not fund a new dwelling until 15 July 1870, so the keeper lived in the tower's watch room in the meantime.[^1]

The roster lists a "Mrs. Merrill Hussey" as first assistant in 1869, the year Merrill Hussey's own stint as principal keeper ended. The archive gives her no first name and no other detail.[^2]

William M. Quinn was principal keeper from 1886 to 1895.[^2] In 1894 the gale wrecked the station, and the Lighthouse Board later recommended paying Quinn $124.75 for property he lost there.[^4][^6] His assistant, George L. Long, is on the roster as first assistant for 1894 to 1895.[^2] His losses in the hurricane of 8 October 1894, $75, were put forward for reimbursement.[^12]

James R. Linton was principal keeper from 1925 to 1933, according to the roster.[^2] The nomination says Linton found the isolation of the station hard to bear. It says that in 1933 he took his own life, and it cites a 2012 magazine article and the Florida Death Index for the year.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The first tower collapsed in a gale on 22 to 23 August 1851. The nomination says the same storm destroyed the lights at Cape St. George and Dog Island.[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives the gale as 23 to 24 August.[^10]

On 30 August 1856 another hurricane struck the cape. The nomination quotes a Lighthouse Board report describing waves that struck the floor of the keeper's dwelling, and it says "a lagoon" now occupied the site of the tower.[^1]

By 1881 the Board reported that "the sea has been encroaching on this tower until its base is in the water," and that the brush mattresses laid to hold back the sand were torn to pieces by the surf.[^1]

On 3 July 1882 the brick tower fell. The nomination quotes a history of lighthouses: the "handsome brick tower became undermined, leaned, then cracked".[^1] Two months later a hurricane destroyed the station's dwelling.[^1]

The Lighthouse Board's notice on the 1894 gale ran in *The Morning News* of Savannah. It said the Cape San Blas light had been wrecked in the recent gale and was discontinued. "The tower is still standing, but badly undermined, and both keepers dwellings have been washed away."[^6] The nomination gives the gale as 8 to 9 October 1894.[^1]

The nomination says a hurricane in 1916 stripped away the beach in front of the tower, and another storm the next year left it 120 feet (37 m) offshore.[^1]

In May 1920 a barge was lost off Cape San Blas. The 1,084-ton wooden barge *Holliswood* broke loose in a storm on the Sunday night, and its crew was taken off by the tug *Jim-Sid*, which reached Tampa on 24 May. The report does not mention the light.[^7]

Two years after the light was deactivated, Hurricane Earl damaged the dwelling nearest the shore. In August 2012 Hurricane Isaac left a fifty-foot (15 m) buffer between the dwellings and the Gulf.[^1]

## After the keepers

The tower was moved in 1918, when it was shifted a quarter mile (400 m) inland. The light was temporarily discontinued on 30 April 1918, and shown from its new site on 22 January 1919.[^1]

The cape took on other uses. A LORAN radio station was built near the light in 1952. In the later 1950s the Air Force set up a radar test site on most of the rest of the cape.[^1]

The record disagrees on when the light was automated. The nomination says the Coast Guard automated it in 1981, after the LORAN station was deactivated. The Florida Lighthouse Association gives 1972, and says the tower was electrified in 1938.[^1][^8]

The light was deactivated in 1996. The Florida Lighthouse Association gives 18 January 1996.[^1][^8] The nomination also says operations at the lighthouse were shut down on 14 October 2012, and it does not reconcile that date with 1996.[^1]

The keepers' dwellings had been left unattended for years.[^1] In 1999 both were moved beside the tower. One was restored that year and the second in 2005.[^1] In December 2012 movers shifted the dwellings and the oil house 100 feet (30 m) inland as a temporary measure.[^1]

On 21 December 2012 the National Park Service told the City of Port St. Joe that it had been awarded the lighthouse. The General Services Administration still had to make the actual transfer.[^1] In January 2014 the lens was removed, and in April the city chose GAC Contracting to move the tower and dwellings for $510,450.[^1]

On 15 July 2014 the tower, the two dwellings and the oil house went about twelve miles to Port St. Joe. The nomination says hundreds of people lined the road to cheer and take pictures.[^1] The lighthouse opened to the public on 12 September 2014.[^1]

## Today

The tower stands in George Core Park, in Port St. Joe, on St. Joe Bay. The city created the park as the lighthouse's new home.[^1] The Florida Lighthouse Association's page still lists Eglin Air Force Base as the operator, which does not match the nomination's account of the city's award.[^1][^8]

The light is no longer working. It was deactivated in 1996.[^1]

Visitors can climb the tower when it is open. A visitor guide gives opening hours of Wednesday to Saturday, 11 a.m. to 5 p.m., a fee of $5 for adults to climb, and no fee for the grounds or the gift shop. It also says the tower may close to climbers in bad weather. The Florida Lighthouse Association's page gives climbing days as Friday and Saturday only, which does not match the guide.[^8] Check the lighthouse's own website before you go, because the guide's hours may have changed.[^9][^13]

The tower stood on the cape until 2014. The nomination does not describe what, if anything, remains at the cape site.[^1]
