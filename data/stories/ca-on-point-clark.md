A white limestone tower with a red, twelve-sided lantern rises from the Lake Huron shore at Point Clark, Ontario. It has marked a shoal off this coast since the spring of 1859 and is one of six "Imperial" towers the Province of Canada built along Lake Huron and Georgian Bay.[^1][^2][^5]

## The place

Parks Canada says the light was built to warn sailors of a shoal about three kilometres offshore.[^2] Lighthouse Friends adds a dangerous reef running out from the point, and a reason for the building campaign: with the St. Marys Falls ship canal at Sault Ste. Marie nearly finished, officials expected far more vessels on Lake Huron and called at Georgian Bay ports, so the province authorized six major lighthouses.[^1] The federal heritage statement ties the same campaign to the opening of the Bruce Peninsula for settlement and an 1854 trade agreement with the United States.[^3] Before it was called Point Clark, after settlers from Clark Township near Toronto, the point was reportedly known as Pine Point, supposedly because a lantern hung from a pine limb there.[^1]

## Building the light

The tower was built between 1855 and 1859 for the Department of Public Works by the contractor John Brown, of Thorold.[^1][^3] Cut stone for the tower and dwelling was prepared in 1856. By the end of 1857 the tower was nearly at full height, and the work was done in 1858, so the light could be shown when navigation opened in spring 1859.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the Imperial towers ran late because Brown lost two steamers, a schooner and two scows, with timber and supplies, during the work.[^1]

The walls taper from 1.5 m (5 ft) thick at the base to 0.6 m (2 ft) at the lantern. A revolving second-order Fresnel lens, imported from France, went into the lantern, and granite was used for the top courses to carry the heavy lantern room.[^1] The light was first lit on 1 April 1859, and its flash set it apart from the fixed lights at Goderich to the south and Chantry Island to the north.[^1] The tower is usually described as 26.5 m (87 ft) tall.[^2][^3] Parks Canada's visitor page gives 24 m (78 ft), and a Government of Canada backgrounder gives 27 m, so sources differ on the height.[^4][^5]

## The keepers

John Young, hired at fifty-five, kept the light until he was seventy-eight, in 1882, on a salary of $435 a year. His work included keeping the lamp burning through the night and winding the weights that turned the lens. Oil was spermaceti at first; colza oil replaced it in 1860 because it cost less and stayed fluid in colder weather.[^1]

David Small followed Young but soon resigned, and John Rae was appointed on 4 June 1883. In 1891 the collector of customs at Kincardine investigated charges of drunkenness against Rae and judged them false. On 27 November 1896 Rae was dismissed after it was established that, in the words of the record Lighthouse Friends quotes, "owing to drinking habits" he had not given the light proper attention.[^1] Lighthouse Friends lists Murdock McDonald as head keeper from 1897 to 1914 and George H. Ray from 1914 to 1924.[^1] The Marine branch's list of keepers in its report for 1901, printed in 1902, shows a Murdoch McDonald at Point Clark.[^6]

In the Marine branch's report for the fiscal year 1908-09 a 55 mm Diamond vapour lamp on an iron stand replaced the duplex lamp then in use.[^7] In the fiscal year 1917-18 the department spent money on pointing the stone tower, painting it with Toxement and repairing the lantern.[^8]

## After the keepers

Point Clark was automated after the 1924 shipping season, but mariners complained about the quality and reliability of the light, and John Ruttle was hired as keeper in July 1926.[^1] After Ruttle came John A. Campbell (1938 to 1946) and John C. Campbell (1947 to 1962), the last official keeper.[^1] A Government of Canada backgrounder says a keeper occupied the site until the mid-1960s, when the light was automated, and that an electric motor replaced the revolving mechanism in 1953; Lighthouse Friends instead dates final automation to 1962 and says paid caretakers lived in the dwelling from 1963 to 1967.[^5][^1]

The lighthouse was declared a national historic site in 1966, the first Ontario lighthouse so honoured, and Parks Canada acquired it in 1967. The Canadian Coast Guard kept responsibility for the light itself.[^1] A museum opened in the keeper's dwelling on 18 June 1988 under an agreement with Huron Township.[^1] In 2010 deteriorated stone on the tower was judged a safety hazard and the lighthouse closed. Restoration began in 2011; when the old surface was sandblasted off, workers found four significant cracks rather than the one known crack, and the original mortar behind a modern layer had no strength. A contract of up to $1.7 million was announced on 10 September 2013 to finish the work, and the tower and museum reopened in June 2015.[^1] The tower was also designated a heritage lighthouse under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act.[^5]

## Today

The tower still shows a white flash every ten seconds as an aid to navigation.[^1][^2] It is federally owned; Parks Canada protects it and the Township of Huron-Kinloss presents it to visitors, with the keeper's house operating as a museum.[^2][^5] Parks Canada's page (updated June 2025) lists it as closed for the winter and gives the address as Lighthouse Road, Point Clark.[^4] Check with the township for current hours before visiting.
