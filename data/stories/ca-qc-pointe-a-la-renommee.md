Pointe-à-la-Renommée Lighthouse, which English-speaking sailors knew as Fame Point, stands on a cliff at the entrance to the Gulf of St. Lawrence, a few kilometres west of the village of L'Anse-à-Valleau on Quebec's Gaspé coast.[^1][^2][^3][^5] The tower standing there now dates from 1907 and is the second lighthouse on the point.[^3][^17] It replaced a wooden tower of 1880 that was built onto the keeper's house.[^1][^3] The 1907 tower was taken down in the late 1970s, moved to Quebec City, and returned to the point in 1997.[^3][^4]

## The place

The name has more than one explanation. Lighthouse Friends gives two stories. In one, many survivors of a shipwreck died of starvation, and the point was called Pointe-à-la-Faim (Point Hunger), which English speakers translated as Fame Point.[^1] In the other, the name comes from *La Renommée*, a vessel wrecked off Anticosti in 1736. Lighthouse Friends says its owner was from La Rochelle.[^1] The station's own history page joins the two stories, and says the name was turned back into French later.[^4] The Amis des phares du Saint-Laurent's 2017 bulletin disagrees. It says that no map or document it found uses the name Pointe-à-la-Faim, that the first map it found with "Fame Pt." is Alexander Keith Johnston's of 1861, and that it found no evidence that a ship called *Fame* was wrecked there.[^5]

The point was chosen to make navigation safer between the lights at Cap-des-Rosiers (1858) and Rivière-la-Madeleine (1876), and in the Honguedo strait, which separates Anticosti from the Gaspé coast.[^6] Fallu says the series of lights on this coast followed many wrecks along the Gaspé shore.[^3]

Until 1925 the point could be reached only by sea.[^5] From the 1930s to the 1960s fishermen from the nearby villages used three small camps on the point from April to November.[^5][^7] The men fished cod, and the women and children dried the fish and worked the land.[^7] The bulletin calls it the last seasonal fishing post on the Gaspé coast.[^7] It also says that since 1964 "les vaches, les pissenlits et le silence sont les seuls habitants de Pointe-à-la-Renommée" (the cows, the dandelions and the silence are the only inhabitants of Pointe-à-la-Renommée; translation by this story).[^7]

## Building the light

Parliament voted $4,000 for the light, and Lighthouse Friends dates the vote to 1879.[^1][^3] The contractor was R. Cameron of Lancaster, Ontario.[^1][^3] The first tower was square and wooden, about 15 m (50 ft) tall, and joined to the keeper's dwelling.[^1] Fallu says that arrangement is why the building is called a *maison-phare*, a lighthouse house.[^3] Rowlett gives the tower a height of 11 m (36 ft). The sources do not explain the difference.[^2]

The light was first shown in October 1880, and the station's history page gives 1 October.[^1][^4] Lighthouse Friends describes it as a fixed white light with red flashes every twenty seconds, at a focal height of 61 m (200 ft), with a range of 32 km (twenty miles).[^1] The tower was painted white with a black band.[^12]

On 1 July 1903 the tower was painted bright red. The Department's report calls this "an experiment", to find out whether the building would be more visible than when it was white.[^12]

A fog signal was added in 1902. The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 says work had started on the foundations of a fog-alarm building.[^9] A Hamilton-Foster siren went into service on 8 October 1902, in a small white wooden building with a flat roof, 55 m (180 ft) northwest of the lighthouse, with the siren 46 m (150 ft) above high water.[^11] It sounded through four megaphones pointed north, north-east, east and south-east, each with its own sequence of short and long blasts.[^11] Lighthouse Friends reads the arrangement as an experiment to let a mariner work out his bearing from which blast was loudest.[^1] A new fog-alarm building with diaphone machinery followed.[^14] The report for fiscal 1905 describes a brick stack 15 m (50 ft) high and a coal shed holding 212 tons.[^14] The report for fiscal 1907 gives the total cost of the building as $6,356.26.[^15]

A wireless station was built on the point in 1904. The Department's report says it was finished on 25 June 1904, and that on that date it held communication with the Allan Line steamer *Parisian*, outward bound.[^13] The station's history page says it was the first maritime radiotelegraph station in North America.[^4] Fallu quotes the station's log book for the contact: "In communication with Parisian bound east, 23 miles west."[^21] The log book's title, as Fallu's note gives it, begins on 20 July 1904, which is after the June date in the report.[^13][^21] The date of that entry is therefore not settled.

A second tower replaced the first. The Department's report for fiscal 1908 says the new tower went into service on 1 October 1907, when the light in the old tower was discontinued.[^17] It is a cylindrical iron tower with a circular iron lantern, painted red, about 15 m (49 ft) from its base to the vane. Its light is first order and dioptric, burning acetylene under an incandescent mantle, 58 m (190 ft) above high water, and visible twenty miles from all points of approach by water.[^17] The report for fiscal 1907 says a first-order double flashing light went into operation at the point that year.[^16] The tower was bought from Ives & Company of Montreal for $1,597 and erected by day labour for $3,046.85.[^17] Lighthouse Friends describes the light as a group of two white flashes every ten seconds.[^1]

## The keepers

James Ascah was the first keeper. The Department's keepers' table for the year ending June 1900 lists him for Fame Point, Gaspé County, with an appointment date of 2 September 1880.[^8] The Musée de la Gaspésie gives the same date, and says he was also the telegraph operator and signal man.[^18] Lighthouse Friends and Fallu give his service as 1880 to 1913.[^1][^3] The Musée's archive gives his life dates as 1830 to 1915 and says that on his death his son Bert Ascah (1884 to 1963) took over as keeper.[^19] The sources therefore disagree about when James's service ended.[^1][^19]

The Ascah family kept the station for generations. Fallu describes a dynasty of keepers that ran the lighthouse, the telegraph, the signal mast and the post office from 1880 to 1943.[^3][^23] Lighthouse Friends lists Hubert Leigh Ascah as keeper from 1913 to at least 1936, and says that a new keeper's dwelling was built in 1926 while he was keeper.[^1] The 1914 report's table has the printed row "Fame point, light and fog alarm", and on that row the keeper is H. L. Ascah, given only as initials. Those initials fit Hubert Leigh Ascah, though the report does not print his full name.[^22]

Yvon Élément, who left his post in 1975, is named by Fallu and the bulletin as the last keeper.[^3][^23]

In 1904 the Marconi company sent an engineer, Joseph Barridon, and an assistant, Hugh Lyle, to build the radio station. Fallu says the keeper met them on arrival, and that the two men announced they had come to build a "station de sans-fil" (wireless station).[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Early on 12 July 1902 the steamer *Monteagle*, bound for Montreal from Bristol, ran onto Serpent Reef, which the inquiry places seven miles east of Fame Point, at about 2.35 a.m.[^10] The Department's inquiry said the Fame Point light was "clear and distinct" that night.[^10] It concluded that the second mate must have altered the course "to almost straight ashore".[^10] It also said that the reef buoy had been washed away earlier and would not have prevented the grounding had it been in place.[^10] The steamer came off on 17 July, went to Montreal, discharged its cargo, and sailed for New York to be repaired.[^10]

In the First World War the Canadian army set up a camp and huts on the point, and important controls were put on the exchange of information there.[^3]

On the night of 11 to 12 May 1942 the British freighter *Nicoya* was torpedoed by U-553 off Cloridorme.[^20] Patrick Mathurin's 2015 article says six crew members died.[^20] Boats from L'Anse-à-Valleau towed the lifeboat to the beach, and villagers took the survivors up the bank in a horse-drawn cart. The village's women cared for the wounded.[^20] Fallu says that on 12 May the military learned of the torpedoing through the station at Pointe-à-la-Renommée, and that nearly fifty survivors were rescued by people from L'Anse-à-Valleau and Cloridorme.[^3] Mathurin's footnote says that the keeper's report counted 49 survivors, and that the keeper had also picked up the ship's distress call the day before.[^20]

## After the keepers

Radio operations on the point ended in 1957, when they moved 26 km east to Rivière-au-Renard.[^3] The light was closed for good in 1975.[^3][^1] The Coast Guard offered the site to the Société historique de la Gaspésie, which declined the invitation to acquire and manage it. The tower had been vandalised after it was left abandoned.[^3][^23]

Sources disagree on when the Coast Guard took the tower down and moved it to Quebec City. Rowlett, the station's history page and the bulletin say 1977; Fallu says 1978.[^2][^4][^23][^3] It was reassembled in 1981 in front of the Coast Guard's administrative headquarters in Quebec's Old Port.[^1][^3]

Local protests against the move ran from 1981 to 1992.[^24] In 1992 the local development committee of L'Anse-à-Valleau took up the campaign. Fallu says that, for the women who led the committee, only the tower's return could revive the local economy.[^24][^3] The tower came back to its site on 3 November 1997.[^3][^1] The Marconi station was rebuilt in 1998, and in 2001 a keeper's house was rebuilt to the outside appearance of the first lighthouse.[^3][^1] The City of Gaspé cited the site as a municipal historic monument in 2010.[^3] In 2012 the Commission des lieux et monuments historiques du Canada commemorated the national historic importance of the creation of the maritime radiotelegraph system here.[^3][^4]

## Today

The local development committee of L'Anse-à-Valleau owns the lighthouse.[^1][^2] The station's history page describes the site as a historic and museum site.[^4] Lighthouse Friends gives opening hours of 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. from mid-June through September, with the tower open in season. Rowlett says the tower takes guided tours daily from early June to mid-October. Check the season before going.[^1][^2] The last stretch is a dirt road with a steep grade, 4.3 km (2.7 miles) long, that leaves Route 132 west of L'Anse-à-Valleau.[^1] The history page says visitors can learn about Marconi, the radio operators and the optical signal keepers, and see the equipment used for communications in Marconi's time.[^4]

The Coast Guard's own light stands nearby, on a 31 m (103 ft) skeletal tower that Rowlett lists as active.[^2]
