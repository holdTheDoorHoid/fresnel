The Entry Island light stands on the south-west side of Île d'Entrée, the first island most visitors see from the ferry to the Magdalen Islands, in the Gulf of St. Lawrence.[^1][^3] The present tower is a 14.5 m (48 ft) concrete tower built in 1969, the third light on the site.[^3][^1] It was automated in 1988[^3] and has been dark since, though the sources differ on the year it went out. The local heritage corporation gives 2011, and the Rowlett directory gives 2015.[^11][^13]

## The place

Entry Island is pentagon-shaped and treeless, and it is the only inhabited island of the archipelago that is not joined to another. Its people are mostly descended from Scottish settlers, and they speak English.[^1]

The Magdalen Islands lights were part of a government building programme. The Quebec record dates the first phase of lights in the islands to 1870 to 1874, and describes the gulf as one where wrecks were frequent.[^3] Both heritage records say more than 700 wrecks are recorded in the islands over the years. That figure covers the archipelago, not this light.[^2][^3]

The sources disagree on where the first light stood. LighthouseFriends puts it on the south-east side of the island; the local heritage corporation puts it on Wash Pond Hill.[^1][^11]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends dates the first light to 1873 and gives its cost as $5,818. The Quebec record and the local heritage corporation date its construction to 1874.[^1][^3][^4][^11] It was a square wooden tower attached to a keeper's dwelling, 8.5 m (28 ft) tall, showing a fixed red light from a bluff that gave a focal plane of 27 m (90 ft). LighthouseFriends says it could be seen for up to about 10 nautical miles (12 statute miles in the source).[^1] James Cassidy, paid $250 a year, lit it for the first time on 20 April 1874.[^1]

In 1890 two Marine Department men, William P. Anderson, an engineer, and William H. Smith, chairman of the board that examined masters and mates, wrote that the lights of the islands were badly sited. The Entry Island light sat on a low point, and higher land cut off its view to the east. They recommended its removal if it had to be rebuilt. LighthouseFriends prints their report, which also mentions a dispute with a Mr. Dixon over the right of way.[^1]

The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1905 records the move. The old light was discontinued and its tower torn down. A new octagonal wooden tower went up on a steep hill about 1.2 km (three-quarters of a mile) away, on ground 76 m (250 ft) above high water. Its fixed white light was 84 m (277 ft) up and visible for about 14 nautical miles (16 statute miles in the source). The work, which included a new dwelling to replace the old one, cost $5,539.85.[^5][^1]

The local corporation tells the story differently. It says the first light was moved to Split Rock in 1904, and later to its present site because of erosion.[^11] The 1905 report is the primary record; the corporation's version has not been checked against another source.

The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1909 records the change to a fourth-order occulting light, which LighthouseFriends dates to 1908.[^8][^1] The report says it was "visible for four seconds and eclipsed for six seconds alternately," and burned petroleum vapour under an incandescent mantle.[^8]

## The keepers

In 1883 the writer S. G. W. Benjamin visited the light. LighthouseFriends prints his account of Cassidy's house, which adjoined the tower, and says the tables held plenty of books and magazines. Mrs. Cassidy, he wrote, had had to send her children to Nova Scotia for their schooling.[^1]

The keeper lists disagree about the Cassidy and Collins years. LighthouseFriends has James Cassidy in charge from 1874 to 1901 and George F. Collins from 1901 to 1930. The local corporation has James Cassidy from 1874 to 1887, Robert Cassidy from 1887 to 1888, James again from 1888 to 1901, Leo Collins from 1901 to 1905 and George Collins from 1905 to 1930.[^1][^11] The Marine Department's keeper tables for 1904 and 1906 both list "Collins, Geo. F" at Entry Island. That fits LighthouseFriends's account of George F. Collins from 1901, not the local list, which gives Leo Collins as keeper until 1905.[^6][^7]

LighthouseFriends has Daniel K. McLean as keeper from 1931 to 1958. Asked whether the fog alarm bothered him, he answered: "The only time I heard the siren was once when it wasn't sounding."[^1] LighthouseFriends tells how, on a foggy night, McLean slept while his assistant stood watch, and the foghorn missed a blast, so McLean woke at once. At one point the house held his wife, eight children and two assistants.[^1]

John R. McLean was keeper until 1988, when the light was automated and the resident keepers on the island ended.[^1][^12]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Quebec records say lightning destroyed the first tower in 1908, and that a second light was built two years later.[^3][^4] The local corporation calls it an electrical storm.[^11] LighthouseFriends has the lantern moved from the 1905 tower in 1910, and it does not mention a fire, so the sources do not agree.[^1]

In the year ended 30 June 1911 a life-saving station was set up at Entry Island, equipped with a self-bailing surf boat and a volunteer crew.[^9]

## After the keepers

The Department's report for the fiscal year 1922–23 said that a new fog alarm station at Entry Island would be in operation early the next season.[^10] LighthouseFriends puts the fog alarm's first service in July 1923.[^1]

In 1927 a new square wooden lighthouse, with a lantern room, was built near the fog-signal building, and the light on the hill was discontinued.[^1]

The present concrete tower was built in 1969. Transport Canada engineers designed it, and the Quebec records say it is the only concrete lighthouse in the archipelago and hexagonal in plan.[^3][^4] LighthouseFriends calls it an octagonal concrete tower 14.5 m (48 ft) tall, and the Rowlett directory calls it an octagonal wooden tower.[^1][^13] The Quebec record and the local heritage corporation both say Prime Minister Pierre Elliott Trudeau visited the light in 1970.[^3][^11]

The 1927 lighthouse was sold in 1982 to a private buyer, who tore it down and used the lumber to build a house on the island.[^1] The Quebec record says the other buildings on the site were reportedly sold to a private owner in 1993.[^4] The lighthouse was designated a heritage building by the municipality in 2006.[^4]

## Today

The tower stands inside a fence, and the Quebec record says access to the site is forbidden to visitors.[^3][^4] Do not enter the enclosed site. LighthouseFriends gives ferry times and directions to the lighthouse, while Rowlett says the site is open and the tower closed.[^1][^13] The sources disagree on access, so the safe course is to view the tower from the ferry.

The ferry brings visitors past the light: the Les amis des phares page says it is the first light visitors see on arriving by ferry.[^12] LighthouseFriends says the 1905 keeper's dwelling was relocated and remained standing in 2013, though Rowlett describes a 1905 keeper's house as falling into ruins.[^1][^13] The Canadian Coast Guard is named as owner or site manager.[^13]
