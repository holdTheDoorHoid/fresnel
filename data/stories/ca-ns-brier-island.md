Brier Island light, known locally as the Western Light, stands at the west point of Brier Island, which Lighthouse Friends calls the westernmost point in Nova Scotia.[^1][^2] A light has been shown there since 1809.[^1][^2] The tower standing now is a concrete octagon built in 1944, after a fire destroyed the wooden tower that had replaced the first one in the 1830s.[^1][^2]

## The place

The hazard lies offshore. The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society's list describes Gull Rock bars, Southwest Ledge and a heavy tide rip running out from Gull Rock, and says the light is set against that hazard.[^2]

Lighthouse Friends quotes a statement in the House of Assembly at Halifax that a light on Brier Island would be of the highest utility to navigation of the Bay of Fundy.[^1] In 1806 £500 was requested to build a stone lighthouse on Brier Island. The next year the legislature instead appropriated £200 for a wooden one, and New Brunswick contributed £200 towards it, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1]

## Building the light

Isaiah Shaw was the contractor.[^1] An inspector who examined the work declared that "in point of strength, and security against storms, there is no defect in the framing and workmanship."[^1] When the light was placed in operation in 1809, Lighthouse Friends says, it was the fourth lighthouse built by the government of Nova Scotia.[^1] The NSLPS calls it the first lighthouse on the south-west coast of the province.[^2]

The NSLPS says that in 1818 the tower was described as "so vilely constructed and ill lighted" that it was a hazard, and that it had been rebuilt by 1832.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives 1834 and a cost of £489 for the replacement.[^1] In 1830 a House committee reported that the timber was "in such a state of decay that they feared it would fall before the end of another year."[^1] The sources differ by two years on the rebuilding, and both dates are given here.

A steam fog whistle was set up on the south side of the tower on 1 March 1873.[^1] On 1 November 1902 the light was changed from a fixed white light to a group-revolving catoptric white light, the Department's annual report says.[^4]

A separate light on the north point of the island, marking the north entrance to Grand Passage, had been put in operation on 12 January 1901.[^5]

Keepers' dwellings were built at the two light stations on the island in 1905. The Department's report says the contract price was $3,150.[^6][^1]

Lighthouse Friends says a new fog-alarm building with a three-inch diaphone went up in 1907.[^1] The Department's report for 1908-09 found that the diaphone worked better when it was placed above the building, on a level with the whistle, and that the room was extended up to encase it.[^10] Shortly after the fog building was finished, Lighthouse Friends says, the tower was given three red horizontal bands. The Department's report for 1907-08 lists Brier Island among the lighthouses repainted with white and red bands, so they would show up better when snow was on the ground.[^1][^9]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends gives the early keepers as J. T. Hughes, for at least 1811 to 1812, and John Suthern, who kept the light from 1820 until his death in 1867. Suthern had served in Admiral Nelson's fleet during the period that included the Battle of Trafalgar, and was granted the post as a pensioner of the Royal Navy.[^1] His grandson Joshua Slocum, whose boyhood home was on Brier Island, later became the first person to sail single-handed around the world.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends' keeper list gives Joseph Suthern from 1867 to 1883, and Joseph E. Bancroft from 1884 to 1901. Its text, however, credits a son, Frank Suthern, with the fog whistle from 1874. The sources do not settle how the Suthern family shared the work.[^1]

John N. Peters kept the light from 1901 to 1922.[^1] The Department's keepers' table in its 1907 report lists him at the Brier Island light.[^7] Braddish H. Morrell took charge of the fog whistle in 1901 and was still there at least in 1937, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] The Department's table of 1905 lists "Brier Island, Fog Whistle" against his name.[^8]

Albert L. Welch kept the light from 1922 to 1961.[^1] In 1966 Gilbert Ingersoll was appointed principal keeper, with two assistants. One assistant post ended in 1969 and the other in 1979, leaving him alone at the light until it was unmanned in 1987.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 16 March 1912, in a heavy gale, the steamer Bona Vista was driven ashore on Brier Island. She was a total loss, and no lives were lost.[^11] The source does not say where on the island she came ashore.

In February 1944 the old wooden tower burned. Lighthouse Friends quotes a newspaper of the next day, which called it the "135 year old Western Light" and said the light was reported to have gone out at 10:10 on the evening of the fire. The fire's origin was not known, and the tall tower collapsed within a short time.[^1] The keeper, named in the newspaper as Albert Walsh (Welch), and his assistant, Murray Pugh, were aided by men and boys from Westport in saving the fog-alarm station, according to the same account.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends also quotes a former assistant keeper, Wickerson Lent, who told an interviewer in 2001 that the fog horn "was one of the most powerful on the coast."[^1] Lighthouse Friends' reference list dates that interview to 2002.

## After the keepers

A concrete octagonal tower was built on the site in 1944. Lighthouse Friends gives its height as 18.3 m and the NSLPS as 60 ft, while the Coast Guard list gives 16.7 m for the tower.[^1][^2][^3] The NSLPS says the light was automated by 1987.[^2] From 1922 to 1941 a third-order dioptric lens showed a group flashing white light every 15 seconds, with an air diaphone.[^2] In 1992 the light received a fourth-order dioptric lens showing a flashing white light every 18 seconds, with a fog signal.[^2]

In December 2021 the Government of Canada designated Brier Island Lighthouse a heritage lighthouse under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act.[^1]

## Today

The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1] Visitors take a gravel road for 3.6 kilometres (2.3 miles) to a gate, then walk a short distance to the light.[^1] The Coast Guard lists the light as a group of three white flashes every 18 seconds.[^3] The sources disagree on who owns it. The NSLPS names the Municipality of the District of Digby, while Lighthouse Friends says the Canadian Coast Guard owns it.[^2][^1]
