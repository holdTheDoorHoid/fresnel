Waukegan Harbor Light is an iron tower at the end of the south pier, where the harbor opens onto Lake Michigan.[^1][^5] Its light was first shown on 31 August 1899, and the tower still displays an occulting green light for mariners.[^2][^1]

## The place

Waukegan started as a trading post and settlement known as Little Fort, and its first light stood on a bluff. Congress provided $4,000 for it in 1847, and two years later a brick tower about 35 feet (11 m) tall and a six-room keeper's dwelling were completed.[^1] Truman Hibbard was hired in 1849 to serve as the light's first keeper.[^1][^3]

The harbor works set the timetable. Two piers begun in 1881 to enclose a basin took nearly two decades to build, and they reached their full lengths in 1898.[^1] The bluff light was replaced by a pier light, and the Society's record gives 1899 as the year it was discontinued.[^3]

## Building the light

The new light was first exhibited from a temporary iron post at the outer end of the north pier, on 10 August 1898.[^4] On 31 December 1898 the fifth-order light on the bluff was discontinued.[^4][^1]

The Board's 1899 report describes a contract for a permanent metal tower for the north pier, but the concrete foundation it reports was made at the end of the south pier.[^4] The tower was erected 36 feet (11 m) from the outer end of the south pier. Its fourth-order lens, fixed white for twenty seconds with four red flashes, was first shown on 31 August 1899, and the old lantern at the north pier was discontinued.[^2] The bluff reservation was sold at auction to the city of Waukegan on 20 June 1899.[^1] An elevated walk 400 feet (120 m) long, with a lifeline, ran out along the pier to the tower.[^2]

In 1905 the tower was moved to the end of the pier, which had been extended some 1,400 feet (430 m), and the light was relit there on 7 February 1905.[^1][^5] A two-story fog-signal building was attached in 1906.[^1] Pepper reports that the sirens ran 442 hours in that first year.[^5] The two accounts disagree about the signal itself: LighthouseFriends describes a fog horn driven by two mineral-oil engines, while Pepper describes sirens powered by compressed air from diesel compressors.[^1][^5] The keepers' brick double dwelling on shore was built in 1909.[^1][^6] The light atop the tower was electrified in 1939, and a new fog signal was installed.[^1]

## The keepers

Peter Dues was the pier light's first head keeper, from 1899 to 1902.[^6] His successor, Frederick W. Raether, was head keeper from 1902 to 1907.[^6] LighthouseFriends reports that in December 1907 Raether slipped on the icy pier and fell into the lake, and that his body was not found until a violent storm stirred the water more than a year later.[^1]

Emil C. Tews served as an assistant keeper here from 1909 to 1915.[^6][^1] His rescue is in the next chapter.

The Society's roster lists 46 stints at the pierhead, from 1899 to 1969.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 9 November 1913 a storm was blowing on Lake Michigan.[^7] Wallace R. Sunderlin, a 35-year-old steelworker, had gone out to retrieve a duck he had shot, and when he broke an oar the wind carried his boat away.[^7] The wind was blowing 35 miles an hour, and waves of about 13 feet (4 m) ran across it.[^7] A tug went to help, but its engine would not work, and the tug was thrown twice against the pier.[^7] Tews, the assistant keeper, heard of the distress, took a small rowboat, and rowed about a mile to reach Sunderlin.[^7] Sunderlin's boat swamped, and he climbed into Tews's boat. Both men were nearly exhausted when they reached the pier after an hour and a half, their clothes covered with ice.[^7]

The Lighthouse Service's bulletin recorded the rescue as Tews saving "a duck hunter" from probable drowning.[^8] Tews had resigned from the service before he received his Carnegie medal in 1915.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he found it hard to support a wife and five children on $49 a month.[^1]

On 1 June 1967 a fire broke out in the fog-signal building and nearly destroyed it and the lantern room of the tower.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives an electrical short as the cause.[^1] Pepper says the cause remains undetermined.[^5]

## After the keepers

The station was officially disestablished on 1 September 1969, after the last keepers left.[^1] Pepper says radar, radio beacons and Loran-C had made the station virtually obsolete by the 1960s.[^5] After the 1967 fire the lantern and the fog building were removed, and the tower was closed off with a flat steel cover and topped with a green acrylic lens.[^5]

## Today

The upper part of the 1899 tower is painted green, and an occulting green light is displayed for mariners.[^1] Pepper notes the bright green painted belly band.[^5] The Army Corps of Engineers completed a renovation of the lighthouse pier in 2019, making it wheelchair accessible.[^1] Pepper's directions say to park near Madison Street and walk out the pier to the light.[^5] The 1909 keepers' dwelling still stands on the corner of Madison Street and Harbor Place, and has recently been home to a deli and a bait and tackle shop.[^1] The present owner of the tower is not recorded.
