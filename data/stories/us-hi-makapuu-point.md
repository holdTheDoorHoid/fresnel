Makapuu Point is the south-eastern corner of Oahu, the headland that ships from the American west coast passed on their way to Honolulu. A short tower set into its lava cliff has long held one of the largest lenses ever used in an American lighthouse.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Makapuu Point is the easternmost tip of Oahu, on the Kaiwi Channel, which runs between Oahu and Molokai. Vessels from Puget Sound, San Francisco, Mexico and Panama bound for Honolulu passed through that channel and by the point, and a report to Congress in January 1906 said it was often their first sight of land after a voyage of several thousand miles.[^2] Putnam's 1917 book on American lighthouses quotes an earlier report saying that before the light there was "not a single light on the whole northern coast of the Hawaiian Islands to guide ships or warn them of their approach to land."[^3]

Sea captains and ship owners petitioned the Hawaiian government for a light in 1888, after the American ship *S.N. Castle* ran aground in the area.[^2] Some planning had been done by 1901, but it was dropped when the territorial government learned that the U.S. Government would soon take over the islands' navigational aids.[^2] On 10 June 1906 Congress appropriated $60,000 for a lighthouse, and on 20 August that year the liner *Manchuria* went aground; a Honolulu newspaper retrospective puts her on the reef 1,500 feet off Waimanalo and about 15 miles off course, while Lighthouse Friends says she ran aground off Makapuu Point.[^2][^13] Both say she was held for about a month before being pulled off.[^2][^13]

## Building the light

John R. Slattery, a West Point graduate sent to Hawaii in 1904 to oversee harbor and navigation improvements, was told to draw up the plans in August 1906. He weighed Makapuu Point against nearby Rabbit Island, chose the point, and designed a short tower to keep the light as low as possible, though still high enough that wind-blown pebbles would not reach the lantern glass.[^2] Work began in the summer of 1907, a notch was blasted into the lava face for the tower, and the keepers' three dwellings were built of local blue lava rock in a hollow near the summit. A trail and a road had to be cut into the point, and water was pumped up from Waimanalo landing into a 10,000-gallon tank.[^2]

The sources do not agree on the tower's size. The Lighthouse Service's own account of the 1925 accident called it "a low structure 25 feet in height from the ground line to the focal plane."[^4] Putnam gave the tower as forty-six feet high and the light as 420 feet above the sea.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says thirty-five feet and calls the tower concrete, which fits the 1909 notice's "cement-gray" tower.[^2][^15]

The lens is the reason the station is remembered. Plans moved from a third-order lens to a second-order and finally to a first-order hyperradiant lens, which Lighthouse Friends says the Lighthouse Board had bought in 1887 from Barbier & Cie. of Paris and shown at the 1893 Chicago fair and the 1907 Jamestown Exposition before shipment to Oahu.[^2] Putnam gave its inside diameter as eight and three-fourths feet, and Lighthouse Friends gives roughly eight feet nine inches and a height of twelve feet.[^3][^2] A 1936 Lighthouse Service article gave 9 feet across and 13 feet high, with 1,140 prisms, and called it the largest lens in the country; Lighthouse Friends counts 1,188 prisms.[^8][^2] The lamp that served it in 1925 was an incandescent oil-vapor lamp with three 55-millimetre mantles.[^4]

Lighthouse Friends says the light was first shown on 1 October 1909 by assistants George Beazley and George Mansfield, and that head keeper McLaughlin arrived after the lighting.[^2] A notice to mariners printed in Honolulu on 3 October 1909 and dated the 2nd confirms that on 1 October 1909 a hyper-radiant light was established, flashing white for 7 1/2 seconds and dark for 1 1/2, about 420 feet above high water, in a "cement-gray, cylindrical tower."[^15] The Bulletin for 1913 reported the station's water system completed.[^16]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists John McLaughlin as head keeper in 1909-1910, followed briefly by John E. Lind, and then George A. Beazley for 1910 to 1922; Beazley had served as first assistant in 1909-1910.[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives McLaughlin's first name as James.[^2] Beazley's station took an efficiency flag, awarded in each district to the station showing the highest general efficiency, for fiscal 1915, and Lighthouse Friends says he won it for at least four years running.[^10][^2]

Staffing was a head keeper and two assistants, and a third assistant appears in the roster in 1927 and 1928.[^1][^2] Assistants came and went quickly: the roster names 34 different men in the first-assistant and second-assistant posts between 1909 and 1945, several of them listed for a single calendar year.[^1] Manuel Ferreira was head keeper from 1929 to 1942 and his station was among the efficiency pennant winners listed in the 1931 Bulletin.[^1][^11] Lighthouse Friends quotes the station logbook for 8 December 1941, the day after the attack on Pearl Harbor: watches were reorganised to four hours on and eight off, with keepers allowed into town for provisions twice a week.[^2]

After the war the roster thins and the Coast Guard appears. Joseph Pestrella took charge in 1960 according to the archive, after the Cape Kumukahi keepers' quarters were destroyed by a lava flow and that light was automated.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends, drawing on his granddaughter, says a seaman who jumped from a passing merchant ship swam all night, was presumed dead, and then arrived bruised at the Pestrella house, where he was given first aid and food. It dates Pestrella's retirement to 1963, a year after the archive's span ends.[^2][^1] The roster ends in 1974 with Ronald G. Cianfarani as head keeper.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Helga and the Izumo both met the new light in its first weeks, according to Lighthouse Friends. The Japanese cruiser Izumo, approaching Oahu from the east in November 1909, found a powerful light not on her charts and stayed offshore until daylight; the British barkentine *Helga* mistook it for Diamond Head and ran aground.[^2]

The accident the sources describe in most detail came at about 3 o'clock on the morning of 9 April 1925. The two keepers were about to change watch when the first assistant, Alexander D. Toomey, suggested to the second that he fill the alcohol lighter, which held the alcohol used to start the oil-vapor lamp. Some alcohol dripped on the floor of the ground-floor service room, Toomey lit a match, and the room filled with flame.[^4] Second assistant John Kaohimaunu, near the door, escaped with burns. Toomey was badly burned. The Bulletin recorded that the tower and lighthouse apparatus were undamaged, and warned of "the greatest caution in dealing with the many highly inflammable substances."[^4][^2]

Toomey had been recommended for promotion to principal keeper at Kilauea Point. According to the Bulletin, when he was taken to the hospital he refused to let his wife go with him and insisted she stay at the lighthouse, which would otherwise have been without a keeper for a considerable time, while the keeper (spelled Alcana there; the roster's head keeper then was Charles K. Akana) took the two injured assistants to Honolulu.[^5][^1] Lighthouse Friends says he told his wife, "Stand by the light and keep it burning."[^2] The Bulletin says he died on 9 April and that the Reverend Akana of Kawaiahao Church, Honolulu, preached on those words at his funeral on 11 April; Lighthouse Friends says he died at noon the day after the explosion.[^5][^2] Kaohimaunu's hand was left partly crippled, and he was moved to lighter duty at Barbers Point.[^2]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1922 lists $3,089 for repairing five miles of station road.[^6] The earthquake of 22 January 1938 cracked a storm pane in the lantern and the walls of the station buildings, and the radiobeacon transmitter was set back one minute by the shock.[^7]

## After the keepers

Hawaii's first radiobeacon was placed in commission at Makapuu Point in 1927, and Lighthouse Friends adds that a generating plant was built on the point and the lamp changed to electric bulbs.[^9][^2] The Coast Guard automated the station on 4 January 1974 and monitored it from Honolulu.[^2]

In 1987 the government declared part of the land around the light and dwellings surplus and passed it to the State of Hawaii. A group of armed Hawaiians occupied one dwelling in a land-ownership protest, were evicted after several weeks without bloodshed, and the state then razed the dwellings.[^2] A high-powered rifle put a bullet hole through the lens in 1984.[^2] In 2001 the State of Hawaii paid $12.8 million for a large tract around the lighthouse.[^2]

## Today

The light is a National Register of Historic Places listing, published on 7 December 1977.[^14] The Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, which is an unstaffed automated light. The tower and the ground right around it are not open to the public; the oil house is the only outbuilding left standing near the trail.[^2] Visitors can see it from the 1.3-mile trail from the roadside parking area to the top of Makapuu Head, or at a distance from the highway, and Lighthouse Friends notes the trail is a place to watch for humpback whales in winter.[^2]
