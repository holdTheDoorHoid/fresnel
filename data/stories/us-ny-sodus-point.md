Sodus Point Light stands on a hill at the western entrance to Sodus Bay, on the shore of Lake Ontario, in Wayne County, New York.[^1][^2] The stone tower of 1825 was replaced in 1871 by a limestone tower attached to a two-story keeper's house. The limestone tower was the bay's main light until 1901. After that the keeper tended the pier lights from the same house, and the Coast Guard kept the station until 1973.[^1][^2]

## The place

LighthouseFriends describes Sodus Bay as six miles long and three miles wide.[^1] In 1822 William Rochester presented a petition to the House of Representatives for a lighthouse at the entrance to the bay. In 1824 the government paid $4,500 for one.[^1]

The entrance was narrowed by piers. A plan of 1828 called for a pair of piers that would contract the entrance to 470 feet (143 m), and the piers were built between 1829 and 1834.[^1] A stone tower of nearly thirty feet (about 9 m) went up on the west pier in 1837.[^1][^2]

## Building the light

William Wickham was paid $68.75 for three acres at Sodus Point in November 1824. The next year a stone dwelling and a conical stone tower were completed. The tower stood forty feet (12 m) tall, with ten lamps and a focal plane sixty-six feet (20 m) above the lake.[^1]

The Lighthouse Board asked Congress for $14,000 for a new dwelling with an attached tower, and Congress voted the money in July 1870. Work began in August 1870, and the new station was finished on 30 June 1871.[^1] The limestone came from the Kingston quarries. LighthouseFriends says the old tower and dwelling were demolished, and the stone was used to build a jetty.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society record gives the new tower as limestone, built in 1871.[^2]

The lenses changed over time. A sixth-order lens went into the pier light in 1854, and a revolving fourth-order lens went into the bay light in 1858.[^1] A gale destroyed the pier beacon in 1857, and a pair of range lights took its place the following year.[^1]

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1895 says the keeper's dwelling at Sodus Point had "extensive repairs and improvements," and that much of it was rebuilt.[^4] The report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1896 says that in the previous year the dwelling had "extensive modifications and repairs," and that "a substantial and commodious beacon" had been built to replace an old one that was unserviceable.[^5]

## The keepers

Ishmael D. Hill became the first keeper in 1826. LighthouseFriends says he had lost his property in the War of 1812 and petitioned for the post as a sailor who knew the lake.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends also says he was disqualified in 1829 "by reason of insanity," and that Bennett C. Fitzhugh was appointed in his place.[^1] The roster lists Fitzhugh in several stints between 1829 and 1846.[^2]

The post of assistant keeper was added in 1871. The roster lists Philander T. Lyman as first assistant from 1871 to 1883, and then James G. Lewis and Wilfred A. Lewis.[^1][^2] Daniel Phillips was head keeper from 1881 to 1917. LighthouseFriends says he was a Civil War veteran who had fought at Gettysburg, and that the main light was discontinued during his term.[^1][^2]

Charles A. Plummer served as assistant keeper for more than twenty years.[^1][^2] Edwin G. Ward, head keeper from 1939 to 1952, was the last civilian keeper. LighthouseFriends says his father and grandfather had also kept lights.[^1][^2] Harold A. Ayers, a boatswain's mate first class, took charge in 1952.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1935 Charles Plummer was recognized for rescuing a boy and a girl from drowning, after their canoe overturned near the lighthouse.[^1] On 31 August 1964 a man fell off the breakwall into the channel. LighthouseFriends says Coast Guard watchstander Thomas Muchow dropped a life ring into the water for him, but he would not let go of a fishing stringer. After he lost his grip on the stringer near the end of the breakwall, Muchow jumped in and pulled him to shore. An attending doctor credited Muchow with saving the man's life.[^1]

## After the keepers

In June 1901 the main light on the bluff was "permanently discontinued."[^3] The same report records that the outer pier light was changed from sixth order to fourth order on 10 June 1901, with a new characteristic.[^6] The keeper kept living in the house, now responsible only for the pier lights.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says an air fog signal was set up on the outer light in June 1926. The square pyramidal pierhead tower was built in 1938, along with a new inner tower and electric power for the light and fog signal.[^1]

The Coast Guard closed the station in 1973, but a Coast Guard commander continued to live in the lighthouse. The lighthouse was placed on the National Register in 1976.[^1] After the Coast Guard left, it suffered vandalism and neglect until a congressman led an eighteen-month effort. In 1984 the lighthouse was transferred from the General Services Administration to the town.[^1] The lighthouse stood just seventeen feet (about 5 m) from the crumbling bluff, so landfill was brought in to widen the buffer.[^1] The Sodus Bay Historical Society opened a maritime museum in the house, dedicated on 4 July 1985.[^1]

## Today

The tower and keeper's house are open as a museum, run by the Sodus Bay Historical Society under a lease from the town. LighthouseFriends gives the season as 1 May to 31 October, Tuesday to Sunday, 10 a.m. to 5 p.m.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a fourth-order Fresnel lens is on display in the tower. The U.S. Lighthouse Society record gives a third-and-a-half-order optic as current, so the sources differ on which lens is there.[^1][^2]
