Carysfort Reef Light is an iron skeleton tower standing on nine screw piles about six miles off Key Largo, in the Florida Keys.[^1][^4] It was first lit in 1852, to mark a reef that had already cost many ships, and the Coast Guard deactivated it in 2014, calling the structure unsafe.[^1][^2] The tower still stands on the reef, inside the sanctuary that covers these waters.[^1][^4]

## The place

The reef takes its name from HMS *Carysfort*, a frigate that "ran aground at the site in October 1770."[^1] LighthouseFriends counts the losses: between 1833 and 1841, "sixty-three, or roughly twenty percent," of the 324 vessels lost on the Florida Reefs went down on Carysfort Reef.[^2]

In February 1845 Colonel J. T. Albert, chief of the Corps of Topographical Engineers, surveyed the Florida reefs and recommended lights along them from Cape Florida to the Tortugas. The nomination says he singled out "Carysfort Reef as the most dangerous reef" in that stretch.[^1]

The reef had a light before the tower. Congress paid for a lightship in 1824, and the vessel *Caesar* sailed from New York for the Keys in 1825.[^2] LighthouseFriends says she was so rotten after five years that Congress had to pay for a replacement in 1830.[^2] Her successor, the *Florida*, was on station in June 1837 when her keeper, John Whalton, and four crewmen rowed ashore on Key Largo. LighthouseFriends quotes a crew member's report of a surprise encounter with Seminole Indians: "Capt. Whalton and one of his men were shot dead."[^2]

The Service's bulletin for 1937 says the tower was begun "to replace the lightship" and was "lighted in 1852."[^15]

## Building the light

Congress provided $20,000 for a lighthouse in 1837 and another $40,000 the following year.[^2] The money went back to the Treasury in 1840, and the nomination says design "was not started until 1848."[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the first of the later appropriations to 1847.[^2] Winslow Lewis proposed a masonry tower, but a screw-pile design submitted by his nephew, I. W. P. Lewis, was selected instead.[^2]

The ground was the problem. LighthouseFriends says test borings showed that "underneath a solid crust was a softer mass of sand."[^2] The nomination says the nine foundation piles were "literally screwed through the coral and sand crust" to a depth of 10 feet (3 m).[^1] The Philadelphia firm of Merrick and Son made the iron skeleton in its yard and shipped it to Key Largo.[^1]

Captain Howard Stansbury was responsible for erecting the tower.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the engineer in charge after him, Major Thomas Linnard, "died shortly after taking the assignment." Lieutenant George Meade finished the job. On 31 July 1852 he reported to the Lighthouse Board that the tower was "composed of a framework of 9 iron piles, occupying the center and angular point of an octagon of 50 feet diameter."[^2] The nomination spells the major's name Lannard.[^1]

The light was first lit on 10 March 1852, as a fixed light. In July it received a first-order revolving Fresnel lens made in Paris by Henry Lepaute.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates that lens to 1855, when it replaced the original lamps, and says a revolving first-order lens was activated on 17 March 1858.[^2] The keepers lived in a two-story iron house, raised on the iron framework below the light, "in the form of a truncated cone."[^1] The Board's records for 1852 describe the skeleton as painted black, the house and tower red, and the windows and doors white.[^1]

## The keepers

The Service's roster for the light lists 196 entries, one for each stint, from 1852 to 1962. Thirty-four were principal keepers, and most of the rest were first and second assistants. The last 20 entries are Coast Guard crew from 1959 to 1962.[^3]

Alexander C. Jenks came up through the ranks. He was a second assistant from 1914, a first assistant from 1925, and principal keeper from 1928 to 1939.[^3] LighthouseFriends tells of a local man, Charles Brookfield, who often fished near the reef and once spent a night at the station, where he met Keeper Jenks. He was woken by a groan. He went up to the lantern room, and Keeper Jenks told him it was an old captain who had died aboard. Brookfield later recalled: "I sat up thinking it may have been a dream." He put the sound down to the iron tower expanding and contracting. LighthouseFriends presents this as his recollection, not a record.[^2]

The Board worked on the iron skeleton as well. Its report for 1896 and 1897 says that the structure "was thoroughly scaled, scraped, and painted."[^8] In 1893 the light was changed, the Board's list shows, "from flashing white to flashing white with three flashing red sectors."[^7] In 1900 the Board reported new machinery to replace the revolving machinery of the light.[^9] In the fiscal year 1917 an oil house was completed at Carysfort Reef "for $188."[^10]

Keeper William H. Curry served from 1917 to 1919.[^3] The Service's bulletin for 1919 says that he "was drowned during the hurricane referred to above."[^12] LighthouseFriends gives the sequence: he left Key West on 6 September 1919 with supplies and mail for the reef, anchored in Boot Key Harbor on the night of 8 September, and his capsized boat was found several days later about twenty-five miles to the north. The page presumes his boat was driven into an arch of a bridge and overturned.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources read for this station name no wreck on the reef itself. They do name several storms and rescues.

On 4 January 1922 the motor yacht *Napinee* of Jacksonville was in trouble about three miles from the light.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses reports that Second Assistant E. J. Whidden "rescued the motor boat Napinee in the face of a strong southeast wind."[^11] LighthouseFriends says the yacht had been adrift for two days, and that the keepers "provided dry clothing and food for their famished guests."[^2]

The Service's 1926 bulletin reports a storm in which the highest wind estimated at Carysfort Reef was 90 miles per hour. One of the station's small boats was washed away, and there was minor damage.[^13] The Service's index for 1924 to 1929 lists "Carysfort Reef light station damaged in hurricane."[^16]

The nomination says a hurricane in 1929 took the light's spiral stair, which was replaced with a wood ladder. Hurricane Donna destroyed the iron balcony around the keepers' house in 1966.[^1]

The nomination also says the tower was used in the Second World War as "one of a series of lookout towers in the watch for German U-boats."[^1]

## After the keepers

The light was switched to electricity in 1926. The nomination says the lower level of the tower was partly enclosed that year to house a generator, and later the storage batteries that power the light.[^1] A fixed third-order lens replaced the revolving first-order lens in 1962, when the last keepers left.[^2] The Service's 1931 bulletin says that after a metal screen replaced every fourth flash panel of the first-order lens, the light gave three flashes in its group.[^14] The third-order lens was removed in 1982, when a modern beacon went into the tower.[^2]

The nomination, prepared in 1984, says the light was de-manned after the Second World War and fully automated. It ran first on underwater cables from Key Largo, which soon failed, and then on storage batteries.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the end of resident keepers to 1962.[^2] The nomination gives the Key Largo National Marine Sanctuary's designation as December 1975. The General Services Administration now calls the sanctuary the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary.[^1][^4]

In the 1990s a plan to turn the lighthouse into a marine research center came to nothing.[^2] In 2014 the Coast Guard deactivated the light after determining that the structure was "unstable and considered unsafe."[^2] The light's flashing is now shown from a nearby structure forty feet (12 m) high.[^2]

The Coast Guard declared the lighthouse excess on 1 February 2019, and no qualified organization took it over.[^2] The General Services Administration put it up for sale by online auction, opening on 22 February 2022 with a $15,000 starting bid and a $5,000 registration deposit.[^4] The light lies within a Sanctuary Preservation Area, where some activities allowed elsewhere in the sanctuary are banned.[^4] Bidding closed on 16 May 2022 with a top bid of $415,000.[^2][^5] The winning bidder had until 30 June to complete the purchase.[^5] The sources read for this story do not name the buyer or confirm that the sale closed.

## Today

The tower is closed to the public, and the Coast Guard has called the structure unsafe.[^2] The Service's record marks it as not open to the public.[^3] It stands about six miles offshore, inside the sanctuary.[^4] Anyone planning to visit should not climb the tower or land on the reef structure.

A preservation group, the Carysfort Reef Lighthouse Foundation, says the structure is deteriorating and is raising money to preserve it.[^6]
