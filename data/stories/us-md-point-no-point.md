Point No Point Light is a red iron cylinder standing on the western edge of the main ship channel of the Chesapeake Bay, with a white octagonal brick house and a black lantern perched on top. It stands about two miles off the Maryland shore in St. Mary's County, in 18 to 22 feet (5.5 to 6.7 m) of water, and it can be reached only by boat.[^1]

## The place

The light was built for a gap in the Bay's lighting. In 1891 the Light-House Board pointed out that a stretch of about 30 miles lay between the Cove Point and Smith Point lights. Heavy-draught vessels trading to Baltimore, it said, could stray onto shoals there, and it asked for $35,000 for a lighthouse, calling it "A lighthouse on the shoal off Point No Point" and a "useful warning".[^2][^3] The station sits on the western edge of the main ship channel, roughly 5⅔ nautical miles north-northeast of Point Lookout, on the north side of the mouth of the Potomac River.[^1]

The nomination says Congress refused the money and the Board repeated the request in 1892 and 1893; by 1894 it considered screw-pile lighthouses too vulnerable to ice in the Bay and recommended a costlier caisson tower, estimated at $70,000.[^1] In 1899, the nomination says, the Board wrote that petitions for a light at this locality had "lately become quite urgent"; the report itself put a caisson light and fog signal at about $65,000.[^1][^4] Congress appropriated $65,000 on 3 March 1901.[^1] Borings were made at the site that year.[^5]

## Building the light

The tower is a pneumatic caisson lighthouse, one of seven built in the Chesapeake out of eleven in the United States, according to the National Register nomination.[^1] A wooden caisson 32 feet (9.8 m) square and 13 feet (4 m) high was built, with cast-iron plates bolted on in tiers above it, and the lower structure was meant to be sunk into the shoal by digging out sand from within.[^1][^3] In the nomination the lowest bid for the metalwork was $9,475, and Toomey Brothers won the contract to erect the caisson at $38,880; the nomination calls them a Connecticut firm in its history and a New York firm in its data block.[^1] They began in August 1902 and launched the caisson on 15 November 1902.[^1]

The work went badly twice. According to the nomination, the caisson was towed from Solomons, Maryland, to the site on 3 April 1903, and during a northwest gale the temporary pier, with the contractors' living quarters, gave way. The assembly filled, overturned and drifted down the Bay, losing its second and third tiers of plates, and was recovered off the mouth of the Rappahannock River about 40 miles south. It went back to Solomons, was fitted with new plates and the pier was rebuilt, all at the contractor's own cost.[^1] The local paper noted on 9 April that work at Point No Point had been stopped "on account of the stormy weather", and a week later that "Some of 4be light material" had been recovered.[^6][^7]

The caisson was put back in position in October 1903: the nomination says it was re-sunk on 21 October, while Lighthouse Friends says it was towed on the 21st and grounded the next day.[^1][^3] About 225 tons of riprap was laid around it against scour.[^1] In February 1904, ice destroyed the second pier along with the compressor, boiler, tools and workmen's quarters; the caisson itself took little damage.[^1] A third pier was begun in March, and in June 1904 the caisson was sunk 13 feet (4 m) into the bottom.[^1]

On 24 April 1905 the light was first shown, from a fourth-order Fresnel lens showing alternating red and white flashes, with a fog bell struck by weight-driven machinery every 15 seconds in thick weather.[^1] (A footnote in the nomination notes that one author, Lawrence Bradner, dates the lighthouse 1902 where others give 1905.[^1])

The house is an octagonal brick dwelling of two storeys under a mansard roof, with the watch room in the roof and the lantern above.[^3] The lower level inside the caisson holds the cellar, lined with brick and pierced by eight portholes, with coal and oil rooms, two 1,100-gallon cisterns and two 550-gallon oil tanks; a central iron column carried the weights for the fog bell.[^1]

## The keepers

The first keeper was Anders Simonsen, previously keeper at Love Point Lighthouse.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society archive lists sixteen head keepers between 1905 and 1962, and first assistants from 1905 to 1942.[^8]

The best-documented episode is 26 September 1914. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded that Keeper George M. Willis Sr. rescued assistant keeper William Yeatman "and his child who were washed overboard in trying to land at the station".[^9] Lighthouse Friends prints Willis's report to the inspector, in which he says a gale from the northwest had come up on Yeatman's return from the shore with mail and provisions, that a heavy sea washed Yeatman and the child off the steps, and that he got a line to them and pulled both to safety, describing it as "a very close shave for both of them".[^3] Yeatman became head keeper soon afterwards and stayed until 1918.[^8][^3] The Chesapeake chapter of the US Lighthouse Society says, without giving a year, that during a hard winter at the station he built a sleigh to cross the ice, fearing the ice would knock the lighthouse down.[^13]

Thomas P. Midgett was head keeper from 1923 to 1930.[^8] The Bulletin reported that on 4 July 1928 he "assisted four men in a small boat" in difficulty near the station.[^11]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A hurricane on 23 August 1933 broke out many of the porthole panes, flooded the cellar and fouled the station's fresh-water supply; the station's sailboat was torn from its davits and lost. The nomination takes this from a letter by Keeper James W. Wilson written the next day.[^1]

## After the keepers

The nomination describes the power source changing from a battery pack to a kerosene generator on 26 October 1938.[^1] Sources disagree on when the station was automated. The nomination says April 24, 1962, "exactly 57 years after it's commissioning", with William Wrightson as the last keeper; Lighthouse Friends and the Chesapeake chapter say the light was automated in 1938, with a Coast Guard crew staying until 1962.[^1][^3][^10] The Coast Guard took the 1,000-pound fog bell down on 10 November 1964.[^1]

Left without resident crews, the interior decayed. In 1988 about 32 cubic yards of debris was removed, and the nomination records that the sealed structure had rotted much of its wood.[^1] In 2001 a Coast Guard team replaced rotten floor beams and planking and the flat part of the metal roof, and the Coast Guard restored and repainted the station in 2015.[^3][^12]

The Coast Guard declared the lighthouse excess in 2006. No public or non-profit body took it up when it was offered free, and in 2007 it went to online auction, which was suspended at a high bid of $135,000 when it was realised that the lighthouse marks a boundary of the Navy's aerial firing range; the auction was cancelled on 8 February 2008.[^3][^12]

## Today

Point No Point remains an active aid to navigation, showing a white flash every six seconds.[^3] The Coast Guard owned and managed it when the nomination was written, and access was by boat; no later source read says ownership changed. It is an offshore aid to navigation, not a place for visitors to land or climb.[^1] The Chesapeake chapter reports that the interior is in poor condition.[^10]
