Egg Island Lighthouse stands on a rocky island at the mouth of Fitzhugh Sound on British Columbia's Central Coast, fully open to the Pacific. Its keepers were cut off for months at a time, and the station's history is one of hunger, isolation and, in 1948, a storm that carried the whole lighthouse away.

## The place

Egg Island lies near the entrance to Fitzhugh Sound from Queen Charlotte Sound and is exposed to the full sweep of the Pacific Ocean.[^1] The Canadian Register of Historic Places says the island is said to resemble a hen's egg on the horizon from a distance, and that mariners relied on the light to guide them past the rocky shoals that reach far out to sea.[^2] The station was established in 1898 as the province's lighthouse system expanded along the Inside Passage, the sea route between Washington and Alaska, whose growing traffic of canneries, lumber camps and mines, and the Klondike rush, called for a well-lit waterway.[^2]

## Building the light

In August 1898 the government steamer *Quadra* landed a large work party to build a lighthouse on an islet off the western shore of Egg Island.[^1] The Department of Marine's report, as quoted by Lighthousefriends, described a white wooden building with a square tower rising from the keeper's dwelling and a red iron lantern, 15 m (50 ft) high in all, with a revolving white light 22 m (72 ft) above high water.[^1] It was built by day labour under D. M. Fraser of Vancouver at a cost of $3,601.86, and it reused the lantern and lighting apparatus from the Yellow Island light.[^1] The light went into operation on 7 October 1898.[^1]

The site proved too exposed. A breakwater to protect the lighthouse was built for $188.24 in the year ended June 1900,[^3] and when that was not enough, the whole building was moved 19.5 m (64 ft) to the highest point of the islet and set on a concrete foundation, as the Department's report for the year ended June 1903 records.[^4] In 1906 a fog alarm was added in a wooden building about 30 m (100 ft) north of the lighthouse: a diaphone worked by compressed air from an oil engine.[^5]

## The keepers

William Brown, the first keeper, took charge just weeks after his wife died, leaving him a young son to raise.[^1] In December 1899 the light was reported out and the *Quadra* was sent to find out why. The Victoria *Daily Colonist*, as quoted by Lighthousefriends, called the station a "desolate corner of the world" where the keeper was out of contact for whole months.[^1] The ship arrived on Christmas Day and found Brown too weak to wind the light's revolving apparatus and barely able to look after his son. He said he had flown his ensign "union down", a distress signal, for several days while steamers passed without sending a boat.[^1] He and the boy were taken to Victoria, and a temporary keeper was left in charge.[^1]

Robert Scarlett, the second permanent keeper, once raised a distress signal merely to get a steamer to carry his eggs to market; a Marine Department captain who waded ashore in the breakers bought them himself.[^1] Another time the supply steamer was a month late and the Scarletts were, in what Lighthousefriends calls a state of starvation, living on what they raised in the garden and from their hens and goats.[^1] Lighthousefriends dates these episodes inconsistently (1901 and 1904 both appear), so no year is given here.

Family life was the other hardship. In September 1919 Keeper William Hartin asked to be relieved at once because his wife had, in his words, "gone crazy"; Lighthousefriends links this to the lack of company and mail only four times a year.[^1] In 1930 Arnold Moran resigned after seven years so that his son could be schooled and his wife could have company. He wrote to the Minister of Marine that "the cruel isolation of Egg Island" had affected her health.[^1] His brother Tom took his place.[^1]

The station's roster, as Lighthousefriends lists it, runs from Brown (1898–1899) through Scarlett, Nicholson, Davies, Forsythe, Drummond, Hartin, Milton and the Morans to John William Webster (1934–1937), and later Wilkins, Dupuis, Gardner, Carson, Stanley Westhaver (1977–2001), Steve Allison, Harry Saliken and Heather Davey.[^1] Laurie Dupuis kept the light from 1949 and brought his wife Peggy, who wrote for the *Vancouver Sun* that the view of the sea on three sides was "unsurpassingly lovely".[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 9 February 1934 the steamer *Newington* called, and Keeper Moran rowed out to the ship to ask permission to take his sick wife to town for medical attention. The ship's captain left seventeen-year-old mess boy James Flewin with Assistant Keeper Dan MacDonald until Moran came back.[^1] A few weeks later the captain of the *Catalla* noticed the light was out; a boat went ashore and found the station deserted. No bodies were ever found, and it was theorised that the two had gone fishing and drowned.[^1] The Canadian heritage statement and the *Encyclopedia of Raincoast Place Names* also say they were presumed or probably drowned.[^2][^6]

The great disaster came in November 1948. Robert Wilkins had moved to Egg Island that year from Green Island with his wife Ada Marie and nine-year-old son Dennis.[^1] In the small hours the sea broke through a window of the dwelling, and the sunroom and walkway were washed away.[^1] Dennis Wilkins later wrote that the family found the grass, flowers and earth gone down to bare rock, and that the fog alarm building was flooded to above the height of its engines. The sea sounded "like a raging waterfall", and then hail "like marbles" began.[^7] Robert Wilkins saw a large wave lift the lighthouse off its foundation and drop it into the ravine.[^1]

The family took shelter in an old chicken coop. Dennis Wilkins says his father salvaged from the wreckage "a tin of Prem, and an egg" as the first real food in five days; the family then rowed out to the fishing boat *Sunny Boy*, which was circling the island, and was taken to the hospital at Bella Bella.[^7] The accounts differ on the count of days: Lighthousefriends says the fifth day, Dennis Wilkins the sixth, and the heritage statement says eight days without provisions.[^1][^7][^2] The supply tender *Alberni*, Wilkins wrote, reached Bella Bella nearly nine days after the storm.[^7] An emergency light was put up.[^2]

## After the keepers

The next year Robert Wilkins returned to the island as part of the crew rebuilding the lighthouse, which was built of concrete.[^1] The heritage statement says the current tower was erected in 1962,[^2] while Lighthousefriends says that in 1963 two dwellings, a fog alarm building and a storage shed went up on the main island and a steel tower was set on its highest point; the heritage statement instead dates its surviving dwellings to 1960, 1966 and 1988.[^1][^2] A helicopter pad now occupies the site of the earlier lighthouses.[^1]

## Today

The present light is a square, open, unpainted steel-frame skeleton tower, among the tallest of the six staffed skeleton towers left on the British Columbia coast, according to the heritage statement. Seven service buildings, including dwellings of 1966 and 1988, are part of the designation.[^2] The Canadian Coast Guard owns the station; Lighthousefriends lists the grounds as open and the tower as closed, and advises that the lighthouse is best seen from the water, including from the ferries on the Discovery Coast and Inside Passage runs.[^1]
