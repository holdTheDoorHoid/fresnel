Bloody Point Range Lights stood at the southern tip of Daufuskie Island, South Carolina, and from 1883 they marked the channel into the Savannah River.[^1] The front light was a lamp in a dormer window of the keeper's house.[^8] The rear light was a 91-foot (27.7 m) iron tower behind it.[^2] The house was moved in 1899, and it is open to visitors today as a museum and gift shop.[^2][^3][^4]

## The place

Daufuskie is one of South Carolina's southernmost sea islands. It lies about a mile southwest of Hilton Head, across Calibogue Sound, and it is reached only by boat.[^5]

The nomination ties the name of the point to a 1728 surprise attack on a scout-boat crew camped there, "in which all but the commander were killed." It calls that battle the one "which gives the site its name."[^5] The same document places an earlier ambush, in 1715, on the island's southwestern point.[^5] The visitor site hhi.com and LighthouseFriends both date the fighting at the point to 1715 and connect the name with it.[^3][^2] The sources do not agree on which fight gave the point its name.

Before there was a light there was a lookout. The nomination dates the earliest recorded reference to the island to 1701, when Fort Passage, "a lookout (clapboard hut and crude tower)," was apparently established at Bloody Point. Its scout-boat crew patrolled the inland passage, "alert for the approach of Indian raiding parties."[^5] By the 1770s the point had a shipbuilding use. The nomination says Bloody Point "was owned by Watts from 1770 until his death in 1775," and was "likely the construction site of several ships."[^6]

The range was a Savannah River project. LighthouseFriends says Congress appropriated $60,000 on 3 March 1881 for twelve iron lighthouses, two of them on Daufuskie, to improve navigation on the Savannah River.[^2] The government bought land from Henry Mongin Stoddard, who had inherited the plantation after the Civil War.[^2] The deal closed on 14 April 1882 and gave the lighthouse service the right to clear trees that blocked the view between the two lights.[^2]

The channel did not stay put. In March 1895 the *Savannah Morning News* printed a notice to mariners, dated 5 March, telling vessels to run on the line marked by the Bloody Point range, to reach the Tybee Knoll Cut range. The paper said what was needed was "a shifting of the range lights" to match the new channel to the sea.[^7]

## Building the light

The iron tower came from the Cooper Manufacturing Company of Mount Vernon, Ohio.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the company's chief machinist, John Michael Doyle, had served with the Union Army as far south as Savannah, and was sent to Daufuskie to oversee the erection of the tower and lamp house.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society's place record for the rear light gives 81 feet (24.7 m) to the lantern, not 91 feet, and the sources read here do not say how the two figures relate.[^22]

Doyle reached the island on 13 November 1882 with James C. LaCoste, who built the front light's dwelling.[^2][^8] The nomination describes that dwelling as a one-and-a-half-story frame house with a "large central gabled dormer which housed the light." It says the house is "identical to the now destroyed Elba Island" light, the "Waving Girl," except for that dormer.[^8][^9]

By day the light's lamp was kept in a brick lamp house at the base of the tower. At night it was hoisted on rails to the top.[^2] The final inspection was held on 22 February 1883. Doyle was appointed the first caretaker on 4 April 1883, at $620 a year, and the lights were first exhibited on 20 April 1883.[^2][^3]

A brick wick house, which the nomination dates to 1884, stands on a circular concrete pad that "once supported range light tower."[^10]

## The keepers

Doyle kept the lights until he resigned on 15 August 1890. LighthouseFriends says he then became a fireman in Savannah.[^2] The archive's roster lists him as principal keeper for 1883 to 1890.[^11] During his service a kitchen was built onto the dwelling and a boathouse was added.[^2]

Robert A. Sisson succeeded him and served to 1908. Charles L. Sisson was principal keeper from 1908 to 1910, and Robert A. Sisson returned for 1910 to 1912.[^11] The sources here do not say whether the two men were related.

The rear light had its own keeper in 1893. The *Savannah Morning News* reported that he lost "all his quarters and other things" in the storm, but "was able to keep his lights going."[^12] No source here names him, and the archive lists only the front range's principal keeper.[^11]

The paper does not name the keeper of the front light who spoke to it. LighthouseFriends names Robert A. Sisson as keeper at the time of the 1893 storm, from his claim for losses.[^2]

Sisson had storm losses too. LighthouseFriends says he claimed $74.68 for property lost in the 1893 cyclone, including shoes, furniture, a safe and food. The payment was not approved until 1911.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The great storm of late August 1893 damaged the station.[^13] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 says the high seas "culminated on August 27-28" in a tide in the Sixth district "higher by 2 feet than any which has been recorded." The report adds that the storm was estimated to have caused "a loss of more than 2,000 lives on the sea islands" of South Carolina. That figure covers the coast as a whole, not this station.[^13]

The Board listed the Bloody Point front beacon among the stations whose structures were badly damaged.[^13] In its Bloody Point entry, the Board said the boathouse, kitchen and storehouse had been "rolled from the places where the tide had carried them" and were reset in their previous positions.[^14]

The *Savannah Morning News* reported on 1 September 1893 what the keeper of the Bloody Point light told it: the station was "almost a total wreck." The oil room, kitchen and boathouse were "torn from their foundations," the paper said, the brick piers were torn out, and two buildings had been washed 500 yards up the island. The keeper, carrying his children and leading his wife, "waded waist deep three-quarters of a mile to a house."[^12] The paper also reported that "Not a single white person on the island came near or offered to render any assistance to the keeper or his family."[^12]

LighthouseFriends describes the occupants of the dwelling as traumatized by the Charleston earthquake on the night of 31 August 1886, but says little damage was done to the structures at the station.[^2]

The sea was a continuing problem for the station. The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1896 says "The sea is encroaching seriously on the site," and that "it is probable that all the structures at the front beacon will require removal in the near future." That year the boathouse was set back 250 feet.[^15]

The October 1898 storm struck the front beacon. The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1899 says that "The foundations of the dwelling were filled in and boarded up."[^4]

## After the keepers

Under an appropriation of $2,700, "made by the act approved on March 3, 1899," the Board arranged to move the front-beacon structures.[^2] Its report describes "removing the structures of the front beacon to the site of the rear beacon."[^4] The next year's report says the removal was "completed in December, 1899," and that "There was no interruption of the light of the front beacon."[^16] LighthouseFriends puts the dwelling's move at about 4,350 feet (1,330 m) inland, to a site near the rear light.[^2]

The new front beacon was an iron tower from Venus Point, which the Board had called a "discontinued iron front beacon."[^4] The Board wrote that the abandoned Venus Point beacon "has become the front beacon of the Bloody Point range."[^17]

The Board's own reports disagree on how far the front light moved. The narrative of the 1900 report moves the structures to "the site of the rear beacon, about 1 mile distant." Its list of lights moved says the front was moved "60 feet nearer rear light."[^16][^18] The sources do not reconcile the two.

LighthouseFriends says that in 1908 the engineer at Savannah reported the range no longer marked the best water at the river's entrance. The Board then asked for $4,500 to establish offshore range lights, and Congress approved the project in 1910 without supplying the money at once.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says the old range was discontinued on 15 November 1912. That day a new offshore range, which kept the name Bloody Point, was activated. Its iron skeletal towers stood a mile and a quarter apart.[^2]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1912 lists the range among lights "changed from fixed to flashing," with "2 acetylene lights." It gives the state as "Ga.", although the range is in South Carolina.[^19]

The Commissioner's list for 1916 still shows a two-light Bloody Point range in South Carolina.[^20] Because the 1912 offshore range kept the name, the list does not show whether the old range was still in service.

The records do not agree on when the range ended. LighthouseFriends gives 15 November 1912, as above. The US Lighthouse Society's place record for the front range gives 1922 as the year it was discontinued.[^21] The sources read here do not settle which is right.

The government sold the keeper's dwelling at public auction on 9 January 1922, to Francis Keenan, for $525.15.[^2]

Gustaf Ohman, who had served at Cockspur and Jones Island, bought the house from Keenan in 1924.[^2] Arthur "Papy" Burn, who had been an assistant at Tybee Range, bought the lighthouse from Ohman in November 1926. He lived there until ill health forced him to move to the mainland.[^2] In 1953 he began making wine in the old lamp house and named the business the Silver Dew Winery.[^2][^3]

## Today

The house is privately owned and open to visitors as a museum and gift shop. Its displays include Civil War relics, maritime exhibits and island history, and the Silver Dew Winery is on the grounds. Hours change with the seasons, so visitors should call ahead.[^3]

Wick Scurry of Calibogue Cruises bought the house in 2015 and opened it as a gift shop and museum.[^3] The house is entry 254 in the nomination's inventory of the Daufuskie Island Historic District, a district of 241 contributing properties.[^8][^5]

Getting there takes a boat. The nomination says Daufuskie is accessible only by boat.[^5] hhi.com advises visitors to book a golf cart with their boat, because the island has no cars for visitors.[^3]

The sources read here do not describe the original 91-foot iron rear tower after 1899. The US Lighthouse Society's rear-light record gives 1899 as the year that light was discontinued.[^22]
