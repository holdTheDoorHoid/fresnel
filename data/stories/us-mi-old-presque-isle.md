The Old Presque Isle Light stands on a point at the entrance to Presque Isle Harbor, on Lake Huron in Presque Isle County, Michigan. Its tapered stone-and-brick tower was first lit in 1840. It was replaced in 1871 by a light about a mile north, and the old tower is now a township museum that can be climbed in season.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Presque Isle is French for "almost an island." The name has been given to a few peninsulas on the Great Lakes. This one lies nineteen miles north of Alpena, and it helps form Presque Isle Harbor to the south.[^1]

In 1837 Lieutenant G. J. Pendergrast, sent to choose light sites on the lakes, reported (as LighthouseFriends quotes his report) that the harbor "ought to be provided with a light, to show vessels how to enter it in a stormy night." Steamboats bound up or down the lake stopped there for wood.[^1] Congress appropriated $5,000 for a light on 7 July 1838. That summer Lieutenant James T. Homans chose a point of land on the northeast side of the harbor entrance and marked it with a stake.[^1] Homans, in the report LighthouseFriends quotes, called the harbor the only "safe haven" on the 240-mile route between Fort Gratiot and the Straits of Mackinac, and vessels of every kind sheltered there in storms.[^1]

## Building the light

Jeremiah Moors, who had built the Thunder Bay Island light in 1832, won the contract on 26 August 1839.[^1] The tower is brick and stone. Its walls are three feet two inches (0.97 m) thick at the base, where the tower is twelve feet (3.7 m) across, and it narrows to a lantern six feet (1.8 m) across. Inside, stone stairs led up to a lantern where eleven lamps, each in a fourteen-inch reflector, gave a flashing light.[^1]

A brick dwelling of one and a half stories, 35 by 20 feet (11 by 6 m), stood just north of the tower.[^1][^2]

The sources disagree on the tower's height. LighthouseFriends gives it as thirty-one feet (9.4 m). The archive's record gives 100 feet (30.5 m) from the base to the lantern, and 123 feet (37.5 m) above the water for the light.[^1][^2]

In 1857 a fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed, and the light became fixed white.[^1]

## The keepers

Henry L. Woolsey was appointed the first keeper on 23 September 1840, at a salary of $350 a year. Before he arrived, Lemuel Crawford, who owned the harbor's wooding station, had kept the light as an interim keeper from 27 August 1840.[^1]

The archive lists five principal keepers: Woolsey (1840 to 1847), George Murray (1847 to 1848), Stephen V. Thornton (1848 to 1853), Louis G. Metivier (1853 to 1861) and Patrick Garrity, Sr. (1861 to 1871).[^2] Garrity was the last keeper of the old light. When the new light went into service he moved his wife and five children just up the road to take charge of it.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1868 the Lighthouse Board wrote, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, that the old light was of little value for guiding vessels into the harbor, because of the character of the entrance. It proposed moving the light about a mile north and adding range lights to mark the channel.[^1] The range lights were completed in August 1870. The new coastal light went into service at the opening of navigation in 1871. The old light was discontinued, and its lantern room and lens were removed for use elsewhere.[^1][^2]

The government kept the empty tower for nearly three decades. In 1897 it was sold at public auction to Edward O. Avery of Alpena, and a few years later Avery sold it to General Duffield.[^1] In 1911 Bliss Stebbins, a Lansing milliner who was building the Grand Lake Hotel nearby, bought the old light at a tax sale for $70, to use as a picnic spot for his guests.[^1]

By 1930 the dwelling's roof had fallen in from lack of maintenance. Francis B. Stebbins, Bliss's nephew, bought the property that year. He demolished the dwelling in 1939 and built a residence on its foundation.[^1] In the 1950s he refitted the tower as a museum. He had bought a fourth-order Fresnel lens at a Coast Guard auction, and in 1961 a replica lantern room was built on top of the tower to house it.[^1]

The lighthouse was listed on the Michigan Register of Historic Places on 14 November 1964, and on the National Register on 11 April 1973.[^1] After Francis died in 1968, his son James ran the site, and in 1977 he hired a retired couple to run it.[^1] In 1995 James Stebbins sold the lighthouse to Presque Isle Township.[^1] A major renovation in 2002 repaired the lantern-room windows, repainted the tower inside and out, and applied new bonding material to hold the stonework together.[^1]

## Today

Presque Isle Township owns and operates the Old Presque Isle Light as a museum and tower.[^1][^3] Admission to the museums is free. In 2025 the township charged a fee to climb the tower, which was $6 for adults and $4 for children. In season, weather permitting, people 42 inches (107 cm) tall or more may climb, and children under twelve must be with an adult.[^3]

The season runs from late May to mid-October. The township's hours page says that the Old and New Presque Isle lighthouses and the 1905 Keeper's House closed for the winter after the 2026 season, and that the New Lighthouse Park stays open year round.[^4]
