Turtle Island Light stood on a small island at the entrance to Maumee Bay, on Lake Erie near Toledo, Ohio.[^1] The island is split between Ohio and Michigan.[^1] The light has been dark since 1904, and the brick tower that stands there now is the second on the island.[^1][^2]

## The place

The island lies about 5.4 miles (8.7 km) from the mouth of the Maumee River, and the main shipping channel ran just south of it.[^1] A Toledo dispatch printed in 1903 says the island and the light were named after Little Turtle.[^3] Little Turtle was one of the chiefs who signed the Treaty of Greenville in 1795.[^1]

The island had changed hands before the light was built.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the government bought it back from a private owner on May 21, 1831, for $300, and that Congress had already appropriated $5,000 for a lighthouse and $2,000 against erosion.[^1] The island is divided between two states. LighthouseFriends says Ohio set its boundary to take in the whole island in 1802, that Michigan disputed the line, and that a Supreme Court decision in 1973 settled a line that split the island equally.[^1]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says the contractor Philo Scovile was hired to build the light in 1831.[^1] It stood on the north end of the island: a conical tower forty feet (12 m) high, with eight lamps and reflectors giving a fixed white light, joined to a one-and-a-half-story dwelling.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record also dates the station to 1831 and lists eight reflectors and lamps as its original optic.[^2]

The lake was eating the island.[^1] By July 1836, LighthouseFriends says, an extraordinary rise in Lake Erie had cut Turtle Island from eight acres to just over an acre.[^1] A plan by Isaac S. Smith, the Buffalo superintendent of public works, reduced it to about half an acre by means of a double row of piles and fill, at a cost of $16,700.[^1] As LighthouseFriends quotes it, an 1837 report by Lieutenant G. J. Pendergrast called the light "highly useful".[^1]

A pile driver was in use at the light-house in July 1837.[^4] The Maumee Express reported that Captain Wilkinson, superintendent of public works at the light-house on Turtle Island, had been "shockingly injured by the hammer, of a pile driver."[^4] The paper does not call him a keeper.

By 1857 the lamps and reflectors were in terrible condition, and a fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed in the lantern room.[^1]

After the Civil War, Congress appropriated $12,000 for a new tower of Milwaukee brick, built attached to the dwelling in what LighthouseFriends calls a church-style arrangement.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it stood 45 feet (13.7 m) high, kept the old lens, carried the date 1866 carved over its door, and first shone on September 12, 1866.[^1] The Fremont Journal of April 27, 1866 listed money for repairs and renovation at Turtle Island in a "Deficiency Bill" that had recently passed Congress.[^5] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1831 as the year the tower was established, but its description, a square brick tower about 44.75 feet (13.6 m) to the center of the lantern, matches the 1866 tower that LighthouseFriends describes.[^2][^1]

## The keepers

The archive lists 23 keepers and assistants: eighteen principal keepers from 1832 to 1904, and five first assistants from 1855 to 1874.[^2] LighthouseFriends lists the same people, with some spelling differences.[^1]

Samuel Choate, who fought in the War of 1812, was principal keeper from 1832 to 1834.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends says he died suddenly of cholera in 1834 and was buried on the island by his son, Captain Seth Choate, who also died of cholera soon afterwards.[^1] Seth's wife and two small children were left on the island, which LighthouseFriends calls four miles from civilization, until a sailor rescued them after noticing the light was not lit.[^1]

Ann M. Edson is listed as principal keeper from 1869 to 1872, after Nathan W. Edson (1867 to 1869).[^2] Clara Jacobs is listed as first assistant from 1873 to 1874, while Samuel Jacobs was principal keeper.[^2] The archive does not say how any of these keepers were related.[^2]

William I. Haynes was principal keeper from 1875 to 1904, the last keeper on the island.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends gives his start date as March 29, 1875.[^1] It says he raised his family on the island and turned the upstairs of the dwelling into a one-room school, and it credits him with saving seven people when their boat broke up near the island.[^1] A 1929 Toledo Blade recollection, quoted by LighthouseFriends, describes a party of young people brought to the island by tug soon after the lighthouse was completed. The Blade says they were guests of the keeper.[^1]

The Light-House Board's report for fiscal 1893 records repairs to the keeper's dwelling, a hand rail put up in the tower, and a rebuilt boathouse of 16 by 26 feet (4.9 by 7.9 m), with new boat ways down to the water.[^6] The next year the boathouse got a new winch and rollers, and materials were obtained for repairs to the boat ways.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends says storms badly damaged the island's pile protection, and that in 1884 a concrete retaining wall was built around its exposed sides.[^1] A storm house, storm sashes and a cellar were added to the dwelling the same year.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says no shipwreck was recorded in the area during the 73 years the light and its keepers served.[^1] The Life-Saving Service's table of places where vessels had stranded in the ten years to 1896 nonetheless lists "Turtle Island, Ohio" in its Great Lakes section.[^9]

## After the keepers

In 1897 the south light of the Maumee Bay range, which had served as the rear light for the range from Turtle Island, was discontinued at the opening of navigation.[^8]

In April 1903 a Toledo dispatch reported that the government had decided to sell Turtle Island and abandon the light that had served as a beacon for 72 years.[^3]

The light was decommissioned on May 15, 1904.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record says the tower was replaced by the Toledo Harbor Light, and that the island was sold that year.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the Toledo Harbor Lighthouse was not activated until eight days later, and that mariners were angry the entrance was left unmarked in the meantime.[^1]

On December 6, 1904, the lighthouse was sold to A. H. Merrill for $1,650.[^1] Its lens was taken to the Maumee Bay range lights and then shipped to the lighthouse depot at Buffalo, New York.[^1] LighthouseFriends says no one knows what became of the lens.[^1]

By the late 1920s the island belonged to Captain George Craig.[^1] A Toledo paper of the time reported that vandals had wrecked the house, leaving the bare walls, which it called "a monument to the service this light rendered," as LighthouseFriends quotes it.[^1]

In 1933 the Associated Yacht Clubs of Toledo took a five-year lease on the island and worked to restore the house and refinish the tower.[^1] LighthouseFriends says they gave it up because the island was too remote for many of their members.[^1] On Palm Sunday in 1965 a tornado reportedly blew the lantern off the lighthouse.[^1]

A not-for-profit group formed in 1991 to restore the light did not succeed.[^1] In 2002 a buyer planned to restore the light and open the island to the public, and built three vacation cabins on it before a Michigan judge issued a stop order for want of a building permit.[^1] In 2009 ice floes caused substantial damage to at least one of the newer structures on the island.[^1] In 2022 the island and the lighthouse passed to a new private owner.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is privately owned.[^1] LighthouseFriends says its grounds and tower are closed, and the US Lighthouse Society's record lists the station as not open to the public.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse is best seen by boat, and that some summer Sunday cruises pass the island.[^1]

LighthouseFriends describes the lighthouse as now just a shell of what it once was.[^1] After the 2009 ice damage, it says, it seems only a matter of time before the island disappears into Lake Erie.[^1]
