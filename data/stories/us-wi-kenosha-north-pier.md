Kenosha North Pierhead Light stands at the outer end of Kenosha's north pier, on the Lake Michigan shore of Kenosha County, Wisconsin. Its cast-iron tower, 50 feet (15 m) tall, was built in 1906. The nomination says it was first lighted in 1907, but LighthouseFriends dates its fourth-order lens and flashing white light to 1906.[^1][^8]

## The place

The town was called Southport before it became Kenosha. "As early as 1837, the people of Southport requested that the U.S. Congress appropriate funds for making improvements to the harbor."[^1] Congress answered in 1844 with "a Federal appropriation of $12,500 to fund additional improvements to the harbor."[^1] The federal government had already built a lighthouse on shore to mark the harbor in 1842, which it named Southport Light.[^1] In 1850 the community "renamed itself and incorporated as the town of Kenosha."[^1]

A Corps of Engineers team was dispatched to Kenosha in 1865 "to keep the harbor's entrance free of shifting sand" and "to refurbish the timber piers."[^1]

## Building the light

The first light on the north pier is disputed. The nomination says that in 1856 "a 12-foot-tall timber beacon was erected atop the north pier," with a sixth-order lens that "could be seen for 9 miles in clear weather."[^1] LighthouseFriends dates a wooden beacon of twelve feet, measured to its focal plane, to 1848.[^8] The two sources agree on the beacon's height and disagree on its date.

The beacon that followed was a taller wooden one. The nomination says "A new taller beacon built of wood was placed on the north pier in 1867," with "a fixed red sixth order Fresnel lens."[^1] LighthouseFriends says a sixth-order lens giving a steady red light went into the pierhead tower that year.[^8]

The light moved outward as the pier grew. LighthouseFriends says it was moved 320 feet (98 m) east to the end of the extended pier in 1875, and that it was moved seaward by roughly 100 feet (30 m) in each of the years 1880, 1881, 1883 and 1884.[^8]

In 1893-94 the Lighthouse Board fitted a conduit between the pierhead light and an outer light, so that a lantern could be run out to make a range.[^4][^8] The Board's report says "A conduit was built on the north pier for running in and out the lantern shown from its outer end," and that "The conduit is 104 feet long and extends shoreward to the pierhead light-tower."[^4] LighthouseFriends says the lantern rode on a small car along the conduit.[^8]

The pier was then rebuilt. The Corps of Engineers built the north pier in 1899 and 1900, with wooden pilings and rock-filled cribs.[^1] The Board's 1901 report says that "Owing to the widening of the entrance to Kenosha Harbor and the reconstruction of the north pier, the sixth-order light contained in the pierhead tower was moved to the outer end of the pier, about 380 feet eastward and 70 feet northward of its old position."[^7] The same report describes an elevated walk built for "a distance of some 1,040 feet."[^7]

The present cast-iron tower went up at the end of a 750-foot (229 m) pier in 1906. The nomination says the fourth-order lens "from the old Southport Lighthouse on shore was removed and installed in the new pierhead light, which was first lighted in 1907."[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the switch from a sixth-order fixed red light to a fourth-order flashing white light, using the lens from the Kenosha Lighthouse, to 24 May 1906.[^8]

## The keepers

The archive's roster starts in 1906, the year the tower was built, and runs to at least 1940.[^9] Louis N. De Diemar was the first head keeper, for "Louis N. De Diemar (1906-1907)." Charles E. Young followed for "Charles E. Young (1907-1932)," a quarter century, and Edward W. Knudsen for "Edward W. Knudsen (1932-at least 1940)."[^9]

The assistants turned over fast. Four second assistants are listed for 1906 alone, including "Antonio Pome (1906-1906)" and "Frank Wagner (1906-1906)."[^9] The first assistant who stayed longest was Frederick M. Bennett, for "Frederick M. Bennett (1916-1938)," twenty-two years on the roster.[^9] A laborer, "Clarence Linderman (at least 1919-)," is also listed.[^9]

LighthouseFriends spells the first head keeper's name "DeDiemer."[^8] The archive lists two similar names, "Anton J. DePrang" (1906) and "Anton De Frang" (1911), as separate entries. This account keeps the archive's separate entries.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The schooner Evaline ran into the pierhead light in 1891. The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1892 says that "The schooner Evaline ran into the light-house on the night of November 20, damaging the watchroom."[^2] LighthouseFriends adds that the collision also carried away a closet and a service table.[^8]

On 25 November 1893 a gale brought another schooner into the harbor. The Board's report says that the rear beacon of the Kenosha Pierhead light was "slightly damaged by a schooner entering the harbor during a gale on the night of November 25, 1893," and that "The beacon was repaired."[^3]

In 1899 a gale destroyed the pierhead light. The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1900 says that "The pierhead light and conduit destroyed by a gale on November 1, 1899," were "reestablished five days later on a post erected 75 feet in front of the rear beacon."[^5] The same report gives a different date in another entry: "Structure carried away in gale November 1; light reestablished on temporary structure November 5, 1899."[^6] Five days after 1 November is 6 November, so the two entries disagree by a day.

The nomination says that in the early 1980s a storm "severely damaged" the cast-iron walkway along the north pier, which was then dismantled.[^1]

## After the keepers

The lens was electrified in 1925, the nomination says, and LighthouseFriends says the fog signal was changed to an air diaphone that year.[^1][^8]

During the 1960s the fourth-order lens was taken out for a modern beacon. The nomination says the lens "is presently on display at the U.S. Coast Guard Station at Kenosha."[^1] In 1997 a fog detector was added to the lantern gallery, and "The aids to navigation at the lighthouse are now entirely automated."[^1]

The nomination also records a break-in. A first-story port-light was "sealed following a 1982 incident when vandals broke through it into the lighthouse."[^1]

## Today

The National Register nomination describes the light as Coast Guard property, and says it "continues to guide vessels navigating Wisconsin's Lake Michigan waters."[^1]

LighthouseFriends reports that in June 2008 the Coast Guard, having judged the light excess, offered it at no cost to eligible public and nonprofit bodies.[^8] After no qualified group took it on, the light went on the General Services Administration auction website on 18 October 2010. The minimum bid of $10,000 was not received before the auction closed on 2 December.[^8] The tower has no living space, and a buyer would need a lease from the Corps of Engineers, which owns the pier.[^8]

A second auction opened in May 2011. It closed on 15 July that year with a winning bid of $17,000 from John Burhani, a local artist who saw the lighthouse as the place for his studio.[^8] After the interior was renovated in the spring of 2012, the tower was opened on select days so the public could see his work.[^8] LighthouseFriends later calls Heather McGee the lighthouse's owner, and it describes an artist-in-residence program she set up there.[^8]

Visitors can walk out the north pier to the light. The nomination says the property is "accessible on foot by way of the harbor entry's north pier at the south end of Simmons Island Park."[^1]
