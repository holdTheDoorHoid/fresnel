The Jones Island Rear Range Light is a red-and-white tower on Jones Island in Georgian Bay, in Carling Township, Ontario.[^5][^11] It is the back light of a pair that leads ships into Parry Sound.[^4] Its front light stands on Gordon Rock, about 2.7 km away.[^1][^3] The Canadian Coast Guard now plans to demolish the rear light.[^11]

## The place

In 1891 Staff Commander John G. Boulton of the Royal Navy surveyed the channels into Parry Sound. He described the coast as having "upwards of 4,000 islands and dry rocks".[^1] He recommended five new lights, and the Department of Marine adopted his plan.[^1] Four of the five formed two ranges: Walton Rock and Snug Island made the Snug Island Range, and Gordon Rock and Jones Island made the Jones Island Range.[^1]

Ships from the west passed south of the Red Rock light and lined up on the Snug Island range, then on the Jones Island range. When a quarter-mile from Gordon Rock, they turned for the light on Hugh Rock.[^1] The Parks Canada register says the rear tower was built because regional trade was growing with the exploitation of natural resources.[^4]

Jones Island takes its name from David A. Jones, a beekeeper who used the island from 1880 to 1884, then gave up the costly venture.[^1] The register puts the rear tower on the island's north-east tip.[^4] LighthouseFriends places it near the southern end of the island.[^2] Quoting an older Department description, LighthouseFriends also puts the light on the south-west point.[^1]

## Building the light

The Department gave Charles Mickler of Collingwood a contract of $3,165 for the five lights.[^1] He had built the Pointe au Baril range lights in 1889, and he did this work in the 1893 season.[^1] Inspector C. F. Cox found so many defects that the Department would neither accept the lights nor pay for them until the work was put right.[^1]

The repairs were put off until spring. The Department's own foreman of works, W. H. Noble, finished the job, re-shingling the roofs, re-siding the walls and renewing the cornices.[^1] The extra work came to $647.40, charged against the contract price.[^1]

The lights went into operation on 29 September 1894, with Thomas W. Huff as the first keeper.[^1] The rear light is a combined house and tower. The register describes a wooden tower rising from the centre of the hipped roof.[^5]

The Coast Guard's list gives the rear light a focal height of 18.7 m and a tower of 15.5 m, with a fixed white light.[^3] The front light has a focal height of 12.4 m and an 11.1 m tower.[^3] The list gives the rear light's nominal range as 17 nautical miles, while the register gives 8 nautical miles.[^3][^4] The two sources disagree, and this page does not choose between them.

At the opening of navigation in 1902 the range buildings were painted white throughout. They had previously had red stripes on the side facing the channel.[^9]

## The keepers

Thomas W. Huff was born in Yorkville, Toronto, in 1845, and moved with his parents to Collingwood at twelve.[^1] He settled in Parry Sound in 1865, working first as a builder and painter and then as an engineer on a small steamer. When he was appointed keeper in 1894 he was forty-nine and working as a jeweller, watchmaker and gunsmith.[^1] His salary was $400 a year, and he was also expected to keep the Hugh Rock light.[^1]

A few months after the lights opened, Parry Sound's mayor, Thomas Walton, complained about Huff. The complaint came through Adam Lawson, keeper of the Red Rock light, who had reported that the lights on Gordon Rock and Hugh Rock were not being lit regularly.[^1] LighthouseFriends infers that Huff's work improved, because he stayed on through 1900.[^1]

LighthouseFriends' keeper list runs Huff to 1901 and gives Edward Taylor as head keeper from 1901 to 1910.[^1] The Department's keeper list for the twelve months ended 31 March 1909 names "Ed. Taylor" at "Jones Island, Georgian Bay".[^10] Ernst A. Greer followed, from 1916 to 1917, and Joseph G. Dixon from 1917 to 1919.[^1] No source here names a keeper for the years 1910 to 1916.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 3 May 1901 the steamer *Arthur Orr* struck a shoal while entering Parry Sound on the Jones Island range, according to the Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901.[^8] The Department's officers later found and buoyed a small uncharted pinnacle near the range.[^6] The report places the rock 450 feet north-east of the range, and records that the master of the *Arthur Orr* was satisfied it was the rock he had struck.[^8]

In September 1901 a gale blew down the Black Rock beacon, a different light at the entrance to Parry Sound on the same approach.[^7] The sources name no rescue at the range.

## After the keepers

On 7 June 1915 the front light changed from fixed red to fixed white.[^1] On 24 August 1915 the rear light was fitted with a duplex lamp and a 25-inch long-focus reflector.[^1] No source here dates the end of resident keeping at the range.

The rear light was recognized as a Federal Heritage Building on 27 March 2008.[^5]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and the grounds and tower are closed.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the light is best seen from the water, and that Georgian Bay Marina runs tours that pass it.[^2]

The Coast Guard's list describes both range lights as operating 24 hours, but also marks them seasonal, although the structures stay in place all year.[^3]

The Coast Guard now plans to demolish the rear light.[^11] In a letter to Carling Township dated 9 July 2025, it reported "extensive structural deterioration, including failing foundations, severe water and insect damage, and compromised support columns."[^11] It estimates that making the structure safe would cost more than $1 million. After demolition it would install an 80-foot tower about 15 metres away, between the present tower and the water.[^11] Lis McWalter, a Carling resident, first learned of the plans in November 2025. She has asked the West Parry Sound municipalities to back a petition to pause the decision.[^11]

Visitors can see the light from the water. The grounds and tower are closed, so the island should not be landed on, and the tower should not be climbed.[^2]
