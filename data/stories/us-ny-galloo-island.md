Galloo Island Light stands at the southwest end of Galloo Island, in eastern Lake Ontario, near the approach to Sackets Harbor.[^1] Congress appropriated money for it in 1819, and it was first lit in October 1820.[^1][^2] The tower that stands today was finished in 1867.[^1][^12] The Coast Guard automated the light in 1963, and the tower is now privately owned and closed to visitors.[^1]

## The place

On 3 March 1819 Congress appropriated $12,500 for a lighthouse on the island, near the outlet of Lake Ontario.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the light was meant to mark the outer edge of a group of islands and shoals that vessels met on their way to the St. Lawrence River or to Sackets Harbor, and that David A. Ogden received $411.37 for thirty acres on the southwest tip of the island.[^1]

Naval Lieutenant Charles T. Platt reported on the station in November 1838.[^1][^2] He wrote that the lake then stood three feet (about 0.9 m) above its usual elevation, and that the shore of the island was now twenty rods (about 100 m) farther in than when the light was built. He put that change down as much to high water as to the shore washing away.[^2] He also described a surge "impelled often by gales that have the sweep of the whole lake," which, he wrote, "envelop the outermost extremity of the island with dashing billows."[^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says Elisha Ely was contracted to build the dwelling and tower, and that the light was first exhibited on 4 October 1820.[^1] A Sackets Harbor paper described it the next day.[^1] As LighthouseFriends quotes that description, "The light is distinctly visible at a distance of 22 miles on the lake."[^1]

The dates do not all agree. The clipping file records the station as "Established in 1820," but Rowlett's directory gives "station established 1829."[^2][^12] This story keeps 1820, the date in the clipping file and in LighthouseFriends.[^1][^2]

LighthouseFriends says a fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed in the tower in 1857.[^1] In 1864 the Board noted that the station needed "various and extensive repairs and renovations," and on 7 April 1866 Congress appropriated $15,000 for repairs and renovations.[^1][^2]

LighthouseFriends says a new tower and dwelling were begun late in 1866 and finished the following year, in gray limestone quarried on the island. It gives the new tower a height of sixty feet (18 m). Rowlett's directory gives 55 feet (16.5 m) and dates the present tower to 1867.[^1][^12]

The fog signal was not built until the 1890s.[^4][^5] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1893 says the signal was "authorized by the act approved February 15, 1893."[^4] The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1897 gives the authority as an act of Congress of 11 June 1896, and says the signal was "practically completed on June 30, 1897." It says the signals were made with a 10-inch whistle regulated by a Crosby automatic apparatus.[^5] The two reports do not agree on which act authorized the work.

In the fiscal year that ended 30 June 1899 the assistant keepers' dwelling was rebuilt, and earthen pipe sewers were laid from both keepers' dwellings.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the gray limestone tower was painted white in March 1902, so that it would be more conspicuous by day.[^1]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster for the station opens with "Zenas Hastings (1820-1837)" and includes Coast Guard crew listed through 1961.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the honor of tending the lighthouse went to Hastings, and calls it "the first to be built by the United States on Lake Ontario."[^1] The roster has Goodale Lewis succeeding Hastings in 1837.[^3]

LighthouseFriends notes that "Most keepers on Galloo Island stayed for just a few years," although a few stayed much longer.[^1] The roster gives F. Byron Johnson as head keeper from 1876 to 1906. LighthouseFriends dates his service as head keeper from 1877 to 1906.[^3][^1]

Robert C. Graves served as first assistant from 1903 and as head keeper from 1906 to 1933.[^3] A Lighthouse Service notice of his retirement says he "was retired from active duty June 1, 1933, after 40 years of Government service," and that during his years on the island "he had many hazardous adventures" in crossing to the mainland in small boats.[^11] The notice also says he entered the Lighthouse Service as keeper on 19 February 1903. The roster has him as first assistant from that year, so the two records describe his first post differently.[^11][^3]

The assistant's name is spelled two ways. The Commissioner's report for 1919 gives "Willie E. Frazier, assistant keeper," and the 1921 report gives "William E. Frazier." The roster lists William E. Frazier as first assistant from 1915 to at least 1930.[^10][^9][^3] LighthouseFriends quotes Frazier, when he left the island for the winter, on the island's shared farming: "the thirty acres are split up among the four families."[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In August 1914 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that Keeper Graves towed a small power boat four miles after it had run out of gasoline.[^7]

LighthouseFriends describes what it calls Graves's most courageous rescue, in 1915. A father and son in a newly bought motorboat encountered a squall near the island, and Graves "made his way out to the boat through the battering seas." It says he brought off both men and a trunk.[^1]

On 19 April 1919, the Lighthouse Service Bulletin says, Graves and Assistant Keeper Frazier went to help the disabled power boat *Louis Donald*, which carried "10,500,000 minnows." LighthouseFriends dates the same rescue 18 April, and says they "towed it back to the island." The men aboard were fed and lodged at the station until 22 April while they repaired their engine.[^8][^1]

In 1921 the Commissioner's report records that "William E. Frazier, assistant keeper of Galloo Island Light Station, New York, towed the disabled power boat Glinda," with two men aboard, to Grenadier Island.[^9]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says that "In 1927, an air diaphone replaced the old steam whistle." It says that in 1928 the light was electrified and "its characteristic changed to a group of three flashes every twelve seconds."[^1]

The light was automated in 1963. LighthouseFriends says this was done by changing the power from direct to alternating current and running a line to the Coast Guard boat station on the island, so that its crew could work the light and fog horn by remote control. "Despite protests from boaters," it says, the change went ahead.[^1]

In 1997 the Coast Guard "declared the station surplus," saying it did not want to keep paying to maintain the aging buildings. With "no governmental agencies interested in the lighthouse," the General Services Administration put the property up for auction with a minimum bid of $50,000.[^1] The tower was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1983, according to the same account.[^1] "The four bids submitted for the lighthouse were opened on September 27, 2000," and the winning bid, $87,654, came from a private buyer.[^1] The brick fog-signal building and the cast-iron oil house were not included in the auction, as they are part of an adjacent twenty-five-acre parcel "owned by the state Department of Environmental Conservation."[^1]

## Today

LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse "is privately owned," and that its grounds, dwelling and tower are closed.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists the station as "Open to Public: No."[^3] Rowlett's directory says that in 2022 the island was bought by new private owners, "apparently including the light station property," who say they will preserve all of the island as a nature reserve.[^12]

The sources disagree about the light itself. LighthouseFriends says the tower "still houses a light maintained by the Coast Guard." Rowlett's directory says the light has been "Inactive since 2011," and that it was deactivated in the late summer of 2011 and replaced by a lighted buoy offshore.[^1][^12] No source read for this story settles which is current.
