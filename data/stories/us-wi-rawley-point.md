Rawley Point Light stands on a sandy headland of Wisconsin's Lake Michigan shore, four miles north of Two Rivers, a tall iron frame that began life in Chicago. The government's records call the place Twin River Point; the name Rawley Point took over later.[^1]

## The place

The Lighthouse Board noted in 1870 that Twin River Point, a few miles north-east of Two Rivers, held a place on the lake's west shore much like Big Sable Point on the east, and that shipwrecks there in the 1860s showed the danger.[^1] The Board wanted a tower of 100 feet (30 m) with a third-order lens. A light had already burned at Two Rivers itself from 1853 to 1859, but that site was judged too far south to mark the point.[^1] The Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources says 26 vessels were lost or stranded on the point before the lighthouse went into service, and calls the sinking of the steamer *Vernon* in 1887, with 36 lives lost, the most tragic in the point's history.[^2]

The name has its own story. According to LighthouseFriends, a trader named Peter Rowley set up at the point in 1835 and surveyors named it for him in 1841; the spelling later became Rawley. Lighthouse Board records used "Twin River Point" almost without exception, and the Coast Guard has said "Rawley Point Light" since 1956.[^1]

## Building the light

The Board asked for money in 1871 and 1872, and received $40,000 on 3 March 1873. Work began that August and went slowly, because materials were hard to land and the diggers struck quicksand. By 7 November the crew had driven pilings into the sand and capped them with timber and cement to carry the stone foundation. They resumed on 23 April 1874.[^1] The brick tower and dwelling were finished that October, but the third-order Fresnel lens arrived late and the light was first shown on 7 December 1874.[^1]

Some sources date the light here to 1853. The National Register file for the station says that "there has been a lighthouse at this location since 1853" and that the 1853 keeper's house still stands, and the Wisconsin DNR likewise says the Coast Guard has operated a lighthouse here since 1853.[^3][^2] LighthouseFriends, by contrast, dates the first light here to 1874 and puts the 1853 light at Two Rivers.[^1] The Digital Archive's keeper roster begins in 1874.[^4] The sources disagree.

A steam fog signal followed. Materials and workmen for it were landed on 17 July 1890, and the signal was started on 10 October 1890.[^5] By the end of the fiscal year, 30 June 1891, its pair of 10-inch steam whistles had run 521 hours, using 5 gross tons of coal and 96 cords of wood.[^5]

The brick tower, the Board reported, was in bad condition because of defective brick.[^5] In fiscal 1892 the station was repaired, the tower "strengthened", a barn built and the yard fenced and seeded.[^6] It was not enough. In fiscal 1894 the Board reported that the tower "continues to crack and crumble" and planned to take the iron tower from the discontinued Chicago River light, raise it and re-erect it here.[^7] The Chicago River light had been discontinued on 9 November 1893.[^7] The light moved to the new tower on 19 November 1894, by the Board's reckoning in its list of changes; LighthouseFriends gives 20 November.[^8][^1] The iron tower stood 47 feet from the old one.[^8] The National Register's HAER form describes the result: about 20 feet of new corner post added at the bottom, new lower stairs, and a watchroom added at the top, with the upper watchroom and the lantern coming from Chicago. It puts the tower at 111 feet (34 m) and the focal plane at 113 feet (34 m) above the lake.[^3]

In 1895 the old brick tower came down to the roofline of the dwelling, and the passage that had joined them became a new two-story section of the house.[^1] The Board's report for fiscal 1896 says the removal of the old tower and the addition to the keeper's dwelling were completed, the dwelling enlarged and the oil house moved into line with the iron tower.[^9]

## The keepers

The Digital Archive's roster, drawn from the federal registers of keepers, lists keepers and assistants from 1874 into the 1970s.[^4] The first head keeper was Samuel A. Stone, listed 1874 to 1880, and a Ruth E. Stone appears as first assistant from 1875 to 1878, followed by William W. Stone to 1880.[^4] The shared surname is all the roster shows, and it does not say how they were related. In 1880 Lawrence A. Easson became head keeper, with Alice Easson as first assistant, both until 1881.[^4]

Andrew J. Allen went from first assistant (1887) to head keeper (1888 to 1895) by the roster's dates; LighthouseFriends puts the start of his charge at 1889.[^4][^1] LighthouseFriends quotes a letter he wrote to the district inspector in 1892, when his assistant John F. Craig quit, saying the man "would not obey my orders or take my advice." Craig had sent to Kansas for a woman he called his wife; Allen noted that her mail came under a different name, and that when he told the pair to stop dancing jigs, which had shaken plaster loose, they answered that they would do as they pleased.[^1]

Some careers appear to run through the ranks, though the roster links them by name only. It shows a Joseph Napiezinski as second assistant from 1893, first assistant from 1898, and head keeper from 1906 to 1911; and a Joseph Kimmes as second assistant in 1904, first assistant 1909 to 1916, and head keeper from 1923 to 1935.[^4] Everitt C. Sterritt served as head keeper from 1915 to 1923.[^4] After the Coast Guard took over, the roster lists a separate run of uniformed crew from 1944.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Life-Saving Service's report for fiscal 1897 describes a call from the point. At 10 a.m. the assistant keeper of Twin River Point lighthouse came to the Two Rivers life-saving station to say that a schooner was a mile north of his post, six miles from the station, "with rudder gone and partially water-logged".[^10] The station keeper and the crew with him went out in the surf boat under sail and oars, reached her at 11.30, and relieved her nearly exhausted crew at the pumps. A tug returning from the fishing grounds towed the schooner to Manitowoc, 13 miles south.[^10] The report does not name the assistant keeper.

In October 1917 Keeper Sterritt wrote to the Milwaukee inspector that, "during a severe rainstorm", with a north-east wind of 40 to 50 miles an hour, one of a flock of wild geese flying south struck a panel of lantern glass and made a hole about 14 inches across. The bird that struck fell to the bottom of the tower outside with three others, and another passed through and fell in the clock room. He boarded up the panel until the wind fell and he could set new glass.[^11]

On 15 June 1934 the keeper of Big Sable Point, across the lake in Michigan, reported seeing the Twin River Point light 53 miles away, though the normal range of such lights was about 20 miles.[^12]

## After the keepers

The steam whistle gave way to an air diaphone, which the Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded in 1919.[^13] LighthouseFriends dates the change to 23 September 1919 and adds that electricity came in 1930.[^1] In 1936 the Bureau of Lighthouses declared much of the reservation surplus, and Wisconsin's State Conservation Commission acquired 73.44 acres the next year, the start of Point Beach State Forest.[^1]

According to LighthouseFriends, the third-order lens was removed in 1952 after a prism was damaged, and twin aerobeacons were installed; a fire on New Year's Day 1962 destroyed the newer part of the dwelling while three families lived there; and the station was automated in 1979.[^1] The HAER form, written in 1979, says the tower no longer held its Fresnel lens but a 36-inch airport-type beacon with a 25-mile range.[^3]

## Today

The tower is a Coast Guard light in Point Beach State Forest. The Wisconsin DNR says the lighthouse may be viewed and photographed from the beach and that tours are normally not available.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports that the dwelling is used as a rental cottage for Coast Guard personnel and that the dwelling and tower are closed.[^1] Visitors pay the state forest entrance fee.[^1]
