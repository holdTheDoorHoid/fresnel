Block Island Southeast Light is a red-brick Gothic Revival tower joined to a keepers' duplex, standing on the top of Mohegan Bluff at the southeast corner of Block Island, about 12 miles (19 km) off the Rhode Island coast.[^3] It was lit in 1875, and by the 1980s the sea had eaten so much of the bluff that the building had to be jacked up and moved inland to survive.[^2][^3]

## The place

Block Island sits across the sea lanes between Long Island Sound and Rhode Island Sound, and the National Register nomination of 1984 calls it a "Stumbling Block" for ships.[^2] The island already had a light at its north end, first authorised by Congress in 1829, but that light gave little help to vessels coming up from the south and southeast.[^2] In 1856, on a petition from the Collector of Customs at Newport, Congress appropriated $9,000 for a second Block Island light. The Light-House Board spent the money rebuilding the existing light instead, and the plan for a southern one lapsed.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the wreck of the *Palmetto* on the reef below Mohegan Bluffs in 1858 sparked urgent calls for a fog signal and a beacon there.[^6]

The effort revived in 1872, when Nicholas Ball, a Block Island merchant and hotel proprietor, circulated a petition among shipping firms. It said the island was "passed by hundreds of vessels daily."[^2] Congress then appropriated $75,000 for a first-order light and a fog signal in the spring of that year.[^2]

## Building the light

The Third Light-House District's engineers drew the plans under the district engineer, Col. I. C. Woodruff, and they were approved in July 1873.[^2] The land came from George G. Sheffield, whom Lighthouse Friends names as the seller of the parcel, for $1,350.[^6] The contract went to T. H. Tynan of Staten Island, who built the tower and dwelling in 1874, while another contractor supplied the cast-iron superstructure.[^2] The Landmark nomination names Paulding, Kemble & Company as the makers of the lantern and gallery.[^3] The contractor's name varies by source: the 1984 nomination has "T. H. Tynan", the Lighthouse Society's archive lists "John H. Tynan", and Lighthouse Friends gives "M.S. and J.H. Tynan".[^2][^1][^6]

The Landmark nomination describes the design as High Victorian Gothic and says the buildings were among the most elaborate the Board built in the 1870s.[^3] The dwelling held two mirror-image apartments: the north one for the keeper, the south one shared by the first and second assistants.[^2] The total cost, including the steam fog signal, was $79,500, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^6] The 1984 nomination describes a tower with an octagonal granite base and an octagonal pyramidal shaft under a sixteen-sided lantern.[^2] The sources give its height inconsistently, as a 52-foot (16 m) tower in Lighthouse Friends and the Providence Journal story on the Foundation's site, and as 67 feet (20 m) in the Landmark nomination.[^6][^5][^3]

The original apparatus was a fixed six-panel Fresnel lens made in Paris by Barbier and Fenestre in 1873, lit by a four-wick lamp that burned lard oil until kerosene replaced it in the 1880s.[^2] The keeper's task was to wind the clockwork that fed the lamp every three hours, and the lamp used about a thousand gallons of oil a year.[^2] The 1984 nomination gives 12,188 candlepower for the light as first shown.[^2] The 1984 nomination and the Foundation both date the first lighting to 1 February 1875.[^2][^5] Lighthouse Friends says the steam siren began sounding on 1 January 1874, while the tower was still being finished, and the 1984 nomination agrees only that the fog signal "apparently" worked in 1874.[^6][^2]

Visitors came early. The 1984 nomination says President Ulysses S. Grant, vacationing on the island, made a point of visiting the new light and judged it a worthwhile expense.[^2] The Landmark nomination says the station soon appeared in tourist guides as a place to see.[^3] An 1893 guidebook, quoted on the Foundation's site, says that at visiting hour "someone of the three families whom the cottage harbors leads the way up the three flights of winding stairs" to the lantern.[^5]

## The keepers

The archive of the U.S. Lighthouse Society has 87 roster lines for the station between 1873 and 1990, drawn from the keepers' registers.[^1] The first keeper was Henry W. Clark, 1873 to 1887, appointed on 4 November 1873 at $600 a year.[^1][^9] His widow, Sarah Whaley Clark, was the daughter of the keeper of Point Judith Light.[^5] His successor was Simon Dodge, who had come as second assistant in 1883 and risen to first assistant in 1886. The register shows him acting keeper from 11 July 1887 and keeper at $700 from 21 June 1888.[^7][^9] Dodge married Henry Clark's daughter, Bessie, who the Foundation says was born and raised in the lighthouse and was known on the island as "Aunt Bess."[^5] Dodge was born in 1858 and the archive records him as keeper until his death in 1922, with his pay rising from $600 to $900.[^7] The Foundation's own list gives 1923 for the end of his term, so the year of the change is not settled.[^5]

Dodge's first assistant from 1887 was Henry Clark's son, Willet H. Clark, who held that post until 1922 and then took over as keeper until 1930, by the archive's count.[^1][^7] The Foundation's list has Willet Clark as keeper to 1930, and Jean C. Napier, quoted there, says he served until 1931.[^5] The Foundation remarks that it was not uncommon for lighthouse families to marry into other lighthouse families.[^5] Napier, a great-granddaughter of Henry Clark, is quoted there on night duty in the tower, which she describes as "a 12-hour shift."[^5]

The assistants changed often. Charles E. Wescott served as second assistant from 1887 to 1905, the longest stint in that post.[^1] Among the later head keepers, the archive lists Carl F. W. Anderson (1930 to 1938), Earl E. Carr (1938 to 1943), Earl A. Rose (1944 to 1947) and Howard B. Beebe (1948 to 1950). After that the roster is mostly Coast Guard personnel.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Annual Light-House Board reports show how hard the fog signal worked. The 1984 nomination puts the average at 831 hours a year, which it calls one of the busiest fog-signal stations in the country.[^2] In the report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1899, the first-class steam siren, in duplicate, ran for about 1,003 hours.[^4] The first signal was a Brown steam siren with a trumpet seventeen feet long sticking out of the east wall of the fog-signal house, and its boiler water came by underground pipe from the pond on the reservation.[^2] The kerosene engines that replaced the steam boilers date from 1906, and a brick fog-signal building replaced the original frame one in 1908.[^2]

The Foundation says that in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries many shipwreck victims were rescued at the site and sheltered in the lighthouse.[^5] No specific wreck is tied to the station by the sources read here, apart from the 1858 *Palmetto*, which came before the light.[^6]

## After the keepers

New technology came to the station in stages. The Coast Signal Service kept a station with signal flags and semaphore at the light in the 1890s. In the fiscal year ended June 30, 1899, the Light-House Board reported its abandoned house removed to the shore end of the Great Salt Pond breakwater, and the same entry records a new telephone linking the light to the north light, the island's two life-saving stations and the Weather Bureau.[^2][^4]

The light itself changed in 1929. The Landmark nomination says the old lens was set aside and a revolving apparatus was installed, turning the fixed white light into a flashing green one, to stop mariners confusing it with Montauk, which also showed a fixed white light.[^3] The 1984 nomination says it was electrified in 1929.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1932 says the light "has in recent years been changed from fixed" white to flashing green and electrified.[^8] A radio station stood on the reservation by 1929 and a radio beacon followed two years later.[^2] In 1934 a Typhon replaced the siren.[^2] Lighthouse jurisdiction passed to the Coast Guard in 1939.[^2] The archive's roster shows Coast Guard names at the station from the 1940s.[^1] The nomination was still describing the station as manned by a resident keeper in 1984, and a note added on the 1990 listing record says it was no longer manned.[^2]

The bluff was the larger problem. The Landmark nomination says about 300 feet (91 m) of lawn lay between the building and the edge of Mohegan Bluffs in 1874, and almost 250 feet (76 m) has since eroded away. Islanders had warned before it was built that the site was a bad one.[^3] The Coast Guard began to measure the loss in the 1950s, and by 1983 only about 80 feet (24 m) remained, according to the Foundation.[^5] The Block Island Historical Society began its campaign in 1983 and the Foundation was set up in 1986.[^5] The National Trust for Historic Preservation put the lighthouse on its endangered lists in 1990 and 1991.[^3] The Foundation reproduces a Providence Journal story that says the Coast Guard had meant to demolish the building and had set aside $125,000 for it.[^5]

In 1990 the Coast Guard turned off the old light and put an automated aero-beacon on a steel tower behind it, and removed the mercury bath on which the lens had turned.[^3][^5] The transfer to the Foundation is dated 27 July 1992 in the Landmark nomination, but the Journal story says 1991.[^3][^5] The move took place between 10 and 28 August 1993, when the building was about 55 feet (17 m) from the edge.[^3] Expert House Movers carried it out under the Army Corps of Engineers, to a plan by Peter Friesen, and the nomination counts a total of 360 feet (110 m) pushed in three legs.[^3] The Journal story puts the net distance at 245 feet (75 m) and says the movers lifted the building on 38 hydraulic jacks, in all a campaign requiring three Acts of Congress and about $2 million.[^5] The Journal adds that no hurricane threatened that summer.[^5]

The light was out for four years. The Landmark nomination says the Coast Guard agreed to relight the tower in August 1994 and installed a first-order Fresnel lens taken from Cape Lookout Lighthouse, North Carolina; the Journal story says only that it came from a deactivated lighthouse in Virginia.[^3][^5] The relighting ceremony was on Saturday, 27 August 1994.[^5]

## Today

The Coast Guard transferred the station to the Southeast Lighthouse Foundation, a local non-profit, which the Landmark nomination dates to 1992 and the Journal story to 1991.[^3][^5] The Foundation describes the building as a National Historic Landmark and runs it for visitors.[^10] A museum of Block Island's maritime history, in the restored keepers' quarters, opened in summer 2021, and the Foundation says one part of the interior is to become a residential rental to pay for upkeep.[^5] Its website lists the grounds as open daily from sunrise to sunset, the museum and tower tours on a seasonal schedule, and winter as closed.[^10] The tower can be entered only on a guided tour.[^10] The site is 1.8 miles (2.9 km) from the town of New Shoreham.[^10]
