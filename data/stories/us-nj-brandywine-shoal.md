Brandywine Shoal Light rises from a concrete pier in about 8 feet (2.4 m) of water in lower Delaware Bay, some 8.8 miles (14 km) west-northwest of Cape May Point.[^1][^2] The shoal it marks first held a lightship, then a wooden tower that the sea wrecked within a year, then the first screw-pile lighthouse in the United States, and since 1914 this tower, which was the country's first offshore light with both pier and superstructure of reinforced concrete.[^1]

## The place

Brandywine Shoal is a patch of shallow water in the open lower bay, near the main shipping channel between the Atlantic and the ports of the Delaware River. The National Register nomination calls it a serious obstacle to navigation since colonial times, and says the light on it is the first offshore lighthouse a mariner meets on entering the bay from seaward.[^1]

Marking it began with a ship. In 1823, the nomination says, the federal government's General Superintendent of Lighthouses, Stephen Pleasonton, assigned a lightship to the shoal, but the exposed position made it hard to keep the vessel on station.[^1] A pile-founded light tower followed in 1827; heavy weather and seas destroyed it in 1828, a lightship was put back, and the shoal was marked that way until 1850.[^1] Congress's attention to the light that year shows in the Library of Congress's newspaper files. In February 1828 a House member proposed money "for preserving and securing" the light-house, explaining that about $36,000 had already been appropriated for building it.[^3] The act printed that June carried $10,000 "for preserving and securing the Lighthouse on the Brandywine Shoal."[^4] The USLHS archive lists a Francis Elberson as principal keeper in 1827, the only name it gives before 1850.[^5]

Records disagree about where the station lies. The Lighthouse Service's own reports file it under Delaware, and the 1915 Commissioner's report puts the shoal "on the Delaware side of the main ship channel"; the National Register nomination and our record place it in Cape May County, New Jersey.[^2][^1]

## Building the light

The 1850 lighthouse was an experiment. Responsibility went to the Army's Bureau of Topographical Engineers, with Major Hartman Bache in charge and Lieutenant George G. Meade assisting. They used the English engineer Alexander Mitchell's screw-pile idea, but added cross-bracing between the piles after the iron-legged tower on Minots Ledge, Massachusetts, was found to vibrate and twist, and they ringed the structure with a pile fence and riprap against ice.[^1] Putnam's 1917 history of the service says nine iron piles with broad screws three feet across were bored about six feet into the sand, and that the house stood in six feet of water with a separate ice breaker around it; it adds that this was the first lighthouse built in the United States by this method.[^6] LighthouseFriends dates the first lighting to 28 October 1850 and gives a total cost of $62,000 across several appropriations.[^7]

A special Act of Congress authorised a Fresnel lens for the new light, and in 1851 only three American lighthouses had one: Navesink, Sankaty Head and Brandywine Shoal.[^1] LighthouseFriends names John Burton as the keeper who first lit it, and the USLHS archive lists a John Burton as principal keeper for 1850 to 1851.[^7][^5]

The structure needed constant care. The nomination cites the Light-House Board's 1854 report for extra bracing, more riprap to replace lost sand, and zinc bands against corrosion, and notes that an inspection after the hard winter of 1874 found one ice-fender pile broken and others badly rusted.[^1]

By 1911 the old light was judged worn out. The nomination says Congress appropriated $75,000 on 11 March 1911; the Commissioner of Lighthouses says the act was of 4 March 1911.[^1][^2] Bids were advertised in September 1912 for a cast-iron caisson tower, and none proposed to build the whole structure. In February 1913 two bids came in: an iron structure at $175,000 from the Phoenix Construction Company and a reinforced-concrete one at $69,850 from the Interstate Construction Company of Mobile, Alabama. The Bureau accepted the concrete bid, but wrote in a clause making the contractor guarantee it for a year.[^1]

The concrete pier was built on the beach at Lewes, Delaware. The nomination says it was finished on 14 May 1913 and weighed about 225 tons; the Commissioner says it was launched on 10 July 1913. Both agree it was towed out and set down on 6 August 1913, on 74 wooden piles, and then pinned with 12 concrete piles.[^1][^2] On 21 October 1913 a hurricane sank the contractor's barge *Hero* beside the site. Second Assistant Keeper Henry P. Marshall, in charge of the station, reported to the inspector that she "parted her anchor chains and at 7 P.M. drug on the South ice breaker staving a hole in the stern below the water line and drifted to the present sight (sic), about 100 yds. E.N.E from station and sank in a S.W. hurricane." Two of the contractor's men took the station boat and brought the barge's captain and deck hand to the light, where the keeper looked after them. The new pier was unharmed.[^1]

The Commissioner records the superstructure complete on 8 October 1914, the station "in commission" on 20 October 1914, and the whole project finished on 1 November 1914 at a cost of $74,960.34.[^2] The Bulletin had earlier announced that the old light would be put out on 18 October and the new one shown on 22 October, so the exact night of the change is not settled by these sources.[^8] The new lighthouse had a three-story dwelling with quarters for three keepers and a third-order lens showing an occulting light, dark for 3 seconds every 30, of 12,000 candles and visible 13 miles in clear weather. Its fog signal was a Daboll reed box with three trumpets, worked by a 5-horsepower kerosene engine and air compressor.[^2] The old tower's superstructure was taken down, but its platform stayed, with an oil house and storehouse that the keepers kept using.[^1]

## The keepers

The USLHS archive, drawing on the keeper registers, lists more than 180 appointments here between 1827 and 1970; the table beside this story carries them.[^5] Some patterns show in it. Samuel Willoughby was principal keeper in 1852 and again from 1854 to 1861. In February 1855 he wrote to the district inspector about drifting ice that shook the house, and LighthouseFriends quotes him: "It certainly is astonishing how the house does to bear the great pressure occasioned by the ice."[^7][^5] William E. Spicer, principal keeper from 1874 to 1890, served longest among the nineteenth-century heads.[^5]

One career shows the ladder of ranks. The archive lists an Eben L. Hudson as second assistant from 1873, first assistant from 1875 and principal keeper from 1893 to 1896.[^5] Edward F. Warrington was first assistant from 1924 and principal keeper from 1939 to 1951, the archive's longest run at the station; LighthouseFriends says no one served longer.[^5][^7] The new tower had quarters for three keepers.[^2] After the Coast Guard took over, the archive shows Coast Guard personnel here through at least 1970.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Bulletin recorded two storms. On 10 to 12 April 1918 a northeast storm made the sea break into the basements of the towers at Brandywine Shoal and Harbor of Refuge, flooding them and shifting the heavy riprap about.[^9] In early February 1920 another storm destroyed a large part of the decking of the platform around the old station, according to the Bulletin's account.[^10] The Commissioner later asked for more riprap at Brandywine Shoal and a steel armoured belt at the water line to take the impact of ice and moving riprap.[^11]

On 8 September 1918 Keeper Charles R. Manlove and First Assistant John Colofen took two sailors from a disabled patrol boat into the light and gave them food and dry clothing. Two days later Colofen and one of the sailors set out to row 9 miles to Cape May; the sailor wore out on the way, and Colofen rowed on alone to report to the naval authorities.[^12]

In December 1935, LighthouseFriends reports, the captain of a Norwegian freighter heard the fog signal sounding on a clear night, took it for a distress call and radioed the Coast Guard at Cape May. A boat found Warrington in great pain, and a doctor at Lewes called it "just colic."[^7]

## After the keepers

The USLHS archive gives 1974 for automation, and LighthouseFriends calls Brandywine Shoal the last manned station in Delaware Bay.[^5][^7] The National Register nomination, which dates the light station's period of significance 1914 to 1955, says the tower now runs as an automated aid to navigation with a solar-charged system, and that the 1850 foundation and platform were removed about 1963.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that the Coast Guard declared the lighthouse excess in June 2011.[^7] The National Park Service's 2013 report on the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act lists it among stewardship transfers to Brandywine Shoal Lighthouse, Inc.[^13]

## Today

The tower is closed to the public and can be seen only from the water. The owner's mission page says a 2014 winter storm took away the catwalk between the dock and the lighthouse, cutting off the Coast Guard's aids-to-navigation team from servicing the light, and that the group plans to restore access, stabilise the crumbling concrete and eventually prepare the building for visitors. It also says the lighthouse has been closed to a keeper since the 1970s and that entering it is trespassing.[^14] LighthouseFriends says the original Fresnel lens is on display at Tuckerton Seaport in Tuckerton, New Jersey.[^7]
