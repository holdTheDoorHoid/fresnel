The Caveau Point rear range light stands on the north side of Cheticamp, on Cape Breton Island in Nova Scotia. It is the back light of a pair that guides ships into Cheticamp's Eastern Harbour.[^1][^3] The pair was put in operation on 3 October 1897.[^1] The rear light now shows a fixed red light from a white square tower with a red stripe.[^2]

## The place

Cheticamp Island, about five kilometres (3 mi) long, lies off the village. The entrance to the Eastern Harbour lies between the island's north-east end and Caveau Point on the mainland.[^1]

The entrance has a shoal in it.[^1] The Department's report for fiscal 1910–11 describes a bell buoy marking "Caveau shoal" at the entrance to Cheticamp harbour, painted black with the name in white letters on its deck.[^7]

The range was there to bring ships past that shoal. Mariners were told to follow the Caveau Point lights up to the line of the harbour range lights, and then follow that line through the dredged channel to the wharves.[^1] The harbour had its own range lights from 1890. The Caveau pair went up in 1897, further north, to help mariners reach the outer harbour.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory describes the front light as an approach range that guides vessels between the north end of Cheticamp Island and the Caveau Shoal.[^3]

## Building the light

Lighthouse Friends prints the Department's account of the first pair. The lights were put in operation on 3 October 1897, from square wooden towers with sloping walls 8.2 m (27 ft) high, built by contract by Mr. Fulgence Aucoin at a cost of $447.[^1]

## The keepers

Germain Chiasson kept the range lights from 1897 to 1912, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] The Department's statement of light-keepers and stations for the year ended 30 June 1906 lists "Chiasson, Germain" against Caveau Point Range Lights.[^6] The statement of stations and lightkeepers in the forty-third annual report, printed in 1911, also puts Germain Chiasson at the Caveau Point range.[^5]

In 1912 N. D. Desveaux took charge. Lighthouse Friends names him as head keeper until at least 1923.[^1] The Department's keepers' table for fiscal 1914–15 gives his appointment at the Caveau Point range as 27 April 1912.[^13]

Lighthouse Friends lists the keepers after him as Jos. D. Deveaux, to 1926; Severin F. Chaisson, from 1926 to 1931; Luc H. Chaisson, from 1931 to 1946; Paul L. Chaisson, in 1946; and Gregoire Patrick Lefort, from 1946 to at least 1964.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says Severin Chaisson was dismissed in February 1931 "because of political partisanship." It gives no source for that.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1912 a life-saving station opened at Cheticamp, a separate establishment from the range lights. After an inspector visited in September 1911, he recommended a station with a power lifeboat and a lookout house on a point about two miles from the town. The recommendation was approved, and steps were taken to set the station up early the following year.[^8]

The Department's report for fiscal 1912–13 says the station was completed that year. It had a boat-house near Aucoin's wharf and a lookout house with a tower about 183 m (600 ft) from the Caveau Point range lights. The lookout house was connected to the boat-house by telephone. The boat was a 7.6 m (25 ft) twin-screw craft of 8 horsepower, crewed by a coxswain, an engineer and four men, and two more men were appointed to the lookout tower. The crew took up their duties in October 1912.[^9] The Department's life-saving expenditure table for 1912–13 gives $5,620.01 for Cheticamp.[^14]

A later Department table lists a telephone line joining the lookout station, the lifeboat house and the Cheticamp point light.[^12] A Cheticamp light-keeper, not named in the report, stayed for some time at Sorel to learn the engine and boat before the boat was sent to Cheticamp.[^11]

On 1 October 1912 a vessel stranded at Cheticamp. The formal investigation, held at Montreal on 10 October by Commander Lindsay, put the stranding down to the vessel striking "an unknown and unmarked shoal," and found the master blameless.[^10] The entry does not say where at Cheticamp she struck, and this story does not tie the stranding to Caveau shoal. The printed entry does not name the vessel.[^10]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says the range lights were changed from fixed white to fixed green in 1950.[^1] The wooden towers were replaced by skeleton towers in the 1970s.[^1][^3] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society gives 1973 to 1980 for the front light; Lighthouse Friends and the Lighthouse Directory give 1976.[^1][^3][^4] Around 1990 an enclosed tower was built for the rear light, according to Lighthouse Friends and the Lighthouse Directory.[^1][^3] The Preservation Society lists the rear in 1989 as a white structure with a red stripe, 8.2 m high.[^4]

The Preservation Society also says the front light had a sixth-order dioptric lens in use from 1922 to 1941, and was automated by 1993.[^4] The Coast Guard list now shows both range lights as fixed red.[^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the range lights.[^1][^3] The Coast Guard list gives the rear light a focal height of 29.2 m (96 ft), a range of 14 nautical miles, and an 8.5 m (28 ft) white tower with a red stripe, in year-round operation.[^2] The Lighthouse Directory gives a focal plane of 31 m (103 ft).[^3] Lighthouse Friends says the grounds and tower are closed, and that the rear light stands alongside Route 30, the Cabot Trail, just north of Cheticamp.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory says the site is private property and closed, though the lighthouse can be seen from the road.[^3] Keep off the private site; the tower is closed.
