Baker Island Light stands on the highest ground of a small island at the southwest entrance to Frenchman Bay, off Mount Desert Island in Hancock County, Maine.[^1][^2][^5] The light was first shown in 1828.[^1][^2] Its first keeper, William Gilley, had moved to the island with his family about 1812, according to Eliot; the park gives about 1806.[^4][^5]

## The place

Baker Island is one of the outermost Cranberry Isles. Acadia National Park has owned a majority of its 162 acres since 1967, and the park says the island has nine historically recorded shipwrecks.[^5] The hazard was the water around it. The park describes shallow shoals, and a rocky bar that connects the island with Little Cranberry Island at low tide.[^5] Charles W. Eliot, who wrote a family history of the Gilleys, notes that the island "has some dangerous ledges near it."[^4] The National Park Service says President John Quincy Adams ordered the light built because the shallow shoals of the Cranberry Isles were a shipping hazard.[^5] The National Register nomination calls it "the first light station established in the vicinity of Mount Desert Island."[^1]

The Gilleys were living on the island when the light came. The NRHP nomination does not say when settlers arrived, but Eliot does.[^4][^5] He says that about 1812 William Gilley of Norwood's Cove and his wife Hannah moved to the island with their three children. He writes that the island "lay in the sea, unoccupied and unclaimed," so "they simply took possession of it."[^4] The park's page dates the move to about 1806, with the same three children.[^5] Eliot's account is the one this story follows, and the difference is noted here. The family grew to twelve children, according to the park and LighthouseFriends.[^5][^3]

## Building the light

Eliot says that "the United States built a lighthouse on its highest part in the year 1828."[^4] LighthouseFriends describes the first tower as built of rubblestone, 26 feet (8 m) tall, with an octagonal lantern, and says the tower and keeper's dwelling cost $3,800.[^3] The park's lighthouse page also describes the first station as stone, and says its stone structures were replaced in 1855. The park's Baker Island page instead says the 1855 tower replaced "the original wooden structure."[^6][^5]

LighthouseFriends reports that an 1842 inspection by I.W.P. Lewis found the tower and dwelling cracked, and that a report in 1853 called the tower "entirely worthless."[^3] The tower and dwelling were rebuilt in 1855.[^1][^3] The nomination says the tower, built that year, is 37 feet (11 m) from its base to the lens focal plane.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 43 feet (13 m) to the centre of the lantern.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives 45 feet (14 m) for the brick tower and says the rebuild cost $4,963.[^3] A fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed in 1855.[^2][^3]

Later works followed. The Lighthouse Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1895, records an oil house built on the island.[^7] The nomination also dates the oil house to 1895.[^1] The report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1899, says a telephone line to Northeast Harbor was completed; LighthouseFriends dates the line to 1898.[^8][^3] The nomination notes a 1903 reinforcement of the tower, though its nature is unclear, and says a fuel house was built in 1905.[^1] LighthouseFriends describes the 1903 work as an external four-inch brick wall.[^3] A 1912 Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists the illuminant change to incandescent oil vapor.[^11]

## The keepers

Eliot writes that William Gilley was appointed light-keeper in 1828, "with a compensation of three hundred and fifty dollars a year in money, the free occupation of a house, and all the sperm-oil he could use in his household."[^4] He held the post until the Whig party came to power. Eliot dates his removal to 1849. LighthouseFriends says 1848.[^4][^3] Eliot says Gilley was offered the chance to keep the post if he joined the dominant party. He refused, saying he "would not change his political connection for all the lighthouses in the United States."[^4] Eliot also says that Hannah Gilley "was able to teach all her children to read, write, and cipher."[^4]

LighthouseFriends says the Gilley sons pressed their claim to the island against later keepers. It says the matter went to court, and the government was awarded nineteen acres around the light and a right-of-way to the boat landing.[^3]

The roster lists seventeen keeper lines, from William Gilley to the Coast Guard's Howard Bryant Clement (at least 1951 to at least 1953).[^2] It names no woman keeper.[^2] Alden H. Jordan served at Baker Island from 1867 to 1883. The US Lighthouse Society roster also lists him at Bear Island from 1883 to 1899, under the same person record, and that is how this story links the two stations.[^2][^13] LighthouseFriends says Vurney L. King, keeper from 1915 to 1930, managed to get a car to the island.[^2][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Life-Saving Service's annual report for 1880 describes the schooner *Challenge*, of Boothbay, Maine, with five men aboard, which sprang a leak and sank about five miles south of Petit Manan Light on the night of 8 March. The crew rowed for fifteen hours and landed on Baker's Island. Exhausted, with badly frost-bitten hands and faces, they could not reach the station, and local people cared for them until the next morning. Then the crew of Station No. 4 went out to the island in the surf-boat and took them to the station.[^9]

In January 1882 the brig *Shamrock*, of Charlottetown, Prince Edward Island, bound from Boston to Calais in ballast with eight men aboard, ran ashore on the northwest part of Baker's Island in a thick fog. She lay about three-quarters of a mile south of the life-saving station on Little Cranberry Island, which the report numbers No. 4.[^10] The life-saving crew carried out the brig's anchors to try to haul her off. She was floated off on 22 January, thirteen days after she went ashore, with the help of wreckers and a steam tug, and was taken to Southwest Harbor for repairs.[^10]

## After the keepers

The light was deactivated in November 1955 and reactivated in April 1957 as an unattended aid to navigation, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1957 as the year it was automated. The park's page gives 1966.[^2][^5] The Coast Guard kept the tower when the land was transferred.[^1] The park acquired the station, except the tower, from the Coast Guard in 1958, and acquired the tower in 2011.[^6] The Coast Guard proposed to discontinue the light in 1991 and again in 1997. Local mariners and residents objected, and it stayed lit.[^5][^3] The light was added to the National Register on March 14, 1988.[^5] A new solar-powered LED optic, a Vega VLB-44, was installed in 2017.[^2]

## Today

The tower is still an active aid to navigation, and the Coast Guard maintains the light.[^6] Visitors cannot go inside the tower. The park says they can see the station from the water or on guided tours.[^6] The island has no dock, so only small craft can land.[^6] For many years, ending in 2024, a local company offered a five-hour boat cruise to the island, interpreted by an NPS park ranger. The park now offers a video as an alternative for visitors who cannot make the trip.[^5] The lens is now on display at the Fisherman's Museum at Pemaquid Point Lighthouse.[^3]

The tower needs repair. In August 2026 Friends of Acadia reported raising more than $400,000 for its restoration. The tower, the group says, "now requires urgent restoration to address cracking masonry, deteriorating exterior surfaces, and failing paint."[^12]
