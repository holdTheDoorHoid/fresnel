Baltimore Harbor Light stands on a caisson in Chesapeake Bay, near Gibson Island in Anne Arundel County, Maryland, at the mouth of the Magothy River.[^1][^3] Its National Register nomination calls it "the last lighthouse built in the Chesapeake Bay."[^1] The nomination says the light was completed 28 years after it was first requested. Its own dates, 1890 and 1908, make the gap eighteen years.[^1][^3]

## The place

The light is "located in 24 to 29 feet of water on the western edge of the main ship channel," the nomination says, "marking the south entrance to Craighill Channel."[^1] The Lighthouse Board's report of 1893 gives the reason. "A light-house is most wanted at the mouth of the Cutoff Channel," it says. The report also names "the impressible character of the shoal" and "the liability to damage or destruction by fields" of moving ice.[^6]

The Evening Star of Washington announced the new station in May 1904. It was "to be known as the Baltimore light station," the paper said, and "the lighthouse that will show above the water will be an octagonal shaped building, two stories high and constructed of brick."[^7]

## Building the light

The Lighthouse Board "first requested a light for this locality in 1890."[^1] The 1895 report describes the appropriation as "the amount, appropriated by Congress, $60,000."[^4] The nomination gives 18 August 1894 as the day Congress made that appropriation.[^1]

The Board's report for 1895 says that borings at the new site "have shown that there is a layer of soft mud extending 65 feet below the surface of the shoal," about 20 m, and that "immediately below this is a stratum of sand."[^4] The nomination says that borings "made at the selected site" in 1895 found "a layer of soft mud 55 feet thick" (17 m).[^1] The Board's report for 1899 also uses 55 feet, and says the expense of building in that mud "will be great."[^5]

A test disk pile was tried in 1898. "On September 9, 1898, an unsuccessful effort was made to plant it at the site," the Board's report for 1899 says.[^5]

Sources disagree on when Congress approved the extra money. LighthouseFriends says the Board made its recommendation to Congress annually three times, until the additional $60,000 "was approved on July 1, 1902."[^3] The Chesapeake chapter dates the approval to June 1902.[^12] The nomination says a caisson design "was accepted on June 10, 1902," and that Congress appropriated the rest on "April 28, 1904."[^1]

Only one bid came in, at "$80,000 over the allocated $120,000." "The bid was rejected and the contract re-advertised," the nomination says. "In early 1904, William H. Flaherty and Frederick Martin Lande of Brooklyn, New York, were the sole bidders."[^1] The Chesapeake chapter of the Lighthouse Society gives a different award date, 1903, "at a price of $91,900."[^12]

The size of the caisson is also in dispute. A Baltimore paper, as the nomination quotes it, gave "48 feet square and 23 feet high." The nomination's footnote says a National Archives plan calls for "a 32-foot square caisson, not a 48-foot square caisson."[^1] LighthouseFriends, quoting the Board's later description, gives a caisson "48 feet wide at the bottom, 46 feet on top, and 21 feet high."[^3]

The metal work was won by Atlanta Machine Works, and "by August 9,1904, twelve courses of the caisson were assembled and launched" at the Lazaretto depot.[^1] The nomination quotes a Baltimore paper's warning that "the ice in winter rushes down the Bay with terrific force."[^1]

The caisson was sunk on 19 September 1904, on 91 wooden piles. Two days later "heavy seas filled the cylinder," and the caisson settled "about seven feet out of level."[^1] On 12 October a storm turned it "flat on its side."[^1]

The contractor defaulted in the spring. "Flaherty had defaulted on his contract," the nomination says, and the company that had bonded him "spent the next three years trying to right the caisson."[^1] Its crews used "80 tons of large stones placed on the high side" and heavy weights, and "succeeded in righting the caisson" on 30 June 1907.[^1] The nomination also says that "By November 20, 1906, the assembly was within 17 degrees of vertical," a date that comes before the righting it describes.[^1]

The caisson was finally sunk "82 feet below the water level." The brick dwelling and lantern were built on it, and the light was "commissioned with the lighting of a fourth-order lens" on 1 October 1908.[^1][^12] The owners' website gives 10 September 1908 as the day construction was completed.[^11]

The first fog signal was a bell. "Baltimore Lighthouse was outfitted with a fog bell signal when first built," the nomination says, but "it was replaced with a foghorn sometime before 1923."[^1] The Chesapeake chapter dates the foghorn's replacement of the bell to 1923.[^12]

## The keepers

John Berentsen, spelled "Berensten" by LighthouseFriends, was "the station's first and longest-serving head keeper." LighthouseFriends says he "was awarded an efficiency pennant for having the model station in the district for 1914."[^3] The Bulletin's pennant list for fiscal 1914 prints his name as "John Berentsen," and the Chesapeake chapter spells it "Berentson."[^8][^12] The roster lists "John Berentsen (1908-1916)" as head keeper.[^2]

Oscar P. Olsen was first assistant from 1913 to 1916 and head keeper in 1916, 1917 and 1918 in several stints.[^2] A 1916 Bulletin names "Oscar P. Olsen, assistant keeper of Baltimore Light Station" among those who gave assistance "to the yacht."[^9]

John Spence was head keeper from 1918 to 1921. LighthouseFriends says he "was forced to take a leave of absence without pay due to illness," and that "Thomas P. Midgett became head keeper of the lighthouse in 1921."[^3][^2] The roster has Midgett as head keeper until 1923.[^2]

The roster's assistants form a long list, from "William T. Midgett (1908-1909)" to "Crawford R. Austin (1922-1923)."[^2] Edward Linton, an assistant in 1921 and 1922, appears in the roster and in the Bulletin.[^2][^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 31 July 1921 six men had been drifting in a yacht all night.[^10][^3] The Bulletin commended "Thomas P. Midgett, keeper" and "Linton, assistant keeper" of Baltimore Light Station, Md., for helping them.[^10] LighthouseFriends prints Midgett's own note of the day, which says that "the yacht and crew of six men were seen" 1/4 mile off with a signal of distress. The men, he wrote, "went off rejoicing singing Jesus Lover of My Soul."[^3]

## After the keepers

In 1923 the illuminant was changed to acetylene. LighthouseFriends says the work was "changed to acetylene at a cost of $1,280," and that the fog bell "was discontinued, and its keepers removed."[^3] The nomination describes the fog signal change differently: the clock-operated signal was "discontinued and was replaced by a fog bell Buoy 6C on May 1, 1923."[^1] The nomination says that "the keeper at Baltimore Lighthouse was transferred to Point No Point Lighthouse," and that the duties were "turned over to the keepers at nearby Sandy Point Shoal Lighthouse."[^1]

In May 1964 the Coast Guard tested nuclear power at the light. "A 4,600-pound SNAP-7B Strontium-90 powered 60-watt isotopic fuel cell generator" was installed by crane. The nomination says "the test ran for a year."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the generator was removed "two years later," and the Chesapeake chapter says "Just after one year."[^3][^12]

About 1983 the windows were bricked over and a steel door was fitted "to keep out vandals."[^1] In 1989 and 1990 the Coast Guard buoy tender Red Birch worked on the caisson, and the nomination says the Red Birch repaired the roof flashing in 1990.[^3][^1] The light was listed on the National Register on 2 December 2002.[^12]

It was offered in 2004, and no non-profit group came forward, LighthouseFriends says. It went to public auction through the General Services Administration, and "the auction ended on June 28, 2006 with a final bid of $260,000."[^3][^11][^12]

## Today

Baltimore Harbor Light is now owned by BHL, LLC of Annapolis.[^11] The owners say they want to open the light "to private prearranged tours," and their own website describes "a group of private individuals."[^11]

The terms of sale require the Coast Guard to be "granted access to the lighthouse for occasional checkups on the still-active light."[^3] The tower is closed to visitors. The nomination says "access to the lighthouse is via boat."[^3][^1]

A lighthouse cruise out of Annapolis passes the light, LighthouseFriends says.[^3]
