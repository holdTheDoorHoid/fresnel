The Île Sainte-Marie light stands on the St. Mary Islands, in the Gulf of St. Lawrence off Quebec's north shore, near the villages of Chevery and Harrington Harbour.[^1][^2][^3] Its reinforced concrete tower was begun in 1912 and completed in 1913.[^2] In 1981 the light moved to a steel skeleton tower, and the islands are now a migratory bird sanctuary.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The islands lie about 20 km (about 11 nautical miles) from Harrington Harbour, within a sanctuary of thirteen islands.[^3] Environment and Climate Change Canada calls it one of the richest nesting sites in the Gulf of St. Lawrence, and many seabirds nest there.[^3] The Quebec heritage register says the Department of Marine and Fisheries chose the site to mark the north shore of the Gulf, which was then still inadequately marked.[^1] The register's wording is translated from French here.

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends dates the start of the reinforced concrete tower and a wooden dwelling to 1912, under the supervision of F. E. Cote, and their completion to 1913.[^2]

The Department's report for the fiscal year ending March 31, 1913 lists St. Mary island among new aids in Quebec. The entry is for a 4.6 m (15 ft) reinforced concrete tower and a wooden dwelling, to be completed the following season. The table names F. E. Coté in its contractor-or-foreman column, the same name LighthouseFriends gives as supervisor, and records $5,315.63 spent that year.[^4]

The sources disagree on the height. LighthouseFriends gives the tower as 8.8 m (29 ft), with a focal plane 34.7 m (114 ft) above the water, and says a revolving fourth-order lens showed two flashes every fifteen seconds.[^2] The register gives it as barely 5 m (16.5 ft).[^1] The 1913 table's 4.6 m is closer to the register's figure than to LighthouseFriends' 8.8 m, and this story does not settle the question.

The Department's report for the fiscal year ending March 31, 1914 lists, against St. Marys Isle, the completion of the concrete tower and dwelling and the installation of a fourth-order dioptric lens.[^5] LighthouseFriends also gives a fourth-order lens.[^2]

The Department's table for the fiscal year ending March 31, 1921 records compressors and engines of 10 horsepower at St. Mary Island.[^6] LighthouseFriends dates the station's fog-alarm building to 1920 and 1921, and says its diaphone gave a four-second blast each minute as needed.[^2] A year later the Department's report says the engine installation was completed, work that had started the year before.[^7]

The Deputy Minister's report for the fiscal year ending March 31, 1922 names "St. Mary island (Labrador)" among four stations where new apparatus had been installed and was giving entire satisfaction.[^8] The 1913 and 1914 tables put the station under Quebec, and the 1921 and 1922 changes tables put it under the Prince Edward Island heading. The register places it in Quebec, so the "Labrador" label looks like an error.[^4][^5][^6][^7][^1]

In the fiscal year ending March 31, 1923 the Department provided and installed a revolving pedestal with a mercury float at the station.[^9] In the fiscal year ending March 31, 1924 it provided storm doors.[^10]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends lists the head keepers as John Harbour (1913 to 1914), Joseph Galibois (1914), F. W. Osborne (1914 to 1948), Donald G. Osborne (1948 to 1956), Samuel S. Anderson (1956 to 1971) and Gordon Foreman (1972 to 1981).[^2] The first two served only a few months each. F. W. Osborne stayed on the island for roughly thirty-five years.[^2]

Donald G. Osborne began as an assistant to his father in 1937, at sixteen.[^2] In 1940 he was transferred to Belle Isle in Newfoundland, and after a year there he returned to St. Mary Island.[^2] He joined the Armed Forces in August 1942 and, after three years of service in England and the Mediterranean, went back to the island.[^2] He became head keeper in 1948, when his father retired. In 1956 the family left for Souris Lighthouse on Prince Edward Island, where he served as head keeper until his retirement in 1974.[^2]

In a 1984 interview with The Evening Patriot, as LighthouseFriends cites it, Osborne recalled that the lights had to be lit and the horn sounded by hand. The lamp burned kerosene vapour: air pumped into one tank drove kerosene up a heated tube.[^2]

## Today

In 1981 the light atop the concrete tower was discontinued and replaced by a light on a skeleton tower.[^2] The register says the concrete tower no longer serves as an aid to navigation, a steel open-frame structure does that job, and the station is now automated. It also says several buildings are in ruins.[^1] The Coast Guard list gives the present light as a white flash every 2.5 seconds, from a square skeleton tower 16.4 m (54 ft) high, with a focal height of 44.4 m (146 ft) and a range of 12 nautical miles. It is listed as seasonal.[^11]

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse. LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds and tower are closed, and that the light is best seen by boat.[^2]

The islands form the Sainte-Marie Islands Migratory Bird Sanctuary. It was established in 1925 to protect common eider and other seabird colonies during nesting season, and covers 3,980 hectares.[^3] Visitors are asked to read the sanctuary's restrictions before accessing the site.[^3]
