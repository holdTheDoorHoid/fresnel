The Sydney Range Lights are a pair of towers on the western arm of Sydney Harbour, Cape Breton, that a ship keeps in line as it comes in.[^1] The first pair went into service in 1905.[^1] Both towers have been rebuilt since, and in 2024 the range was changed to a new line with new towers.[^2][^3]

## The place

Two bars reach into the harbour from either side, the Northwest Bar and the Southeast Bar, and they narrow the navigable channel between them.[^4] A lighthouse was built on the Southeast Bar in 1872.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says the range lights came more than thirty years later, built near Point Edward to show the line for passing between the two bars.[^4]

The Department's report places the range buildings differently: they were "erected near Dixon point, on the southerly side of the west arm of Sydney harbour."[^1] The front tower stood on a point, on ground 4.3 m (14 ft) above high water and 50 m (165 ft) back from the water's edge, inside the sand bar.[^1] The report says the front light "should be visible 13 miles in, and over a small arc on each side of, the line of range." A ship keeps the two lights in line to stay on that arc.[^1]

## Building the light

The Department's annual report, printed in 1906, says the range buildings were erected under contract by P. L. McFarlane of Baddeck, N.S.[^1] The front tower was an octagonal wooden building with sloping sides, painted white, under a red iron lantern. The back tower was square in plan, with a square wooden lantern, also painted white.[^1] The front light was a fixed white catoptric light (one that works by mirrors), elevated 18 m (58 ft) above high water, and the back light was elevated 37 m (120 ft).[^1]

The sources do not agree on the height of the front tower. The report gives 53 feet, or 16 m, from base to the vane on the lantern.[^1] Lighthouse Friends prints 63 feet, or 19 m.[^4] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society lists 58 feet, or 18 m.[^5]

The Department's 1908 report, printed in 1907, says that "A fourth order lens with an occulting screen has been provided for this light."[^6] Lighthouse Friends dates the lens to 1907, and says it changed the front light from fixed white to revolving white.[^4]

## The keepers

John B. Rudderham was hired as the first keeper of the front light in 1905, at $250 a year, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^4] The Department's statement of light-keepers, printed in its 1911 report, lists "J. B. Rudderham" at the Sydney Range front light, appointed 15 January 1905 and paid $300 a year.[^7] The keepers' lists in the 1907 and 1908 reports print "Rudderham, S." for the same post and the same appointment date.[^8][^9] The sources disagree on that initial, and the name is not settled.

Alexander J. Lewis kept the back light.[^10] The Department's 1911 report prints the back-light entry as "B. A. J. Lewis," appointed 22 May 1905 and paid $180 a year.[^7] Lighthouse Friends says he was keeper of the back light until at least 1923, and that he started at $150.[^10]

Lighthouse Friends lists the later keepers of the front light as Howard Lewis (1913 to 1916), Gordon Rudderham (1916 to 1917), Andrew Musgrave (1917 to 1922), G. Rose (1922) and W. Daley (1922 to 1926). It has Roy Lewis keeping both lights until 1965.[^4][^10] Those later names have not been checked against the Department's lists. The sources give names, dates and pay, and very little about the people who held the posts.

## After the keepers

In 1965, when the lights were fully automated, Roy Lewis's post became that of a caretaker, Lighthouse Friends says.[^4] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society says the lights were automated by 1993, so the sources do not agree on the date.[^5]

Lighthouse Friends records the towers' paint in some detail. In December 1947 the superintendent of pilots asked that the range towers be painted international orange, so that they would show when snow was on the ground. A Notice to Mariners the following year said they had been painted yellow. They were white again within a couple of years, with a red horizontal band added. In 1972 the bands were changed from horizontal to vertical.[^4]

The rear tower's 1905 wooden structure was replaced with a skeletal tower in 2008 or 2009.[^10][^2] The front tower was found to be badly rotten. In 2010 the Department of Fisheries and Oceans built a replica of the 1905 structure, a twin to the Henry Island Lighthouse, and placed the refurbished iron lantern on top.[^5][^4]

## Today

The federal impact assessment for a Canadian Coast Guard project says "the existing two sets of ranges require relocation and upgrades." The project was meant to enable use of a deep-water commercial channel created under a public-private partnership in 2012, and the assessment says the work "will involve purchasing property."[^11]

The Lighthouse Directory says that in 2024 the range line was changed and new towers were required, and that the 2010 front tower has been inactive since then.[^2] Neither that source nor the Coast Guard list says what will become of the old tower.

The Coast Guard's 2026 list gives the new front light as a fixed green light with a focal height of 23.8 m. It stands on a 9.0 m square skeleton tower with a white daymark and a red vertical stripe, on a point on the east side of the North West Arm.[^3] The rear light lies at a bearing of 216°35' and 3,352.8 m from the front light. It shows a green light on for nine seconds in every ten, and the list gives its focal height as 60.2 m.[^3] The Lighthouse Directory gives the rear light's focal plane as 37 m (121 ft).[^2] The two sources do not agree on the rear light's height.

The Coast Guard is the owner and site manager.[^2] For the front light, the Lighthouse Directory calls the site and tower closed, and private property.[^2] For the rear light, it says the site appears open and the tower closed.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the grounds and tower of both are closed.[^4][^10] Keep off the point and do not climb the towers.
