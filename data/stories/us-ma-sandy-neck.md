Sandy Neck Light stands on the eastern point of Sandy Neck, a thin peninsula six miles (10 km) long that forms Barnstable Harbor on the north side of Cape Cod.[^1][^2] A light was first shown there in 1826 or 1827, depending on the source, and the brick tower that stands today was built in 1857.[^1][^3] The Lighthouse Service gave the light up in 1931 or 1932, and a steel tower took over its work.[^1][^3] The old brick tower lost its lantern room in 1934, and volunteers relit it in 2007. It is privately owned and closed to visitors.[^1]

## The place

The light sits at Beach Point, the eastern extremity of the neck, and it is also known as Barnstable Light.[^4][^1] LighthouseFriends says that the neck's shifting sands constantly redefine its margins. In the nineteenth century, it adds, Barnstable was an important port for fishing, whaling and coastal trade, and the neck was home to a try-works for rendering whale blubber into oil.[^1]

On 18 May 1826 Congress approved $3,500 for a light at the neck's eastern tip, and the town of Barnstable sold the light a two-acre parcel for one dollar.[^1]

The sea was already encroaching in the 1840s. In 1842 workmen were building a timber bulkhead to check the inroads the sea was making on the lighthouse property.[^1] The Light-House Board's 1891 report says the bulkhead, 71 feet (22 m) long, was rebuilt to protect the site against the sea.[^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says the light went into service on 1 October 1826. Its first light was shown from a sixteen-foot (5 m) wooden tower standing at the center of a brick keeper's dwelling.[^1] The USLHS record gives 1827 as the year the station was established, and the Barnstable Harbor history says the first light station was built on Sandy Neck in 1827.[^3][^4] The sources disagree about the first year, and the story does not choose between them.

The first light had ten lamps with fourteen-and-a-half-inch reflectors in two tiers.[^1] In 1838 Lieutenant Edward W. Carpender reported that the premises were in good shape, but believed the tower and dwelling should be separate, because a fire could destroy both at once.[^1] The same year a report recommended suppressing the four lamps in the upper tier to save fuel.[^1] An 1850 inspection found the lantern raised and seven lamps with fourteen-inch reflectors installed.[^1]

The present brick tower was built in 1857, just north of the original dwelling. LighthouseFriends gives its height as forty-eight feet (15 m).[^1] In 1880 the keeper's house was reported beyond repair, and a frame house soon replaced it. In 1887 two iron hoops and six vertical staves were set around the cracked tower to strengthen it.[^1]

## The keepers

The USLHS roster lists fourteen principal keepers from 1826 to 1932.[^3]

Joseph Nickerson, the first keeper, was paid $350 a year and served seven years, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1][^3] Henry Baxter took over in December 1833. LighthouseFriends quotes his first logbook entry, which records that he moved his family into the lighthouse at Beach Point that day.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Baxter's son James took over in 1844, but the roster carries Henry Baxter to 1846, and the sources do not reconcile the two.[^1][^3]

Thomas P. D. Baxter kept the light from 1846 to 1862.[^3] In 1862 he was traveling by dory to Barnstable through the icy harbor, when his leg was crushed between the dory and the ice. The injury led to gangrene, which killed him, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] His wife, Lucy Baxter, officially assumed her husband's duties, and the roster lists her as principal keeper from 1862 to 1867.[^1][^3] She resigned in 1867 and moved to Barnstable so that her children could go to school.[^1]

Edward Gorham kept the light from 1867 to 1875.[^3] Jacob S. Howes kept it from 1875 until his death in 1880. His widow, Eunice Crowell Howes, took over and stayed on until 1886, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3][^1]

George A. Jamieson kept the light from 1897 to 1906.[^3] In December 1898 a fierce storm took the family's chicken coop, with all forty chickens in it, and the family was sure the chickens had died. The coop washed up intact in Barnstable, with the chickens still aboard.[^1] A daughter of the keeper, interviewed in 1974 and quoted by LighthouseFriends, described her birth at the light. The family had a blanket signal for the keeper's return from shore: "Of course, there were no telephones, so whenever he went ashore they had worked out the blanket signal."[^1] On the day after Christmas in 1903, Jamieson left town by boat without his brother-in-law, and a squall came up. He was forced to turn around and spend the night at a friend's house. His wife, watching from the shore, lit the light at sundown and paced all night.[^1] He resigned from the Lighthouse Service in 1906.[^1]

James Jorgensen kept the light from 1906 to 1909.[^3] Henry L. Pingree kept it from 1909 until his death in October 1918. The Bulletin's death notice names him as keeper of Sandy Neck Light Station.[^5][^6]

The archive lists Henry M. "Harry" Bailey as keeper here from 1918 to 1921. The same archive record is his Monomoy Point Light entry for 1916 and 1917, so the archive connects the two stations.[^3][^7]

William L. Anderson kept the light from 1921 to 1932.[^3] A Bulletin list of assistance rendered, printed in the 1926 volume with entries dated in late 1925, includes a 2 December entry: William L. Anderson, keeper of Sandy Neck Light Station, rendered assistance to a disabled power boat with two men aboard. The entry does not print a year, so 1925 is inferred from the other dates in the list.[^8] The 1924 to 1929 Bulletin index lists him as keeper of Sandy Neck under its entries for assistance to vessels, at pages 119 and 256.[^9]

Simeon A. Orne, the last keeper on the roster, served in 1932. LighthouseFriends says he was furloughed when the light was given up.[^3][^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Like many Massachusetts lighthouses, Sandy Neck Light was plagued by storms, ice and erosion, LighthouseFriends says.[^1] On 15 December 1834 the keeper's log recorded a heavy gale from the southwest with snow. A schooner, the Enterprise, came ashore, and the keeper got her captain and two women ashore "with much trouble."[^1]

An October 1837 storm swept away six feet (1.8 m) of land from the station's east side.[^1]

In 1842 the engineer I.W.P. Lewis judged the lighthouse "another specimen of contract work where the Government had been losers by the operation." He noted that a long and dangerous bar extending from the point had cost many lives, and he recommended a screwpile beacon to mark it.[^1]

In 1875 the ice in the harbor was almost impassable from late January to the middle of March. Several schooners were caught in it, and some of their crews got to the light.[^1]

The Light-House Board's report for 1896 and 1897 records that part of the bulkhead was rebuilt.[^10] In the year to 30 June 1900 the boathouse, which had been wrecked by a storm, was rebuilt, and 160 running feet (49 m) of bulkhead was built to protect the site from the sea. The report does not date the storm.[^11]

## After the keepers

As Barnstable Harbor lost importance and the dunes pushed the light inland, the government decided to surplus the lighthouse in 1932, LighthouseFriends says.[^1] An automated acetylene light on a white steel skeleton tower about 200 feet (61 m) closer to the tip of the neck then took over the job of guiding ships into the harbor.[^1] The USLHS record gives the discontinuance as 1931, so the two dates stand side by side.[^3]

Two years on, according to LighthouseFriends, officials took the lantern room off the old tower and dropped it into the harbor, so that seafarers would not be confused by its reflections.[^1] Warren J. Clear bought the buildings and 1.93 acres in 1933 for $711.[^1] The Coast Guard leased the station from Francis and Margaret Ellis in December 1942, and the station housed a beach patrol until 1944. That year the property was sold to the radio personality Fred Lang. A private family bought it from Lang in 1950.[^1]

A restoration committee formed in 2003 turned down government grants, because they would have required the lighthouse to be open to the public. The committee raised private donations for the repairs instead.[^1] The Great Lakes Lighthouse Keepers' Association supplied a mold for a ten-sided, cast-iron lantern room. On 20 October 2007, in the tower's 150th year, a group of supporters on a Hyannis whale-watch boat saw a cover come off the lantern room, and the beacon shone again.[^1] A Barnstable Harbor history places the restoration in the summer of 2007.[^4] In 2010 an LED beacon raised the light's range from 4.2 to 10.4 nautical miles.[^1]

## Today

The light is privately owned. LighthouseFriends reports that its grounds, dwelling and tower are closed, and the USLHS record says the site is not open to the public.[^1][^3]

Reaching the light by land takes a four-wheel-drive vehicle and a permit, or a walk of six miles (10 km) one way in sand, LighthouseFriends says. A distant view is possible from the marina at Barnstable Village.[^1] Visitors should keep off the private property and treat the tower as closed.
