Cape Forchu is the southern tip of the peninsula at the mouth of Yarmouth Harbour, Nova Scotia, where a light has shone since January 1840. The tower now on the point is the red-and-white "apple core", the first of its kind on any Canadian coast.[^1][^3]

## The place

Samuel de Champlain named the point "Cap Forchu", meaning a forked tongue of land, in 1604, according to the municipal timeline.[^4] By the mid-nineteenth century Yarmouth was a booming seaport, and the Friends of the Yarmouth Light say it was the second largest port of registry in Canada by 1878.[^6] The station was built to warn mariners of shoals and to mark the entrance to the harbour.[^1]

The first request for a light came from Yarmouth's shipping men. Lighthouse Friends dates their petition to 1836 and names John Killam among the petitioners; the municipal timeline says Yarmouth's representatives began petitioning in 1835.[^3][^4] The House of Assembly of Nova Scotia voted £750 in 1838 and £250 more the next year.[^3]

## Building the light

The first lightstation was built in 1839 and lit for the first time on 15 January 1840.[^1] Lighthouse Friends describes a wooden octagonal tower about 18 m (59 ft) high.[^3] The municipal timeline counts it the thirteenth lighthouse built by the Nova Scotia government and only the second in the province with a revolving white flashing light.[^4]

The first lamps were five reflector lamps and five bare Argand lamps on a revolving frame, as described in 1857; by 1874 the apparatus was ten lamps with 12-inch reflectors, six of them on a revolving frame and four fixed, looking out to sea.[^3] The municipal timeline dates a ten-lamp arrangement to 1877.[^4]

Fog signals came in steps. A fog bell was working by 1857.[^3] A steam whistle followed, which Lighthouse Friends and the heritage statement date to 1869 and the municipal timeline to 1868, with a first blast on 20 November 1868.[^1][^3][^4] In the year from June 1873 to June 1874 the whistle ran 1,336 hours and burned 78 tons of coal and seven cords of wood.[^3] A stone fog-alarm building replaced the wooden one in 1891, the timeline says.[^4]

The big change came in 1908. A second-order Fresnel lens, bought in Paris for $38,000 and weighing about 3,300 pounds, went into a lantern room built 12 feet taller; the municipal timeline instead gives 5 tons for the lens in its copper frame.[^3][^4] The Department of Marine and Fisheries' report for 1907-08 says a second-order single flashing light "has been received and will be put in operation before the close of the year".[^8] A later report describes the new apparatus as a second-order dioptric single flashing light, giving a flash every two and a half seconds, that "replaces the revolving white catoptric light", burning petroleum vapour under an incandescent mantle.[^7] The old keeper's house was judged poorly sited by 1911 and replaced in 1912 by the duplex that still stands, built to a standard Department plan for a keeper, an assistant and their families.[^1] A steel bridge across the gully between the dwelling and the fog-alarm building was added in the 1923-24 year.[^11]

## The keepers

James Charles Fox, a Royal Navy veteran who had been teaching school in Cornwallis, took the new light in 1839 and died on 27 March 1840, just over two months in.[^3] Within weeks of the lighting, the Friends of the Yarmouth Light say, he answered a skipper's complaint that the light had been dark with a letter to the Yarmouth newspaper, written in verse; it insisted the lamps had burned every minute of the night.[^5]

His son Cornelius John Thomas Fox succeeded him and stayed 33 years, longer than any other keeper at the cape.[^3] He gave notice in the Yarmouth paper in June 1843 that Sunday visitors were no longer welcome.[^5] In 1859 he saw a small boat with three young men capsize at the entrance to the sound; the neighbours' search found only a cap, and two bodies were recovered later.[^5] On 1 September 1873 he went on a pension and his son James took the post at $800 a year, part of which was to pay an assistant; James resigned the next year.[^3]

John H. Doane became keeper of the light and fog whistle on 1 July 1874.[^3] He had been a coasting-schooner master and pilot before that; his brother Thomas came to the cape as assistant.[^4][^5] The family lost six of ten children in these years, three of them in two months in 1876.[^4] John retired on 14 December 1904, and Thomas was appointed chief keeper on 31 December at $800 a year.[^4][^5]

Thomas Doane's name turns up in an official report. An inquiry into the grounding of the steamer *Cohan* near Bunker Island light found that the pilot who took her in was Thomas Doane, "lighthouse keeper and fog alarm engineer at Cape Fourchu", who was not a certificated pilot; the inquirer recommended a pilot commission for Yarmouth.[^9] In December 1916 a gale carried off his new-engined motor boat, which he had meant to use for the lobster season; a piece of it came up on a schooner's anchor in 1921.[^5] He retired in 1922, having served at the cape, the Friends say, for 48 or 49 years.[^4][^5]

Herbert Cunningham took over in 1922 and stayed thirty years, with a farm of pigs, hens and a cow beside the light; he reckoned he had climbed the tower stairs about 47,000 times.[^4][^5][^6] Albert Smith, who had been his assistant, followed in 1952, then Raymond Baker (Cunningham's son-in-law), J. E. Chetwynd, D. Earl Flemming, Wayne O'Connell and Lawrence Wentzell.[^3][^5] The station's last keeper, Marjorie Fairservice, left in 1993, though the Friends' keepers page says the last keeper was decommissioned in 1994.[^3][^4][^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 21 December 1875 the schooner *J.K. Howard* dragged her anchors in a heavy sea and a west-southwest wind and went ashore on Sunday Point; keeper Doane saw her signals, ran to Yarmouth and organised a boat, and most of the crew were taken off. The mate was lost, according to the Friends.[^5] The following December, the same account says, the harbour ice trapped Doane's sailboat on his way home and the tug *G. W. Johnson* towed him back.[^5] In July 1898 he sounded the fog whistle to raise the alarm for a house fire across the harbour at Kelly's Cove.[^5]

The Department of Marine and Fisheries recorded that the schooner *M. J. Solay* missed stays and stranded on Cape Fourchu in 1902 (the report prints the date as "November 31"), and that the crew of the Yarmouth lifeboat went to her aid.[^10] Lightning hit the tower twice at about 1:15 a.m. on 17 September 1939 and set fire to the wooden floor where lamp oil was stored; keeper Cunningham and helpers held it until a Yarmouth fire truck arrived.[^4] Electricity reached the cape in October 1940, and in the war years keepers watched for submarines and aircraft as part of coastal defence.[^4]

## After the keepers

By 1961 the old tower's timbers were rotten and it was feared it would not stand a hard Atlantic gale.[^1] The heritage statement says it was demolished in 1961 and the concrete "apple core" tower, nearly 23 m (75 ft) high, built the next year; Lighthouse Friends says the new tower began to rise beside the old one in 1961, and the municipal timeline says the old tower was pulled down in 1962.[^1][^3][^4] People were alarmed at how different the new tower looked, but it won most of them over.[^3] The Fresnel lens is displayed in the Yarmouth County Museum.[^3][^4]

In 1980 the station became the monitoring point for automated lights on Nova Scotia's south shore; it was automated and destaffed in 1993.[^1] The municipality bought the property to keep it as a tourist attraction, and in 2001 it became the first federally owned lightstation in Canada granted to a municipal government; the Friends of the Yarmouth Light took on stewardship that August.[^1][^2] The Friends' own history dates the transfer to 1 June 2000.[^6] In 2019 the municipality ended the Friends' lease and took over the site itself.[^3][^4]

## Today

The Municipality of the District of Yarmouth owns the station, and it is a public tourist site with grounds, a museum and a gift shop.[^3][^4] Since 2021 guided "Climb the Light" tours take visitors up 77 steps to the lantern room.[^3][^4] The tower is still an operating light.[^2] It is registered as a municipal heritage property (2001) and a provincial one (2003).[^1][^6]
