Ontonagon West Pierhead is a steel beacon at the outer end of the west pier at the mouth of the Ontonagon River, on Michigan's Lake Superior shore. A light has marked the pier since 1875. The tower there now dates from 1900, when an iron frame replaced the tower that a storm had carried away.[^1][^2]

## The place

The Ontonagon is the largest river flowing into Lake Superior's south shore, and a bar at its mouth kept ships from using it as a harbour.[^1] After the Minesota Mine opened near Ontonagon in 1848, vessels that brought in supplies and carried out copper had to anchor offshore and load and unload with the aid of lighters.[^1] The first piers at the mouth, built in 1851, proved no match for the lake.[^1] After Charles T. Harvey finished the Soo Canal locks in 1855, he was recruited to build piers at the river's mouth, and his 900-foot (274 m) piers proved inadequate as well.[^1] A petition from Ontonagon citizens asking for piers was presented to the federal government in 1852.[^1] A federal project adopted in 1867 found the entrance only seven feet (2.1 m) deep. It set out to confine the river between parallel piers and to dredge a channel twelve feet (3.7 m) deep.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the work was completed in 1889 and had cost $377,296 by 1908.[^1]

The main Ontonagon Lighthouse stands on the river's west side. LighthouseFriends dates it to 1853, and the US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1852.[^1][^8] The pierhead beacon was placed in the charge of that light's keeper.[^1]

## Building the light

The pierhead light was first lit on 20 September 1875, about 200 feet (61 m) from the outer end of the west pier.[^1] As the pier was extended, the light moved lakeward: 535 feet (163 m) in 1879 and 865 feet (264 m) in 1884, when a 767-foot (234 m) elevated walkway was built atop the pier.[^1] In 1889 an iron gallery was placed round the lantern so the keeper could clean its glass.[^1]

On 30 September 1893 the light was moved 378 feet (115 m) nearer the outer end of the pier.[^3] The Light-House Board also provided new bed sills, extended the elevated walk and built a short flight of stairs from the walk up to the tower.[^3] LighthouseFriends gives the 1893 move as roughly 400 feet (122 m).[^1]

On 12 December 1899 a severe storm carried away the open framework tower at the outer end of the west pier.[^4] On the night of 16 April 1900 the Board showed a temporary fixed red light from a square house, painted brown, with a square lantern.[^4]

The iron beacon that replaced the tower was a square, pyramidal steel frame, "26 feet 10 inches high to the focal plane" (8.2 m). Its fixed red light of the fourth order was shown on 10 November 1900.[^5]

In the Great Lakes storm of 8 to 10 November 1913, the Ontonagon Pierhead was one of several light stations damaged. The Lighthouse Service put the damage for the group, which also included Whitefish Point and Big Bay Point, at about $4,000 and gave no separate figure for each station.[^6]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends says responsibility for the pierhead beacon was given to the keeper of the main Ontonagon light, so the pierhead had no keeper of its own.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's roster of keepers, drawn from National Archives registers, lists the main light's principal keepers from 1853, among them Thomas Stripe (1864-1883) and then James Corgan (1883-1919).[^9] In the sources read, James Corgan is the only keeper named at the pierhead.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says that when James Corgan was keeper of the main and pierhead light in 1910, he reached the tower by a 750-foot (229 m) boardwalk from the main light, then walked 600 feet (183 m) along the pier and took a 1,200-foot (366 m) elevated walkway to the tower.[^1] The same account lists him at various times as harbour master, deputy collector of customs and village president.[^1]

In December 1909 the Ontonagon Herald printed a letter signed "Jams Corgan", as the scanned text prints the name. It was written from Washington, where the writer had been sent to plead the harbour's case before the board of engineers. The Calumet News reprinted it on 13 December 1909 under the headline "CORGAN BELIEVES ONTONAGON."[^7] The letter gives no office for its writer. No source read ties this letter to the keeper. LighthouseFriends describes a 1909 reply by James Corgan, as village president, to the Corps of Engineers, and does not mention this letter.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the pierhead tower, according to LighthouseFriends, and the tower is closed.[^1] The Lighthouse Society's record says the station is not open to the public.[^2]

The main light was discontinued in 1963, LighthouseFriends says, after a battery-operated light was installed on the east pier.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1964 for the discontinuance, and says the main light's optic was removed that year.[^8] The two sources disagree, and this story gives LighthouseFriends' date.

The pierhead changed well before that. In 1933 an electric siren was established in the iron tower on the west pier, replacing a hand fog signal.[^1] A plastic beacon later took the place of a fourth-order Fresnel lens, and in 2013 a solar-powered LED light was installed in the pierhead tower.[^1]

The tower was made available under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act on 15 May 2012. The Ontonagon County Historical Society applied and was recommended as the new owner, but LighthouseFriends says the transfer never happened.[^1] The General Services Administration (GSA) held a public auction on 20 June 2019, with a starting bid of $5,000.[^1] The high bid of $7,500 ended the auction on 17 September.[^1] LighthouseFriends infers that the winner did not complete the purchase.[^1] In June 2024 the GSA again offered the lighthouse to qualified organizations under the same Act.[^1]

The tower is closed to visitors. LighthouseFriends suggests seeing it from the main Ontonagon light, or from the beach on the eastern side of the river.[^1]
