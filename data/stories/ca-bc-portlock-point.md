Portlock Point Light stands on the eastern point of Prevost Island, in the Gulf Islands of British Columbia, where Swanson Channel meets Trincomali Channel.[^1] It has shown a light since November 1895.[^1] The keepers left in 1964, and Parks Canada owns the light today.[^1]

## The place

Captain Daniel Richardson of the Royal Navy named the island's eastern extreme in 1861, after Captain Nathaniel Portlock, who had served as a master's mate on HMS *Discovery* and *Resolution* in the 1770s.[^1]

The point was where ships on the Vancouver-to-Victoria run passed from Swanson Channel into Trincomali Channel. An unmanned stake light was set on the point in 1890, at the request of George Rudling, captain of the steamer *Islander*.[^1] When the lighthouse was built, the island was a hunting preserve stocked by the Prevost Island Club for the exclusive use of its members.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1895 G. A. Frost built a square pyramidal wooden tower, with a kitchen attached, on the point, at a contract price of $870. The tower stood 14.6 m (48 ft) from base to vane. It showed a fixed white light from a focal plane of 22 m (72 ft), with a red sector over Enterprise Reef, from a seventh-order dioptric apparatus, and it went into operation on 1 November 1895.[^1]

The Department of Marine's list of stations gives the Portlock Point appointment as 2 December 1895, at $460 a year.[^2] A fog bell in a small white wooden tower, 274 m (900 ft) southeast of the light, was ready for use on 1 September 1896. Machinery struck it once every fifteen seconds when conditions called for it.[^1]

The Department's report published in 1912 records that the seventh-order apparatus had been replaced by a fifth-order dioptric apparatus. The illuminant was petroleum vapour burned under an incandescent mantle.[^3]

## The keepers

The Prevost Island Club at first wanted to choose the keeper from among its own members. When it learned how small the salary was, it let the government appoint John S. Richardson, whom LighthouseFriends calls an "intruder." He served for a decade and resigned in 1905. LighthouseFriends says little more about him.[^1] W. J. Gillespie followed him until 1911.[^1]

George Andrew Watson took charge in 1911. The Department's list for 1913 gives G. A. Watson at Portlock Point.[^4] From the start he complained that his salary of $460 could not support his wife and two children. He wrote to his Member of Parliament, and in 1914 he wrote to the Minister of Marine and Fisheries, describing his duties in detail. The Minister passed the appeal to the Marine Agent at Victoria, who wrote that if Watson was not satisfied he should resign.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Watson's pay was raised by $90 after the station was reclassified upward.[^1]

In June 1917 Watson was too ill to carry on. He flew his flag inverted at half mast, a signal of distress. For four days he crawled on his hands and knees to reach the fog bell and tend the light, until he had to give up. He was reluctant to leave the station, because he had no money to pay a doctor or an assistant. A friend from Mayne Island chartered a launch and took him to hospital in Vancouver, where he recovered slowly over the next month.[^1]

In 1919 Watson wrote to the editor of the *Family Herald and Weekly Star* about an article on the easy life enjoyed by Canada's lighthouse keepers. He called the article a gross misrepresentation of his own life, and described his station as "a slave pen," because he could not leave it without written permission.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the letter paid off: pay increases followed in 1920 and 1921. In 1924 a leaking oil drum fell on him and ruptured him. The local superintendent of lights found him unfit for duty, and he was granted a gratuity of $188.33 and an annual annuity of $262.29.[^1]

LighthouseFriends lists the keepers after Watson as Sidney Elliot Church (1924 to 1925), James Nicol Waugh (1925 to 1936), Henry Georgenson (1940 to 1948), Jack Reddy (1948 to 1964) and James Heanski (1959 to 1964).[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A vessel stranded off Portlock Point on 28 June, most likely in 1916. The Wreck Commissioner's statement in the Department's report for fiscal 1916-17 records the stranding, and a formal investigation at Victoria on 1 July. The finding was that the pilot, Jones, "committed grave error of judgment and is reprimanded," and that no blame attached to the master or officers.[^5] The statement prints no year beside the date. Because the inquiry is in the same entry and the same fiscal year, the stranding was most likely on 28 June 1916. The vessel's name is not legible in the copy read.[^5]

## After the keepers

A new dwelling, detached from the tower, went up in 1949. LighthouseFriends says that with regular doctor visits, radio and a correspondence school for the keeper's children, life at the station had improved.[^1] A 1941 water test had found the station's well "highly contaminated." A cistern was then put in place, fed by gutters and downpipes from the roofs.[^1]

On 3 March 1964 keeper James Heanski included in his scheduled weather report, at 7:45 in the evening, a note that the beacon on Enterprise Reef was out. It was the last transmission anyone heard from him. Around 8:15 that evening, witnesses on Galiano and Pender islands reported an explosion and a fireball from the keeper's dwelling on Prevost Island. Two ferries interrupted their runs to send staff ashore, and the Coast Guard tender *Sir James Douglas* brought a portable pump. By then the dwelling was fully engulfed, but the fire did not spread to other buildings at the station. The next morning, LighthouseFriends says, the keeper was found in the smouldering remains of the dwelling, and fragments of a kerosene lamp were ruled the cause of the accident.[^1] The station was automated and de-staffed in 1964.[^1]

The present tower was built in 1987. It is shorter than the one before it, and a wooden footbridge leads to it from a helicopter landing pad.[^1] Parks Canada took the lighthouse over in 2006, and it became part of Gulf Islands National Park Reserve.[^1]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard List of Lights gives the light a quick-flashing white characteristic with a one-second period, a focal height of 15.5 metres and a range of 7 nautical miles.[^6]

Almost all of Prevost Island is privately owned.[^1] The lighthouse and the shoreline at Richardson Bay are part of Gulf Islands National Park Reserve. LighthouseFriends says that this part of the park can be reached only by private powerboat, sailboat or kayak, and that the grounds are open while the tower is closed.[^1] The rest of the island is private land, so visitors should not cross it. Do not climb the tower.
