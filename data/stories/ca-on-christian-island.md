Christian Island Light stands on Lighthouse Point, at the southeast tip of Christian Island in Georgian Bay, Lake Huron.[^1][^2] It was one of six stone towers built on Lake Huron and Georgian Bay in the late 1850s. A 2007 plaque, as LighthouseFriends prints it, calls it the first of the six to be completed.[^1]

## The place

In the nineteenth century three islands off Midland were named the Christian Islands, in honour of the Canadian Martyrs.[^1] The largest of them, which had served as a refuge, is now Christian Island.[^1] In 1649 thousands of Hurons and a few Jesuit missionaries from Saint-Marie among the Hurons took refuge on a nearby island. Most of them left the area for Quebec in 1650, after nearly starving through the previous winter.[^1]

The Lake Huron coast was poorly marked.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes the Commissioners of Public Works of the Province of Canada, reporting for 1852 to 1853, that on Lake Huron "the mariner is wholly unaided by either Lights or Buoys, with the exception of the Solitary Light at Goderich."[^1] A Captain MacGregor, who had commanded a steamboat on the lake, and the Honourable W. B. Robinson proposed nine lighthouses, "including one on the southeast tip of Christian Island."[^1]

The island was not empty ground.[^4] On March 29, 1857, the government made a treaty with the chiefs and headmen of the Yellowhead, Assance and Snake bands to surrender ten acres (about 4 ha) at Bar Point "for lighthouse purposes."[^4] A 2023 Anishinabek News article datelined Beausoleil First Nation says the project was well under way "Without consultation or approval from the leaders of the Chippewas of Lakes Huron and Simcoe bands."[^4]

## Building the light

In 1855 John Brown, a builder from Thorold, was contracted to erect eleven lighthouses, but only six were built at that time.[^1] LighthouseFriends calls him renowned for his skill, but the remote sites made the work "a money-losing affair for him."[^1]

The walls are hand-faced limestone, about 1.8 m (6 ft) thick at ground level.[^1] The stone was quarried from the Niagara Escarpment and carried by sailing vessels, "several of which were lost to severe weather before reaching their destination."[^1] The 1856 season went badly across the six sites.[^1] LighthouseFriends says two steamers and a schooner were wrecked and "much timber was lost," without saying where.[^1]

The sources do not agree on when the tower was finished. Anishinabek News says the lighthouse and keeper's home "was completed in 1856."[^4] LighthouseFriends' own text says the dwelling and tower were finished during 1856, save for the lantern and lighting apparatus.[^1] The 2007 plaque, as LighthouseFriends prints it, says the lighthouse was "Constructed in 1857."[^1] The Lighthouse Directory gives "1859 (John Brown)."[^2] The lantern and lighting apparatus were "provided by the Parisian manufacturer Louis Sautter."[^1]

The light was first shown on May 1, 1859.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the fixed white light from its fourth-order Fresnel lens "was not displayed until May 1, 1859," which made it "the last of the six Imperial Towers to be officially activated."[^1]

The tower's size is also given differently. LighthouseFriends says it "stands fifty-five feet tall from its base to the centre of its light" (about 17 m), with a focal plane about 19 m (61 ft) above the water.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory gives an 18.5 m (61 ft) tower and a 19.5 m (64 ft) focal plane.[^2] The Coast Guard list gives a 17.5 m structure at 20.2 m above the water.[^3] This story does not choose between them.

The land question came back in 1866.[^4] Anishinabek News says "Pre-confederate Treaty #120 was agreed upon between the Assance Band" and a visiting superintendent of Indian affairs, and that it added land for the light.[^4] The article says that in 1874 the two departments wrote to each other because "no payment had been found for Treaty #120," and that $373.71 "was the amount of principal and interest due." It adds that it is not known whether one department paid the other, or the government paid the band.[^4]

## The keepers

William Hoar, the first keeper, "was born in Devonshire, England in 1809 and joined the navy at the age of sixteen."[^1] He left the navy for Penetanguishene in 1844, served on the government steamer Mohawk, and was appointed keeper "through the influence of Honourable W.B. Robinson," who had recommended the lighthouse.[^1] In 1863 Hoar complained that a garden fence he had put up was being torn down.[^4] The Superintendent of Lighthouses, D.G. Smith, "had met with the Assance Band on December 16, 1863, to create an arrangement with the band."[^4] LighthouseFriends says "Keeper Hoar served as keeper for over a decade."[^1]

His son John took charge in 1868, when his father retired.[^1] John Hoar "served as keeper until 1891, when he swapped positions with the keeper of nearby Hope Island Lighthouse."[^1] A daughter of William Hoar married Captain George Huff, commander of the local steamer Georgina. The Huffs later moved to British Columbia, where their daughter Minnie married Thomas Patterson, "keeper of Cape Beale Lighthouse."[^1]

Anishinabek News lists the later keepers as "Allan Collins (1891-1914), and Thomas Marchildon (1914-1922)."[^4] LighthouseFriends gives the same keepers under the heading "Head Keepers."[^1]

Works continued through the keepers' years. In 1878 Darius Smith, the Superintendent of Lights above Montreal, visited on his annual tour and found the wall cracked.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes his report: "The wall of this lighthouse is cracked from near the top to the bottom," and the keeper "thinks the foundation has given away."[^1] A breastwork with groins was built in 1887, "at a cost of $213.20."[^1] The Department's report for the fiscal year ended March 31, 1909, printed in 1910, lists a change at this station: "A 35 mm. Diamond vapour installation and adjustable brass stand replaces the duplex lamp."[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On November 22, 1906, the steamer J. H. Jones of Goderich "left Owen Sound during a heavy gale, bound for Lion's Head," and was last seen off Cape Croker that evening.[^5] The Department's report says the steamer was presumed to have foundered off Cape Croker, "although the two life boats and other wreckage came ashore at Christian island." It says "All the crew and passengers, amounting to about twenty-two persons, were lost."[^5]

Four years later, on November 25, 1910, "The tug Saucy Jim of Collingwood was totally destroyed by fire while lying at Christian island."[^7] The Department's report for the fiscal year ended March 31, 1911 gives the cause as unknown, with no casualties.[^7]

## After the keepers

The light was discontinued on April 1, 1922, after an offshore buoy replaced it.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that during World War II "its iron lantern room was scrapped to support the war effort."[^1] The Lighthouse Directory describes the station as "Reactivated (inactive 1922-1965)."[^2] In 1965 the Coast Guard "placed a beacon that displayed a white flash every four seconds" on the tower.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that in the early 2000s "the tower was given a lantern room," and the Lighthouse Directory says the present lantern was installed around 2000.[^1][^2]

In 2007 the Huronia Lightstation Preservation Society marked the 150th anniversary of the light with a plaque.[^1][^4]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse.[^1][^2] Its list gives a white flash every four seconds from a "White cylindrical tower," with a nominal range of 7 nautical miles.[^3] The island is a reserve of the Beausoleil First Nation, and Christian Island "is home to roughly 700-year-round residents."[^1]

Ferry service runs from Cedar Point.[^1] The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1][^2] The Lighthouse Directory says the one-and-a-half-storey stone keepers' house is "in ruins."[^2]
