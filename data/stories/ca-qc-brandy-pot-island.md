The Brandy Pot Island Lighthouse (phare de l'Île-du-Pot-à-l'Eau-de-Vie) stands on the south-eastern tip of Pot du Phare, one of three small islands in the St. Lawrence River off Rivière-du-Loup, Quebec. It is a white clapboard keeper's house with a round tower rising through its red roof, and the statement of significance in the Canadian Register of Historic Places calls it the last combined lighthouse and dwelling still standing on a St. Lawrence River island.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The archipelago is a cluster of three small rocky islands, Gros Pot, Petit Pot and Pot du Phare, which join at low tide. LighthouseFriends says the name dates from French rule, when sailors compared the reddish-brown rainwater held in pockets in the rock to brandy.[^2] Quebec's heritage register describes the islands in the first half of the 19th century as a stopping place, a gathering point and a trading post known to river pilots, on a stretch of the south channel noted for reefs and shoals that caused many wrecks.[^3]

Two earlier incidents are told for the islands. In 1740 the royal vessel *Rubis* was forced ashore when about 160 people aboard fell ill with fever, and in late November 1835 the barque *Endeavour* was wrecked there. According to LighthouseFriends, the captain lit a signal fire that the river pilot Joseph Pelletier of Rivière-du-Loup eventually saw; he organized a rescue party of eight men with two canoes. By the time they arrived fourteen men had died of starvation and exposure, and the merchants of Quebec gave Pelletier a gold medal for rescuing the other fifteen.[^2]

## Building the light

Quebec's register dates the construction to 1860 and 1861 and says the light went into service in 1862. LighthouseFriends and the Lighthouse Directory give 1862, and the Route des phares gives 1861.[^2][^3][^4][^5] The design was by the Public Works engineer John Page, who drew two plans: in the first the whole building was stone, and in the one adopted only the tower was stone, with a timber keeper's house around it. The contractor was Louis Déry of Quebec, who built five coastal lights in the same period; the one on Long Pilgrim Island was identical to this one, and this is the only one of the five to survive.[^3]

The sources disagree about the tower. LighthouseFriends describes a cylindrical brick tower about 9.15 m (30 ft) high; Quebec's register describes a stone tower covered in white-painted sheet metal. Other heights given are 12.6 m (Route des phares) and "approx. 15 m" (Lighthouse Directory).[^2][^3][^4][^5] The house was a square of clapboard with a four-sided red roof, and an annex was added in 1884 as a storehouse and summer kitchen.[^2][^3]

The light was a fixed white light from a fourth-order Fresnel lens, burning roughly seventy gallons of oil a season in a flat-wick lamp.[^2] In the Department of Marine's report labelled 1909 the old lamp was replaced by a 35 mm "Diamond vapour" installation, and the same report records $272.05 spent on building a shed at the station.[^7][^8]

The station never had a fog alarm. LighthouseFriends says the channel nearby is narrow and ringed by shoals, so deep-draught ships could not pass safely in thick fog and anchored near its approaches to wait, and that the Department of Marine doubted a fog alarm's sound could be depended upon there.[^2]

## The keepers

The first keeper, Jean-Baptiste des Trois Maisons dit Picart, had earlier run a small hotel and supply station on Gros Pot for pilots and mariners, according to LighthouseFriends, which lists him from 1862 to 1873. It then lists Narcisse Richard (1874 to 1878) and Alphonse Richard (1878 to 1914), Captain Arthur Leclerc (1914 to 1920), and Louis Dubé (1920 to 1943) and Roger Dubé (1943 to 1964).[^2] Alphonse Richard appears as keeper of Brandy Pots in the Department of Marine's list in the report labelled 1908.[^6] Quebec's register dates the Dubé service differently: Louis Dubé and his four sons served in turn as keepers or assistants for more than forty years, from 1921 to 1964. It says the station was kept by people of Saint-André-de-Kamouraska.[^3]

The keepers kept a visitors' register, which according to the heritage register shows summer holidaymakers making excursions to the lighthouse from the early 20th century.[^3] About Prohibition, the register says that in the 1920s cases of liquor from Saint-Pierre-et-Miquelon were hidden on the island and loaded onto boats at night, and that the keepers are said to have been passive and powerless witnesses.[^3]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1964, the register says with a battery-powered system.[^3][^4] It says a skeletal structure took over as the navigation aid in 1975, after which the house was largely abandoned to vandals and weather.[^3] LighthouseFriends adds that the lens and lantern room came off in 1978 because they blocked the light on the new tower; the Lighthouse Directory dates the skeletal tower's light to 1964 instead.[^2][^5]

In 1979 biologists founded the non-profit Société Duvetnor. On LighthouseFriends's account it leased the lighthouse and restored it in 1989 from historic photographs and Page's plans, and a new copper lantern was hoisted on 20 August 1989.[^2] The register adds that the summer kitchen was rebuilt and outbuildings from about 1990 were added for the house's new use as a guest house.[^3] Duvetnor became the owner in 2014, the year the federal government designated the building a heritage lighthouse and the municipality of Saint-André cited it as a heritage site.[^3]

## Today

Duvetnor owns the lighthouse and offers overnight stays in it; its cruises leave from Rivière-du-Loup, and the Route des phares lists Duvetnor as manager.[^2][^4] The islands are uninhabited apart from birds and seals and are reached by boat; a Fresnel lens formerly used here is shown in the lighthouse, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes the grounds and tower as open and the interior stair as climbable, while the Lighthouse Directory, notes of the modern skeletal tower beside it that it has been inactive since December 2025 and that the site is open and its tower closed.[^2][^5] Visitors should check with Duvetnor before landing.
