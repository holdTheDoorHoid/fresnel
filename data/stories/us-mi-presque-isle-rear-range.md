The Presque Isle Harbor range lights are a pair of beacons on the western shore of Presque Isle Harbor, on Lake Huron, in Presque Isle County, Michigan.[^1][^3][^4] They were built in 1870 to lead ships into the harbour past shallows on either side of the entrance.[^2][^3] The front light is a small wooden tower that now stands in a township park.[^2][^3] The rear light first shone from a tower on the roof of the keeper's house, and now stands on a tall steel tower.[^2][^3]

## The place

The Presque Isle light of 1840 had two jobs, guiding ships along the coast and into the harbour.[^3] When its keeper's dwelling needed rebuilding in 1868, the Lighthouse Board judged it better to spend the money on separate lights.[^3] In 1869 Congress appropriated $7,500 "to build two range lights marking the channel into Presque Isle Harbor."[^1]

In June 1869 Fredrick Burnham sold 8.5 acres on the harbour to the United States government for $100, according to the Township museum.[^2]

## Building the light

The marker says that Orlando M. Poe submitted plans in May 1870, and that the range lights were in operation by August.[^1] The Township museum dates the range to 1870 but gives its service as starting in 1871, when it took over the harbour guidance from the old 1840 light.[^2]

The front light was shown from a twenty-one-foot (6.4 m) wooden tower near the shore.[^3] The rear light was on a tower on the keeper's house, with its focal plane 36 feet (11 m) above the harbor.[^3]

The Board's later reports record small works. In the fiscal year 1899 the porch of the dwelling was renewed, and 500 feet (152 m) of walk between the ranges was relaid.[^5] In October 1899 the rear light was changed to show in the harbour.[^6]

## The keepers

Isaac Coddington was the first keeper. He was named on 8 September 1870 at a salary of $540, and he served until his death in 1875.[^2][^3] William Sims was then placed in charge of the lights.[^3] His wife Adeline died in 1881 and was buried along the range line.[^3]

Thomas Garrity was appointed head keeper in 1887, after serving as assistant keeper at the new light under his father, Patrick.[^3] Patrick Garrity had moved to the new light in 1871, when the old one was discontinued.[^3] In 1891 Patrick became keeper of the range lights, at the age of seventy-two, and Thomas transferred to the new light.[^2][^3][^4]

Patrick's daughter Anna took over after he died in March 1903.[^3] The marker says she "maintained this light from 1903 to 1926."[^1] The archive's roster gives the same dates.[^4] LighthouseFriends says she cared for the lights until 1927.[^3] The Township museum, which credits her with 23 years of service, names Vincent Newagon, Clement Richardson and Gustav Hansen as keepers after her, for the period 1923 to 1939.[^2] The archive lists Clement E. Richardson from 1927 to 1929.[^4] The sources do not agree on who kept the lights in the late 1920s, and both accounts are given here.

## After the keepers

In 1926 the front light was changed to a white flash every second.[^3] The rear light, still fixed white, was set on a black, pyramidal, skeletal tower 33 feet (10 m) above the ground.[^3] In 1940 the lights were electrified and changed to fixed green, which is the characteristic they show today.[^3]

A light on a pole replaced the front range light in 1967, and the old wooden tower was moved near the Old Presque Isle Lighthouse.[^1] The township acquired that tower in 1995, with state funds and private donations, and moved it back near its original site in 2002.[^1] Much of its lower structure was rebuilt to the original plans.[^1] The Township museum gives a different order of moves: the wooden tower went first to the entrance of the Old Lighthouse Park, and was moved back into Range Light Park some years later.[^2]

## Today

The site is now a township-owned park, open from dawn to dusk through the year.[^2] A statue of Anna Garrity stands near the front tower.[^2] Neither light is open for climbing.[^3]

LighthouseFriends says the rear range light is privately owned, while the front light belongs to the township.[^3] The Township museum places the rear light at the back of the park's parking area, and says the former keeper's house is now a private home.[^2] It also says the Coast Guard still supervises both lights.[^2]

The Township museum says that after more than 140 years the range light remains an active aid to navigation.[^2] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record gives "Active Aid to Navigation?: No."[^4] The two sources disagree, and this story does not settle the question.
