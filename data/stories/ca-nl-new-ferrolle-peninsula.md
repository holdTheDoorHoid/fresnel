A hexagonal reinforced-concrete tower on a flat headland on Newfoundland's Great Northern Peninsula, completed in 1913 by the Canadian government for the shipping that used the Strait of Belle Isle. A regional tourism page says it was kept by one family, the Beaudoins, for three generations.[^7]

## The place

The Ferolle Peninsula juts into the Strait of Belle Isle roughly midway between Port au Choix and Flower's Cove; LighthouseFriends reports that Basque fishermen are believed to have named it after El Ferolle, a fishing village in northern Spain.[^1] In 1860 John Page, chief engineer of the Department of Public Works of the Province of Canada, reported on possible sites for new lighthouses and ranked Point Ferolle above the other Strait of Belle Isle sites he examined. He described a headland that was flat, of moderate height, stood out prominently and looked like an island from a distance, though joined to the mainland by a very narrow neck. He proposed an 85-foot tower with a second-order light.[^1] Other lights came first: Cape Norman in 1871, Cape Bauld in 1884 and the north-east end of Belle Isle in 1905.[^1]

## Building the light

Work on the lighthouse, a fog alarm building, a double dwelling for the keepers, an oil house and a combined boathouse and storehouse began in 1911 and the station was finished in 1913, according to LighthouseFriends. It was built by day labour under a succession of supervisors: T. Thibodeau in 1911, D. Bilodeau in 1912, and F. E. Cote and E. Lavergue in 1913.[^1] The Department of Marine and Fisheries report printed in the 1913 sessional papers (fiscal year ended 31 March 1912) lists, under Ferolle Point, the "Erection of a lighthouse tower, fog alarm building. double dwelling" and storehouses, with T. Thibodeau named for the work.[^2] The next year's report (fiscal year ended 31 March 1913) lists Ferolle Point among the new aids to navigation in its Quebec section, for the construction of a reinforced-concrete tower, double dwelling and fog-alarm building.[^3] A regional tourism page gives 1912 for the lighthouse, while LighthouseFriends gives 1913 as the year the station was completed, and the heritage statement dates the lens to 1913.[^7][^1][^5]

The tower is a tapered hexagon of reinforced concrete, 19.2 m (63 ft) high, with engaged buttresses that flare at the top to support the cornice; the heritage statement says the buttresses were meant to limit wind sway that could disturb the delicately balanced optical equipment.[^5] Its lantern holds a third-order Fresnel lens made in Paris by Barbier, Bénard and Turenne, installed in 1913 and, according to the heritage statement, still in place.[^5] The station was built and maintained by the Canadian government with the approval of the Newfoundland government, which was then a separate colony; the Federal Heritage Buildings Review Office linked the tower to the northern shipping route through the Strait of Belle Isle to Britain in the age of steamers.[^5][^6] The fog signal was a diaphone sounding three blasts of two and a half seconds, three seconds apart, in each minute.[^1]

## The keepers

Damase Beaudoin of Montmagny, Quebec, moved his wife and six children to Newfoundland to become the first keeper, beginning a family tradition of lightkeeping.[^1] (LighthouseFriends spells his first name "Demase" once in its narrative.) LighthouseFriends lists the keepers as Damase Beaudoin (1913 to 1930), Joseph Aime Beaudoin (1930 to 1953), Edmond Beaudoin (1953 to 1970) and Jerome Beaudoin (from 1970).[^1] Its narrative says that Aime Guy, who followed his father, had sixteen children, and that his son Edmond took charge at his death in 1953. When Edmond was transferred, his brother Jerome, who had been assistant keeper, was promoted to head keeper.[^1] The listing's name for the second keeper (Joseph Aime, in the roster; Aime Guy, in the narrative) differs, and both come from the same page. The Canadian Register says the Beaudoin family kept the light "for several generations."[^4]

In 2005 Jaye Roberts became principal keeper, which LighthouseFriends reports was the first time a woman held that post in Newfoundland.[^1]

## After the keepers

The station went onto generated electricity in 1967 with new fog alarm equipment; commercial power reached it in 1970, by which time the double dwelling had been replaced by two bungalows. A new fog alarm building followed in 1976.[^1] A regional tourism page says the lighthouse was automated in June 1992,[^7] yet LighthouseFriends describes a staffed station, with Roberts becoming principal keeper in 2005.[^1] The two accounts disagree and the sources do not resolve it.

## Today

LighthouseFriends lists the lighthouse as owned by the Canadian Coast Guard, with the grounds open and the tower closed, and describes it as a staffed station.[^1] It still gives a group of four flashes every 7.5 seconds through its original lens.[^1] To reach it, travel south from Saint Barbe on Route 430, turn west through Reef's Harbour and Shoal Cove to New Ferolle, then continue 2.7 km (1.7 miles) to the light; the road beyond New Ferolle is unpaved and a bit rough.[^1] The heritage statement calls the tower the dominant feature atop 10-metre cliffs and the region's primary cultural heritage attraction, with a view over the Gulf of St. Lawrence.[^5]
