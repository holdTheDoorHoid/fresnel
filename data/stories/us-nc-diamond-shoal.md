Diamond Shoal Light is a steel tower on the outer shoals about 13 miles off Cape Hatteras, North Carolina, and it has been dark since 2001.[^23] Lightships had marked the shoal from 1897 until the tower went up in 1966.[^21][^20]

## The place

The shoal lies off Cape Hatteras, where the Life-Saving Service kept stations at Cape Hatteras, Creeds Hill and Big Kinnakeet.[^10] The Commissioner of Lighthouses said in 1919 that Diamond Shoal was "one of the most important and most exposed light vessel stations on the coast of the United States or in the world."[^17]

## Building the light

In 1891 the contractors Anderson & Barr set out to found a light-house on the outer shoal.[^2] They built a caisson at Norfolk, Virginia, which the Light-House Board's report calls "the base of operations," and towed it out.[^1] The caisson reached the site and was anchored there on the morning of 1 July 1891.[^1]

Scour under the caisson made it sink out of level. It went on sinking until the morning of 4 July, when it stood only "5 or 6 feet out of water."[^1] On 4 July the contractors began building the caisson, but the sea soon stopped them, and their vessels left the site to fetch a boiler that could use salt water. No further work was attempted. A storm on 8 July carried away the machinery, one dredging tube and the upper part of the caisson.[^1] The attempt was given up.[^2]

The Board tried again in 1894 with a boring frame, "a skeleton iron frame, 20 feet square and 32 feet high," which was set on the shoal in May.[^2][^3] In 1895 the superintendent of construction, Julius E. Eettig, reported the frame "considerably damaged" but found no settlement and no sign of scour.[^4]

The act of 11 June 1896 directed that part of the appropriation for the Diamond Shoal light-station be used to build a light-vessel, and all work on the plans for the light-house was stopped.[^5] Two composite steam light-vessels, Nos. 69 and 71, were built in 1897–98.[^6] The Board's report says No. 71 kept the station until 24 November 1898, when No. 69 relieved her.[^6]

The Coast Guard's steel tower was established in 1966.[^20] The US Lighthouse Society's archive describes it as a skeletal steel tower on steel pilings, with the light 125 feet (38 m) above mean high water. Its keepers' quarters are built into the tower, and the record also lists a helicopter pad and radio beacon.[^20] The last vessel on the station, WLV-189, served there from 1947 to 1966.[^21]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin names a few of the lightship's officers. In 1913 A. T. Loss, who had been mate of No. 71, was transferred to a Virginia light vessel, and C. H. Pertner was moved into the mate's post on No. 71.[^12] Sidney Atherton was appointed second mate of No. 71 in 1914, and in 1915 he was transferred to the tender Ivy as second officer.[^13][^14] The Bulletin gives their posts but no more about their work.

The tower has its own roster, from the US Lighthouse Society's archive. Leon R. Scarborough is listed as principal keeper from 1966 to 1967, then Royce L. Jones from 1967 to 1968, and Malcolm S. Daniels from 1968 to at least 1969.[^20] The archive lists twenty-one Coast Guard crew for the tower, from 1966 to 1977; two of them have only an end year.[^20] The roster gives only names and years; it does not say what the crew did from day to day.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the evening of 19 February 1893 the barkentine Formosa struck the outer Diamond Shoal and "immediately bilged and fell over on her beam ends."[^9]

On 2 December 1893 the steamship Wetherby stranded on the outer shoal. Her master wrote that the Cape Hatteras station's crew, under Captain Etheridge, "came to our assistance," and that men from the Creeds Hill and Big Kinnakeet stations helped bring the vessel's twenty-four men ashore.[^10]

In January 1899 a heavy gale set light-vessel No. 69 dragging her anchor. The Board's summary says she broke adrift on 8 January and was "replaced on station January 11, 1899."[^6][^7] In the cyclone of August 1899 she was driven from her anchorage off Diamond Shoal and thrown ashore near the Creeds Hill life-saving station. The Board's report says an agreement was made "to haul her off and deliver her at port" for $15,000.[^8]

On 21 October 1913, during a southwest gale, No. 71 was blown about 1 mile off station. The Bulletin says she was returned to her position the next day.[^11]

On 4 April 1915 No. 71 took aboard the captain, his wife and the crew of ten men of the five-masted schooner M. D. Cressy, who had been picked up by another steamer.[^15]

On the afternoon of 6 August 1918 No. 71 "was fired upon and sunk by an enemy submarine while moored on her station off Cape Hatteras, N. C."[^16] Her twelve men, two officers, two radio operators and eight crew, took to one of the boats and landed about a mile north of Cape Hatteras Lighthouse. They lost all of their belongings, which were left on board. The report gives their numbers, not their names.[^16]

## After the keepers

The act of 4 November 1919 appropriated $450,000 for a replacement light vessel for the station.[^18] By 1921 the station was marked by light vessel No. 72, "which was a relief vessel."[^19]

The US Lighthouse Society's list of the station's vessels gives No. 69 from 1897 to 1901, No. 71 from 1898 to 1918, No. 72 from 1900 to 1922, No. 105 from 1922 to 1942, No. 114 from 1945 to 1947 and WLV-189 from 1947 to 1966. It lists no vessel from 1942 to 1945.[^21]

The tower was automated in 1977.[^23][^20] The light was extinguished in December 2001.[^23] The archive gives the year it was discontinued as 2001.[^20]

The General Services Administration offered the tower at auction in 2012. A GSA inspection made two years earlier had concluded that renovation would cost $2.3 million.[^23] A Minnesota entrepreneur was the only bidder, and he paid $20,000 for the tower and its platform in September 2012.[^23]

## Today

The US Lighthouse Society's record lists the tower as privately owned, not open to the public, and "still standing in private hands."[^20] The sources describe no public access. The station's own website says the station has been vacant since 2001.[^22]

Sources disagree on when the Coast Guard stopped manning the light. The archive and the 2012 news report give 1977 for automation.[^20][^23] The station's own website says the Coast Guard manned it "from 1966 until 1979."[^22]
