Six Mile Point Range is a pair of lights on the St. Marys River in Michigan. One stands behind the other, and together they mark the axis of the Hay Lake Channel at Little Rapids Cut.[^1]

## The place

The St. Marys River carries the water of Lake Superior down to Lake Huron, past the Soo Locks.[^1] Traffic on the river grew after the federal Weitzel Lock opened in 1881, and on August 2, 1882 Congress appropriated $200,000 to improve Hay Lake Channel.[^1] That work widened the channel to a minimum of 600 feet (183 m).[^1] Hay Lake, now called Lake Nicolet, is the water the channel runs through. Six Mile Point lies on its western shore, between 5 Mile Road and 6 Mile Road, two of a set of parallel roads about a mile apart.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress's act of August 18, 1894 provided $43,550 for lighting Hay Lake Channel.[^2] The Light-House Board's report for the year ended June 30, 1895 says negotiations for the light sites had begun. Title papers for a site at Six-Mile Point were obtained and sent to the United States attorney for his opinion, and the deed for the rear range's land was recorded.[^2]

The channel's new lights were numbered 1 to 27. At Six Mile Point the rear light (No. 20) stood on land bought for it, and the front light (No. 19) was set offshore on a crib. The lighthouse tender Warrington carried the materials.[^1]

The Board's report for the year ended June 30, 1896 says the channel's seven skeleton iron towers were built under contract by the Indiana Bridge Company of Muncie, Indiana, and shipped to Sault Ste. Marie. It does not say which of the seven stood at Six Mile Point.[^3] The same report says the system was tested on September 27 and that the lights went into operation on September 30, 1895.[^3] Its list of lights established during the year, however, gives October 5, 1895 for the two range lights.[^11]

The archive's record gives steel for the range's 1895 towers. Those towers may or may not have been among the seven iron towers the Board describes, and these sources do not settle the material.[^3][^5]

The towers were then modified so that the focal planes stood 40 feet (12.2 m) above lake level for the front light and 54 feet (16.5 m) for the rear.[^3] The Board's report for the year ended June 30, 1899 records that an oil house was built and a landing dock 310 feet (94.5 m) long was carried out to water 6 feet (1.8 m) deep.[^4] LighthouseFriends dates the oil house to 1898.[^1]

The heights in the later record do not agree with the 1896 figures. The archive's record for the 1895 towers, whose source is the 1901 Light List, gives the light 54 feet (16.5 m) above water for the front and 82 feet (25 m) for the rear.[^5][^6] LighthouseFriends gives focal planes of 28 feet (8.5 m) and 47½ feet (14.5 m) for the 1907 pair that replaced those towers. Those are not figures for the 1895 towers.[^1][^7]

In 1906 pile foundations were built for a new pair of range lights. The replacement pair was first shown on December 1, 1907, on round steel towers painted white.[^1] The lens lanterns were lifted and lowered by an iron winch in the base of each tower, working a wire cable. Each lantern ran in a cage on a wooden base, steadied by two wire cables fixed to the roof and floor.[^1] At the close of the fiscal year 1922 the Commissioner of Lighthouses reported that the foundations for the Frechette Point and Six Mile Point ranges had been rebuilt.[^8] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1922 gives the same work as completed.[^9]

## The keepers

The Board's reports cited here do not name a keeper for the range. The archive's roster for the rear range lists one name: Richard L. Mitchell, a laborer, with 1913 as the last year and no start year given.[^10]

LighthouseFriends says that in 1909 a man who owned land beside the oil house, about an eighth of a mile from the rear tower, was being paid to attend the lights from his own boat. It does not name him.[^1]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends dates the electrification of the range lights to 1933.[^1] The 1907 steel towers were later removed from the range and taken to museums, at a date that source does not give.[^1]

## Today

The front and rear lights now stand on modern towers, a cylindrical one and a skeletal one. A brick oil house still stands beside them.[^1]

The 1907 front tower is on the grounds of the DeTour Passage Historical Museum in De Tour Village.[^1] The 1907 rear tower can be seen at the Les Cheneaux Historical Museum in Cedarville.[^7] LighthouseFriends describes the grounds at both museums as open and the towers as closed.[^1][^7] The US Lighthouse Society's archive records the range as not open to the public, so the two sources disagree about access.[^5]
