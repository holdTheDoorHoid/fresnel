Minot's Ledge Light stands on a granite reef about a mile off the Massachusetts shore at Cohasset and Scituate, a stone tower. It is the second lighthouse on the ledge: the first, an iron frame on legs, fell into the sea in April 1851 with its two assistant keepers inside.[^1][^2]

## The place

Minot's Ledge is a rock about twenty-five feet wide, part of the Cohasset Rocks, a reef that runs some two miles off Cohasset and Scituate.[^1] The engineer William H. Swift, writing in 1848, said the rocks "have been the terror of mariners for a long period of years" and lay at the entrance to Boston, where ships were "constantly passing and repassing". Vessels running in before a heavy northeast wind, he explained, were liable to be driven onto the reefs if they fell to leeward of Boston Light.[^2] The National Register form puts the case for a light in numbers: forty ships had been lost in the area between 1830 and 1840.[^1]

At extreme low water, Swift wrote, an area about thirty feet across was exposed, and the highest point stood only about three and a half feet above low water.[^2]

## Building the light

The first light was Swift's iron skeleton: an octagonal structure on nine heavy wrought-iron piles, begun in 1847 and finished, in Heap's account of Swift's report, in November 1848.[^2] The National Register form dates its first lighting to 1 January 1850.[^1] The Lighthouse Society's archive lists Isaac Dunham as head keeper from 1849 to 1850.[^9] LighthouseFriends quotes his journal on an easterly gale: "an ugly sea which makes the light reel like a Drunken Man".[^10]

After the iron tower fell, Heap says, Congress promptly made an appropriation to relight the ledge, with the tower to stand on the outer Minot.[^2] General Joseph G. Totten of the Lighthouse Board designed it, and Lieutenant Barton S. Alexander of the Army Corps of Engineers supervised the work.[^1][^3] The ledge could be worked only at low water in calm weather. The National Register form counts the hours worked on the rock: 130 in 1855, 157 in 1856, 130 in 1857, 208 in 1858 and 377 in 1859.[^1] Heap's figures for 1855 and 1859 agree.[^2]

The work met trouble in its second winter. On 19 January 1857 the rigging of the bark *New Empire* crashed against the scaffold during a storm, and much of the previous year's cutting of the rock had to be done again.[^1] To make the stones stick to the part of the rock that lay underwater, the engineers worked out a method with muslin: each finished stone was laid on cloth spread with mortar, and the cloth was folded around it.[^1] The granite was cut and fitted together on the mainland, at Gulf Island in Cohasset Harbor, before being carried out.[^1]

The Light-House Board's report of 1894 gives the last stone as laid on 29 June 1860, "five years after the beginning of the work on the ledge", and the cost, including the keepers' houses ashore, as about $300,000.[^3] Heap gives the same date and cost, and says the light was first shown at sunset on 15 November 1860.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of December 1935, marking the seventy-fifth anniversary, gives 18 November.[^7] The National Register form says the shore station of keepers' dwellings was established at Gulf Island about 1860.[^1]

The height is given differently by different sources: 89 feet (27 m) in the Board's 1894 report, 97 feet (30 m) in the 1935 Bulletin, and 102 feet 9 inches (31 m) overall in the National Register form.[^3][^7][^1]

The Light-House Board showed a model of the tower at the Columbian Exposition at Chicago, and its report on the exhibit called the lighthouse "one of the most important engineering works of the Light-House Establishment".[^3] On 22 August 1871 the poet Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, visiting with Sumner, wrote that it was "rising sheer out of the sea like a huge stone cannon". Some of the party were "hoisted up forty feet in a chair", while others climbed an iron ladder.[^2]

## The keepers

The first tower had a head keeper and two assistants. The head keeper at the end was Bennett, whom the Worcester *Daily Spy* of 18 April 1851 calls Joshua and the archive's roster lists as John W. Bennett (1850 to 1851).[^6][^9] The Worcester *Daily Spy* of 18 April 1851 named the assistants as Joseph Wilson, "an Englishman, aged 20", and Joseph Antone, "a Portuguese, aged 25".[^6] The Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the keepers' registers, spells the second name Antoine and lists both men as serving from 1850 to 1851.[^9]

The archive's roster for the stone tower lists Joshua Wilder as head keeper in 1860 and 1861, then James J. Tower from 1861 to 1874, with assistants under them.[^9] Milton H. Reamy followed as head keeper, listed from 1887 to 1915.[^9] The *Lighthouse Service Bulletin* reported his death on 29 September 1925 and said he had been keeper of Minots Ledge for 28 years. It also said he was put in charge of the lighthouse exhibits at expositions in Nashville in 1897, Buffalo in 1901 and Springfield in 1917, and had been commended several times for life-saving work.[^8] The archive lists his successor as Octavius H. Reamy, who had served at the ledge as an assistant from 1909 and was head keeper from 1915 to 1924, then Per F. Tornberg, who kept the light from 1924 to 1936.[^9]

The shore houses were not good ones. In its report for 1899 the Light-House Board said the keeper and one assistant, with their families, lived in "an old dwelling converted, about forty years ago, to that use from a barn". It added that the house gave the two families "neither the necessary room, privacy, nor conveniences".[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Before the final storm, the old tower had already been shaken. The Worcester *Daily Spy* of 5 April 1851 reprinted the *Journal*'s account, from one of the assistant keepers, of an earlier northeast gale. The wind came with heavy snow, and by the Monday morning the tower swayed so much that the men "could with difficulty keep on their feet" and were "frequently knocked down". They withdrew to the store room, where they stayed four days, living on dry bread and uncooked meat.[^5] The paper does not say which Sunday the storm began.

Swift reported that on the night of Monday 14 April the wind, easterly for several days, gradually increased, that by Tuesday it was a severe northeast gale, and that the height of the storm came on Wednesday the 16th, when it was "a perfect hurricane".[^2] The head keeper, Bennett, had gone up to the city on business on the Monday. When he returned to Cohasset on the Tuesday afternoon the sea was too heavy for him to get back to the light.[^6] The lamp was seen burning on the Tuesday night, and the paper's account has the tower still standing at nearly 4 p.m. on the Wednesday, with the light not seen that night.[^6] Swift's report says the light was last seen from Cohasset on Wednesday night at 10 o'clock. At 1 o'clock on Thursday morning, 17 April, the lighthouse bell was heard ashore, and the tower was believed to have gone down about that hour.[^2] The 1935 Bulletin and LighthouseFriends date the loss to 16 April 1851.[^7][^10]

Bennett was at the shore at five o'clock on the Thursday morning. It was "strewed with indubitable evidence of the fatal catastrophe", including fragments of the lantern and a window frame, and some of the keeper's own clothing. An India-rubber life buoy looked as if it had been put round the body of one of the men and then torn away.[^6] Swift wrote that, so far as he knew, the bodies were never recovered.[^2] The *Spy* reported that a lightship would be sent to the spot.[^6]

Swift's explanation put part of the blame on the keeper. He said that upon the second set of braces the keeper "had improperly built a sort of deck or platform" for fuel and water barrels, and had tied a hawser from the lantern deck to a granite block on the bottom, so that the sea had far more surface to work on. The platform came ashore at Cohasset ten hours before the tower fell.[^2] Swift does not name him.

A later close call came in the stone tower's time. Per Tornberg and a Cohasset boatman set out for the tower in a dory in February 1936 because the harbour was frozen. According to LighthouseFriends the boat sprang a leak, the offshore wind carried them past the tower, and Coast Guard men from North Scituate finally rescued them.[^10]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1947 and the resident keepers left.[^11] The National Register form records that Gulf Island was then sold to the town of Cohasset.[^1] The lens went too. LighthouseFriends says the old second-order lens was taken down and a third-order lens put in, and that the third-order lens stood in the tower until 1971 and now sits in a replica lantern room on Government Island, as Gulf Island is now called.[^10][^1]

In the Blizzard of 1978, the National Register form says, the access platforms, entrance and masonry were damaged. In 1983 the light was converted to solar power.[^1] The form lists an extensive rehabilitation of the masonry as planned by the Coast Guard for 1987.[^1] The Cohasset Historical Society's account of the renovation of the summer of 1987 says the old watchroom blocks were "airlifted ashore" to Government Island.[^12]

In June 2007 Coast Guard divers searched the seabed for the remains of the first tower, found iron beams thought to be its legs, and lowered a plaque to Wilson and Antoine, according to LighthouseFriends.[^10]

## Today

The light was put up for sale in 2014 through a government online auction under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act.[^13] The Boston Globe reported the top bid as $222,000, from Robert "Bobby" Sager, chairman of Polaroid and a philanthropist, who was to become the new owner.[^11] It stands a mile out, and the Globe describes it as "accessible only by boat"; no one should try to land on the ledge. The Globe added that the Coast Guard would keep checking the light and horn after the sale.[^11]

On shore, the National Register form (written in 1986) says the keepers' duplex dwelling on Government Island survives, converted to apartments, with the waterfront used by a sailing club.[^1]
