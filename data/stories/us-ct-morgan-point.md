Morgan Point Light stands on a point of land on the west side of the mouth of the Mystic River, in the village of Noank, Groton, Connecticut.[^4][^5] It was lit in 1831, and it was kept by a run of keepers that included two women and a brother and sister who passed the light between them.[^4][^5] Its life as a light ended in 1921, when an automatic beacon on a nearby ledge took over.[^2][^4]

## The place

The point takes its name from the Morgan family.[^4] LighthouseFriends says Deacon James Morgan gained the southern tip of the peninsula in 1712, and that his descendants were mariners and shipbuilders.[^4] In 1831 Roswell Avery Morgan, who ran the local boat shop, sold the point to the federal government so that a light could be built there.[^4]

An 1838 inspection report by Lieutenant Bache, as quoted by LighthouseFriends, describes the light as established "for the benefit of the trade in its immediate neighborhood."[^4]

## Building the light

The first tower, finished in 1831, was a 25-foot (7.6 m) granite tower beside a six-room stone keeper's house.[^4] Bache found the lamps in its lantern out of order in 1838. A wooden spacer had been wedged between each lamp and its reflector, so that, in his words as LighthouseFriends quotes them, "the brightest portion of the reflected light is thrown away."[^4]

Complaints that the light was too dim led to a sixth-order lens in 1855.[^4][^5] D'Entremont says a sixth-order Fresnel lens took over from the old lamps and reflectors.[^6]

The first tower was replaced during 1867 and 1868.[^4][^5] The sources do not agree on what went up. D'Entremont says construction began in 1867 and describes a two-story granite house of eight rooms with a cast-iron light tower on the front of its roof.[^6] LighthouseFriends describes a two-and-a-half-story granite structure with an octagonal iron lantern.[^4] The Lighthouse Society's record lists an octagonal wooden tower on a granite dwelling.[^5] None of the three describes a cone.

## The keepers

Ezra Daboll, a veteran of the War of 1812, was the first keeper.[^4][^5] When he died in 1838, his widow, Eliza, was appointed in his place, and she kept the light until 1854 with six children to look after.[^4][^5][^6] Bache's 1838 report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, found the station "kept in great neatness by the widow of the former keeper."[^4] LighthouseFriends also records a legend that sailors heard Eliza singing loudly during storms to keep her courage up. It is a legend, and the source presents it as one.[^4]

Silas Spicer became keeper in 1854.[^5] D'Entremont tells one story about him. He saw a burning vessel offshore, rowed out, and brought the captain, the captain's wife and their child back to the light, where the family spent a few days recovering.[^6] D'Entremont's account gives no date beyond the 1850s, when Spicer became keeper.[^6]

Frances McDonald was the second woman keeper. Her husband, Alexander, had to give up the light because of poor health, and she took it over in 1869.[^5] The LighthouseFriends keeper list gives 1870 for the change, so the two sources differ by a year.[^4] She kept it until 1871, when she passed it to her brother, Thaddeus Pecor.[^4][^5] LighthouseFriends says the couple then moved to Florida, where Alexander became keeper of the St. Johns River Light in 1871, with Frances as his assistant. Alexander died there in 1879 while still serving. Frances stayed on as assistant until 1886.[^4]

Thaddeus Pecor, a Civil War veteran, was keeper from 1871 until 1919.[^1][^4][^5] The Norwich Bulletin, reporting in April 1919 that he was to be relieved, described him as the keeper "of Morgan Point lighthouse for 48 years of continuous service," said he was being relieved because he had passed the age limit, and reported that the service "desires to put in younger men throughout the lighthouses."[^1] The paper said Secretary Boutwell had appointed him.[^1]

Charles R. Riley, the last keeper, served from 1919 to 1921.[^5] D'Entremont spells his name Reilly.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Norwich Bulletin reported in 1919 that during Pecor's first year he saw three wrecks cast upon the point.[^1]

D'Entremont tells of one winter when the survivors of a wreck rowed to Morgan Point, and the keeper had to cut frozen clothing and shoes from some of the frostbitten sailors. He dates it only to Pecor's time as keeper.[^6]

## After the keepers

The Norwich Bulletin reported in September 1921 that the light had been discontinued about two weeks earlier, so service probably ended in late August of that year. The Lighthouse Society's place record gives 1919, the year Pecor retired, as the year the light was discontinued.[^5] The paper said Riley had been transferred to Stuyvesant Landing, New York.[^2] The same report says the light had been in operation for ninety years.[^2]

An automatic beacon on Crooks Ledge, in the channel to the east, replaced the keepers' light.[^4][^6] In December 1921 the government decided to sell the site.[^3] LighthouseFriends quotes the sale notice, which asked for sealed proposals to be opened in February 1922.[^4]

D'Entremont says the property was sold to a private party for $8,625.[^6] LighthouseFriends says it was sold at auction in 1922.[^4] The sources disagree about the lantern room. D'Entremont says one account has the original lantern destroyed in the hurricane of September 1938, and another has it removed in the 1920s.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the reports conflict on who removed it and where it went.[^4]

Later owners used the lighthouse as a summer residence for more than two decades.[^4] In 1991 the property was purchased by a private owner, who gutted the interior and fitted a new aluminum lantern room made from Lighthouse Service designs.[^6]

## Today

Morgan Point Light is a private residence. Its grounds and dwelling are closed to the public, and LighthouseFriends says it is best viewed from the water, where cruises from nearby Mystic Seaport and from Cross Sound Ferry may pass it.[^4] It is privately owned, so do not land on the point or walk onto the grounds.[^4][^5]
