Chantry Island Lighthouse stands on a small island 1 to 1.5 km (about a mile) off Southampton, on the Lake Huron shore of Bruce County, Ontario.[^1][^9] It is a limestone tower of the late 1850s, one of the group known as the Imperial Towers, and it had resident keepers until 1954.[^1][^2]

## The place

The island was recommended as the site for one of the first Canadian lights on Lake Huron.[^1] The Commissioners of Public Works of the Province of Canada reported for 1852 to 1853, as LighthouseFriends quotes the report, that along the Canadian shore "the mariner is wholly unaided by either Lights or Buoys," with one exception, "the Solitary Light at Goderich."[^1] They recommended nine lights, one of them on the northeast point of Chantry Island, and a pier of about 150 m (500 ft) from the island toward the mainland, to form a harbour.[^1] The Coast Guard's list still places a buoy off the northeast end of Chantry Shoal.[^3]

## Building the light

In 1855 the Commissioners of Public Works contracted with John Brown, a builder from Thorold, to build eleven stone lighthouses and keepers' dwellings on Lake Huron and Georgian Bay. Only six were built at that time, and those six are the Imperial Towers.[^1] The heritage register calls the Chantry tower "a very good example of the work of the contractor John Brown" and says it counts among the few towers constructed of stone.[^2]

In 1855 money was also appropriated for a breakwater joined to the island.[^1] By 1856 the tower's shaft had reached within 3 m (10 ft) of its full height and the breakwater was finished. The commissioners' report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says the breakwater "withstood the heavy storms to which it was exposed last fall, without sustaining any damage."[^1]

A temporary fixed light was established atop the tower in 1857, while it still had no lantern.[^1] The lantern room and a second-order Fresnel lens, made in Paris, were installed in time for the light to be shown at the opening of navigation in 1859.[^1] The walls taper from 1.8 m (6 ft) thick at the ground to 0.6 m (2 ft) at the top.[^1] The lamps burned spermaceti oil until colza oil replaced it in 1860.[^1]

In 1877 a pyramidal trelliswork light was set at the end of the breakwater. It was placed under the care of the island's keeper, whose pay was raised $75 for the extra work. Lined up with a tower on shore, it formed a range for entering the harbour.[^1]

The sources do not agree on the tower's dimensions. The Coast Guard list gives the structure as 28.0 metres above ground and the focal height as 29.5 metres.[^3] LighthouseFriends gives 26.2 metres for the tower and 31.4 metres for the focal height.[^1]

## The keepers

Robert Mills looked after the temporary light for one season.[^1] Duncan McGregor Lambert, a man with long years at sea behind him, took charge of the light in 1858.[^1] In 1854 Lambert had been first mate on the steamer Bruce Mines when she sank near Stokes Bay. He got the crew into two small boats and took them to Owen Sound, a voyage of more than 160 km (over a hundred miles).[^1] He had eleven children. A family of six lived on the island, and in 1876 an inspector wrote, as LighthouseFriends quotes him, that the lighthouse was "the best kept on the upper lakes."[^1]

Lambert retired in 1880 after twenty-two years, and his son William, who had been his assistant from the age of fifteen, succeeded him.[^1] The Department's 1900 list of lightkeepers gives "Lambert, Wm. McGregor" on the same line as Chantry Island, with the date 1 October 1880.[^4] In the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 the Department discontinued the use of a hand fog horn at the station.[^5]

William Lambert was in charge for twenty-seven years. When he stepped down in 1907 he received the Imperial Service Medal for more than twenty-five years of service, which included numerous rescues.[^1] In the fiscal year ended 31 March 1908 the front range tower was repaired and a new summer kitchen and woodshed were built, at a cost of $466.90.[^6] In the following fiscal year a 55 mm diamond vapour lamp replaced the duplex lamp.[^7]

Malcolm McIver kept the light from 1908 to 1916, and John Klippert from 1917 to 1937.[^1] The Department's annual report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1917 has a table of stations, keepers and salaries with a line for "Chantry Island, Ont", against a keeper's name that the scan garbles.[^8] A visitor who often came to the station in the 1930s recalled the work of Keeper Klippert in an interview for Fo'c'sle Magazine, as LighthouseFriends quotes it. "We had to climb to the top to light the light," she said.[^1] After Klippert came Clayton Knetchel (1937 to 1941), Alfred Huber (1941) and Cameron Spencer (1942 to 1954).[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 4 September 1879 the scow Mary and Lucy foundered on a reef south of Chantry Island. The crew of the steamer Quebec, lying in the harbour, watched the accident and lowered a boat. The boat overturned several times on its way to the reef, and two of its rescuers drowned. One was Duncan Ross Lambert, the keeper's son, who was twenty-three. Everyone aboard the Mary and Lucy did reach shore.[^1]

On the night of 31 August 1892 the schooner Nettie Woodward capsized while trying to make Southampton Harbour in heavy seas. Two men drowned. The captain and two other sailors clung to the wreck until daybreak, when they were rescued by Keeper Lambert. The Canadian government gave him a gold watch for it.[^1]

In the night of 31 August to 1 September 1906 the schooner Cavalier of Sarnia wrecked near the island, leaving Captain John Glass, four seamen and a female cook in danger. Keeper William Lambert found the wreck the next morning, put out in his small boat through heavy seas, and had everyone on the island within the hour. The Royal Canadian Humane Association later gave him a medal for bravery.[^1]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1954. Three years later the island was declared a Federal Migratory Bird Sanctuary.[^1] When a former visitor returned after many years away, the keepers' dwelling had no roof, and much of its walls and floors had fallen in.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the Supporters of Chantry Island was formed in 1998, and that it is now the Southampton Marine Heritage Society. The society's own page says the Marine Heritage Society "emerged in Southampton" in 1997.[^1][^9] The rebuild needed wood that could not be bought in the area, so thirty-one large hemlock trees were cut, milled and brought to the island.[^1] More than 250 volunteers gave over 300,000 hours of labour, and a grand opening for the restored keeper's cottage was held in August 2001.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard's current list describes the tower as white and cylindrical with a red upper portion. It gives the light as white, flashing every four seconds, with a focal height of 29.5 metres and a nominal range of 7 nautical miles. The list marks the light as seasonal, though the structure is in place year round.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard owns the lighthouse and leases it to the Town of Saugeen Shores.[^1]

The Marine Heritage Society runs guided historical boat tours from Southampton, and the tours include the keeper's house.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the tours run in the summer, and that the grounds and tower are open for guided tours. The island is part of a bird sanctuary, and LighthouseFriends says access to it is restricted, so the society's summer tours are the way LighthouseFriends describes visiting.[^1][^9]
