Fort Pickering Light, also called Winter Island Light, stands on a cast-iron tower just offshore from Winter Island, at the mouth of Salem Harbor in Essex County, Massachusetts.[^1][^2] It first showed on 17 January 1871.[^2] After the Coast Guard left the island, an offshore buoy took over the light and the tower fell into disrepair; it was relit as a private aid to navigation in 1983.[^3][^7]

## The place

Winter Island was fortified by 1643. In 1799 the fort was renamed Fort Pickering, for Timothy Pickering, a Salem man who had been Washington's Secretary of State.[^2] Salem Harbor's busiest years as a seaport ran from 1750 to 1810; after the War of 1812 its importance fell, though it stayed significant.[^2]

Before the Fort Pickering light, the twin lights on Baker's Island were the only guides into the harbor.[^3] LighthouseFriends reports that $30,000 was allocated in 1869 for three small lights, Fort Pickering among them, so that vessels could enter safely at all times.[^2] Ships that had passed Baker's Island lined up the Fort Pickering and Derby Wharf lights to find their way into Salem Harbor.[^2]

## Building the light

The tower is cast iron lined with brick.[^1][^2] It was built just offshore and joined to the island by a wooden walkway, 52 feet (16 m) long, 10 feet (3 m) wide and 7 feet (2 m) high.[^2] The light was shifted from a temporary structure into the new tower, and on 17 January 1871 it first shone through a fifth-order Fresnel lens.[^2][^1]

The height is recorded two ways. LighthouseFriends puts the light 28 feet (8.5 m) above sea level. The archive's record gives 32 feet (9.8 m) above mean high water.[^2][^1]

In its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891, the Lighthouse Board said the station's barn had been rebuilt and a brick cistern provided for the dwelling.[^4]

## The keepers

The archive lists four principal keepers: Charles A. Johnson (1871 to 1877), Abram F. Barenson (1877 to 1881), John Harris, Jr. (1881 to 1919) and James Yates (1919).[^1]

John Harris held the post longest. He was an Ipswich native and a Civil War veteran. LighthouseFriends says he was absent from the light only five nights in his time there.[^2] The archive gives 1881 as his first year, and LighthouseFriends gives December 1881. D'Entremont's guide gives December 1882.[^1][^2][^3]

In 1919 Harris retired at 75, and he and his wife moved to a street corner in Salem.[^2] LighthouseFriends quotes the Boston Daily Globe of 19 January 1919, in which Harris said that when he first went to the island it had been "nothing but a lot of pasture land." By then he had laid out walks and planted lawns.[^8] His wife Annie died seven months after they left the island, and Harris died the following April.[^2]

LighthouseFriends also reports that another keeper, not named there, took a second job as superintendent of the Plummer Home for Orphaned Boys. That broke his keeper's contract and ended his service.[^2]

In 1895 the Board reported that water pipes had been laid to connect the keeper's dwelling with the Salem water system.[^5] James Yates followed Harris in 1919, and he is the last name on the archive's roster.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Harris could say that in his 37 years on the island no ship was wrecked at Winter Island, and LighthouseFriends repeats the claim.[^2]

Ice destroyed the wooden walkway in 1879 and again in 1904. It was rebuilt both times.[^2]

In January 1934 a blizzard shut about thirty Coast Guardsmen into the keeper's house. D'Entremont says temperatures dropped to 12 degrees below zero and the heating fuel had almost run out before a snowplow broke through.[^3][^2]

The blizzard of 1978 tore the tower door off its hinges, and the door fell into the harbor. It stayed underwater for several years, until the association formed in the early 1980s fished it out and put it back on the tower.[^2][^3]

## After the keepers

In 1934 the Coast Guard built an airplane hangar on Winter Island. Its men lived temporarily in the keeper's house until new quarters were built, and the house later became the officers' club for the air station.[^7]

Sources differ on when the Coast Guard left. D'Entremont and the Essex Coastal Scenic Byway say 1969, when an offshore buoy replaced the light. LighthouseFriends says 1970.[^3][^7][^2]

The Fort Pickering Light Association was formed in the early 1980s. It restored the tower, and the light was relit in 1983 as a private aid to navigation.[^7][^3]

The light went dark for a few months in early 1995, when conduit erosion cut off its power from shore.[^3] It was converted to solar power in April 1995, and the $2,300 cost was split between the City of Salem and the association.[^3][^2] In 1999 a Salem firm, the American Steeple Corporation, carried out $13,800 worth of ironwork repairs and tower painting.[^2] After the light failed again in July 2009, the Coast Guard installed a rebuilt solar system in February 2010.[^2]

A Commissioner's list in the bound annual reports for fiscal 1915 to 1922 places Fort Pickering among lights changed from oil-wick lamps to electric incandescent lamps. LighthouseFriends says an incandescent oil-vapor lamp was in the lantern by 1920.[^6][^2]

## Today

The City of Salem owns the light.[^1][^2] The grounds at Winter Island Marine Park are open, and the tower is closed to visitors.[^2][^7]

LighthouseFriends reports that the keeper's house, the walkway and the barn no longer stand. The light now flashes once every four seconds.[^2] The Friends of Winter Island works to preserve the island's historic resources.[^7]
