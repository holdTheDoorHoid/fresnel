Buffington Harbor Breakwater light stands at the end of a breakwater in Buffington Harbor, a cement-works harbor on the Lake Michigan shore at Gary, Indiana. Its red concrete tower dates from 1926 or 1927 and is still active, but the breakwater is private property, and the light is best seen from the water or from a distance.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Buffington Harbor was built for a cement plant. Illinois Steel's cement department became a separate firm, the Universal Portland Cement Company, and the harbor was named for E. J. Buffington, president of Illinois Steel.[^3][^2] Pepper says the breakwater's limestone was cut and shipped from the quarry at Calcite in northern Lake Huron.[^2] LighthouseFriends notes a large limestone deposit near Rogers City, Michigan, on Lake Huron, but no natural harbor in that area.[^3]

Dredging of the turning basin began in the summer of 1925. Pepper gives its size as sixty acres and LighthouseFriends as fifty-five.[^2][^3] The Lighthouse Service's Bulletin reports a gas buoy established at Buffington Harbor in July 1925.[^5] An east pier was finished on 1 November 1926, and a 1,300-candlepower acetylene light on a white skeletal iron tower at its outer end was shown from it.[^2]

## Building the light

Pepper dates the concrete light to early 1927, as the breakwater neared completion. Rowlett gives 1926.[^2][^1] Pepper says the light was designed and built under the supervision of Universal's engineers, and that it was cast entirely of concrete. It stood on a cylindrical base 30 feet (9 m) across and 3 feet (0.9 m) thick, set on a ring of steel sheet piling driven 27 feet (8 m) into the lake bottom.[^2]

Pepper gives the shaft as 50 feet (15 m), and it carried a 4,000-candlepower red electric lamp rated for 13 miles of visibility in clear weather.[^2][^3] Rowlett gives the tower as 46 feet (14 m).[^1]

The base held a compressor for a diaphragm fog signal, supplied by a submarine cable from shore, and a pair of acetylene tanks as a backup light.[^2] The focal plane is 48 feet (14.5 m), and the light gives a red flash every four seconds.[^1]

Buffington Harbor's opening, with Vice President Charles G. Dawes, is dated 10 June 1927 by Pepper and by LighthouseFriends.[^2][^3] The *Indianapolis Times*, reporting on 10 June, said Dawes had formally opened the harbor on Thursday, and it counted a salute of nineteen guns where Pepper counts sixteen.[^4][^2] The sources name no keeper for this light.

## After the keepers

In 1995 part of the cement plant property was sold for casino development.[^1] Pepper says most of the harbor was sold, while LighthouseFriends says one-third of the Buffington property was sold. All three accounts put two floating casinos in the harbor.[^2][^3][^1] In 2000 the rest of the plant was sold to the City of Gary for $25 million.[^3]

## Today

Rowlett lists the light as active and privately maintained, owned by the City of Gary, with Gary New Century LLC as site manager. Pepper says the breakwater and light are on private property.[^1][^2] The site and tower are closed.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's place record for "Buffington Harbor East Breakwater" marks it as not open to the public. Its printed coordinates are not valid, so the match with this light rests on the name alone.[^6]

Rowlett places a distant view from Industrial Drive, and LighthouseFriends one from the Majestic Star casino.[^1][^3] Pepper calls the light "virtually impossible to see from the land" and says a private boat, or a vessel chartered from a local fisherman, gives the only good view.[^2] The sources differ on how much of the light can be seen from land.
