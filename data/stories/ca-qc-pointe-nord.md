Cap-de-Rabast light, also called Pointe-Nord (North Point), stands at the north point of Anticosti Island, in the Gulf of St. Lawrence in Quebec. Its octagonal concrete tower, a 22 m (72 ft) landmark, was built to light the north coast for ships taking the shorter route from the Strait of Belle Isle to the St. Lawrence.[^1][^8] The heritage statement dates the tower to 1917–1918; LighthouseFriends and Les amis des phares du Saint-Laurent give 1919.[^1][^3][^8] The Canadian Coast Guard owns it, and it still shows a light in the navigation season.[^1][^7]

## The place

LighthouseFriends says Jacques Cartier named the prominent cape in 1535, and that Cap de Rabast is an old French term for a place where one turns back.[^3] Until these lights were built, the register's statement of significance says, traffic through the Jacques Cartier Strait was mostly local fishing boats and supply ships for the island's residents.[^2]

The north side of the island was the preferred passage. The Department of Marine and Fisheries' report for 1918–19 described a winter voyage through the ice, and said that the north side of Anticosti was "generally conceded" to give vessels "a better chance of getting through" than the south, where "the ice is always more or less packed."[^5]

## Building the light

The department's works table for 1917–18 lists the "Erection of lighthouse and fog alarm plant at Charleton point, Table Head, and North point," which "Will be completed next year."[^4] The heritage statement says the tower was built in 1917–1918 by the department, and that the station began as "a double dwelling, and a fog alarm building."[^1] In 1918–19 the department's report records new towers "erected at Table head, Anticosti, North point, Anticosti."[^5]

LighthouseFriends says the three north-shore lights were all established in 1919, and that a double dwelling was built in 1918–1919 and later replaced by separate residences.[^3] The Deputy Minister's report for 1920–21 described the three north-shore stations as "built during the war" and "now in permanent operation."[^6]

The heritage statement, however, dates "the two dwellings" to the 1950s, so the sources do not agree on the houses.[^1]

The tower is 22 m (72 ft) high, reinforced concrete and octagonal, with "the pedimented cap over the doorway and the triangular lintels over the windows" as its decorative features.[^1] The Coast Guard list gives today's light as Fl(3) W 30s, from a focal height of 23.8 m, with a nominal range of 17 nautical miles, and describes the tower as a "White octagonal tower with red top."[^7] LighthouseFriends says the light originally showed three white flashes, then an eclipse of twelve seconds.[^3] A diaphone in a fog-alarm building near the tower sounded two blasts of about two and a half seconds each, five seconds apart, then sixty-five seconds of silence.[^3]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends lists nine keepers, from J.R. Bergeron (1919–1926) to Roger Poulin (1970–1980).[^3] Joseph Barriault was the keeper from 1927 to 1948. His son, who grew up at the station while his father was keeper, recalled the place in the 1930s. The buildings then were a light tower, a duplex converted into an eight-bedroom house, a fog-alarm building, a workshop, a stable, a chicken coop, a food shed, a large garden, and a fenced area for their horse.[^3]

With no overland road to the station, the keepers were 56 km (35 miles) by boat from Port-Menier and from Havre-Saint-Pierre, on the mainland. In winter the mail sometimes came by air, and pilots threw mailbags out when they were above the light.[^3] A 1953 article in the News of the Department of Transport described supplies landing through the surf: barrels of oil were tossed overboard from the surf boat, guided in with pike poles by crew who stood knee-deep in the water, and rolled up the shore, as LighthouseFriends reports it.[^3]

Three keepers on the list carry the Barriault name: Joseph (printed as "J. Barriault," 1927–1948), Cléophas (1948) and Lionel (1966–1967). LighthouseFriends does not say how they were related.[^3]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1980, and the keepers' posts ended.[^3] The tower was declared a Recognized Federal Heritage Building in 1990.[^3] The heritage statement gives historical and environmental reasons for the designation.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the tower.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^3] Overnight guests can stay in the two former keepers' dwellings, which LighthouseFriends says a private outfitter, the Pourvoirie Lac-Geneviève d'Anticosti, offers as lodging.[^3][^8]

To reach the light, the Quebec route page directs visitors to kilometre 19 of the island's main dirt road: "tournez vers le nord et parcourez 16 kilomètres" (translated: "turn north and drive 16 kilometres").[^8] The light has been automated since 1980, so no keeper is on duty. Stay out of the closed tower.
