Price's Creek Light was a pair of brick range lights on the west bank of the Cape Fear River, near Southport, North Carolina, built in 1849 and 1850 to guide ships up the channel toward Wilmington.[^4][^5][^8] The front tower is the only one of the pair that survives, and it stands on land owned by an industrial company.[^4] The Society's record says only the bottom of its rubble tower remains.[^5]

## The place

For years, merchants and mariners had asked for lights on the twenty-five-mile passage from the river's mouth to Wilmington.[^4] On 14 August 1848, $35,500 was appropriated for seven beacons and a lightship on the river, and the Price's Creek pair was among them.[^4] The Treasury's annual report for 1855 printed an inspector's note of a "new channel over Horse-shoe Shoal, opposite Price's Creek beacons."[^3]

In March and April 1850 the *Wilmington Journal* printed the government's proposals for two lights "at Price's Creek, on Cape Fear River," set to range with the channel around the Horse Shoe.[^1][^2]

## Building the light

The April 1850 specification called for a dwelling "of hard bricks or stone, 36 feet by 20," one story with an attic, and, in front of it, a light "to be 20 feet high, to be built of hard bricks, the form round," laid in Rosendale cement.[^2] The house was also to carry an octagon tower, 13 feet (4 m) above its walls.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes the rear light as a "square" brick building with a lantern room and an overall height of 35 feet (11 m).[^4] The two accounts differ, and this story does not choose between them.

Each beacon first had eight lamps and eight 14-inch reflectors.[^4] In 1853 the inspector took three lamps out of each beacon, "finding them to shine only upon the land."[^3] By the 1855 report, two sixth-order lenses had replaced the sixteen reflectors.[^3][^4] The inspector put the oil saved at 198 gallons, "nearly enough to pay the salary of the keeper."[^3]

## The keepers

The first man named keeper was Samuel C. Mabson, in 1850.[^5] LighthouseFriends says that "for some reason he never took up the post," and that John Bell worked as the first keeper.[^4] The Society's roster lists Bell from 1850 to 1853.[^5]

Hanson K. Ruark was keeper from 1853 to 1861, and again from 1866 to at least 1867.[^5] LighthouseFriends says he was keeper when the Civil War broke out, and that his daughter, Mary Catherine Ruark, later recalled climbing the tower as a child and watching the first and second battles for Fort Fisher.[^4] That recollection reaches this story only through LighthouseFriends.

In 1868 the keeper's son, Robert M. Ruark, was visiting the tower when its stairs gave way. He fell to the bottom and died about thirty minutes later, LighthouseFriends reports.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In July 1861 a Confederate captain was sent on a tour of the Cape Fear defences. The *Richmond Whig*'s account of it, as the *Hillsborough Recorder* summarised it in September, named Price's Creek among the defences on his route.[^6]

On the evening of 1 February 1865, a Union vessel left its anchorage at Price's Creek to reconnoitre. The *Wilmington Journal* reported that it came into view of a Confederate fort and "threw several shells," which fell short.[^7]

By the end of the nineteenth century, storm damage had led to the rear light being torn down, and local people carried off its bricks, LighthouseFriends reports.[^4]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says that, as Union forces took control of the coast, Confederate troops took apart or wrecked as many of the river's lights as they could.[^4] In 1867 the Lighthouse Board proposed new towers at Price's Creek and repairs to the old dwelling, but LighthouseFriends says that work was apparently never carried out.[^4] The Society gives 1867 as the year the station was discontinued, while Rowlett's directory says the light had been inactive since 1861.[^5][^8]

By 1947 a Colonel J. S. Crawford of Washington, D.C. owned the three-acre plot that included the lighthouse.[^4] LighthouseFriends reports that no lantern room survives and that the windows have gone.[^4] The land now belongs to Archer Daniels Midland, which runs a citric acid plant beside the river.[^4]

## Today

The front tower stands in disrepair on private industrial land, and LighthouseFriends reports that there is no public access.[^4] The tower can be seen from a distance on the Southport–Fort Fisher ferry, but not from the Southport landing.[^4] Do not go onto the plant's land. A visitor in October 2012 provided LighthouseFriends with pictures of the stairs in the tower and of what is believed to be the foundation of the keeper's dwelling, a short distance away.[^4]
