Hendricks Head Light stands on the east side of the mouth of the Sheepscot River, at West Southport on Southport Island, Lincoln County, Maine. A light was first shown there in 1829. The brick tower that stands today replaced the station's first buildings in 1875.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The light stands "behind a shallow rock ledge on the east side of the mouth of the Sheepscot River," according to the National Register nomination.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the first light was built to guide vessels up the Sheepscot River to the shipbuilding center at Wiscasset Harbor.[^2] The name is older than the light. In a note on LighthouseFriends, Kraig Anderson says that in 1735 Nathaniel Hendrick traded his land in Arundel, Maine, for property on Southport Island.[^2]

The inspector's concern was confusion with another light. In 1842 Inspector I. W. P. Lewis wrote that the light's fixed character was "highly objectionable, from its contiguity to Burnt Island light," which was similar. He added that "this fact has already caused sad disasters and the loss of numerous lives, on being mistaken for the other." LighthouseFriends quotes his report.[^2]

## Building the light

The first station was built in 1829 by Joseph Berry, according to LighthouseFriends, at a cost of $2,662.[^2] The Society's roster lists Joseph Berry and George Witham under the heading "Contractor."[^3] The nomination describes the first station as "a low one-and-a-half-story rubble stone cottage that was three bays in width," with a polygonal tower mounted at one end of the roof ridge.[^1] John Upham, the first keeper, lit the tower's eight lamps "on December 1, 1829."[^2]

By 1875 the Lighthouse Board judged the station beyond repair. Its report for that year, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says the station "is now in such an advanced state of dilapidation and decay that it has become uninhabitable, and new buildings are being erected."[^2] The nomination says the old structure was pulled down that year and replaced by "the present brick tower and frame keeper's house."[^1] LighthouseFriends describes the new tower as square, brick and forty feet (12.2 m) tall, with a keeper's dwelling of about 22 by 31 feet (6.7 by 9.4 m).[^2] The Society's record gives the tower's height from base to lantern as 39 feet (11.9 m), a foot less than LighthouseFriends.[^3] The keeper's family moved into the new dwelling on 30 September 1875.[^2]

The lens dates are not settled. LighthouseFriends says a fifth-order Fresnel lens was installed in 1857, changing the light from fixed to a white flash every sixty seconds.[^2] The Society's record gives 1875 as the year its original fifth-order lens was installed.[^3]

A fog signal came next. The nomination says the bell tower "that was built in 1890."[^1] The Lighthouse Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 lists the bell among the new fog signals: "bell struck by machinery, July 15, 1891."[^4] LighthouseFriends describes the bell as weighing 1,200 pounds and hung in a pyramidal frame of yellow pine bolted to the ledge.[^2] The brick oil house was "built here in 1895," the nomination says. The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1895 lists "An oil house was built" at the station.[^5][^1]

The Board's survey list for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1892 names "49. Hendricks Head, Me.," among the stations where the lighthouse lands were surveyed and "their boundaries were marked with stone posts or copper bolts."[^8]

## The keepers

The roster lists the principal keepers from 1829 to 1933, with two contractors.[^3] John Upham served from 1829 to 1837. Thomas Pierce appears twice, in 1841–1845 and 1849–1853.[^3]

Jaruel Marr kept the light from 1866 to 1895.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he was given the post partly to make up for injuries he suffered in the Civil War. It says he walked sixty miles to Portland to enlist in the Union Army, and was wounded and held in a Confederate prison in Virginia, where a fellow prisoner, a Union Army doctor named Wolcott, nursed him back to health. The Marrs named their next child Wolcott for the doctor.[^2] A cabinet Jaruel made is on display at Hendricks Hill Museum in Southport, LighthouseFriends reports.[^2]

Marr died in Bath in March 1907. The *Daily Kennebec Journal* reported that he "was for 28 years keeper of the Hendricks Head light of which his son is now the keeper."[^6] The logbook entry LighthouseFriends quotes gives the length of his service as 29 years.[^2]

His son Wolcott H. Marr followed him. LighthouseFriends says Wolcott had served at the Cuckolds and at Cape Elizabeth before returning to replace his father.[^2] A logbook entry, which LighthouseFriends quotes, records that he arrived "to relieve Mr. Jaruel Marr, who has been keeper here for the past 29 years."[^2] A grandson's account, which LighthouseFriends also prints, describes the keeper's maintenance duties and his frequent inspections of the lamp at night and in foul weather.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for the fiscal year 1922 records that "W. H. Marr, keeper, Hendricks Head Light Station, Me., towed a disabled motor boat to safe anchorage."[^7] Wolcott Marr served until he died in 1930, at 61, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2]

Charles L. Knight followed, from 1930 to 1933, when the light was discontinued.[^3][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends, citing an October 1879 newspaper account, says the "schooner Leader, mistook Hendricks Head light for that of Burnt Island, and struck on Green Island, near Southport, and sunk." Its crew, it says, "effected a safe landing in a boat at Southport."[^2]

LighthouseFriends also repeats a story printed by Edward Rowe Snow in 1945. It says that around 1870, in a blinding snowstorm, a schooner ran aground on a rocky ledge just seaward of the light, and that about an hour after dark a bundle came drifting toward the light, holding a baby girl in a box. Barbara Rumsey of the Boothbay Region Historical Society argued against the story in a two-part article in the *Boothbay Register* in 1997, LighthouseFriends says. The keeper's descendants maintain that the story is true.[^2] The story is disputed, and this account does not take a side.

In 1932, on a blustery night, a dog named Shep, belonging to the keeper's son, barked at the shore until the keeper rang the fog bell as an alarm. LighthouseFriends says two nearby powerboats then searched the water, and a couple adrift in a rowboat with no oars was found and rescued.[^2] The Anti-Vivisection Society of New York later awarded Shep a bronze medal.[^2]

In January 1978 a winter storm destroyed the boathouse and the walkway that linked the dwelling to the fog-bell tower, LighthouseFriends says.[^2]

## After the keepers

The Society's record gives the years the light was discontinued as "1933-1951."[^3] LighthouseFriends says the light was discontinued on 16 October 1933 as a cost-saving measure, after a lighted buoy was set nearby.[^2]

The sources differ on who bought the station. The nomination says the light "was discontinued in 1933 and sold to W. Prichard and Mary L. Browne in 1935."[^1] LighthouseFriends says it was sold in 1935 to "Dr. William P. Browne of Connecticut" for $4,100.[^2]

In 1951 the Coast Guard recommissioned the light, and the nomination says the buildings remained in private ownership.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that when electricity reached the area in 1951, the owner allowed the Coast Guard to automate and recommission the light.[^2] In 1979, LighthouseFriends says, the tower's fifth-order lens was removed in favor of a modern optic.[^2]

The Browne family kept the station until 1991, when it was sold to new private owners. By 1993 the new owners had renovated all of the structures.[^2] The Society's record gives the current optic as a 250 mm unit.[^3]

## Today

Hendricks Head is privately owned and not open to the public. The Society's record says so, and so does LighthouseFriends, which lists the grounds, dwelling and tower as closed.[^3][^2] The light is still an active aid to navigation, according to the Society's record.[^3]

Visitors can see the tower from land. LighthouseFriends directs them to West Southport Beach, from which the lighthouse can be seen, and says that public cruises passing the light are run from Bath and from Boothbay Harbor.[^2]
