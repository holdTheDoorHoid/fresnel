Lime Kiln Light stands on Lime Kiln Point, on the west side of San Juan Island in Washington, on the shore of Haro Strait.[^8] Its reinforced-concrete light tower and fog-signal building were placed in commission on June 30, 1919.[^7][^10] The National Register nomination ranks it among the latest of the area's major lights to be set up.[^1]

## The place

Ships bound between Puget Sound and the Strait of Georgia use Haro Strait, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' 1919 description gives the station's purpose as meeting "the demands of increasing traffic between Puget Sound, British Columbia, and Alaska."[^8]

The point was not the first site considered. The Commissioner's 1915 report says a light was authorized in 1915 for a station "at or near Kellett Bluff, Henry Island," or at some point on the west coast of San Juan Island, but that no money was appropriated that year.[^4] Further investigation then showed "the best location for this light is at the Lime Kiln."[^4] Ships heading north from Point Wilson had a run of about 32 miles before the point, with no fog signal on the American side, and the depths were too great for soundings, the report says.[^4]

LighthouseFriends says the Lighthouse Board first recommended a light on the west side of San Juan Island in 1908.[^2] The 1919 report records the money. An act of October 22, 1913 appropriated $30,000 for aids to navigation in Puget Sound, and an act of July 6, 1916 appropriated $40,000 for a light and fog signal.[^7] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's list of lights established in 1914 includes an acetylene light at Lime Kiln, on Haro Strait.[^9] The nomination says the light "was established in 1914" and that the present structures "were built subsequent to 1917."[^1] LighthouseFriends says that funding from the 1913 act paid for it.[^2] The point's name comes from the lime kilns that were built in the area in the 1860s, LighthouseFriends says.[^2]

In its 1917 report the Commissioner's office recorded the decision: "the point known as Lime Kiln" was "decided upon as the location for this aid," to be a fourth-order flashing light with a third-order fog signal.[^5]

## Building the light

Proposals were invited for the buildings, but the bids were "considered excessive" and rejected, and the work went to hired labor, the 1918 report says.[^6] Construction began in August 1918.[^7] The 1919 description says "the metal work and portions of the millwork were procured by contract, and the balance of the work accomplished by the district force."[^8]

The 1919 description calls the main structure "a combined light tower and fog-signal building of reinforced concrete built on solid rock, with the base about 20 feet (6 m) above high water." The focal plane was 29 feet 7 inches (9 m) above the ground floor.[^8] The lens was fourth order and turned on a mercury float, and the lamp was a 35-millimeter incandescent oil-vapor lamp. The light gave a group flash every ten seconds, "visible approximately 13 miles," the report says.[^8] The two dwellings were one-story reinforced concrete houses, each with six rooms, a bath and a basement.[^8] The archive gives the keepers' quarters as built in 1914 of wood, and Rowlett describes wood keepers' houses.[^3][^13] The nomination and LighthouseFriends describe reinforced concrete, as the 1919 description does.[^1][^2] The sources disagree on the material.

The reports do not agree on the tower's height. The 1919 report calls the light tower "30 feet high" (9 m).[^7] The archive gives 38 feet (11.6 m) from base to the centre of the lantern, and the Rowlett directory gives 38 feet (12 m) for the tower.[^3][^13] The nomination gives a different measurement, 24 feet 6 inches (7.5 m) from the fog-signal room floor to the top of the cornice.[^1]

The nomination calls the illuminant "IOV" and says the light "continued using IOV as the illuminant until after World War II."[^1] The nomination explains the delay as the difficulty of bringing reliable electric power to the site.[^1]

The Rowlett directory says "the present light tower was added in 1938."[^13] The 1919 description already gives a concrete light tower, and the archive dates the tower to 1919.[^3][^8] This story keeps the 1919 date and treats the 1938 claim as unconfirmed.

## The keepers

Clifford B. Hermann was the first head keeper, from 1919 to 1922.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he was moved from the head keeper's post at Destruction Island that year and that he had begun there as a second assistant in 1905.[^3][^2] The Bulletin shows him later as keeper at Heceta Head, in Oregon.[^12] LighthouseFriends says he served three years at Lime Kiln, three at Cape Arago and then twenty-five at Heceta Head.[^2] Frank DeRoy, the first assistant, served from 1919 to 1920.[^3]

Louis Betteker kept the light from 1922 to 1932.[^3] A Bulletin index covering 1918 to 1923 names a "Betteker, Lewis," keeper at Semiahmoo Harbor, with an efficiency flag awarded.[^11] LighthouseFriends says Betteker had earlier served at two Alaska lights and at Semiahmoo, and that he ended his thirty-year career at West Point Lighthouse in 1938.[^2]

Ernest E. Day was first assistant from 1924 to 1932 and then head keeper until 1935.[^3] Arvel A. Settles, head keeper from 1935 to 1945, transferred from Grays Harbor in 1935.[^2] LighthouseFriends says he brought his wife and five children. Their father paid the children ten cents to wind up the weights that turned the lens.[^2]

The archive lists Nels A. Howe as head keeper from 1948 to 1952, then Clyde W. Crawford (at least 1959 to 1960) and Thomas E. Anderson from 1960.[^3] Coast Guard names from the late 1940s into the early 1960s, in the archive's list, are Niles C. Pendleton, Louis C. Knight, Robert D. Benson and Gerald L. Nevils.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1923, LighthouseFriends says, Keeper Betteker gave dry clothing to two men whose boat, the *Fairplay*, had caught fire, and later took them to Friday Harbor. LighthouseFriends is the only source read for this rescue.[^2]

## After the keepers

The light used oil vapor until after the Second World War, the nomination says.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the Bonneville Power Administration laid a submarine cable from Anacortes to the San Juan Islands in 1951, that power lines reached the lighthouse, and that the Fresnel lens was later replaced by a non-rotating 375-mm drum lens with an electric bulb.[^2]

The light was automated in 1962, and a chain-link fence topped with barbed wire went up around the lighthouse.[^2] On December 15, 1978 the light station was declared a historic site by the state and placed on the National Register.[^2]

In 1985 the lighthouse and the sea around it were dedicated as a whale sanctuary and research station. The Whale Museum in Friday Harbor leased the light station for whale tracking and research.[^2][^13] With the lighthouse staffed again, the fence came down.[^2] The Friends of Lime Kiln Society (FOLKS) was formed in 2011 to develop educational programs at the lighthouse.[^2] The light's centennial was celebrated in June 2019.[^2]

## Today

Lime Kiln Point is a Washington State Park. The state parks page says the lighthouse is open to the public from Thursday to Sunday, 12 to 4 p.m., and that the interpretive center, the lighthouse and the special programs are run by volunteers from FOLKS.[^14]

The light flashes white once every ten seconds, and LighthouseFriends says the fog signal has been discontinued.[^2] The Rowlett directory still lists a fog horn that sounds every 30 seconds.[^13]

The sources do not agree on ownership. LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard owns the lighthouse and Washington State Parks manages it.[^2] The Rowlett directory gives the owner as "Washington State Parks (?)".[^13] The 1977 nomination says the Coast Guard kept only the light and fog-signal building and the oil house, and that the keepers' houses stood on land it no longer controlled.[^1] The keepers' houses now house state park staff, according to LighthouseFriends and Rowlett.[^2][^13]
