Little River Light is a small cast-iron tower on an island at the mouth of Cutler Harbor, in Washington County, Maine. The harbor behind it is the last sheltered harbor on the Maine coast before the Canadian border,[^1] and the light's present owners call it the most northeastern island light station in the United States.[^2]

## The place

In 1842 I. W. P. Lewis wrote that no place on the Maine coast would be better served by a light than the island at the entrance of this harbor, where, he said, "a small beacon light" was all that was needed.[^3] Between Little River and West Quoddy Head, as LighthouseFriends summarizes his report, there was no usable harbor, only two indents, Moose Cove and Baylie's Mistake, lined with reefs and unsafe to enter. Lewis believed a light would stop mariners mistaking those false harbors for Little River.[^3]

The National Register nomination adds the wider purpose: the station was meant to guide vessels to the refuge at Cutler Harbor and to serve as a navigational mark between West Quoddy Head to the northeast, Libby Island to the southwest and the Canadian light on Machias Seal Island.[^4] The nomination describes Cutler as a village built on a deep, ice-free harbor under high protective bluffs, incorporated in 1826, with 820 people in 1850 and local industries of fishing, shipbuilding and a herring box factory.[^4] The island itself is about 15 to 17 acres, depending on the source.[^4][^5]

## Building the light

Sources disagree on the first year. The US Lighthouse Society's archive gives 1847 as the year the station was established,[^6] while TravelMaine says a lighthouse was placed here in 1846;[^13] LighthouseFriends says Congress appropriated $5,000 in March 1847 for a 23-and-a-half-foot (7 m) conical stone tower with an attached keeper's house, and that the light's seven lamps and reflectors first shone in the spring of 1848 under Keeper Zebediah Tucker.[^3] The archive's roster likewise begins Tucker's term in 1848.[^6]

About two years after the first lighting, an inspector found the dwelling in poor shape. As LighthouseFriends quotes him, "The whole establishment is very leaky," though "the keeper is a good one."[^3] In 1855 a revolving fifth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps and reflectors,[^6][^3] and in 1871 a fog-bell tower was built about 200 feet (61 m) southeast of the lighthouse, with a Stevens striking machine tolling the bell once every 30 seconds.[^3]

By 1876 the stone tower was judged "unworthy of repairs," and a cast-iron tower, lined with brick, was begun.[^3] The National Register nomination, which names the US Army Corps of Engineers as designer, describes four sections of iron on a steel-and-brick frame, an octagonal lantern, and a height of 35 feet (11 m) to the centre of the lantern.[^4] The archive and LighthouseFriends instead give 41 feet (12.5 m) for the tower.[^6][^3] The top of the old stone tower was removed in 1879, and in 1888 the old stone dwelling was torn down and the present wooden house was built on its foundation.[^3][^4] The boathouse dates from 1881 and the brick oil house from 1905.[^4]

The tower's colour changed several times. LighthouseFriends reports it red in 1878, white by 1881 and brown from at least 1892;[^3] the Light-House Board recorded the change of colour on 20 May 1900.[^7] In 1916 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin listed Little River among lights whose illuminant changed from oil to incandescent oil vapor, with the order of the lens going from fifth to fourth.[^8]

## The keepers

The archive lists eleven principal keepers of the civilian era, from Tucker (1848-1849) to Gleason W. Colbeth (1944-1946).[^6] John McGuire was in charge from 1853 to 1865 and, LighthouseFriends says, earned $250 a year when the Fresnel lens arrived.[^6][^3] Lucius Davis served 26 years, 1870 to 1896,[^6] and LighthouseFriends records that he gave food and shelter to a schooner's crew that ran aground on 21 November 1875, and to a badly frost-bitten crew wrecked on 28 January 1881.[^3]

Frederick W. Morong kept the light from 1898 to 1913.[^6] LighthouseFriends tells a family story of his daughter Myra's suitor from Cutler, kept overnight because of the weather, who was later found to have rowed home for his pajamas.[^3]

Willie W. Corbett followed Charles A. Kenney (1913-1923) and, in the archive's roster, kept the light from 1923 to 1944;[^6] Jeremy D'Entremont's history gives 1921 to 1939 instead.[^1] His son Myron wrote about unloading coal: launches brought 200-pound bags, men carried them up the beach on their backs, and eight or nine tons had to be moved, then wheeled over the island's hump on a planked trestle.[^3] Both the light and the fog bell ran on clockwork that had to be wound by hand every few hours, and LighthouseFriends adds that there could be more than 500 hours of fog in a month.[^3] D'Entremont gives 525 hours in one July.[^1] The keeper's children went to school on the mainland by boat, and the keeper ferried them across; on days too rough for the crossing they stayed with families ashore, according to Corbett's grandson Gordon.[^3]

The Lighthouse Service became part of the Coast Guard in 1939. LighthouseFriends says Colbeth chose to join the Coast Guard, was urged to retire in 1950, was ordered to take a physical, and was put on disability in July 1950; the archive roster lists his term at Little River as 1944 to 1946, and the two accounts are not reconciled here.[^3][^6]

In the Coast Guard years crews were small. In the early 1950s, D'Entremont writes, the officer in charge and another man each spent 21 days on the station followed by seven off, so each was alone for about a week a month; there was no electricity, the light still burned kerosene, and the fog bell had to be wound by hand. Glendon Jones remembered starting the bell on a Saturday morning at 7:15 and winding it every two hours until the fog lifted a week later.[^1] Little River was a three-man station until 23 July 1973, when orders made it a one-man family station.[^1] Chester Nichols Jr., the last Coast Guard keeper, arrived in July 1974 with his wife and two-year-old daughter.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The best-documented wreck near the station was that of the Spanish steamer Eduardo, which struck Old Man Island, two miles south of Cutler Harbor, at low tide at midnight on 21 July 1889 in dense fog. She filled with water and was a total loss, but her crew of 40 was saved. She registered 2,308 tons and had cost $285,000.[^9] The Light-House Board's district inspector reported calm weather with a rough sea and dense fog; the Eduardo had heard neither the station's automatic fog bell nor the whistling buoy off the entrance.[^9] The Board added, from a look at the chart, that Little River opens to the southeast and that the high land of Little River Head hides the light station until a vessel bears about north.[^9]

The Board asked Congress for a steam fog signal at Little River Head. Its report for 1893 estimated $10,500,[^9] and the 1900 report said the recommendation had been made in six annual reports and that the estimate had risen to $12,500.[^10] The Board's own reasoning was that the Eduardo's master had said the accident would not have occurred with a proper fog signal there.[^10] In December 1903 the Ellsworth American reported that the Secretary of Commerce and Labor had asked $15,000 for a steam fog signal at the entrance to Cutler Harbor.[^11] LighthouseFriends says the recommendation was repeated for over fifteen years and a powerful fog signal was never built.[^3]

The bell that remained had limits. In the Board's fog-signal survey, covering the year to June 1893, the Little River keeper (unnamed in the report) reported that in a south wind the bell could not be heard two ship lengths away, while the Libby Islands whistle, 7.5 miles away to the west, was usually heard at Little River.[^12]

In 1917, after the United States entered the First World War, the station came under the Navy; the station log records a telephone cable laid on 10 July and a telephone installed on 16 August, and by early October it was an official Navy signal station.[^3]

## After the keepers

The station was automated in 1975. The Fresnel lens came out of the tower and a modern light went on a skeleton tower nearby.[^3][^4] In 1981, LighthouseFriends reports, the Coast Guard planned to demolish the lighthouse and sell the island, but a prominent local resident intervened and the plan was tabled; with no resident keeper, the buildings deteriorated.[^3] The University of Maine at Machias held a lease for a time, and a teacher lived there as caretaker from April 1977 to August 1978.[^1]

The American Lighthouse Foundation was licensed in 2000 and helped pay for the rebuilding of the boardwalk from the boathouse to the lighthouse that summer, which the Coast Guard carried out.[^1] In 2001 it restored the lantern room so the Coast Guard could install a beacon in the tower, dark since 1975. The relighting on 2 October 2001 drew more than 1,000 people, and, LighthouseFriends says, the tower was draped in a large flag because of the attacks of the month before.[^3] On 27 July 2002 ownership passed to the foundation under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, the first such transfer in New England.[^3][^1] Volunteers organized as the Friends of Little River Lighthouse in 2007, and overnight stays began in 2009.[^3] The Light Station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 14 March 1988.[^13]

## Today

The island is owned by the American Lighthouse Foundation and cared for by its Friends chapter.[^5][^3] The archive records a solar-powered VLB-44 optic installed in February 2019, so the light is active.[^6] The lighthouse is not visible from the shore, and the island is reached by boat. The foundation runs open houses with a boat from the town ramp on Wharf Road in Cutler, cancelled in high winds, dense fog or rough seas, and takes overnight guests in the restored 1888 keeper's house in summer.[^5][^2] Do not land without arranging a visit through the foundation.
