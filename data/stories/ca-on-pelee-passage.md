Pelee Passage Light stands on the Middle Ground, a shoal at the western end of Lake Erie, where ships pass between Point Pelee and Pelee Island.[^1][^2] Its light has shown since 4 July 1902, from a steel tower on a caisson. The tower was replaced in 1975, and the light still runs.[^3][^4][^5]

## The place

Point Pelee is Canada's southernmost mainland point.[^1] It lies on the north side of the Pelee Passage, which runs between the point and Pelee Island.[^1] The passage is a narrow deep-water channel into the western end of the lake.[^2] A reef runs out from the point. An earlier light there, on a caisson sunk on the reef in 1857 after a first foundation was swept away, was burned in 1900.[^1]

The Middle Ground is a shoal in the passage, and the light stands on its north end.[^2] In 1900 the Department chose the Middle Ground for the new light in preference to South-east Shoal, "because vessels can shape a course directly for it from both directions," and because "it is doubtful if a stable foundation could be obtained on the shifting sand of South-east shoal."[^6]

## Building the light

Building began at Amherstburg in August 1900.[^6] The foundation was a steel caisson in the form of a frustum of a cone, filled with concrete and ringed with timber cribwork. The Department's report says it was "successfully sunk in 13 feet of water on the north end of the Middle ground, January 1, 1901," under the supervision of W. H. Noble.[^7][^1] Lighthouse Friends gives the sinking as 1 June 1901. The 1901 report had the foundation "ready for sinking on the opening of navigation next year," so the date is not settled.[^6][^1]

The tower was steel plates built in the form of a cone's frustum, with a polygonal iron lantern. The Department recorded it as 20 m (66 ft) from the deck of the foundation to the vane. The pier was painted brown, the tower white and the lantern red.[^3] The light went into operation on 4 July 1902.[^3] Lighthouse Friends quotes the description: a dioptric lens of the third order, elevated 23 m (75 ft) above the lake and visible 14 miles.[^1] A steam fog siren went into service on 1 October 1902.[^8]

## The keepers

William A. Grubb Jr. was head keeper at Point Pelee until the fire of 1900, when he was trapped under fallen timbers and seriously burned, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] The Department's report says the principal keeper was "temporarily pinned down by fallen timbers," and that he and his assistant "narrowly escaped destruction."[^9]

William A. Manson was head keeper at Pelee Passage from 1902 to 1908, Lighthouse Friends says.[^1] The Department's list of light-keepers printed in 1908 gives "Manson, Wm. A." at the Pelee Passage, Lake Erie, Light and Steam Siren station.[^10] Lighthouse Friends gives the later head keepers as Louis Langlois (1909 to 1910), Stafford Bottom (1910 to 1914), R. McCormack (1914 to 1915), and Joseph Ouelette (1915 to at least 1937). Coleman Main is the last, from at least 1953 to 1959.[^1][^4]

The 1903 keepers' list gives "Bratt, James" at the Middle Ground, Pelee Passage. It also gives "Fortier, Theodore" at the Middle Ground-Pelee Passage. The list does not say what roles these two men held or for how long.[^11]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The fire of 17 April 1900 destroyed the Point Pelee light and its fog alarm. The Department thought it was started by a lamp thrown down when a heavy sea running at the time displaced the wall of the fog-alarm building.[^9] The keeper and his assistant escaped, and a temporary light was shown from an anchor lantern on the wreck of the pier four days later.[^9]

The Department's 1902 report records two warnings respecting the wreck of the Specular in Pelee Passage.[^12]

The barge Tasmania sank in the vicinity of Pelee Island, and her owner did not remove her. The Midland Towing and Wrecking Company removed her under a contract for $3,900.[^13] In May 1906 the schooner Armenia sank about 5.6 km (three and a half miles) from the Pelee Passage light. Her owners abandoned her, and a contract for $5,850 went to the same company to remove the wreck.[^14] The Department's report printed in 1910 records the removal by the same company, for the same $5,850.[^15]

## After the keepers

In 1911 a diaphone replaced the steam fog siren, Lighthouse Friends says.[^1] By 1962 the foundation was heavily damaged.[^1]

In 1975 a new structure replaced the steel tower.[^4] It is a one-storey keepers' building with a helicopter pad on its roof and a cylindrical light tower at one corner, all on a steel caisson.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says Dean Construction dismantled the old tower and, in 1980, re-erected it on the Detroit River shore in Windsor as a gift to the City of Windsor.[^4] The Lighthouse Directory says the old tower was relocated to Lakeview Park in Windsor when the present light went up in 1975.[^2] The two sources do not agree on the year.

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns and manages the station.[^2] Its list of lights gives the characteristic as Fl W 4s (one second of light in every four, dark for three), with a focal height of 28.3 m and a range of 10 nautical miles, and shows the light operating year round.[^5] The site and tower are closed, and the station is reached only by boat.[^2] Keep off the pier.
