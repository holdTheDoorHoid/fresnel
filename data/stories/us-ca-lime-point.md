Lime Point Light stands on a rocky spur on the north side of the Golden Gate, in Marin County, California, at the foot of the north end of the Golden Gate Bridge. It began in 1883 as a fog signal, twin steam whistles that sounded for ships in fog, and it did not show a light until November 1900.[^1][^2]

## The place

The spur is "just twenty feet wide" (6 m) and runs roughly 100 feet (30 m) out into the bay.[^1] The station was built for fog, which LighthouseFriends describes as a very serious danger to vessels near San Francisco.[^1] In 1882 the San Francisco Chamber of Commerce asked Congress for a fog signal at Fort Point, where a fog bell was then in use. LighthouseFriends says the Board found that Fort Point obstructed the transmission of sound, and concluded that the signal should go on Lime Point, on the opposite side of the Golden Gate.[^1]

The land was steep.[^1] A trail ran along the hillside from the station to Sausalito, the nearest town. The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1893, says that "The trail along the hillside leading from the station to the nearest town, Sausalito, was carried away by a heavy storm and landslides."[^6] The report adds that it was rebuilt on a new location, for three-quarters of a mile (1.2 km).[^6]

## Building the light

Congress provided the money, and work on the fog signal began in early March 1883, LighthouseFriends reports.[^1] The site was narrow and steep, and progress was slow at first.[^1] The fog-signal building stood closest to the water, with its twin 12-inch steam whistles powered by coal-fired boilers, and a coal shed and a two-story keepers' duplex went up on the spur.[^1] The archive's record dates the fog-signal building to 1883.[^2] The archive's place record lists no keepers' quarters, which disagrees with LighthouseFriends on the dwelling.[^2] LighthouseFriends dates the start of operation to 10 September 1883.[^1]

Water was a problem from the start.[^1] The first spring could not supply enough, so a pipe was run to a second spring. Heavy rain in the first winter brought a landslide that damaged the water tank, and the tank was moved to the top of the bluff, some 1,800 feet (550 m) from the fog signal.[^1]

The light came later. The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1901 lists "Lime Point light-station, San Francisco Bay, California," with "A lens-lantern light November 26, 1900."[^5] The same report says that "A white lens-lantern light was established and a small oil house was built" at the station.[^4] LighthouseFriends places the lantern on the southeast corner of the fog-signal building's gallery, 19.5 feet (5.9 m) above the water.[^1] The archive's record gives the same height, and describes the tower as square and brick, its foundation mounted on the wall of the signal building.[^2] In 1902 the boilers were converted from coal to oil. LighthouseFriends gives the cost of running the signal for a day as $25.44 before the change and $6.91 after it.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive lists 23 roster lines for the station, from 1883 to 1960.[^2] Reinhold Holzhuter was the first head keeper, from 1883 to 1901. Patrick J. Quinlan was the first assistant, from 1883 to 1887.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends says each of the station's two keepers stood two six-hour watches a day, on alternate shifts.[^1]

John McKenna took over as head keeper in 1901. LighthouseFriends reports that on 22 December 1902, when he was returning from a day of Christmas shopping in San Francisco, he fell down an embankment and was killed. He was forty-seven, and had earlier served at Point Bonita and Point Reyes.[^1][^2]

Peter C. Nelson became head keeper in 1903 and stayed until 1931. LighthouseFriends says he had the longest tenure of any keeper there, twenty-eight years on the point.[^1][^2] Edward Wiborg was first assistant from 1906 to 1916.[^2] Peter Ditlevson was first assistant from 1916 to 1931, then head keeper until 1940.[^2] Elmer R. Williams (1940 to 1946) and Richard E. Powell (1946 to 1958) followed him. The latest keeper the archive names is Nieves Saldate, "at least 1960."[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the fiscal year ended June 30, 1894, the Board's report says the 12-inch steam whistles at Lime Point "were in operation some 1,061 hours, and consumed about 95 tons of coal."[^7] The report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1900 gives 906 hours and about 94 tons of coal for the same whistles.[^3]

In September 1909 Keeper Nelson saw three young men in a sailboat that had lost its sail and was being carried out toward Mile Rocks by the tide.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he cheered them on through a megaphone, told them he would call the life-saving crew at Fort Point, and was credited with saving their lives.[^1] In 1910, LighthouseFriends says, three men tried to climb a rocky cliff near Lime Point, and one gave out halfway up.[^1] Nelson called the Fort Point crew again, and the man was not rescued until well after dark.[^1]

LighthouseFriends also says the 1906 earthquake wrecked the station's large water tank, and that a new 30,000-gallon redwood tank went up on ten concrete piers.[^1]

Nelson's own accident is in the newspapers. The San Francisco Call of 23 April 1913 reported that he "narrowly escaped death by drowning yesterday when he fell from the rock on which the lighthouse stands into the water." He had lost his balance while whitewashing the rock. His assistant, Ed Wiborg, and "a party of soldiers from Fort Baker" pulled him out.[^8]

On 11 December 1932 the fog signals were sounded during a heavy snowfall. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin says the signals at the entrance to San Francisco Bay were operated "on account of a heavy fall of snow." Five stations reported their signals in operation that day: "Bonita Point, Mile Rocks, Lime Point, Point Diabolo [sic], and Fort Point." The Bulletin adds that "No previous entry of this nature is found in the records of these stations."[^10]

On 3 June 1960, LighthouseFriends reports, the 440-foot (134 m) freighter India Bear struck the concrete causeway to the station while the fog signal was sounding.[^1] The only loss at the station was an outhouse, which the Coast Guard was already planning to remove.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1923 the Point Diablo light, 1.2 miles (1.9 km) west of Lime Point, came under the Lime Point station's care.[^1] The Bulletin says that "siren and light are controlled from Lime Point Light Station," and that telephones were located at both stations.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the keepers took on the added job of maintaining a minor light there.[^1]

When the Golden Gate Bridge was completed in 1937, a foghorn and light were placed at the base of the bridge, making Fort Point Lighthouse unnecessary.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the Lime Point site stayed useful for a light and foghorn.[^1]

Lime Point was automated in July 1961. LighthouseFriends says the three-story dwelling and the other outbuildings were torn down.[^1]

## Today

LighthouseFriends says the pocked fog-signal building is all that remains of the station.[^1] The archive lists the station as not open to the public, and as an active aid to navigation.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports, without giving a date, that the Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, that it is being transferred to the Golden Gate National Recreation Area, and that the grounds are closed.[^1] The dirt road to the station is not open to the public.[^1] Visitors see the station from the Golden Gate Bridge or from the bay cruises that pass near it.[^1]
