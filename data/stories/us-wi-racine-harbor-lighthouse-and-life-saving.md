Racine Harbor Lighthouse stands on the north pier at the mouth of the Root River in Racine, Wisconsin, on the western shore of Lake Michigan. Its brick keeper's house and tower were finished in 1866, and the Life-Saving Service station beside it was completed in 1903. The pier light was the second light at this river; the first, on the shore, was lit in 1839.[^1][^2]

## The place

In 1837 Lieutenant G. J. Pendergrast examined lighthouse sites on the Great Lakes for the Board of Navy Commissioners. LighthouseFriends quotes his report as saying that a light at the river would be useful "not only as a guide to the anchorage, but to warn vessels of a dangerous reef lying about two and a half miles from the shore."[^1] Congress had already appropriated $5,000 for a light at Root River in March 1837.[^1] In 1859 the Racine board of trade petitioned Congress for a pierhead light on the north pier, and Congress answered the next year with $30,100 for "the construction of a suitable beacon light at the port of Racine."[^1]

The 1975 National Register nomination describes the town as "once a leading Wisconsin commercial lake port," which became a shipbuilding and shipping center in the second half of the nineteenth century.[^2]

## Building the light

The first light stood on the bluff above the river.[^1] Benjamin Pratt, the first brick manufacturer in Racine, began work in 1838 on a conical brick tower just over 28 feet (8.5 m) tall, with lamps set in reflectors.[^1][^3] It went into service in 1839 with Amaziah Stebbins as its first keeper.[^1] A fifth-order Fresnel lens replaced the reflectors for the 1858 navigation season.[^1]

The pier light was a second project. The nomination says it was "Begun by the federal government in 1863" and was "finally completed in 1866."[^2] The Board's 1862 report says the contractor had failed to procure enough timber, and a scarcity of labour then delayed the crib work until 1865.[^1] The brick house and tower went up on the completed pier in 1866.[^1] The sources disagree on the brick: the nomination says the building was "cream-colored brick," and LighthouseFriends calls it yellow.[^2][^1] The light was activated on 10 September 1866 with the fifth-order lens moved from the old shore light, which was then discontinued.[^1]

In 1870 the old shore light was sold to two men, L. S. Blake and James T. Elliott, for $1,625. In 1876 Elliott became sole owner, tore the old tower and the adjacent keeper's dwelling down, and used the bricks to build a house on the site.[^1]

A wooden framework tower with a red light went up at the end of the pier in 1872, to range with the white light on the crib.[^1] The Light-House Board's annual report in the 1896-97 volume records the pierhead's sixth-order light as "reestablished, June 18,1896."[^5]

## The keepers

The archive's roster for the shore light runs from 1839 to 1865. Amaziah Stebbins came first, served to 1846, and was followed by John F. Trowbridge and others. Isaac B. Gates held the post twice, once from "1853-1855" and again from "1856-1857".[^3] The keeper's house there measured twenty by thirty-four feet, with a kitchen attached.[^1]

From 1865 the archive's roster for the pier light begins with James Ginty, who served to 1883. Lawrence A. Easson, George Larson and Martin N. Knudsen followed as head keepers, Knudsen to 1918.[^4] Edward W. Knudsen served as a first assistant in 1899 to 1902 and again from 1907 to 1911, then as head keeper from 1918 to 1932.[^4] The archive also lists Theresa W. Knudsen as a first assistant in 1907.[^4]

In 1888 the keepers' crib work was rebuilt around the dwelling. LighthouseFriends, quoting the Board's 1889 report, describes a picket fence 267 feet (81 m) long built on top of the crib work to keep the occupants from falling overboard.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Life-Saving Service's reports of the early 1880s name the station as Station No. 14 (Racine, Wisconsin), Eleventh District.[^7][^8]

In August 1880 the station patrol reported a vessel ashore near Racine. The keeper and crew went to the scene and, finding no help needed, "set a watch over the stranded vessel," which was the schooner Alleghany of Erie.[^7] In 1881 a vessel grounded on a reef off Racine in a heavy fog. She showed a torch, which, the report says, "was not visible at the nearest life-saving station."[^8]

On 10 June 1882 the crew of Station No. 14 rescued George Swift, a young man of Racine, from drowning in Lake Michigan. The report says he had, it appears, rashly tried to make the seven-mile passage between North Point and the harbour.[^9]

The station's own building changed too. The Service's 1902 report says the widening of the Racine channel "made it necessary to move the station there back from the river nearly into one of the city streets, for temporary use."[^10] A new station was begun on a better site. The 1903 report calls the old building one "originally designed and used for a boathouse," and says the new one was "in process of construction."[^11] The 1904 report says that new station buildings, begun in the fiscal year 1903, had been "completed and occupied" at Racine.[^12] The nomination says the two-story frame station and boathouse were completed in 1903.[^2]

The station's work was search and rescue along the Milwaukee-Kenosha shore and out into Lake Michigan. In 1915 the Life-Saving Service was merged into the Coast Guard.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1901 the Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 says the board had "decided to discontinue the pierhead light," and would establish a fourth-order red light at the pierhead. The report also says plans were prepared for a metal pierhead beacon.[^6] LighthouseFriends gives the date the old pier light was discontinued as 23 November 1901, and says its fourth-order lens went into the metal tower.[^1] The nomination says that in 1903 the white light "was removed to a steel tower on the end of the north pier."[^2] The sources disagree on the year.

The keeper's dwelling was altered as well. The Board's report records that "Additions and alterations were made to the dwelling" that year.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the dwelling was remodelled to give an assistant keeper an apartment on its second storey, with the head keeper living on the ground floor.[^1] The tower was capped with a hipped roof.[^1]

The Bulletin of 1912 lists "Racine Pierhead, Wis. (acetylene)" among the lights changed from fixed to flashing or occulting.[^13] The Racine Harbor light became part of the adjacent Coast Guard station in 1939. That station was decommissioned in 1971, and its buildings are now part of Pugh Marina.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse and the old life-saving station building were added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1975.[^1] The nomination named the Coast Guard as owner, which was then in litigation.[^2] LighthouseFriends now says Pugh Marina owns the lighthouse, and that its grounds are open while the dwelling is closed.[^1] LighthouseFriends places the red metal pierhead tower on the breakwater at Racine.[^1]

The 1975 nomination also described a 120-foot (37 m) galvanized steel storm-warning tower behind the station. It warned ships entering the harbour or sailing along the coast of any impending storm. At night, it says, lanterns "helped to point out possible storms and their direction."[^2]
