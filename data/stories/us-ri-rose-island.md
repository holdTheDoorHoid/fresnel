Rose Island is a 14-acre island in the middle of Narragansett Bay, south of today's Newport Bridge, and its small red-and-white lighthouse sits on a circular stone bastion left over from an unfinished fort.[^1][^3] The house-and-tower light was lit in January 1870, went dark in 1971, and was brought back by local volunteers, who relit it in 1993.[^2][^3]

## The place

The nomination for the National Register of Historic Places describes the island as lying in the main passage up the bay, northwest of Newport Harbor and half a mile south of the Newport Bridge. It says the light was built in the heyday of coastal steamers and freighters to warn navigators off the shoals north of the harbour.[^1] The lighthouse stands on a circular stone bastion, 85 feet (26 m) across and 12 feet (3.7 m) high, which the nomination dates to about 1798 as the southwest work of Fort Hamilton.[^1] The island kept its military use: according to LighthouseFriends, the Navy used it to store explosives in both world wars, and a keeper's cow was once housed in a building that also held explosives.[^3]

## Building the light

Congress allocated $7,500 in 1868, according to LighthouseFriends, and the house went up in 1869. The same source says it replaced a private light kept by the Bristol Steam Boat Company.[^3] The nomination gives 1869 for construction and January 1870 for service; LighthouseFriends and the Rose Island Lighthouse Foundation both give the first lighting as 20 January 1870.[^1][^3][^6]

The building is a 1½-storey wooden dwelling with a mansard roof, 30 by 26 feet (9 by 8 m), with an octagonal tower and iron lantern rising from its west face. The nomination puts the whole at about 40 feet (12 m) from the ground to the top of the lantern.[^1] The lantern held a red sixth-order Fresnel lens, which the nomination says showed 61 feet (19 m) above the water and could be seen for eleven miles.[^1] (Today's foundation gives the tower as 48 feet (15 m) over the bay, so the old and new heights differ.[^6]) The house was divided into two dwellings, one on each floor, and its wide gutters fed rainwater to a brick-vaulted cistern in the cellar.[^1]

A fog bell on an A-frame tower was put into operation on 10 August 1885 and struck a double blow every fifteen seconds in thick weather, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] The Light-House Board's report for 1901 records that a brick oil house was built at Rose Island.[^4] In 1912 a brick fog-signal building went up on the rocks just west of the lighthouse, and the foundation says a fog horn replaced the bell on 12 November 1912.[^3][^6] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1913 reports that horns at Rose Island were tested for how far they carried in different directions, with satisfactory results.[^9]

## The keepers

The register of keepers, as transcribed by the Rhode Island Lighthouse History site, shows John Bailey Cozzens appointed on 25 November 1869 at $500 a year, and the US Lighthouse Society archive lists him as head keeper from 1869 to 1872.[^7][^2] The foundation's website instead names George C. Williams, a Civil War veteran, as the first keeper; the register has Williams appointed on 6 November 1872.[^6][^7] The nomination says the station's first keepers were wounded Union Army veterans who lived there with their families.[^1] Gustave A. Clark was appointed acting keeper on 14 June 1879 and confirmed in February 1881.[^7]

Charles S. Curtis was appointed acting keeper on 23 March 1887 and confirmed on 14 September 1887 at $540 a year; the archive lists him as head keeper until 1919, while other sources end his service in 1918.[^7][^2][^3][^6] A New England Lighthouse Stories article says he was a Civil War veteran who had been first assistant at Sakonnet Light the year before.[^8] It describes him mounting a mirror on a pole outside his window so he could watch the light from the house.[^8] LighthouseFriends calls his tenure the longest on the island and says he earned two life-saving medals, one of them in 1914 for rescuing men in a disabled powerboat that was being carried out to sea.[^3]

Rose Island was not as isolated as many stations, but the keepers' wages were small. Curtis and a later head keeper, Jesse Orton, both kept cows for milk, and the animals sometimes strayed into the military compound, to the officers' annoyance.[^3] Curtis's daughter Mabel rowed more than a mile to school in Newport, the same article says.[^8] LighthouseFriends and New England Lighthouse Stories both tell how she once kept the fog bell going by hand with a hammer for hours, when fog came down while her parents were ashore.[^3][^8]

His grandson Wanton Chase, born in 1909, was sent to live on the island as a sickly baby. LighthouseFriends, drawing on his own written memories, has him there until about age seven; the foundation says he lived there from 1913 to 1918, from age five to ten.[^3][^6] His recollections later guided the restoration, and the lighthouse museum is arranged to look as it did in 1910 to 1918.[^6]

LighthouseFriends says Julius Johansen came in 1912 as the station's first assistant keeper, to help with the work of the new fog signal; the archive lists him for 1912 to 1915.[^2][^3] After Curtis came Thomas Pickup (1919 to 1921) and Jesse Orton (1921 to 1936), then Charles H. Eldridge, Douglas H. Shepherd and George S. Bell Jr., according to the archive.[^2] The foundation's own list gives some of these years differently.[^6] The nomination says lighthouse tending stayed a family affair until the Coast Guard took over in 1941.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends quotes the station log for 1876. On 2 February the wind came close to hurricane force and shook the tower; two weeks later another storm dragged the station boat and its 600-pound mooring up the beach; in April another gale brought down a chimney and the roof leaked; in November a flood ruined the winter's stored vegetables. The log's December entry reads: "Stormiest December on record, much damage to property and according to report, much loss of life at sea."[^3]

LighthouseFriends says the steamer *Plymouth* went aground on the south shore of the island in 1894 with 700 passengers, who were taken off unhurt.[^3] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 separately records that the day-beacon spindle on the south side of Rose Island had been bent over by the steamer *Plymouth* (the scan reads "Plymotith").[^10]

In the great storm of February 1899, the Providence News reported on 16 February, Curtis and his family were "in such dire straits" that he climbed the flagstaff, with the halyards frozen, to hang out a distress signal. The revenue cutter *Dexter* saw it and broke through ice to reach the island; the sick were taken to the torpedo station and the cutter landed food and coal.[^5] The New England Lighthouse Stories article puts the date at 14 February and says the flag was flown upside down.[^8] The same article says that in April 1894 Curtis's son saw grave robbers digging on the island and chased them, but they escaped by boat into the fog.[^8]

On 7 August 1958, LighthouseFriends reports, two tankers collided in fog near Fort Adams and caught fire. One of them, the *Graham*, drifted past within 200 yards of the lighthouse, and the walls grew so hot that the keepers left the station. Eighteen men from the two ships died.[^3]

## After the keepers

The Newport Bridge opened in 1969, and the nomination says its construction changed the main channel and made Rose Island Light obsolete; it was deactivated in 1971.[^1] The nomination adds that the building served for a time as a University of Rhode Island monitoring station, and by 1987 it was vacant and being vandalized.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the university's tenure from 1976.[^3] The foundation says the original lens was left in the lantern and that vandals broke it, with only pieces remaining.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the lens's whereabouts are unknown.[^3]

A group led by Charlotte Johnson and Curt Bunting formed the Rose Island Lighthouse Foundation in 1984, and the lighthouse was deeded to the City of Newport on 2 October 1985.[^6] LighthouseFriends dates the foundation's formation to July 1984, with an exterior refurbishment finished in 1990, and puts the restoration cost above a million dollars.[^3] The light shone again on 7 August 1993, as a private aid to navigation maintained by the foundation.[^3][^6] The foundation bought the rest of the island in 1999, and a replica sixth-order Fresnel lens was installed in 2013.[^3] The nomination form for the National Register was received by the National Park Service in 1987, and the foundation says the building is on the Register today.[^1][^6]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by the City of Newport and run by the Rose Island Lighthouse Foundation; the island is reached by ferry, and LighthouseFriends gives opening as daily, 10 a.m. to 4 p.m., April through October.[^3] Check the foundation's current schedule before travelling. The ground floor is a museum, and visitors can stay overnight in the lighthouse and other buildings.[^3]
