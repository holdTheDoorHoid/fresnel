West Chop Light stands on the west side of the entrance to Vineyard Haven harbor, on Martha's Vineyard.[^1] It was first lit on October 5, 1817, and it was the last light on the island to lose its resident keeper, when it was automated in 1976.[^2] The Coast Guard still owns the station, and its grounds, dwellings and tower are closed to the public.[^2]

## The place

Holmes Hole, later called Vineyard Haven, was one of the island's earliest settlements.[^2] LighthouseFriends says its residents felt neglected because a light on Cape Poge, built in 1801, guided ships to Edgartown, even though more ships came to Holmes Hole. They appealed to their congressman, and on March 3, 1817, Congress appropriated $5,000 for a light "on the west chop of Holmes' Hole Harbor."[^2]

The site was four acres, bought for $225 from Abijah and Mary Luce in April 1817.[^1][^2] Duncan McBean built a 25-foot (7.6 m) rubblestone tower and a stone dwelling on it.[^2]

The sea was already at work on the bluff. In 1838 Lieutenant Edward W. Carpender reported, as LighthouseFriends quotes his report, that "the sea is encroaching upon this tower and dwelling," and he recommended moving the light about three hundred yards (274 m) south if it became necessary.[^2]

## Building the light

Captain James Shaw West was hired as the first keeper, and the light went into service on October 5, 1817.[^2] Carpender's 1838 description gives ten lamps in two tiers, and the survey form says the lamps sat in an octagonal iron lens room.[^1][^2]

The survey form, which gives no year, says the first tower leaked and was sheathed in wooden siding.[^1] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1892 calls the tower of 1846 rubble masonry, and says it was demolished when a brick tower, 45 feet (13.7 m) high, was built on its foundation.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the new conical stone tower and keeper's house went up in 1846 about 1,000 feet (300 m) to the southeast.[^2] The survey form, though, says the light was first established at its present location in 1847, and its chronology puts a brick tower on the site in October 1846.[^1]

Winslow Lewis installed a system of reflectors in 1848. A fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced them in 1857.[^2]

LighthouseFriends reports that a steam fog whistle went into operation on December 1, 1881, in a new frame engine house.[^2] On August 20, 1891, the light moved to a temporary tower about 60 feet (18 m) from the old one, and on December 31, 1891, it moved into the new brick tower built on the old site.[^4] The Board's report gives that tower as 45 feet (13.7 m) high and 17 feet (5.2 m) taller than the one before it; the survey form gives it as 52 feet (15.8 m).[^3][^4][^1]

In the fiscal year ended June 30, 1895, the Board reported that an oil house was built and the fog-signal machinery overhauled.[^7] The survey form, though, dates a brick oil house with a slate roof to 1847.[^1]

## The keepers

Keeping the light ran in families. James Shaw West kept it for thirty years, until 1847, and LighthouseFriends says he resigned that year and was replaced by Charles West, who was not his son.[^2][^8]

Charles West kept the light until 1868, when his son, also named Charles, took over.[^2] Charles P. West was head keeper from 1868 to 1881, listed as first assistant from 1881 to 1883, and head keeper again from 1883 to 1909.[^8]

Keepers reported on the work. In 1838 Carpender found the light "in admirable order," as LighthouseFriends quotes his report.[^2] In 1843 West, in an account LighthouseFriends also quotes, described the tower's winter, with ice on the inside walls and on the lantern glass, and the ice he had to cut off the deck with an axe.[^2]

George E. Dolby was first assistant from 1885 to 1898 and head keeper from 1909 to 1919. James Yates was head keeper from 1919 to 1940, and Charles A. Ellis, first assistant from 1923 to 1940, was head keeper until 1945.[^8]

Octave Ponsart was head keeper from 1946 to 1957. His daughter, Seamond Ponsart, lived at the station with him and later wrote a book about the family's life there, which LighthouseFriends quotes. In one story from the book, the family's two geese flew off the bluff into Vineyard Sound, and a Coast Guard boat crew from the Menemsha station picked them up and brought them home.[^2]

The roster has a separate Coast Guard section, with keepers and assistants from 1961 to 1975.[^8][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Carpender's report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says "no less than 127 vessels have been ashore here during the twenty years this keeper has attended the light."[^2]

The bluff was the storm danger that kept coming back. In his 1843 account West wrote that the bluff had washed away to within 37 feet (11 m) of the base, and that he had known 30 feet (9 m) of it to wash away in one storm.[^2] He also complained that he was "not allowed a boat," which kept him from helping the vessels that got ashore nearby.[^2]

The fog whistle ran often. The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1892 gives 485 hours and about 24 tons of coal, and its report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1899 gives "some 559 hours" and about 32 tons.[^5][^6]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says an oil-engine air compressor replaced the steam fog signal in 1923. The survey form says a compressor replaced the steam boiler in 1933.[^2][^1]

After automation the keepers' dwellings were used for a few years as offices for the Vineyard Environmental Research Institute. Today the nearer house is a Coast Guard residence, and the second is a vacation house for military personnel.[^2]

The nomination form that covers the station is stamped May 1987.[^1]

On August 7, 2000, President Clinton signed the Oceans Act of 2000 at the Coast Guard station at West Chop. The Presidency Project's record says he "spoke at 11:41 a.m. at the U.S. Coast Guard Station at West Chop Lighthouse." LighthouseFriends gives the date as August 8.[^9][^2]

## Today

The station is Coast Guard property. LighthouseFriends says the grounds, dwellings and tower are closed, and it lists the Coast Guard as owner.[^2][^1]

The light is an active aid to navigation. It shows a white light that occults every four seconds, with a red sector over Squash Meadow and Norton Shoal, and its fourth-order Fresnel lens is still in use.[^2][^1]

The light can be seen from the ferries running between Vineyard Haven and Woods Hole.[^2]
