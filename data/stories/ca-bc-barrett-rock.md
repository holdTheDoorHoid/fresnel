Barrett Rock Light stands on a rock just offshore from Barrett Point, at the entrance to Prince Rupert harbour on the north coast of British Columbia.[^1] The Department of Marine built a combined concrete tower and fog alarm on the rock in the year to 31 March 1920, with a keeper's dwelling on the shore.[^2]

## The place

Just offshore from Barrett Point lies a rock that LighthouseFriends describes as a "submerged and dangerous obstruction" for mariners.[^3] The Department's 1907–08 buoy list places a Barrett Ledge in Chatham Sound, which may be a different feature.[^4]

## Building the light

The Department's report for the year to 31 March 1920 says the concrete light tower and fog alarm were put up on the rock, with a wooden keeper's dwelling and engine room on the shore opposite.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the work was especially difficult, because the rock was uncovered only four or five times in the summer of 1919.[^6] It names E. H. Shockley of Prince Rupert as the builder of the 12 m (40 ft) tower.[^6] Its generators were driven by gasoline engines in the keeper's dwelling, and submarine cables carried the power out to the tower, where an air compressor worked the diaphone fog signal.[^6]

A notice of 1919, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, announced that an unwatched occulting red acetylene light would be lit about 1 March 1920. Its fog signal was to be worked from a dwelling on shore, and the gas and bell buoy was to be discontinued when the light went into operation.[^7]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends says James Hall was hired as the first keeper of the light and fog alarm.[^8] Its list continues with Mrs. H. Hall (1921), Arnold Moran (1921 to 1922), H. Greenwood (1922), John L. Milton (1923 to 1932), John Milton, Jr. (1933 to at least 1937) and John T. Moran (1942 to 1945).[^9] This roster comes from LighthouseFriends alone.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In March 1936 the lighthouse tender Newington called at the station to offload oil at the keeper's dwelling. While backing up, she grounded on the rock, and it was hoped the next high tide would free her.[^10] LighthouseFriends also reports that a fishing vessel, the Nemesis, struck the concrete tower and cracked it. It gives no date for that accident.[^11]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the station was "supposedly" de-staffed in 1963, but that Light Lists show resident personnel until 1973.[^12]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard list gives the present light as a flashing red light, once every six seconds, 6.8 metres above the water and visible for seven nautical miles. The tower is white and square, with a red band at the top, and stands 4.6 m high.[^13] LighthouseFriends describes the 2020 tower as a white square skeletal tower with a red band, and says it displayed a flashing red light.[^14] Rowlett describes a round white tower, 7 m (23 ft), atop a square stone tower.[^15] The sources disagree about the structure.

The rock is reached only by boat. Rowlett gives the site as open and the tower as closed, with the Canadian Coast Guard as owner and site manager.[^15] LighthouseFriends says the modern light can be easily seen from the ferry that calls at Prince Rupert.[^16]
