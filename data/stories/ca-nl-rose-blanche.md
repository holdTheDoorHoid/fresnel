Rose Blanche Lighthouse is a granite light on the eastern head at the harbour entrance of Rose Blanche, on Newfoundland's southwest coast. It was built in 1871 to 1873 and first lit in 1873 or 1874, depending on the source. It was kept by a run of keepers, fell into ruin after the last of them left, and was rebuilt in the late 1990s, so visitors can now go up its tower in season.[^1][^3][^4][^5]

## The place

LighthouseFriends says the name Rose Blanche is a corruption of *roche blanche*, white rock, for the white quartz outcrops nearby.[^1] Fishing and mercantile interests were important at Rose Blanche in 1871, when P.H. Sorsoliel and other residents petitioned for a light.[^1] The Journal of the House of Assembly for 21 February 1871, as quoted by the restoration society, records petitions from Rose Blanche and nearby places for lighthouses in the Burgeo and LaPoile districts.[^4] The town was isolated until 1961, when a road from Port aux Basques reached it.[^1]

## Building the light

On 26 July 1871 Inspector J.T. Neville chose a rocky point about 29 metres above the sea for the light, as the society's history gives it.[^4] The tower and keeper's house were built of granite quarried on the spot, by contractors Smith and Haw(e), who were paid $7,066. The total cost was $8,304.62.[^1] The heritage statement says the granite came from a quarry close to the site.[^3] LighthouseFriends puts the construction in the summer of 1873, while the heritage foundation and the society date the work from 1871 to 1873.[^1][^3][^4]

The designers are not agreed. The society says the building was designed by either Robert Oake or J.T. Neville.[^4] The Heritage Foundation of Newfoundland and Labrador says it was "likely designed by J.T. Neville".[^3] David and Thomas Stevenson of Edinburgh supplied the lantern and the lighting apparatus, a fourth-order dioptric light showing a fixed white light.[^1][^4] The society adds that it is not known whether Robert Louis Stevenson, who worked in his family's engineering firm during school breaks, had any part in it.[^4]

The sources also disagree on the first lighting. LighthouseFriends and the Heritage Foundation say John A. Roberts, an Englishman, lit the light for the first time on 1 January 1874.[^1][^3] The society says it was lit for the first time in 1873, after two years of work.[^4]

The 1874 light had a focal plane of 29 metres. The society and the heritage statement say it could be seen in clear weather for 13 miles (21 km).[^1][^3][^4] The tower rose from one corner of a one-and-a-half-storey granite keeper's house, which was built into the same structure.[^1][^3][^5]

## The keepers

John A. Roberts was the first keeper. He kept the light for about two decades, from 1873 to 1892 in the society's list.[^1][^4] In 1875 the inspector found that Roberts had made unauthorized alterations to the rooms of the house, and ordered him to replace the partitions or be dismissed. Roberts reversed the changes and stayed on.[^1] In 1879 the inspector noted that the kitchen stove made the whole house "intolerably warm" in summer, and a small outside kitchen was added the following year.[^1]

In the autumn of 1889 an accident damaged the lens and badly burned Roberts' hands. He blamed bad oil, but a sample tested as good.[^1] The next year a brick tank with an arched roof was built under the storehouse to hold the rainwater that ran off the buildings.[^1]

John Cook was keeper from 1893 until 1909, when his son Bruce took over.[^1][^4] Bruce Cook married Minnie Barter-Pennell, a widow with three daughters, and they had six more children. He built a wooden extension on the east side to house the family of eleven.[^1] In 1924 Minnie's widowed sister moved in with six of her eleven children, and eighteen people lived in the lighthouse for the next six years.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Bruce Cook served at home and in the community as a mechanic, blacksmith, dentist, barber and surgeon.[^1]

The keepers after the Cooks were Philip Hatcher (1932 to 1933), James Skinner (1933 to 1938), and Philip Hatcher again, from 1938 into the 1940s. The society calls Hatcher the last lightkeeper there.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A particularly fierce storm in August 1939 severely damaged the lighthouse.[^1]

## After the keepers

By 1946 the keeper's dwelling was unstable.[^1] The keeper's house collapsed in October 1957. LighthouseFriends says it did so "in a puff of dust"; Rowlett says it collapsed during a storm that month and left the tower standing.[^1][^5] The heritage statement says the building was all but collapsing during a 1957 storm.[^3]

The sources disagree about when the light stopped. The heritage foundation and the society say it was decommissioned in the early 1940s, before Newfoundland joined Canada, and replaced by an automatic beacon, which the society says stood on a wooden support.[^3][^4] LighthouseFriends and Rowlett say the light moved to a wooden skeletal tower in the 1940s, then to a steel skeletal tower in the early 1960s, and was not deactivated until the 1980s.[^1][^5]

By the late 1980s only the tower was still standing. Its spiral stone staircase, which led to the light at the top of the tower, extended into the tower walls and kept the tower from crumbling.[^4][^3] In 1988 the Southwest Coast Development Association and other community groups began their quest to save the lighthouse.[^4] Rowlett says the association also identified the ruined station as a site for tourism development that year.[^5]

The restoration began in the fall of 1996. The society says 70 per cent of the original stone blocks were used to rebuild, and the rest was cut, using the original method, from the nearby quarry that had supplied the stones in 1871.[^4] Rowlett gives the rebuilding as 1996 to 1997.[^5] The official opening ceremony was held on 23 July 1999.[^4]

## Today

The roof is Newfoundland slate from Britannia, in Trinity Bay.[^3] The lighthouse was designated a Registered Heritage Structure in 2002, the first lighthouse in the province so recognized.[^3][^4]

The light was lit again on 3 August 2002, when a flashing red light donated by the Canadian Coast Guard was activated.[^1][^4][^5] The active light is listed as a red flash every ten seconds, with a focal height of 29 metres and a range of 13 nautical miles.[^2] The 6th-order Fresnel lens on display inside the tower is, according to the society, a gift from the Canadian Coast Guard; LighthouseFriends says it was given to the people who worked on the restoration.[^4][^1] The society says it is one of only 27 such lights still in existence.[^3][^4]

Rowlett says the lighthouse and tower are open daily from early June to late September, for an admission fee. LighthouseFriends gives the tourist season as mid-May through October.[^5][^1] Rowlett lists the operator as Rose Blanche Lighthouse, Inc.[^5] The keeper's residence houses a community museum.[^3] The station's 150th anniversary was marked in July 2023.[^5]
