Burrows Island Light stands on Burrows Island, near Anacortes, Washington, on the Rosario Strait shipping route.[^2] It was first lit on 1 April 1906,[^2] and the roster of its keepers runs from that year to 1972.[^1] The Northwest Schooner Society is restoring the station.[^2] The dwelling and tower are closed to visitors.[^2]

## The place

The Lighthouse Board called Burrows Island "a point of departure for most of the vessels plying the strait."[^3] It also noted strong, variable tides and several dangerous reefs close by.[^4] LighthouseFriends reports that in 1897 the Board asked for $15,000 for a light and fog signal on the island's southwest point.[^2] The Board's 1899 report says such a light and fog signal "would be of great use to commerce and navigation."[^3] The Board renewed the request in its 1900 report,[^4] and its 1901 estimates list a "Burrows Island light and fog-signal station, Washington" at $15,000.[^5]

The request was repeated every year until Congress appropriated the money on 24 February 1903, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The government obtained the site by condemnation on 28 June 1904 for $350, and bids for the work were opened on 10 May 1905.[^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says the wood-framed station was built to a design by Carl W. Leick.[^2] The tower was a 34-foot (10.4 m) square tower on the east side of a fog-signal building, which originally held a Daboll trumpet.[^2] The USLHS record gives the tower as wood on a stone foundation, and the keepers' quarters as a duplex.[^1] It gives the height from base to the centre of the lantern as 27 feet (8.2 m), a different measure from the tower's overall height.[^1][^2]

The light went into operation on 1 April 1906, shining from a fourth-order Fresnel lens with a white light and a red sector. LighthouseFriends says it helped warn of the dangers of nearby Dennis Shoal and Lawson Reef.[^2] The Anacortes American of 5 April 1906 reported that the light "entered commission on April 1st," and named "Captain J. B. Hermann, keeper, and his assistant, Mr. Edward Pfaff," as LighthouseFriends prints the paper's words.[^2] In 1907 the fog trumpet blew for 329 hours and used 215 gallons of oil.[^2]

## The keepers

James B. Hermann was the first head keeper. Edward Pfaff, who had been assistant keeper at Patos Island, was his assistant.[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends says Hermann was in charge of Burrows Island only a few months before he left the lighthouse service. He had been a music teacher before he became a keeper, and census records show him back in that work, giving piano lessons in Anacortes.[^2]

The roster also lists first assistants by name. Lulu Pfaff served in 1908, and Ann Thomas is listed for 1909 to 1910 and again for 1910 to 1911.[^1] Michael Ludescher served as first assistant from 1913 to 1914.[^1] John T. O'Rourke was first assistant from 1924 and head keeper from 1932 to 1943.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he spent more years on Burrows Island than any other keeper.[^2]

In 1934 a keeper ordered a milk cow, which the tug *Klatawa* was to bring out. The cow jumped off the raft about a mile from the island. She floundered until "a rope was thrown around her horns," and the tug completed the trip with the cow cutting the water with her nose.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 25 June 1914 assistant keeper Michael Ludescher towed a disabled launch with seven persons on board to the station. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin says the party was cared for at the station overnight.[^6]

The Bulletin's issue of 1 November 1934 describes a storm of 21 to 24 October that swept Washington and Oregon. It lists Burrows Island among the stations where considerable damage was done. The damage at those stations included washed-out roads and battered buildings.[^7]

In March 1936 a small open boat landed a party on the island in bad weather. After the motor failed, the party had to make a second landing. The keeper, John O'Rourke, kept them in sight after they ignored his advice. He then took the humbled party in, fed them, and gave them dry clothing.[^2]

Seven months later the keeper sheltered two boys who had lost their way in a rowboat in dense fog. He took them to the mainland the next morning.[^2]

## After the keepers

The light's characteristic changed on 1 May 1927. It had been fixed white, and it became two flashes every fifteen seconds.[^2]

In 1971 the station's staff was cut from three men to two. David J. Grotting, who was officer in charge from 1969, oversaw the automation of the station.[^2] The light was automated in 1972, when the last Coast Guard personnel left.[^2]

In 1978 Washington State Parks received forty acres on the island, and the state has since acquired most of the island as Burrows Island State Park.[^2]

Under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000, the station was offered to an eligible entity in 2006, and five nonprofits applied.[^2] On 23 June 2010 the Secretary of the Interior signed paperwork to transfer the lighthouse to the Northwest Schooner Society. The title would pass after the Coast Guard cleaned up lead contamination on the site.[^2] The Society planned "an interpretive center within the lighthouse itself."[^2]

Restoration started in 2011, with grants from state and private sources.[^2] In 2023 the Coast Guard had contractors remove contaminated soil and replace the failing roof on the keeper's dwelling.[^2]

## Today

The USLHS record names the Northwest Schooner Society as owner and manager, and says the station is not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open, the dwelling and tower are closed, and the Society will start offering access to the station once it is restored.[^2] A distant view is possible from the ferries between Anacortes and the San Juan Islands, and a closer one from a kayak tour, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The light still emits a white flash every six seconds at night.[^2]
