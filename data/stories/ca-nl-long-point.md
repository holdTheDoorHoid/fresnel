The Long Point Lighthouse stands on Devil's Cove Head, at the north-western tip of North Twillingate Island, Newfoundland and Labrador. Its red-painted brick tower, encased in concrete, has marked the way into Twillingate Harbour since 1876.[^1][^2]

Twillingate was a fishing and sealing port. Heritage documents describe how the town had become a central commercial base for the area by 1783, and how the lighthouse was built "at the request of local interests" to serve that sea-based economy.[^2] Lighthouse Friends adds that in 1867 a petition from John W. Owen and other residents of Twillingate reached the Newfoundland House of Assembly, asking for a light on the headland to mark the harbour entrance.[^1]

Designs and specifications were prepared in 1874 and put out to tender, and the contract went to Messrs. Colman and Kelly.[^1] The lantern and lighting apparatus, ordered from Chance Brothers of Birmingham, England, arrived too late in the 1875 season to be shipped to Twillingate, so the light was not exhibited until 1 September 1876.[^1] The tower rises from a square base and angles in at the corners to an octagonal section below the lantern, and its original cast-iron lantern with two rows of triangular glass panes is now rare in Canada.[^2] The Parks Canada statement and Lighthouse Friends differ slightly on the height of the light above the sea: 101 m (331 ft) in one and 102 m (335 ft) in the other.[^3][^1]

Samuel Roberts was the first keeper. Inspector of lights John T. Nevill reported in 1876 that the light was an excellent one, and in 1878 called the station "the best and most completely equipped station in the island."[^1] In 1879 diphtheria struck the keepers at three Newfoundland stations, Long Point among them. Only Roberts's household fell ill; Nevill noted that the assistant keeper, James H. Preston, and his family, who lived under the same roof, got their water from a different, purer source, and he asked for an enclosed well the next year.[^1] Roberts retired in 1893 and was succeeded by his son, and the Roberts name stays in the station's keeper list for decades.[^1]

A fog alarm was set up in 1920 in a wooden building seaward of the lighthouse and dwelling.[^1] On 18 November 1929 an earthquake centred on the Grand Banks cracked the brick tower. It was afterwards encased in 30.5 cm (one foot) of reinforced concrete, which Parks Canada calls a highly effective treatment.[^1][^3]

The original lighting apparatus was removed in 1956. A diesel generator brought electricity in the early 1960s, and commercial power reached the station about ten years later.[^1] The lighthouse and double dwelling were recognised as federal heritage buildings in 1989, and Parks Canada's statement of significance now treats the tower as a heritage lighthouse.[^1][^3]

Long Point is still staffed. It is one of 23 Newfoundland light stations that still had resident keepers when a United States Lighthouse Society podcast described it in 2023, and Barry Porter, one of its later keepers, served there in 1984 to 1988 and again from 2003 to 2006.[^4] The Canadian Coast Guard List of Lights lists a white flash every five seconds, visible 16 nautical miles, with a fog horn sounding a four-second blast each minute.[^5] The site is open to visitors, seasonally: a heritage museum and interpretation centre operate there, and tours of the tower are offered.[^6][^2] Lighthouse Friends lists the station as owned by the Canadian Coast Guard and says the tower is open at the keeper's discretion, so the tower is entered only on a tour or with the keeper's permission.[^1][^6]
