West Sister Island Light stands at the west end of a small island in the southwestern basin of Lake Erie, about eight miles from the Ohio shore.[^1] It was built in 1848 to mark the west end of the South Passage through the Bass Islands. It was automated in 1937, the year the island became a federal wildlife refuge. The Coast Guard still owns the light, and the island is closed to visitors.[^1][^2]

## The place

LighthouseFriends describes the island as 82 acres, in the southwestern basin of Lake Erie.[^1] In 1837 Lieutenant G. J. Pendergrast reported on the need for two more lights on Lake Erie. As LighthouseFriends quotes him, "Should a light be placed on this island, it ought to be colored."[^1]

LighthouseFriends places the island in the region where Commodore Oliver Perry defeated the British on 10 September 1813.[^1]

## Building the light

The first tower was built in 1848. As LighthouseFriends describes it, it was a forty-foot (12 m) conical stone tower, about fifteen feet (4.6 m) across at the base and seven feet (2.1 m) at the lantern. A brick keeper's dwelling, measuring twenty by thirty feet (6 by 9 m), stood near the tower.[^1] In 1848 the light used ten lamps with fourteen-inch reflectors. According to LighthouseFriends, a fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed in the lantern in 1857. The U.S. Lighthouse Society record instead gives a fourth-order original optic, installed in 1848.[^1][^2]

By the 1860s the tower was failing. The Light-House Board's report for 1866, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, noted that the wall "shows dampness," and that the keeper's dwelling was dilapidated.[^1] The Board asked for an interior brick cylinder, a cast-iron stairway, stone caps and sills, and a new lantern, and for a new dwelling joined to the tower by a covered passage. LighthouseFriends says the money was appropriated in 1867 and the work carried out in 1868. A boathouse was added that year.[^1]

Water was a problem for years. A cistern was the only supply until 1886, when a well was sunk through granite to a depth of thirty-two feet (10 m).[^1] The landings suffered from ice. A pier built in 1887 was destroyed by floating ice the following February, and a boat harbor built in 1889 was soon damaged by ice too.[^1] A Light-House Board report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1896 records that the corners of the new wharf extension were fitted with wrought-iron plates "to protect them against ice," and that the boathouse was moved some 250 feet (76 m).[^5] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1900 says new boatways had been built, and a protection crib added to shield the landing from the seas.[^6]

A circular iron oil house went up on the island in 1890, lined with brick. Before that, the oil had been kept in the passage between the tower and the house.[^1] A second story was added to the dwelling in 1902.[^1]

The sources give the tower different heights. LighthouseFriends says forty feet (12 m); the U.S. Lighthouse Society record gives 50.5 feet (15 m), built of limestone and brick.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

The roster lists sixteen keepers and assistants, from Alexander A. Cromwell in 1847 to George B. Gampher, who ended in 1937.[^2] Harriet B. Haynes is listed as first assistant from 1871 to 1882.[^1][^2]

Nathan Edson was keeper until his death at the station in February 1869. His son-in-law, Martin Goulden, set out in the station's boat for Toledo to fetch a coffin. A blizzard drove him to the light on Turtle Island, and the next day Nathan Wint Edson, the keeper of that light and the son of the dead keeper, went with him. Their boat was crushed by lake ice, and their bodies were later found "frozen fast in the ice."[^1]

Harrison Haynes was appointed head keeper in 1871.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says the family raised turkeys on the island. One year, shortly before Thanksgiving, they killed seventy-five birds, but ice held the tug back from taking them to market.[^1]

Chancie Fitzmorris "started his lengthy service on West Sister Island in 1909," and he kept the post until 1924.[^1][^2] Most keepers on the lakes left their stations in mid-December, but LighthouseFriends says he often stayed on the island through the winter.[^1] He also raised cattle, rabbits, turkeys and quail there.[^1]

George B. Gampher was keeper from 1924 to 1937, when the light was automated.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 7 September 1916 the yacht Luella capsized near the station in a squall. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin says Fitzmorris "rescued six persons from" the yacht and brought them to the station, "where they were furnished food and clothing."[^3] The two accounts disagree on where the yacht went over. LighthouseFriends places it two miles (3.2 km) west of the island and says that after half an hour battling the storm in the station's small boat, Fitzmorris saved five adults and a baby.[^1]

On 10 May 1920 Fitzmorris rescued two men who "had been adrift in a disabled power boat for 36 hours."[^4]

In 1919, according to LighthouseFriends, Fitzmorris was "gored by a bull on March 31." A red distress signal burned from the lighthouse for nearly two weeks before someone on Locust Point noticed it. A rescue crew from Marblehead took five hours in choppy seas to reach him. He recovered in hospital and went back to the island. LighthouseFriends reports that his claim for compensation was refused, on the ground that "the claimant was engaged on his own personal business."[^1]

In December 1927, according to LighthouseFriends, heavy ice cut a fishing boat in two. The two men had been in their dory after a gale tore its oars away, and they bailed water with their hats until they passed out in the cold. Their boat washed up on the island, and Keeper Gampher found them and carried them into the lighthouse. Gampher fired flares, but ice kept boats from reaching the island, and an airplane sent to help crashed while trying to land. The fishermen and the two pilots stayed at the lighthouse until tugs could reach the island.[^1]

## After the keepers

On 3 July 1936 the glazed part of the tower's lantern was removed, and the light was shown from a lens lantern placed on top of the tower.[^1] West Sister Island Light was automated in 1937, and the keeper's dwelling, built in 1868, was abandoned. The same year President Franklin D. Roosevelt set the island aside as a refuge and breeding ground for migratory birds.[^1]

During the Second World War the island was used by the United States Army for artillery practice. LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse was not seriously damaged, but the keeper's quarters were destroyed. It also reports that fuel tanks dropped by dive bombers can still be found on the island.[^1] In January 1975 the government designated seventy-seven of the island's eighty-two acres as wilderness.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the lighthouse and five acres. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service owns the rest of the island, which is part of the West Sister Island National Wildlife Refuge.[^1] The light is still an active aid to navigation.[^1]

The island is not open to the public. LighthouseFriends says visitors would threaten the nesting birds, and that the lighthouse is best seen from the water. The grounds and tower are closed.[^1] Do not try to land on the island; it is closed to visitors.
