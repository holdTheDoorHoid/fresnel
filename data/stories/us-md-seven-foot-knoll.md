The Seven Foot Knoll light stood on nine screw piles at the mouth of the Patapsco River, where the channel to Baltimore leaves Chesapeake Bay, for more than a century. Its light was first shown on 10 January 1856. Since 1988 the house has stood on a pier in Baltimore's Inner Harbor, restored as a museum.[^1][^3][^16]

## The place

The National Register nomination describes the first site as "on a rocky shoal at the mouth of the Patapsco River, marking the channel to the harbor at Baltimore."[^1] Two lights already marked the entrance. The Register says Congress had authorized lights at Bodkin Point and Sparrows Point as early as 1819.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the years the Bodkin Island light was built, 1822, and the North Point light, 1824.[^3]

The sources do not agree on how the Seven Foot Knoll light began. The Register says Congress appropriated $10,000 in 1850 "for a structure in the channel to replace the light on the mainland at Bodkin Point."[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the appropriation to 3 March 1851 and gives the sum as $17,000, for a light to replace Bodkin Island.[^3] The year and the amount both differ between the two accounts.

General context, not this station's own history: an 1889 account of the service counted more than seventy light-houses, "principally in the Southern waters," some built with screws and some without.[^18]

## Building the light

The Light-House Board chose a screw-pile design. LighthouseFriends says the Board recommended a screw-pile lighthouse built "on the same principle as that erected at the Brandywine shoal," and that it rejected an earlier plan by I. W. P. Lewis for a wooden house on an iron frame.[^3] The Register gives a different earlier proposal: a 60-foot (18 m) brick tower, which the Board set aside in favor of another screw-pile design.[^1] The first screw-pile light of this kind had been built on Brandywine Shoal, off Delaware.[^1][^3]

The Baltimore firm of Murray and Hazelhurst was engaged to build it. The Register notes that the firm listed itself in the city directory as "ironfounders," not as builders.[^1] A contract signed before the Board was formally set up in 1852 expired when the contractors had difficulty getting a proper foundation. About $34,000 was reappropriated on 3 August 1854.[^3]

The Register describes nine cast-iron screw piles, one at the center and eight set 20 feet (6 m) out around it.[^1] The piles did not all reach the same depth, so the builders cast extensions of different lengths "to create a uniform level for the superstructure nine feet above mean high water."[^1]

The first house is described three ways. LighthouseFriends describes "a one-story, circular, iron dwelling" forty feet (12 m) across.[^3] The Register says the original house was square, of cast-iron panels, and in another passage calls it a wooden square house.[^1] The archive's record gives a fourth-order Fresnel lens and a light height of 43 feet (13 m) above mean high water.[^2] LighthouseFriends puts the focal plane at 44 feet (13 m), with a fixed white light.[^3]

The dates differ too. The archive gives 1855 as the year the tower was established, and LighthouseFriends says the work was completed in 1855 and that the light was first exhibited on 10 January 1856.[^2][^3] The Register dates the building and the start of service to 1856.[^1]

The house that stands on Pier 5 is a later one. The Register describes it as round, of riveted rolled-iron plates, two stories plus a lantern, "built about the last quarter of the nineteenth century."[^1] The Register does not know when it replaced the cast-iron house. It says the construction is typical of the post-Civil War period, "circa 1875."[^1]

## The keepers

The archive's roster has 173 entries for the station, from 1855 to 1945. Counted by role, 29 are head (principal) keepers, 107 first assistants and 37 second assistants.[^2] Samuel Ayers was the first head keeper, from 1855 to 1856.[^2] George McCutchen served as first assistant and then head keeper in 1856, and was head keeper until 1860.[^2]

In February 1870 an assistant keeper, Joseph F. Worthington, wrote to the Secretary of the Treasury that he intended to resign. He had heard that the principal keeper, Mr. Lucas, had his wife and two children living at the station, and he had not known the government allowed that.[^16] The archive lists Worthington as principal keeper for 1870, which disagrees with the letter.[^2] The museum's page quotes the letter.[^16]

The Lighthouse Service's rule of 1880, as the museum quotes it, barred women and children from isolated stations with two or more keepers unless the Light-House Board gave special permission.[^16] That is general context: it describes the rule of the service, and the sources do not say how the rule was applied here.

James T. Bowling was head keeper from 1874 to 1879, and his family lived at the station.[^2][^16] His daughter Eva Marie was born there on 23 June 1875.[^3] The archive also lists a Margaret Bowling as second assistant keeper from 1874 to 1879.[^2] The sources do not say how she was related to James Bowling, and the museum says it is not known whether the family's stay was official.[^16]

Eva Bowling later described the station in a 1936 Baltimore newspaper interview, as the museum quotes her. The family traded the seafood they caught for vegetables from the farmers. They kept a hog pen under the living quarters, and storms swept the barnyard away several times.[^16] One night a storm kept her father ashore, and her mother "tended the light and rang the fog bell all night."[^16]

Staffing was hard to keep. An inspector reported that between January 1913 and May 1916 six assistant keepers came and went, and blamed much of it on the cold in the winter quarters.[^16] A district report of January 1936 called the station "of minor importance" as an aid to navigation, because lighted buoys and range lights marked the dredged channel.[^16]

Thomas J. Steinhise was head keeper from 1930 to 1941.[^2] LighthouseFriends calls him the light's final civilian keeper. It says his family lived ashore and saw him about eight days a month on his shore leave, and that he thought a good keeper needed the skills of a painter, carpenter, navigator and cook.[^3] The roster ends in 1945, with Willie P. Willis as head keeper from 1941.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 29 January 1884 the German-language Der Deutsche Correspondent of Baltimore reported that the icebreaker *Maryland* had gone down the river the day before, and had found the ice about one inch thick from Fort Carroll to North Point and three inches thick "from Seven-Foot-Knoll to the mouth of the Craighill channel."[^15] The English is this story's translation of the German.

LighthouseFriends dates the trouble to 19 January 1884, when ice floes broke one of the iron piles. It says the tender *Holly* removed the keepers five days later, because the house was judged unsafe.[^3] Only LighthouseFriends gives the tender or the removal. The Register says the ice of 1884 destroyed one of the screw piles, and that the Board had fifteen clusters of ten wooden piles driven around the light.[^1]

General context: one 1889 history of lighthouses gives two remedies for screw-pile lights in running ice, a wall of riprap or ice-breakers set apart from the foundation.[^19]

In its report for the year ended 30 June 1894, the Board recorded that a diver had found all the water braces and some of the cast-iron sleeves broken by heavy masses of ice. The clusters of oak piles placed round the light in 1884 had disappeared.[^4] The Board decided to put riprap around the base "to strengthen it and receive the brunt of any future attacks by the ice." The same entry records an October 1894 job at the station, and says that in November about 790 cubic yards of stone were laid around the site.[^4] The Register gives 700 cubic yards for the riprap, dated ten years after 1884.[^1] The Board's own report is the primary source, so this story uses its figure and notes the difference.

In the year ended 30 June 1897 the Board placed about 300 tons more riprap around the light, against "the attacks of moving ice masses."[^5]

In January 1922 the schooner *Catherine* was icebound off Seven Foot Knoll. The Commissioner's report says the tender *Maple* towed the schooner to Baltimore Light Station, and towed the steamer *Avalon* from the Knoll to Fort Carroll.[^6] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin says the *Maple* gave this help on 25 and 26 January.[^7]

On 8 July 1922 the Bulletin reports that the keeper, C. W. Evans, rescued six men from a disabled launch that was adrift in a heavy sea with a strong northeast wind blowing.[^8] The archive lists a Caleb W. Evans as first assistant at the station in 1921 and 1922.[^2] The sources do not say whether the two are the same man.

On 7 August 1928 the Bulletin reports that assistant keeper G. C. Hunley and additional keeper W. M. Goesky helped rescue two women and one man from a motor boat, disabled near the station during a thunderstorm.[^9] The archive lists George C. Hunley as first assistant from 1927 to 1931, and does not list Goesky.[^2]

On the morning of 21 August 1933 the tug *Point Breeze*, hauling scows from a dredging job in the bay, sank off Seven-Foot Knoll. The Associated Press reported that the chief engineer was lost, and named him as Percy Harrison, whose body had been reported ashore at the lighthouse.[^12] The Bulletin says one more man was found dead, and does not name him. The sources do not say whether that man was Harrison.

Keeper Thomas J. Steinhise pulled five men from the water into his small boat. The Bulletin says he did it despite engine trouble caused by seas breaking over the boat.[^10] LighthouseFriends says he heard a distress whistle shortly after midnight and lowered the station's small motorboat, in waves it puts at fifteen feet (4.6 m) and winds it puts at ninety miles an hour. Only LighthouseFriends gives those figures.[^3] The Bulletin also reports a silver lifesaving medal for Steinhise, and the Evening Star reported in September 1933 that the Secretary of Commerce had commended him.[^11][^13]

The same gale struck the coasts of New Jersey and Maryland and took at least ten lives along the Atlantic seaboard, the Washington Daily News reported. Its photograph shows the tug off the lighthouse just before it went over on its side.[^14]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard automated the light in 1948.[^1] The archive records the station as discontinued in 1987.[^2] LighthouseFriends quotes the captain of the port, George E. McCabe, saying that "keepers are hard to get."[^3]

The Coast Guard declared the house excess property in 1969. It was donated to the Mariners' Museum in Newport News, but the museum gave up its claim in 1975, after the contractor hired to maintain the house went bankrupt.[^3] A 1982 inspection found the iron legs in poor condition, and LighthouseFriends says the inspectors concluded the house would ultimately fall into the bay.[^3]

In 1988 the title passed from the Coast Guard to the City of Baltimore, which had the house moved to Pier 5 rather than let it be demolished for a new light structure.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the move was made in October 1988. Cold-weather divers cut the house from its iron pilings, and a crane lifted the 200-ton house from its foundation and it was barged up the Patapsco River. The move cost $600,000.[^3] The house was listed on the National Register on 22 August 1989.[^1] In 1997 it became part of the Baltimore Maritime Museum.[^3]

## Today

The lighthouse stands on Pier 5 in Baltimore's Inner Harbor, next to the Baltimore Aquarium, and the City of Baltimore owns it.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the Baltimore Maritime Museum manages the lighthouse.[^3] The museum's website, Historic Ships in Baltimore, says the lighthouse holds an extensive exhibit of artifacts and information about lighthouses around the Chesapeake.[^16] Both give the same telephone number for the museum.[^3][^17]

The museum's hours list the lighthouse as open only from Memorial Day to Labor Day, Friday to Sunday, 10 am to 5 pm. It is closed the rest of the year, including September through November.[^17] The lighthouse's own page showed it closed on the day this story was checked, 27 October 2026.[^16] The same page says that "an update on the Seven Foot Knoll Lighthouse will be coming soon."[^16]

The archive gives the station's position as 39° 09.17′ N, 76° 24.35′ W. Its location field reads "RELOCATED FROM PATAPSCO RIVER/CHESAPEAKE BAY."[^2] A modern light still warns mariners of Seven Foot Knoll, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3]
