The Sambro Harbour light stands on Bull Point, on the south-west side of the entrance to Sambro Harbour, Nova Scotia, in the Halifax area.[^4] It was first lit in December 1899.[^1] The small wooden tower that marked it for more than a century was torn down at the end of May 2016 and replaced with a tower of much the same shape.[^1][^7]

## The place

Sambro Harbour sits at the inner end of a bay, with Pennant Point on one side and Cape Sambro on the other.[^1] An 1860s sailing guide, as Lighthouse Friends reports it, judged the anchorage poor in south-westerly winds and warned that large vessels should stay out unless the need was extreme.[^1]

The 1899 light was built to guide small vessels to a safe anchorage inside the harbour.[^1] Sambro Island, nearby, has a separate and much older light, built in 1758.[^7]

## Building the light

The Department of Marine and Fisheries described the new light in its annual report for 1899, as Lighthouse Friends quotes it.[^1] The tower was wooden, with sloping sides and a square lantern on top, all painted white. It stood 10 m (33 ft) high to the lantern vane.[^1] The light went into operation on 1 December 1899. It showed a fixed red light, 11.6 m (38 ft) above high water, visible seven miles, through a seventh-order dioptric lens.[^1] The department built the tower under foreman carpenter McLellan, at a cost of $676.04.[^1]

The Nova Scotia list gives the light's elevation as reduced from 38 to 34 feet between 1966 and 1970, and notes that a new tower may have been built then.[^4] In 1971 the deteriorated lantern room was removed, and a watertight mount was fitted on top of the tower for the automated light.[^1][^5]

## The keepers

John H. Findlay was appointed on 7 December 1899.[^1][^3] The 1901 list places him at Bull Point, and the keepers' table lists him at the Sambro Harbour light.[^2][^3] Lighthouse Friends spells the name Finlay and says he served until 1920.[^1] The Marine tables print it Findlay.[^2][^3]

Lighthouse Friends gives W. Gray as keeper from 1920 to at least 1923.[^1] The sources used for this story name no keeper for the years from about 1923 to 1929.

Albert Gilkie started tending the light in 1929 and was still keeper when it was electrified in 1947.[^1] He gave notice that he would retire at 65 in July 1961. Rather than hire another caretaker, the government installed a photo-electric switch that would switch the light on when needed.[^1]

## After the keepers

The light was electrified in 1947 and was automated by 1961.[^1][^4]

The Nova Scotia list gives the characteristic as fixed green, dated 1990. Its entry for 1922 to 1941 gives a fixed red light, and the sources do not date the change.[^4]

In May 2016 the Coast Guard demolished the old tower and built a new one in its place.[^1][^5] Lighthouse Friends quotes a Coast Guard regional superintendent describing the new tower as "slightly larger but basically the same shape".[^1] The Coast Guard said the new tower would follow the original salt-shaker style, with a red cupola.[^7] The old tower's lantern room had been lost years before, but the light still shone from an exposed lamp.[^7]

Leslie Harnish, president of the Sambro and Area Community Association, called the old tower "quite an eyesore and a bit of an embarrassment". She also said her great-grandfather had once filled in the weather logs kept inside the light around the turn of the century.[^7]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light, and its tower is closed to visitors.[^5] A short walk from the parking at the end of Bull Point Road, off NS 349 in Sambro, takes you to the site.[^5] Sambro Harbour is also home to a Coast Guard search and rescue unit.[^1] The Coast Guard list gives the present light as a fixed green light on a white pyramid tower with a red top, 8.4 m high.[^6] The Rowlett directory gives the tower as 10 m (33 ft) instead, so the two sources disagree on its height.[^5]
