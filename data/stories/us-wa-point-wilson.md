Point Wilson is a low sandspit at the northeastern tip of Jefferson County, Washington, about two miles north of Port Townsend, where the Strait of Juan de Fuca narrows into Admiralty Inlet and every ship bound for Puget Sound must swing south.[^1] A light and a steam fog whistle went into service here in 1879, and an octagonal concrete tower built in 1914 still carries the light.[^1][^2]

## The place

The spit runs more than half a mile (0.8 km) out into the water. Here the main shipping channel squeezes together and turns sharply south into Puget Sound, and HistoryLink's account names nearby shoals, heavy rip-tides and persistent fog as the reasons for putting a light on the point.[^1] Captain George Vancouver named it on 6 June 1792 for a colleague, Captain George Wilson.[^1]

Early surveys wanted two lights at the mouth of the inlet, one on Whidbey Island and one here. In 1856 Congress paid for only one, and the Lighthouse Board chose Admiralty Head, which was lit on 21 January 1861, leaving Point Wilson to wait 18 more years.[^1] In the meantime ships stopped at Port Townsend to clear customs, and the town's shipping interests and citizens kept lobbying for a light and fog signal on the point.[^1] The National Register nomination puts the need plainly: seafarers had been working their way past the point "without a lighthouse or a fog signal to aid them."[^3]

Before there was a fog signal there was a church bell. In 1865 a ship's captain gave a bell to St. Paul's Episcopal Church in Port Townsend on condition that it be rung in fog; the two sources that tell this spell his name differently (Sheldon in LighthouseFriends, Seldon in HistoryLink).[^1][^4]

## Building the light

Congress set aside $8,000 on 20 June 1878, not enough for both a light and a fog signal, so the fog signal came first. Its machinery was made in Portland over the winter, and on 3 March 1879 Congress added $12,000.[^4] The 12-inch steam whistle began work on 1 September 1879, sounding an eight-second blast every minute.[^4] Construction of the light started the next day under C. W. Holt, with a crew of eighteen men.[^4]

The first light was a square wooden tower on the roof of a two-story Cape Cod style keeper's house, with a fixed fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the lens had earlier served at Point Bonita, California.[^4] The light was first shown on 15 December 1879.[^3][^1] That night the keeper at Admiralty Head, four miles across the inlet, entered in his log: "Light at Point Wilson in operation for first time."[^1] HistoryLink gives the range as 13 miles; an earlier version of the same author's history, in the Lighthouse Society's journal, says nine miles, and neither says nautical.[^1][^5]

Equipment kept changing. In the Light-House Board's fiscal year 1894 a new boiler for the fog signal was bought, an iron oil house went up, and a new lens with revolving apparatus replaced the old one in the tower.[^6] The following year a new boiler was set up and a new engine room built.[^8] In fiscal 1899 the fog signal and lighting apparatus were put in good condition, the tramway from the beach to the fog-signal building was rebuilt, and eight new storm windows went onto the south side of the dwelling.[^10] Electricity reached the station in 1907, according to HistoryLink.[^1] The wooden tower on the roof gave way in 1914 to an octagonal tower of concrete and masonry, joined to a new fog signal building and set just east of the old house on the most exposed part of the point; the lens was moved across to it.[^1] The tower's height is given three ways: HistoryLink says 46 feet (14 m) in its account of the building and 51 feet (15.5 m) in its summary, which appears to be the focal plane, and LighthouseFriends says 49 feet (15 m).[^1][^4] The old tower came off the roof, and the house stayed in use as the keepers' home.[^1] HistoryLink says the steam whistle was replaced by a chime diaphone; the Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for fiscal 1917 records a double-mouth horn fitted to a siren at the station that year at a cost of $32.[^1][^12]

## The keepers

The first keeper, David M. Littlefield, was a Civil War veteran and a Port Townsend farmer, paid $800 a year.[^1][^3] The U.S. Lighthouse Society archive dates his service 1879 to 1883; HistoryLink says he left in 1884 after four years.[^2][^1] He went on to be county sheriff, mayor, city councilman and collector of customs in Port Townsend.[^1] The archive lists William H. Jakins, first assistant from 1881, as acting keeper in 1883-84, and then a run of keepers through the 1880s and 1890s: George Draper (1884-88), Edmund Bailey (1888-94), Hans P. Score (1895-99) and Charles W. Sheldon (1899-1900).[^2] Thomas J. Stitt held the post from 1900 to 1913.[^2]

William J. Thomas was keeper from 1914 to 1926.[^2] The Lighthouse Service's list of stations awarded efficiency flags for the fiscal year 1915 names him for the Seventeenth District.[^13] In 1917, when the government urged keepers to grow food, he sent the lighthouse inspector a parcel of vegetables from the station's sand and reported that peas, potatoes, carrots, lettuce, garlic and squash did well, while tomatoes, cabbage and turnips failed and the beans came through only "after planting four times."[^14][^4] The department commended him for it.[^14]

Water was a worry until 1905. The station had to catch rain from its roofs for the household and the boilers, and in dry seasons water came by barge; in February 1905 it was connected to Port Townsend's supply through the new Fort Worden next door.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that on the foggy night of 29 September 1896 the fog signal could not be run for lack of water.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Board's reports give a sense of how much the whistle was needed. It ran some 157 hours in fiscal 1894, some 361 hours in fiscal 1895, and some 337 hours in fiscal 1899.[^7][^9][^11]

On 15 December 1880 the 948-ton bark *David Hoadley*, inbound in ballast from San Francisco for Port Gamble, was driven ashore by a gale on North Beach, not far from the light. The crew got ashore and the wreck stayed on the beach as a local landmark for several years.[^1] In 1886 a picket fence 5 feet high and 440 feet long was built across the low part of the spit to catch drifting sand where a breach seemed likely, and by 1904 the point was eroding badly enough to threaten the station, so 1,542 tons of stone were piled on its north side.[^4][^1] LighthouseFriends says that on 29 September 1896 the steamer *Umatilla* struck rocks about a mile west of the point in fog and her captain beached her a few hundred yards from the lighthouse; all passengers were landed safely.[^4] That account has not been checked against a 1896 newspaper or report.

The best-documented night is 1 April 1921. At about five past midnight the passenger steamer *Governor* and the freighter *West Hartland* collided off the point.[^1][^4] The Lighthouse Service's bulletin records that Thomas, who was on watch, heard the collision and at once telephoned Port Townsend for help and warned the Admiral Line's office in Seattle.[^15] In an account LighthouseFriends reproduces he said the night was clear, the ships plainly in sight about three quarters of a mile away, and that he got several launches out.[^4] The *Governor* sank in some 240 feet of water; HistoryLink says within 30 minutes, while Thomas reckoned it was not more than an hour.[^1][^4] Many passengers took to the lifeboats and many climbed onto the *West Hartland*'s bow.[^1] The death toll is given differently: the Lighthouse Service bulletin and the Commissioner of Lighthouses' report both say nine lives; HistoryLink, the National Register nomination and LighthouseFriends say eight.[^15][^16][^1][^3][^4] The Commissioner's report also recorded Thomas's call.[^16]

During the Second World War the light was put out as a defence measure for Fort Worden and the entrance to Puget Sound.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1939 the Lighthouse Service was folded into the Coast Guard, and civilian keepers stayed on until retirement, gradually replaced by Coast Guard crews.[^1] The archive's roster shows Coast Guard names at Point Wilson from at least 1955.[^2] The 1932 Lighthouse Service bulletin already lists Point Wilson among the latest additions to the Pacific coast radiobeacon system, while HistoryLink says a radiobeacon was installed in 1976; the two may be different equipment.[^17][^1] In the 1960s the Coast Guard built more housing for the ten-man crew of a patrol boat stationed in Port Townsend, and in November 1976 the light and fog signal were automated.[^1] Coast Guard personnel lived in the keepers' quarters until 2000.[^4] Winter storms in 2005 and 2006 flooded the basement of the keepers' dwelling and tore the fog horn from its wall, and the Coast Guard has had to keep patching the rock armour around the point.[^4]

## Today

The station is Coast Guard property, leased since 2019 to the U.S. Lighthouse Society.[^4] The grounds are next to Fort Worden State Park; the lighthouse itself is not open to the public, though it can be seen from the beach.[^1][^4] The Society rents out the 1879 keepers' duplex and the Coast Guard house to overnight guests, and says it celebrated the reactivation of the historic Fresnel lens in 2025.[^18][^19] LighthouseFriends records that a VRB-25 beacon took over from the Fresnel in 2007, so for some years the light came from a modern beacon rather than the lens.[^4] The tower is closed, and the buildings and rock armour are working Coast Guard and Society property, so visitors should stay on the public parts of Fort Worden.
