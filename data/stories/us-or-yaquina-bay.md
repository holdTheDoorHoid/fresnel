The Old Yaquina Bay Lighthouse stands on a bluff above the mouth of the Yaquina River, in Newport, Oregon.[^1][^2] It was first lit in November 1871 and was put out three years later, when a light farther north on the coast took over.[^1][^3] Its frame house is now a museum in a state park, and a modern light on the same site has shone since 1996.[^3]

## The place

LighthouseFriends describes Yaquina Bay, in 1869, as the most populated port on the West Coast between San Francisco and Puget Sound.[^3] The nomination describes the bay light as a harbor entrance light. It says Royal A. Bensell, one of the earliest promoters of the bay, publicized its entrance as a "harbor of refuge" for coastal traffic between San Francisco and Puget Sound.[^1] The Oregon legislature asked Congress for a light at the bay's mouth, through a memorial that Henry W. Corbett presented to the Senate in 1869. An appropriation followed in 1870.[^3]

## Building the light

The 36 acres came from the homestead of Lester P. and Sophronia S. Baldwin, among the first white settlers on the bay. The nomination says the Baldwins sold the land to the United States for "$500 U.S. Gold Coin," under a warranty deed dated 28 April 1871.[^1][^3]

The house was built between May and October 1871.[^1] Construction records in the National Archives show two principal contracts: the lantern and ironwork went to Joseph Bien, a lampist in San Francisco, and the labor to Ben Simpson, a local contractor.[^1] The house has two stories of frame on a high brick basement, with redwood siding, and a square lantern platform rises from its east side.[^1]

LighthouseFriends gives 3 November 1871 as the night the light first shone.[^3] The nomination says the light was most likely a whale oil lamp inside a fifth-order Fresnel lens.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

Charles H. Peirce was the only keeper the government appointed to the reservation.[^1][^2] The nomination spells his name "Pierce." The Lighthouse Society's roster spells it "Peirce," and LighthouseFriends says it is pronounced "Purse."[^1][^2][^3] The nomination calls him "a Scot and former Civil War Captain for the Union Army." LighthouseFriends also describes him as a former Civil War captain in the Union army.[^1][^3]

The nomination says Peirce "cared for a wife and eight children" at the house.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he brought his wife and six children, and that his ninth child, Kate, was born in March 1872.[^1][^3] The sources do not agree on the count, and this story does not settle it.

The light was put out on 1 October 1874.[^1][^3] The new lighthouse at Yaquina Head had been completed in August 1873, and the nomination puts it three miles north of the bay light. LighthouseFriends says four miles.[^1][^3] Peirce was then assigned to Cape Blanco, where LighthouseFriends says he served as head keeper for ten years.[^1][^3]

The nomination says that Royal A. Bensell and his wife "succeeded to occupancy of the building for a time as caretakers."[^1] Bensell is remembered for a journal he kept of army life at Fort Yamhill.[^1] He is not on the Society's keeper roster.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 17 November 1889 lightning struck the house.[^4] The Morning Oregonian of Portland reported it from Newport: "Lightning struck the old lighthouse building occupied by J.S. Polhemus, government engineer." The building was badly splintered, and Mrs. Polhemus and her child narrowly escaped.[^4]

The Polhemus family was there because the Army Corps of Engineers had been given permission to occupy the house from 1888, during the jetty work.[^1][^3] In 1906 the Life-Saving Service, which had been active on South Beach across the bay since 1896, "was allowed to use the building as crew quarters and a lookout." That use ended in 1933, when the Coast Guard set up quarters elsewhere in Newport.[^1]

## After the keepers

The federal government kept the lighthouse and reservation, with minimal repairs, through 1885.[^1] In 1877 an effort was made to sell the station, but the offer was so small that the property was kept.[^3] In 1878 the Lighthouse Board described the structure as almost uninhabitable, and its roof and outside sheathing were renewed that year to keep it from falling into ruin.[^3]

In 1934 the federal government released the property, and the State of Oregon took it for highway and park purposes.[^1][^3] The nomination dates some alterations to the first floor to 1934 to 1940, when park personnel occupied the building.[^1] The nomination says it was scheduled for demolition in 1946, but local citizens opposed the move, and a Lincoln County Historical Society was formed by 1948.[^1] LighthouseFriends says plans to demolish it were abandoned by 1955, and that in 1956 the house was dedicated as a historical site under the Society's jurisdiction.[^3] The nomination says the Society ran it as a museum from 1965.[^1]

The house was listed on the National Register in 1974, the year Oregon State Parks began a year-long restoration.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends says it is the only wooden lighthouse still standing in Oregon.[^3] Its ghost stories have a fictional origin, according to LighthouseFriends: a piece in the 1899 Pacific Monthly, about a fictional girl who vanished in the lighthouse, led to a lasting belief that the place is haunted.[^3]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by Oregon State Parks and managed by Friends of Yaquina Lighthouses, a group formed in 1988.[^3] It stands in Yaquina Bay State Park in Newport.[^3]

The modern light was lit on 7 December 1996, using a 250 mm optic donated by the lighthouse historian James Gibbs.[^3] It is recognised by the Coast Guard as a privately maintained aid to navigation, showing a fixed white light visible for six miles.[^3]

In late 2024 Oregon State Parks announced a closure for the first several months of 2025, for a $1.6 million exterior restoration.[^3] The lantern room was shipped to Alabama to be refurbished and was lifted back onto the tower on 12 August 2025. LighthouseFriends says the lighthouse opened to visitors later that fall.[^3]

LighthouseFriends gives the opening hours as noon to 4 p.m. daily, except on a few holidays, and says visitors can climb to the watchroom but not into the lantern room.[^3] Hours may have changed, so check before you visit.
