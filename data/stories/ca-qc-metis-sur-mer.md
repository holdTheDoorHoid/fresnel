The Phare de la Pointe-Mitis stands at the end of a long, rocky headland at Métis-sur-Mer, on the south shore of the St. Lawrence in Quebec's Bas-Saint-Laurent. Its white and red tower is one of Canada's early reinforced-concrete lighthouses.[^4] The town of Métis-sur-Mer bought the station from the federal government in 2016.[^2]

## The place

Métis lies on the south shore of the river, 157 nautical miles below the port of Quebec, and the local lighthouse society describes it as both a stopping place and a refuge in storms.[^2] The society's history counts eight vessels lost or stranded off Métis in the seven years from 1841 to 1847, among them the three-master *Amanda*, wrecked in October 1841 with the death of 45 passengers and crew.[^2] Pilots boarded ships at Pointe-au-Père, upriver, so vessels passing Métis went by without a pilot aboard.[^2]

The Quebec heritage register says that petitions reached the Trinity House by 1850, and that the number of wrecks near Métis became an argument for a light.[^3] The light itself did not come for another quarter-century. The site chosen was the end of the point, which runs far out into the river and was, in the local society's words, also the place of dangerous islets and submerged reefs.[^2] Lighthousefriends, which also quotes an engineer's survey of 1860 for a site on the point, records that the land was bought from J. H. Ferguson of Métis in April 1876 for $100.[^1]

## Building the light

R. Cameron of Lancaster, Ontario, won the contract in 1873, and the first light was lit on 20 October 1874.[^1][^2] It was a square wooden tower of 12.2 m (40 ft), attached to the keeper's dwelling and painted white, according to the local society, with the lantern dome and the dwelling's roof in bright red; Lighthousefriends describes the tower itself as bright red.[^1][^2] Lighthousefriends gives the total cost as $3,518.84, of which $1,425 was for the lighting apparatus; the local society's history gives $3,518 and $1,425 for the lantern, optics and revolving mechanism.[^1][^2] The light came from two lamps with silvered reflectors in a revolving mechanism; Lighthousefriends says it made one turn every two minutes.[^1][^2] The Quebec register says the fog signal was already being asked for in the 1880s.[^3] Lighthousefriends says a ruby glass was put before one lamp before the 1877 season, so that the light showed alternating red and white flashes.[^1]

The wooden building did not last as the main light. In 1901 a Marine branch inspection of the lower St. Lawrence stations found that the surroundings of Métis were "so unfavourable" that no fog alarm should be built there, and that it would be difficult to improve the light without a new lighthouse to contain it.[^5] The Quebec register adds that a new tower for a more powerful light was being planned from 1903, and credits Robert Wilson Reford, a Montreal shipping agent who summered at Métis, with collecting petitions for a better light from 1896.[^3] In the year to June 1904 the lighthouse was painted bright red, along with Matane and Martin River, to make it easier to tell apart.[^6]

The new tower was to be of reinforced concrete. The Department's report for the year to June 1906 says the tower was under construction and that the light was expected to be working early in 1907.[^7] It was not: the report published in 1909 records the new tower as erected, a cylindrical reinforced-concrete tower painted red, built by the Steel Concrete Company of Montreal under a contract of $3,900.[^8] Lighthousefriends says $3,000 and gives 1908 as the year of completion.[^1] The next report, published in 1910, says the old alternating red and white catoptric light had been replaced by a third-order dioptric light showing three flashes in each group, at 55,000 candlepower.[^9] The Quebec heritage register and the local society both give 1909 for the tower, and the society says the new light was lit at the start of the 1909 navigation season.[^2][^3] The tower is 25 m (82 ft) from base to vane.[^2] The lens was made by Chance Brothers of Birmingham.[^1][^2] Canada's federal heritage record notes that the building of this tower was the first year the Department of Marine and Fisheries used reinforced concrete for lighthouses.[^4]

A new keeper's dwelling and outbuildings and a cistern are listed among the works at "Little Metis" in a Marine branch report published in 1913.[^10] In 1923 and 1924 the tower was wrapped in a reinforcing coat of cement, which gave it the hexagonal form it has now; the heritage record also notes buttresses at the base 3.9 m (13 ft) high.[^4]

The fog signal came at last in 1918. The local society says a diaphone was installed in May 1918 in a wooden building 12 m (40 ft) north of the tower, and the Quebec register agrees on 1918, while Lighthousefriends says 1917.[^1][^2][^3] The society says it served for 53 years and was removed in 1971.[^2] Lighthousefriends says the signal broke down on 1 April 1971 and was never replaced.[^1]

## The keepers

The first keeper was J. Jules Martin, who lit the lamps in October 1874.[^1] In 1876 the inspector's report, as Lighthousefriends summarises it, found the keeper's dwelling very cold and too small; the Marine Department ordered a kitchen of 20 by 18 feet to be added, and Martin was paid $450 in 1877 for enlarging the house.[^1] He died in November 1879, his widow was granted two months' salary, $50, and his eldest son, Jules Gabriel Martin, aged 23, took over.[^1] A Marine branch report published in 1906 lists Jules G. Martin as keeper of Little Metis, and one published in 1909 names Élisée Caron.[^11][^12]

The sources disagree about the middle of the century. Lighthousefriends gives Caron as keeper from 1906 to 1936 and Octave Gendron from 1936 to 1954; the local society gives Caron to 1931, then Antonio Laudry from 1931 to 1936, then Gendron.[^1][^2] The Quebec register says that on 10 July 1942 Gendron saw a U-boat off Métis and reported it to the Defence department and the air base at Mont-Joli.[^3] On 9 October 1942 he was woken by an explosion, thought the light itself was under attack and, with the Mont-Joli commander's permission, put the light out. The explosion was the torpedoing of the *Carolus*, a 2,245-ton steamer, by U-69 off Pointe-aux-Senelles; eleven of her crew drowned.[^3]

In 1954 Georges Fafard, who had kept Pointe des Monts for twenty-six years, took over at Métis and died suddenly in January 1958.[^1] Émile Chouinard succeeded him and died in December; his son, who had been working as assistant, ran the station until Évariste Ferguson became keeper the following spring.[^1] The last keeper is Marcelle Ouellet in Lighthousefriends and Marcel Ouellet in the local list, which dates his service from 1965 where Lighthousefriends says 1970; the station was de-staffed in 1972.[^1][^2] The society counts nine keepers over 98 years.[^2]

The local society describes the keeper's routine in general terms: going up the tower several times a night to refuel the burners, lighting them at dusk and putting them out at dawn, winding the rotating mechanism, and starting the diaphone at the first sign of fog; it adds that the keeper was expected to put out to sea to help ships in difficulty.[^2] Lighthousefriends says the point has about 1,000 hours of fog a year.[^1]

## After the keepers

The station was left to run without a keeper from 1972, and Lighthousefriends says the dwellings and the fog-alarm building passed to the Department of Natural Resources in 1978, the fog-alarm building becoming an insect and marine research laboratory.[^1] A semaphore and telegraph station had opened at the lighthouse on 17 November 1879, according to the local society, but the society says semaphore signalling was dropped in the mid-twentieth century.[^2] The Coast Guard withdrew the light as a navigation aid in 1997 according to the local society; its other pages give 1999.[^2][^13] The town now maintains the lens and the Quebec register says it is still operating.[^3]

The site was cited as a heritage site by the municipality of Métis-sur-Mer on 13 January 2014.[^3] In November 2016 the town bought the lighthouse and the land around it from the Government of Canada, and a local residents' association (the Association des résidents de la Pointe-du-Phare) took charge of conservation.[^2]

## Today

The site is run by volunteers, who say it is open every day from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m., reached only on foot or by bicycle: visitors leave their cars at the parking lot at the mouth of the road and walk the kilometre to the point.[^13] The keeper's house can be rented by the week from May to October for up to eight people.[^13] The sources read do not say whether the tower can be climbed. Lighthousefriends lists the grounds and tower as closed and the road as private, but its page still shows the federal government as owner, so that note appears to predate the town's purchase.[^1]
