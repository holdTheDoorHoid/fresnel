Borden Flats Light stands on a reef at the mouth of the Taunton River, where the river runs into Mount Hope Bay between Fall River and Somerset, Massachusetts. Its iron tower was first lit on 1 October 1881. It was automated in 1963. Since 2010 the lighthouse has been privately owned, and since 2013 it has offered overnight stays.[^1][^2][^3][^10]

## The place

Shoals and rocks at the mouth of the river were the danger. Before the tower went up, an unlighted day beacon had marked it since at least 1868, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The 1981 National Register form says: "Because of the dangerous reef surrounding the tower, approach can only be made from one side (the north)."[^1]

The light is hard to see from land. The form says the lighthouse "is not easily visible to anyone other than mariners," because the Braga Bridge passes almost directly overhead, there are no shore roads, and warehouses and mills block the view.[^1]

The name comes from the Borden family, who owned textile mills along the river. The form calls them "one of Fall River's wealthiest families."[^1] Textile mills were among the reasons for the city's prosperity in the nineteenth century, and LighthouseFriends says Fall River at its peak had over 100 cotton mills that employed more than 30,000 workers.[^2]

On 16 June 1880 money was set aside for a light at the flats, after district officers of the Lighthouse Board recommended one. The sum was $25,000.[^2]

## Building the light

Work began in 1881, when three sections of an iron pier were sunk, leveled and filled with cement. The cylindrical iron tower and lantern were cast elsewhere and shipped to Fall River that July.[^2] The National Register form says the caisson was "sunk and firmly secured with cement on the reef," and that "about 15' of cement comprises the foundation of the structure, most of which is below the water line."[^1]

The National Register form gives the cost as $24,000 and the completion as 1881.[^1] A notice that LighthouseFriends quotes announced that from 1 October 1881 "a fixed red light of the fifth order will be exhibited from the light-house recently constructed."[^2] The notice said the fog bell, struck by machinery, would be sounded "during thick and foggy weather."[^2] The Lighthouse Society's record gives a fourth-order Fresnel as the original optic.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the light was changed from fifth to fourth order in 1932.[^2] The sources disagree on the order of the optic.

The light's color changed in 1900. The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 records that the "color of the light was changed from red to white."[^4] Its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1901 says that on 3 December 1900 "the fifth-order fixed white light was changed to a flashing white light."[^5] The Board's list of characteristic changes gives 31 December 1900 for the same change.[^6]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society's roster for the station runs to thirty-five lines, from 1881 to 1967. Gardner M. Sherman was the first keeper, from 1881 to 1882.[^3]

Herman C. B. Georgy kept the light from 1885 to 1898.[^3] In September 1897, LighthouseFriends reports, he was struck by appendicitis. His wife rang the fog bell and waved her arms to flag down passing boats for some time, before passers-by realized she was not merely in a friendly mood. "For two days, he suffered intense agony," the Boston Globe wrote, as LighthouseFriends quotes it.[^2]

Joseph Meyer kept the light from 1905 to 1912.[^3] LighthouseFriends says his station log is now held by the National Archives. On 15 February 1908 he wrote that the fog bell had broken, and that he struck it by hand until 1 p.m.[^2]

In 1912 the Boston Globe reported, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, that Meyer was iced in and stranded at the light from 4 January to 1 February. The tug that brought him ashore had to leave before he was ready to return, so he set out at midnight for the lighthouse, got caught in drift ice, and was more than two hours getting back to shore. The paper said: "In consequence of his absence the beacon failed to shine for the first time in seven years."[^2]

John H. Paul kept the light from 1912 to 1927.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he filled many log pages with the endless scraping and painting of the tower. On 3 August 1912 he saw a rowboat overturn as two local men aboard changed places, and he rescued one of them. The other, who could not swim, drowned. Paul received a bronze Carnegie lifesaving medal for that day.[^2]

Joseph T. Covo kept the light from 1927 to 1944 in the archive's roster. LighthouseFriends gives 1943 as the end.[^2][^3] His daughter, as LighthouseFriends quotes her, remembered the brass: "Everything was brass," she said.[^2]

Truman Sawyer, a Coast Guard man, served at the station from November 1955 to December 1956. LighthouseFriends says he viewed the posting as punishment. The light, refrigerator and stove then ran on kerosene, and the fog bell still had to be wound by hand. The light was electrified in 1957.[^1][^2]

Roger J. Perreault was the last Coast Guard officer in charge, from 1962 to 1963.[^2][^3] The archive's roster lists two lamplighters, Alice O'Neil until 1965 and Wilfred Caron from 1965 into 1967. LighthouseFriends says that after automation an onshore civilian lamplighter was paid to flip a switch whenever the fog bell was required. It does not name that person.[^2][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Winter often found the light surrounded by frozen sea or chunks of ice, LighthouseFriends says.[^2]

On 8 October 1907 a gale hit the harbor. The keeper's log, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says: "A barge of about 800 tons of coal sank in the harbor—two men drowned, and a sloop of 10 tons was wrecked on the beach."[^2]

On 1 August 1920 Keeper John H. Paul rescued two men whose boat had capsized, the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports.[^7] The Commissioner's report for that year calls the station "Borden Flats Light Station, Rhode Island," although it is in Massachusetts. The Commissioner's own list gives "Borden Flats Light Station, Mass."[^8][^9]

In the 1938 hurricane the seas hit the tower with such force that it tipped a little to the north. The lean remains, LighthouseFriends says, and a larger caisson was built around the tower afterwards.[^2][^10]

## After the keepers

The light was electrified in 1957, and on 17 May 1963 the station was made unattended.[^1][^2][^3] In 1977 a modern optic, flashing white every two and a half seconds, replaced the Fresnel lens. An automatic horn took the place of the fog bell in 1983, and in 1989 the light was converted to solar power.[^1][^2]

In 2006 the station was offered to local, state or federal agencies, non-profits and historical societies under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act of 2000. When no qualified owner was found, it was auctioned in 2008. A Nevada attorney won with a bid of $55,000, but he defaulted on closing, and the General Services Administration put the lighthouse back up for sale on 30 June 2010.[^2]

Nick Korstad won it with a bid of $56,569 on 31 August 2010.[^2] In 2011 he painted a red band around the middle of the tower. He began offering public tours in June 2013, and then overnight stays. The fog signal was deactivated in May 2013.[^2] Korstad put the lighthouse on the market in 2017, and it sold the following year.[^2] USA TODAY Network's Herald News reports that Kevin Ferias took ownership in May 2018.[^10]

## Today

The latest report found, from 1 November 2024, names Kevin Ferias as the owner. It was for sale at $1,250,000, and the Herald News describes it as a popular overnight destination.[^10] No report of a later sale was found, so the status in 2026 is not confirmed. LighthouseFriends dates the first listing to 2023; the Herald News reports it in November 2024.[^2][^10]

Borden Flats is privately owned. LighthouseFriends says the tower is open for tours and overnight stays, and the Lighthouse Society's record lists it as open to the public.[^2][^3] The public can view the lighthouse from the Borden Light Marine lot at the end of Almond Street.[^2]
