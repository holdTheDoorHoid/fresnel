The Moloka'i Light stands at the northern tip of the Kalaupapa Peninsula, a flat leaf of land at the foot of sea cliffs on the north coast of Moloka'i, Hawaii.[^4] Its octagonal concrete tower was lit in 1909 for ships crossing the channel between O'ahu and Moloka'i, and for fifty-seven years it was operated by resident keepers and later Coast Guardsmen living beside the Hansen's disease settlement.[^1][^3]

## The place

By 1902 a report to Congress on the islands' needs said that "the great bulk of the Pacific coast commerce" passed through the channel between O'ahu and Moloka'i, with no lighthouse on the exposed points of either island except Diamond Head.[^1] The National Register nomination, prepared by the Coast Guard, describes the light as a guide for mariners coming to Honolulu from the American west coast or by way of the Panama Canal.[^3] The peninsula itself juts out to sea from an otherwise incurving north coast, which the Park Service gives as the reason it suited a navigational light.[^1]

The same peninsula held the settlement where people with Hansen's disease were sent. The Park Service says many lawmakers opposed a lighthouse so close to it.[^1] According to Lighthousefriends, the Lighthouse Board first put a fixed red lens-lantern on a 34-foot mast at the point, known as Makanalua Light, which went into service on 1 March 1906.[^4] That account rests on one source.

## Building the light

The Park Service says Congress appropriated $60,000 in 1908; Lighthousefriends says the temporary light ran a year before the money came, which would put it nearer 1907.[^1][^4] The Lighthouse Board had ordered the lantern and lens from Chance Brothers and Company in England in September 1907.[^1] Lighthousefriends names Captain C. W. Otwell of the Corps of Engineers as designer, and says everyone working on the site needed a Board of Health permit to enter or leave the peninsula.[^4] The same page tells of a Portuguese laborer, John Souza, who fell about ninety feet to his death from the tower while unloading concrete cars; its text dates the accident to "December 2008," evidently a misprint, since the tower was built in 1908 and 1909.[^4]

The lens is a second-order Fresnel lens of 26 sections.[^1] The Park Service brochure says the station was finished on time and under budget by only $23.[^2] The tower, by the 1977 nomination's measurements, is 132 feet (40 m) from ground to top with a focal plane 213 feet (65 m) above sea level, and its spiral concrete stair has 189 steps.[^3] The nomination's statement of significance rounds the tower to 138 feet; Putnam's *Lighthouses and Lightships of the United States* (1917) gave 132.[^3][^5]

Accounts of the first lighting differ on the day. The Park Service's park page says Keeper James Keanu lit the lamp thirty minutes before sunset on 1 September 1909, and the Hawaiian Cultural Center agrees; another Park Service page says 9 September.[^1][^7][^6] The nomination and the USLHS archive both give only the year, 1909.[^3][^8]

## The keepers

James M. Keanu held the head keeper's post for most of the station's first thirty years, in three stints, 1909 to 1926, 1929 to 1932 and 1936 to 1939.[^8] Lighthousefriends says he had been keeper of the temporary light and that on 8 August 1917 an attempt to dynamite a shark blew off his right hand near the wrist; the district inspector doubted he could continue, but Keanu bought a typewriter for his reports and an artificial hand, and after an inspection the next year the inspector found the station in excellent condition.[^4] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index lists an efficiency flag awarded to Keanu as keeper at Molokai, and another to the station.[^9]

The USLHS archive lists two or three men at a time below the head keeper: first and second assistants who often moved on within a year or two, among them Frederick E. Nihoa, who appears as second assistant in 1918, first assistant from 1928 to 1932 and head keeper from 1932 to 1936.[^8] A 1930 Lighthouse Service Bulletin report on a superintendent's inspection said the station was kept by a keeper and two assistants, all natives of Hawaii.[^10] Fred E. Robins, the head keeper listed from 1940 to 1953, is called a "Hawaiian folk hero" in the nomination, which says he spent four decades in the lighthouse service and served at most of the older Hawaiian lights; it counts his time at Moloka'i as ten years, while the archive's dates span thirteen.[^3][^8]

Keepers and their families lived under rules set by the Hawaiian Department of Health, with permits needed whenever they or their guests came or left.[^1] Contact with the patients, who lived about a mile and a half away, was prohibited.[^1] Rose Lelepali, a patient, recalled: "They were so lonesome we would sneak up to the light station and talk story with them."[^1] Men from the settlement and men from the lighthouse played baseball together.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The lens floated in a vat of mercury, which made earthquakes a problem.[^4] Two show up in the Lighthouse Service Bulletin. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that a heavy shock on 25 December 1923 threw and scattered the mercury from the vat, with no damage to the apparatus, tower or houses.[^11] The 1938 volume reports another shock: the mercury was thrown out of the trough, "causing the lens to stop suddenly, but within 15 minutes" the keeper had replaced it and the lens was turning again.[^12] Lighthousefriends dates a quake of that kind to 22 January 1938 and quotes assistant keeper Ed Marques, who recalled carrying hundred-pound containers of stored mercury up the stairs; the archive lists Marques as second assistant from 1936.[^4][^8] The Bulletin does not give the date, so the two accounts are matched only by their content.

## After the keepers

Lighthousefriends says the station received its own generating plant in 1934 and that the last keepers left by the start of August 1966, when nightly attention was no longer needed.[^4] The Hawaiian Cultural Center puts automation in 1966 as well, noting that about 1,800 vessels a year were passing by the 1960s.[^7] The nomination's total of 57 years of keepers and resident Coast Guardsmen matches 1909 to 1966.[^3]

In January 1985, Lighthousefriends says, mercury was found leaking from the vat, and the lens was taken apart section by section and replaced with a rotating beacon.[^4] The Park Service's caption dates the lens's removal only to the early 1980s.[^1] The lens went to Maui for display by the Lahaina Restoration Foundation; after protests from Kalaupapa residents it was shipped back, and Lighthousefriends says the Park Service holds it in storage.[^4] Richard Marks, a patient-resident, said of the light: "It is the Kalaupapa Light."[^1]

The station went on the National Register in 1982 (the nomination was prepared in 1977), and was awarded to the National Park Service in 2003.[^3][^4]

## Today

The tower is an active aid to navigation, and the USLHS archive lists its current optic as a DCB-24.[^8] Lighthousefriends gives it as owned by the National Park Service, part of Kalaupapa National Historical Park, with grounds and tower closed.[^4] The Park Service says Hawaii law requires a permit to enter the park, general visitors are admitted only through Kalaupapa Airport on a guided tour, and no one under sixteen may visit.[^13] Lighthousefriends adds that no permitted tour operator was active when it wrote, so the park should be asked about current access, and that the best public view of the tower is from the airport, which is beside it.[^4] The tower is not open to climb.[^4]
