Tibbetts Point Light stands on the point at Cape Vincent, New York, where Lake Ontario narrows into the St. Lawrence River. A light has marked this entrance since 1827;[^1] the brick tower there today dates from 1854, and its fourth-order Fresnel lens is still in use.[^2]

## The place

The point takes its name from Captain John Tibbetts of Troy, New York, who held a large grant of land here. In early 1827 he deeded three acres to the federal government for a lighthouse.[^1] The Tibbetts Point Lighthouse Historical Society gives the same three acres but places the gift in "the early 1820's", so the date of the transfer is not settled between the sources.[^2]

The light marks the entrance to the river from the lake. In the early 1890s the Light-House Board put the point's importance plainly: because the light was "at the entrance of St. Lawrence River", its position had to "be made known at all times and in all kinds of weather".[^3]

## Building the light

In May 1827 a contractor, Elbridge Potter, agreed with the customs collector at Sackets Harbor to build a lighthouse, lantern and keeper's dwelling for $1,747. As described by Lighthousefriends, the contract called for a stone tower 30 feet (9 m) high, and the first light was nine lamps with 14-inch reflectors.[^1] The historical society instead describes the first tower as a rectangular wooden one of the same height; the sources disagree on its material.[^2]

By 1838 a naval inspector, Lieutenant C. T. Platt, found the light "very useful" but in "bad order", its lantern and the dwelling roof both leaking, according to Lighthousefriends' summary of his report.[^1] In 1852 the Lighthouse Board, answering a petition from people with interests in Lake Ontario and St. Lawrence commerce, asked for funds to rebuild what it called a "notoriously inefficient light". Congress gave $5,000 on 3 March 1853, the work was finished on 15 July 1854, and the new fourth-order Fresnel lens was first lit on 1 August 1854.[^1] A newspaper description of the time, as reproduced by Lighthousefriends, called it the second Fresnel light on the northern lakes, a brick shaft 47 feet (14 m) high with an octagonal lantern.[^1] The National Register's data page lists 1854 and 1883 as significant years for the station.[^4]

In 1877 the Board called the station's buildings old, much decayed and too small, and asked for $10,000 to rebuild the station; a new two-story frame dwelling followed in 1880, and in 1882 the brickwork above the parapet was rebuilt and a new lantern added, leaving a tower about 59 feet (18 m) tall.[^1] A second duplex keepers' house was added in the 1900s: Lighthousefriends and the USLHS archive give 1907, the historical society 1906.[^1][^2][^5]

## The keepers

The Light-House Board's records, as printed by the US Lighthouse Society archive, name Judah Williams as the first keeper (1827 to 1831), followed by Nelson B. Williams, who served until 1846.[^5] Elnathan Madison, listed in the roster with the alternative spelling Matteson, followed (1846 to 1849), then James H. Meigs (1849 to 1853), Henry Cunningham (1853 to 1861) and William Collins (1861 to 1865).[^5]

Adam Cratsenberg kept the light from 1865 to 1885, according to the archive's roster; his obituary in a local paper says he went to the light in 1866 and served twenty years.[^5][^6] He was a tanner and currier before the Civil War. He enlisted, rose to first sergeant, fought at Antietam, Fredericksburg and the Wilderness, and lost an arm at Cold Harbor on 2 June 1864; he was held in Libby Prison for two months and eleven days. The register records his pay rising from $350 to $480.[^6] Lighthousefriends says he resigned in 1885, fearing a Democratic administration would replace him.[^1]

The Montonna family held the station for about half a century. David H. Montonna was keeper from 1885 to 1898.[^5] His son William E. Montonna was born at Somerset on 30 January 1869, worked on lake steamers, and, in his obituary's words, was the "successful appointee" in a civil service examination for the post, which that notice calls the first ever held for it. The notice dates the appointment 1 April 1896; the archive's roster lists him as first assistant from 1897 and head keeper from 1898 to 1937.[^5][^7]

Once the fog signal needed two keepers, both families lived in a single small dwelling. The Board's reports of 1899 and 1900 both said so, adding that they did so "much to the discomfort of both", and renewed a request for a new assistant's dwelling, put at no more than $3,500 in 1899.[^8][^9] The request was still on the Board's estimates in the 1901 report.[^10]

William Montonna's station earned official recognition. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index records an efficiency pennant for him in the 1912 to 1917 volume.[^11] His obituary instead says he won the pennant in 1922, "when the award was first made", so the dates differ.[^7] The long-serving assistants in the archive's roster include Joseph McNulty (1909 to 1920) and Edwin G. Ward (1920 to 1939).[^5] Olin W. Stevens succeeded Montonna in 1937.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

No wreck is tied to the point in the sources used here; what they record in detail is the fog signal. The Board first recommended a steam fog signal for $4,300 in the early 1890s,[^3] and Congress authorized it on 6 February 1893 without paying for it.[^12] The money came by the act of 2 March 1895.[^13] The Board's report for the year ended June 1897 records a 10-inch steam whistle established at the opening of navigation in 1897 (listed under 15 April 1897), blasts of three seconds separated by 87 seconds of silence.[^14] The historical society dates the signal to 1896.[^2]

The Board's reports give the signal's use. It ran about 160 hours in the year covered by the 1899 report, burning about six tons of coal.[^8] The next year it ran about 257 hours and about nine tons, and at the opening of navigation in 1900 the pattern was changed to three-second blasts followed by alternating silences of 17 and 37 seconds.[^9] The 1899 report also records an excavation in the rock below the waterline, covered by a crib, to bring the fog signal's water supply nearer.[^8]

One recorded act of help comes from the Commissioner of Lighthouses. On 2 May 1922 W. E. Montonna supplied the fish tug *Cole*, of Cape Vincent, with gasoline so it could reach port, and the report's next entry, undated, says he furnished the cabin cruiser *Miss Esther May* of Oswego with enough gasoline to reach port.[^15]

## After the keepers

A steam whistle gave way in 1927 to an air diaphone, and in November 1930 the lamp became electric; the light became occulting the following season.[^1] The Coast Guard assumed responsibility for the station in 1939, but a civilian stayed in charge here until Edward Sweet retired in 1967 after forty years, one of the last civilian keepers. In winters Sweet carved decoy ducks.[^1] A radiobeacon was added in 1949.[^1]

The sources disagree on automation. The historical society and the station record say 1981;[^2] Lighthousefriends says the light was automated in the early 1970s but Coast Guard crews stayed through 1981.[^1] A visitor brochure says the fog whistle ended in 1972 in favour of a radiobeacon.[^16]

When the Coast Guard left, the keepers' dwellings became a youth hostel in 1984, the year the station was entered on the National Register.[^1][^4] Cape Vincent took ownership in 1991, a visitors center opened in 1993, and the fog signal building opened to the public in 1998. The hostel closed in 2014.[^2][^1]

## Today

The tower is not open to visitors, because the light is still an active aid to navigation maintained by the Coast Guard. The grounds are open every day, and the visitors center and museum open seasonally, staffed by volunteers of the Tibbetts Point Lighthouse Historical Society.[^2] Lighthousefriends calls the owner the village of Cape Vincent, and the historical society the town; the 1991 transfer was to the town.[^1][^2]
