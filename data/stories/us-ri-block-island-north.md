Block Island North Light stands at Sandy Point, the northern tip of Block Island, Rhode Island, where a sand bar runs out from the shore. The granite tower there was built in 1867, the fourth light on the point. Its first floor is a maritime museum, but the tower itself is closed to visitors.[^1][^3]

## The place

Sandy Point is the northern end of Block Island, not quite five miles from Old Harbor.[^1] The nomination calls the point particularly dangerous, and the sand bar runs several miles out from it.[^1] An 1884 account, quoted in the nomination, describes swift currents that "struggle for supremacy" where the bar is "in terrible combat being alternatively laid bare," with seas towering 50 feet (15 m) above it.[^1]

## Building the light

The nomination says a lighthouse was first erected on the point in 1827. It was 45 feet (14 m) high and visible for 12 miles, but in ten years shifting sands had made a new structure necessary.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive gives 1829 as the year the station was established.[^2] The two dates disagree.

The second light stood a quarter mile farther inland, at a spot known as the "blow hole." It was 50 feet (15 m) high and built of granite, and it survived only two decades.[^1] The third tower was put up as far out as the first, and within ten years storms had put it out of use.[^1]

After three lighthouses were lost at the north end of the island within 38 years, the government set about building the present tower in 1867. The Town gives its cost as $15,000 and says it was built of "huge, imported stone blocks."[^3]

The nomination describes a two-and-a-half-story granite block, 30 by 33 feet (9 by 10 m), with a fireproof iron tower on the northern gable above the central entrance.[^1] It says the building was "Well built of stone and better protected than its predecessors."[^1]

The light was fixed white, 61 feet (19 m) above sea level, and is said to have been visible for thirteen and one-half miles, about 11.7 nautical miles.[^1] The lantern was a kerosene Fresnel optic built in Paris.[^1] The Town says the tower was the prototype for five sister lights, three in Connecticut and two in New York.[^3]

## The keepers

Hiram D. Ball, "brother of the Hon. Nicholas Ball who had been largely responsible for the promotion of Block Island as a Victorian resort," was appointed keeper by President Lincoln in 1861. He kept the light for thirty years, and the nomination calls him the first keeper of the present building.[^1] The archive lists him from 1861 to 1891.[^2]

The archive's roster starts with William A. Weeden, from 1829 to 1839. Simeon Babcock and Edward Mott then alternated in the post until 1853.[^2] Enoch Rose, Jr. (1853 to 1859) and Nicholas Littlefield (1859 to 1861) followed.[^2] Elam P. Littlefield served from 1891 to 1923.[^2] Later keepers were John F. Anderson, Ezra B. Dunn, Howard B. Beebe, John J. Lee, Jr., and William H. McAffee. The last keeper on the roster is Donald M. Lawson, who is listed for 1955 to 1956.[^2] Two contractors, David Melville and John Stevens, appear on the roster with no dates.[^2]

During those years the light was "reachable by way of the Neck Road from both Old and New Harbors," and it was "a favorite resort of visitors to the island."[^1] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' summary of work completed in the fiscal year ended June 30, 1921 lists "Block Island North Light Station, repairs to dwellings, etc., $1,887."[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The nomination says the three earlier towers, over a period of 38 years, "had all been rendered useless by storms and shifting sands."[^1] No dated wreck or storm at this light was found in the sources read.

The Life-Saving Service's report for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1898 says that contracts were made that year for two stations, one at Great Boars Head and one at "Sandy Point, on the northern extremity of Block Island," both of which were "nearly finished."[^5] The nomination says a rescue station was also maintained at the Point, and that the small frame building north of the lighthouse was its "Skipper's quarters."[^1] It also lists three life-saving stations on the island, at Old Harbor, the West Side and Sandy Point.[^1]

## After the keepers

The nomination says the station was "Manned by civilians until World War II." After that the Coast Guard kept it until 1955, "when the structure was automated."[^1] The nomination says the light was electrified during the 1940s and a flasher was installed during the 1950s.[^1] According to the nomination, the station was abandoned as an active light in 1970. The archive gives 1970 to 1989 as its discontinuance years.[^1][^2]

When the nomination was written, the 28-acre site was a wildlife refuge and bird sanctuary run by the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife.[^1] The Town Council established the North Light Commission on 30 December 1974 to oversee the North Light.[^3] The restoration of the tower and roof reached a milestone on the evening of 23 October 2010, when the Town and the commission marked the culmination of the work with a relighting ceremony under a full moon.[^3]

## Today

The first floor has been renovated and is open to the public as a maritime museum. There is no tower access.[^3] The archive lists the station as open to the public, and it still shows the light as an active aid to navigation.[^2] The North Light Commission manages the house and grounds, subject to approval by the Town Council.[^3]

The Town lists hours of Saturday 10 a.m. to 4 p.m. and Sunday noon to 4 p.m. The light closes for the season after Columbus Day, and it closes in rain. Admission is $3 for visitors 12 and over.[^3] The walk is a half mile (0.8 km) along a rocky beach from the end of Corn Neck Road.[^3]
