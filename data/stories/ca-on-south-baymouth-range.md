The South Baymouth range lights are a front light and a rear light on the south-east shore of Manitoulin Island, Ontario. The Department of Marine built them in 1898 to lead ships into the mouth of South Bay, and they still show: the front light near the ferry terminal at South Baymouth, and the rear light in the woods behind the village.[^7][^8][^9]

## The place

The early inhabitants called the community at the entrance to South Bay Sagradawawong, which LighthouseFriends gives as meaning "The Outlet."[^7] After the settlement at Michael's Bay faded, South Baymouth grew into the chief harbour on the island's south shore.[^7] A local tourism page says the lights were built a year after Booth Fisheries began fishing out of the village.[^10]

The Department's 1898 report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says the lights were built to lead into the entrance to South Bay, or Manitoulin Gulf.[^7] In its annual report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 the Department describes a plan of the entrance, drawn from a hydrographic survey by W. J. Stewart, that was furnished to the hydrographer of the United States Navy to show the position of the range lights. The Department says the American office had it published as a plan in its Notice to Mariners No. 29 of 1900.[^1]

## Building the light

Before the range lights there were temporary pole lights. The 1898 report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says they had been privately maintained since September 1897 by Captain A. Macauley, master of the steamer J. H. Jones.[^7]

In September 1898 the two range lights were built and put in operation.[^7] J. Candlish Kennedy of Owen Sound did the construction for a contract price of $927, and the report gives the total cost of the station as $1,067.09.[^7]

The front light was a square wooden tower with sloping sides and a square wooden lantern, painted white and 8.5 m (28 ft) high to the vane. Its lens was dioptric, of the seventh order, and it showed a fixed white light 8.5 m (28 ft) above the lake, visible ten miles.[^7] The rear light stood in the woods behind the village, 235 m (772 ft) from the front light on a bearing of north 28 degrees east. It was a 12 m (40 ft) tower of the same style. The report says its fixed white catoptric light, 14 m (46 ft) above the lake, should be visible twelve miles.[^7]

The 1898 description gives a white light.[^7] Rowlett's directory describes a continuous green light today.[^8] None of the sources read here dates the change.

## The keepers

James Ritchie tended the range lights from 1898 to 1903.[^7] A list of lightkeepers and stations in the Department's annual report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1902 gives a James Ritchie at the South Bay range lights.[^2]

In 1903 his cousin, John A. Ritchie, took charge.[^7] The Department's table of appointments gives John A. Ritchie's appointment at South Baymouth as 10 September 1903, at a salary of $150.[^4][^5] He appears at the range lights in the 1906 list[^3] and again in 1914.[^6]

As John A. Ritchie grew older, his daughter Pearl helped care for the lights. After she married Henry B. Chisholm, he became the official keeper.[^7] Chisholm had enlisted in 1916, at the age of twenty-one, with the 119th Battalion. He was injured in France while serving as an engineer with the Canadian Expeditionary Forces, and he was awarded the Military Medal.[^7]

LighthouseFriends gives Chisholm's death as February 1958 in its text, but its keeper table ends his service in 1957.[^7] His brother-in-law, Leonard Leeson, then cared for the range lights until they were automated.[^7]

## Today

The range lights belong to the Canadian Coast Guard. The grounds are open and the towers are closed.[^8]

The sources do not agree on where the front light stands. The 1898 report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, puts it on the south-east end of a bare limestone island at the mouth of the bay, and the Canadian Coast Guard's list of lights calls it the south-east end of a small island.[^7][^9] The Lighthouse Directory places it on the rocky beach just west of the ferry terminal.[^8] LighthouseFriends says the front light can be seen from the ferry, and that visitors reach the rocky beach by taking Water Street and a boardwalk.[^7]

LighthouseFriends says the range lights welcome visitors who arrive from Tobermory on the Chi-Cheemaun ferry, which has been in use since 1974.[^7]

The sources do not give the year the lights were automated. LighthouseFriends says only that Leeson cared for them until that happened.[^7] A local tourism page says the lights and fog horn were later automated.[^10]

In 2012 the medal was offered for sale on an online auction site. The community raised enough money for a collector to buy it from a seller in Indiana, and visitors to South Baymouth can now see it at the Little Schoolhouse and Museum.[^7]
