Lights have marked the mouth of Conneaut Harbor, on Ohio's shore of Lake Erie, since 1835.[^2] The light there now is a square steel tower of 1936, at the end of a breakwater that is not connected to land. It has been automated since 1972.[^14][^15]

## The place

Conneaut is a lake port in Ashtabula County, in the northeast corner of Ohio.[^14][^15] Congress appropriated money for piers at the mouth of the Conneaut River in 1829, LighthouseFriends says. The port later shipped grain, whiskey and forest products, then limestone and coal.[^14] By 1885 the harbour had fallen into disrepair.[^2][^14] The Light-House Board's report for 1894 says: "The commerce of the place was not maintained and the piers were allowed to decay and fall to ruin."[^2] New enterprise followed. The Board said the harbour was "being improved at great expense, and lights to mark the entrance are now important," and that a new enterprise there would probably make Conneaut a leading port for shipping iron and coal.[^2]

## Building the light

The first light, the Board says, was "established in 1835, as a guide to vessels entering the harbor of Conneaut," on the outer end of the west pier.[^2] LighthouseFriends says a lighthouse was built in 1835 atop the eastern pier. It adds that a new pierhead beacon, first lit in 1875, was placed on the west pier instead of the east.[^14] The two accounts disagree on the pier, and the sources do not settle it.

In 1885 the old pier was so decayed that the beacon had to be moved.[^2] The Board records that "the pier was so much decayed that the beacon was insecure and it was removed to the bluff on shore."[^2]

On 11 November 1892 a new fixed white pierhead light was first shown, the Board reported.[^1] An eight-day tubular lantern showed it until the close of navigation that year, "owing to a delay in the arrival of the lens lantern ordered by the Board." A five-day lens lantern has been used since the opening of navigation in 1893.[^1]

LighthouseFriends dates the new square beacon on the west pier to 8 September 1895.[^14] On 1 March 1897 the older light was moved to a tower on the shore side of the entrance.[^4] The Board says it "was transferred to a beacon built near the shore line on the west side of the entrance to Conneaut Harbor," to form a range with the light on the outer end of the pier, and that "the light was first exhibited in the new tower on the night of March 1, 1897."[^4]

The breakwater light came later. LighthouseFriends says Congress appropriated $8,400 on 28 June 1902 for a light on the detached west breakwater.[^14] After the War Department completed a new pierhead on the breakwater in 1906, the light was placed there that October.[^14] Its first tower was a wooden cylinder covered in iron, about 31.5 feet (9.6 m) tall, with a fixed white light from a fifth-order lens.[^14] A temporary light had run on the new breakwater from 1914.[^14] A Lighthouse Service Bulletin list of lights established includes a Conneaut West Breakwater light, "group flashing white, lens lantern, acetylene."[^12] The fixed white light LighthouseFriends describes and the group flashing light the Bulletin lists may refer to different lights, and the sources do not say which.

The permanent lighthouse took several years to build. The Commissioner's estimates printed in 1915 asked $63,500 for a light and fog signal at Conneaut, and a note in the same report says the act of 3 March 1915 authorised the work.[^6] The act of 1 July 1916 then appropriated $63,500 "for a light and fog-signal station and improving aids to navigation at Conneaut Harbor, Ohio."[^7] The Commissioner's report for 1918 says the concrete foundation was complete and "Contracts have been awarded for the metal work, brick, and stone."[^7]

The 1919 report says work on the superstructure "resumed in June, 1919." The report for 1920 gives the date as "June 9, 1920."[^8][^9] The two dates do not agree; the later report gives the later date.

The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1921 says the "light placed in commission September 15, 1920," and that "bids on fog-signal apparatus have been received." It gives the total appropriation for the station as $90,100.[^10] LighthouseFriends gives 15 April 1922 as the day the diaphone fog signal began operating, and a total cost of $89,667 for the lighthouse and the other harbour aids.[^14] That is not the same as the $90,100 appropriation, and the sources do not explain the gap. The lens in the new lantern was of the second order, and it flashed twice every twelve seconds.[^14]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends lists head keepers from 1835, beginning with Walter Woodward (1835 to 1841), and running through George W. Miller (1869 to 1885) and John H. Starkey (1885 to 1894).[^14] The keeper's dwelling dates from 1872 in LighthouseFriends, and from 1873 in the archive's record.[^14][^15]

Edward Pfister stayed longer than any other keeper at the station, LighthouseFriends says.[^14] It dates his appointment as head keeper to 1894 and his stay to more than forty years.[^14] The archive's roster for the breakwater station gives his service as 1906 to 1937.[^15] The two dates do not agree, and the sources do not explain the difference.[^14][^15]

Joseph F. Lasko was first assistant from 1927 and head keeper from 1937 until 1950.[^15] Eugene A. Ray (1950 to 1961) and Thomas Baker (1961 to 1965) followed.[^15] The archive lists eight head keepers between 1906 and 1972. Its assistants include Frank LaRose, who appears twice as first assistant (1917 to 1920 and 1920 to 1927), and John L. Specht, first assistant from 1937 to 1942.[^15]

Ice closed the lakes each winter. LighthouseFriends says the light was unlit from 25 December to 1 March, and that the keepers took their vacations in turn during that break.[^14]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The west pier was hard to reach in a blow. The Board's report for fiscal 1895 says about 400 feet (120 m) of elevated walk was rebuilt "to provide sure means for reaching the light on the outer end of the pier during storms, when seas wash completely over the pier."[^3]

In the winter of 1898 to 1899 the Board kept the Conneaut lights burning for part of the season, for a "car ferry line from Conneaut, Ohio, to Canada."[^5]

On 10 January 1922 the tug Oregon was towing the lighter T. F. Newman when the tow broke. LighthouseFriends says the cable snapped and the lighter was wrecked on the breakwater near the lighthouse.[^14] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin says the lighter "had run adrift in vicinity of the station."[^13] The Commissioner's report for 1922 says the lighter was "wrecked on the breakwater in vicinity of the station," and that "Seven men on the scow were taken off by" the keeper, with a small boat, and given food and lodging.[^11] LighthouseFriends says Keeper Pfister took the men back to the lighthouse.[^14]

## After the keepers

In 1934 the west breakwater was reshaped.[^14] LighthouseFriends says a 980-foot (300 m) section was removed and a rubble-mound extension of 852 feet (260 m) was built lakeward.[^14] The 1920 concrete light was blasted from its footing, and a steel tower replaced it.[^14] The square, steel tower stands sixty feet (18 m) tall. LighthouseFriends dates its commissioning to 1 April 1936, at a cost of $70,000.[^14][^15]

The lantern room from the 1920 light stayed on the new tower until 1972, when the light was automated, LighthouseFriends says.[^14][^15]

The Ohio State Historic Preservation Office nominated the light to the National Register of Historic Places in 1992, and it was then added to the list.[^14] The reviewer, Patrick Andrews, said the design was "a clear attempt to look modern and to make a break from the past."[^14]

## Today

The Coast Guard declared the light excess in 2007, and in May that year it offered the light at no cost to eligible public and non-profit bodies.[^14] An online auction opened on 9 September 2008. No bids came in that month, and a New York restoration company's president, Gary Zaremba, eventually won it with a bid of $35,000.[^14] When Zaremba did not pursue a lease for the bottomland, the lighthouse reverted to the General Services Administration. A further auction, set for October 2009, was called off at the last minute.[^14]

The second auction, in 2011, sold the light for $46,000 to a private buyer.[^14] The buyer had to carry $1 million of general liability insurance to get an Army Corps of Engineers lease for the breakwater.[^14] In 2018 the owners put the light up for sale at an asking price of $72,000, and the family kept it.[^14]

The archive still lists the light as an active aid to navigation, and as not open to the public.[^15] LighthouseFriends describes the tower as producing alternating red and white flashes with a five-second period.[^14]

The light stands on private property, on a breakwater that is not connected to land.[^14] LighthouseFriends says some people wade or swim out to it, and that there are views from several places at the end of Broad Street.[^14] Please do not wade, swim or walk out to it.
