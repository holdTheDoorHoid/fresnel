St. Joseph Point Light was a range light on the Florida Panhandle, set on the mainland at Beacon Hill across the entrance to St. Joseph Bay from the peninsula where the bay's first light had stood.[^6] It went into service in 1902 to guide ships over the bar into the bay.[^6][^7] A modern tower replaced it in 1955, and the Lighthouse Society's archive gives 1960 as the year the station was discontinued.[^5][^6][^7]

## The place

St. Joseph Bay was the only refuge for some sixty miles (97 km) of coast, and the Light-House Board pressed for a light at its entrance for years.[^1][^6] Its report for 1895 put the case: fishing vessels were numerous, a southerly gale would drive them onto a lee shore, and at night the entrance was hard to enter without a light.[^1] "The only harbor of refuge for some 60 miles is St. Joseph Bay. This is easily accessible in the daytime, but at night it is difficult of entrance without a light."[^1] In 1867 the Board had already called the bay the second harbor on the Gulf coast for capacity and safety, excelled only by Pensacola, and without a light.[^6]

The bay had a light before.[^6] A tower on the cape was lit in early 1839 by Ephraim Andrews, and it remained active until 1847, when it was put out for the last time.[^6]

## Building the light

Congress made the money available in 1898.[^2] The Board's report for 1900 records that $15,000 was appropriated by the act of 1 July 1898 for a light at St. Joseph Point.[^2] The following year the Board reported that, under that money, soundings were made and borings were taken to a depth of 26 feet (8 m).[^3] Because the 5-fathom curve came close inshore, the Board decided the light should stand at high-water mark on the beach near the point, inside the peninsula.[^3]

The first intention was to build on the peninsula.[^3][^4] The Board's 1901 report says a thorough investigation showed that the peninsula "would not be a suitable site for this light," and that the light's erection on the mainland was decided upon and a site secured.[^4] LighthouseFriends describes the preferred site as the mainland at Beacon Hill, opposite the northern tip of the peninsula.[^6]

LighthouseFriends prints the Board's 1902 description of the finished station.[^6] It was a one-story frame building on brick piers about eight and a half feet (2.6 m) above the ground, with five rooms, a watch room over the center, and an iron third-order lantern on top.[^6] The range beacon was a square black pyramid, and a 245-foot (75 m) wharf and a well served the station.[^6] A lens lantern showed from the gallery from 25 August 1902 until 3 March 1903, when the third-order lens inside the lantern room was lit.[^6] The archive gives 1902 for the original lens; LighthouseFriends dates the lens lit in the lantern room to March 1903.[^5][^6]

## The keepers

Charles Lupton was the first keeper.[^5][^6] The archive gives his stint as 1902 to 1928.[^5] LighthouseFriends says he lived at the station with his wife, Minnie, for twenty-six years. The couple had seven children, and the family once hired a teacher to live at the station for a few winter months so the children could be schooled.[^6] When supplies were needed to supplement the family garden, the family occasionally had to travel more than fourteen miles (23 km) west to Farmdale, the nearest community.[^6] The family lost two of their children while stationed there.[^6]

The archive also lists Minnie I. Lupton as a laborer in two stints, 1903 to 1922 and 1922 to 1926.[^5] Charles Lupton retired in February 1928.[^6] Thomas Clairmon Brooks then kept the light from 1928 to 1938, and Walter Andrew Roberts from 1938 to 1952.[^5] LighthouseFriends gives Roberts as Walter A. Roberts, Jr.[^6]

During the Second World War ten Coast Guardsmen were stationed at the lighthouse to watch for enemy activity.[^6] The area beneath the lighthouse was converted into a barracks, but the men had to pass through the station's living quarters to reach the lookout in the lantern room.[^6] The daughters of Keeper Walter Roberts would iron the coastguardsmen's uniforms for ten cents each.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1921, a year into Prohibition, Keeper Lupton was commended for telling county authorities that the gasoline launch Curlew had grounded and that contraband goods had washed up on the beach.[^6]

In October 2018 Hurricane Michael destroyed the tower that displayed the rear range light.[^6] The Coast Guard later erected a new tower; LighthouseFriends dates its photograph of the new tower to 2021.[^6]

## After the keepers

A modern light on a 78-foot (24 m) iron tower replaced the 1902 station in 1955.[^6][^7] The archive gives 1960 as the year the station was discontinued.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the old dwelling was sold five years after the new tower went up.[^6] It was moved inland to a farm, where it was first used as a dwelling and then as a hay barn, with cattle feeding from its porches.[^6]

In 1970 a private owner bought the old dwelling, and in November 1979 it was moved to its present waterfront site on St. Joseph Bay.[^6] The owner restored it over a long period, and in 2011 a replica lantern room, built to the original plans, was placed on it.[^6][^7]

## Today

The restored dwelling is a private home south of Port St. Joe and is no longer an aid to navigation.[^7] Its grounds, dwelling and tower are closed without the owner's permission, but the house can be seen from the public road.[^7][^6]

The rear range light is displayed from a modern tower that can be seen at the intersection of Lighthouse Avenue and Beach Hill Park Road in St. Joe Beach.[^6]
