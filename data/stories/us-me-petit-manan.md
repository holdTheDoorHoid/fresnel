Petit Manan Island is a rocky, windblown island about two miles off the Maine coast near Milbridge, and the granite tower on it was described in 1987 as having the second-highest light in the state.[^1] It marks a bar and reef off Washington County, at a station where the Lighthouse Service recorded more fog than at any other it kept figures for.[^2][^3]

## The place

The 1987 National Register of Historic Places nomination calls the island "a rocky and windblown site 2 miles from the mainland"; the Friends of Maine Coastal Islands, who describe a ten-acre island, put it 2.5 miles south of Petit Manan Point.[^1][^4] The government acquired the land on June 15, 1816, from the Commonwealth of Massachusetts.[^1] The nomination says the Lighthouse Board of the early 1850s found the old light too weak and too low, "considering the ever-increasing use of the Northern shipping lanes," and it counts the paddle steamer *New York*, which burned and sank in 1826, among the unusual vessels that wrecked nearby.[^1]

Fog was the other hazard. The Lighthouse Service reported in 1915 that the highest annual average of fog on its records was at Petit Manan: 1,691 hours a year over 31 years, "or over 19 per cent of the period."[^2] In 1842, a petition signed by seven navigators, twelve ship owners or merchants and thirteen citizens asked for a fog alarm on the island, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3]

## Building the light

An appropriation of $8,000 was made on April 27, 1816, and the first tower was completed in 1817.[^3][^1] LighthouseFriends names the builders as Frederick and William Pope, quoting an 1831 letter that says the lighthouse was built by them in 1817; the Digital Archive of the U.S. Lighthouse Society lists the two Popes as contractors.[^3][^5] The nomination describes a rubblestone tower 25 feet (7.6 m) high with 13 whale-oil lamps, each with a 15-inch reflector, at a focal plane of 53 feet above the sea and visible for 14 miles.[^1] It says the tower was "extensively rebuilt between 1823 and 1825" and the lamps cut to eight; LighthouseFriends, describing an 1842 inspection by I. W. P. Lewis, also counts eight lamps, but gives the tower as 26 feet tall, its walls "badly cracked" and its lantern glass so thin that it blew out in storms.[^1][^3] Lewis found thirty-three of the lantern's panes cracked, and an assistant keeper had broken through the lantern platform while measuring the tower, so rotten was the wood.[^3] The nomination dates a fog bell, a 1,200-pound hand-rung brass bell, to 1852; LighthouseFriends says 1853.[^1][^3]

By 1853 the new Lighthouse Board's verdict was harsh. Its report, as quoted by LighthouseFriends, called this "the most eastern first class light on the coast of the United States" and "one of the worst of all the lights," built so badly and so old, with a lantern so small, that little could be done to improve it. It asked for $45,000 for a new tower with a second-order Fresnel lens and a dwelling.[^3]

The present tower was commissioned in 1854 and finished in 1855 by the Corps of Engineers, of granite quarried on the mainland.[^1] The nomination gives it as 109 feet (33 m) tall, 20 feet across at the base and 12 at the top, brick-lined, with a cast-iron spiral stair of 147 steps.[^1] LighthouseFriends and the Society's archive both give 119 feet (36 m); the archive measures that from the base to the centre of the lantern, and puts the light 123 feet (37 m) above the water.[^3][^5] The lens was made by Henri Lepaute of Paris (the nomination spells it "Lepante").[^1][^3] It first showed a fixed light; in 1857, the nomination says, three revolving panels were added so that the light was "varied by flashes," driven by weights that had to be wound by hand every five and one-half hours.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the flashing arrangement to the original 1855 installation.[^3]

The same years brought the rest of the station. A fog bell machine was installed in 1855; the first bell cracked and was replaced in 1868 by a 1,500-pound bell from the McShane foundry in Baltimore.[^1] A steam fog signal followed in 1869, with a brick engine house for a duplicate signal in 1876 and a 32-foot-square boiler house in 1887.[^1] The second keepers' dwelling dates to 1876 in the nomination and to 1875 in the Society's archive and LighthouseFriends.[^1][^3][^5]

Tower and fog signal both gave trouble. A chain holding the clockwork weights broke in 1869, damaging eighteen of the iron steps; the repairs can still be seen.[^1] After storms in 1856 and 1886 loosened the top courses of the masonry, six sets of tie rods were run down the outside of the tower in the summer of 1887.[^1] An inspector's 1881 remark, quoted by LighthouseFriends, was that "it vibrates so much during heavy weather that the plate glass of the lantern is cracked." The Lighthouse Board's 1888 report said the lantern was secured "before the severe gale of December, 1887, which might have otherwise proved disastrous at this station."[^3] Fresh water was a chronic problem for the boilers. LighthouseFriends records that the well dug for the steam whistle drew from a swampy source that damaged the boiler, that the dwelling was re-roofed and fitted with gutters and tanks and two rain sheds were built to fix it, and that a new brick cistern was added in 1890.[^3]

## The keepers

The first keeper is disputed. The Society's archive, built from the keeper registers, and LighthouseFriends name Robert Upton, who served from 1817 to 1821 at $350 a year; the nomination, citing the payroll ledgers, says Jeremiah Leighton.[^1][^3][^5]

One story is told of the Leightons. In 1831 the Treasury's Fifth Auditor, Stephen Pleasonton, wrote after an inspection that the station was "Very bad indeed," with the dwelling leaking badly, and that the keeper had left: "the man has gone off, being tired of his state of independence. His wife had charge of the whole concern."[^3] The archive lists Robert Leighton as keeper from 1825 to 1832, and LighthouseFriends says he returned and the light was repaired; it then names his wife as Jane, and says she applied for the post after his death and was denied.[^3][^5] The nomination tells it differently: the wife was turned down for the appointment but was paid for the work, and so "became the first woman lightkeeper in the United States."[^1] LighthouseFriends records that this is a claim some make, and that her petition was "eventually denied."[^3]

Moses Thompson, keeper from 1838 to 1849, dug a well to avoid rowing six miles to the mainland for fresh water, and reported that nothing had been repaired since his appointment "except what I have done at my own expense."[^3][^5] When the new tower opened, the station grew to three people. John P. Small was head keeper at $500 a year, with Darius Fickett and Alfred Moore as assistants at $240 each.[^3][^5] The nomination says that when the three keepers' families had enough children, an addition to the rain shed served as a schoolroom; it gives no date.[^1]

Two men named Upton ran the station for most of a generation: George L. Upton (1876 to 1890), then William D. Upton (1890 to 1902), who had served as second and then first assistant since 1885.[^5] The sources do not say whether they were related. The fog signal's demands show in the figures. In the year recorded in the Light-House Board's report for 1901, the 10-inch steam whistle ran 2,064 hours and burned about 79 tons of coal.[^6] LighthouseFriends says that coal, carried in 80-pound bags, would have had to be carried by hand from the boat landing, and that a 760-foot railway was built in 1899 to move it; the nomination calls the same line a tramway.[^1][^3]

Pierre A. Fagonde was head keeper from 1927 to 1933, with Earle B. Ashby and later Roscoe L. Fletcher among his assistants.[^5] The station became a men-only "stag" station in 1939.[^1] The archive's roster runs on through the Coast Guard years to 1972; the full list is in the table.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

How far the fog signal carried depended on the wind. In the Light-House Board's report on fog-signal tests (the trial at Petit Manan itself is dated November 1893), the keeper at Moose Peak light is reported as saying that "in a light NE. wind he always hears Petit Manan whistle," which the report puts at 20 miles against the wind. The same keeper said it could not be heard in a heavy northeast wind, and the keeper at Nash Island said it was generally not heard in a strong south or southwest wind, on account of the surf.[^7] A newspaper account by a member of a press party on a steamer, published July 17, 1890, describes how, as evening gathered, "the light in the lantern of Petit Manan light-house flashes out over the sea."[^8]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin, which printed the commendations of keepers, records several answers to vessels in trouble. On August 19, 1912, keeper Eugene W. Larradee (the archive spells it Larrabee) and assistant Lennie Foster helped a power yacht with six people aboard, preventing it from drifting to sea.[^5][^9] On August 8, 1923, a schooner, which the Bulletin's printed text gives as *Northern Light*, went ashore on Petit Manan Reef; a lighthouse tender and assistant keepers Nathaniel S. Alley and Frank W. Alley helped her occupants.[^10] Pierre Fagonde obtained the help of a Coast Guard surfboat to tow a fishing sloop that had gone ashore near the station about 3 a.m. on September 30, 1928.[^11]

On August 4, 1929, a Canadian schooner was dismasted and wrecked on Petit Manan Bar. Fagonde and Ashby found her abandoned that evening but could not salvage anything "on account of rough seas and floating wreckage." The Bulletin says the four officers and crew, destitute and penniless, took refuge at Narraguagus Light, where keeper Charles E. Tracy cared for them until the 16th, and then they rowed the ship's boat roughly 95 miles toward Nova Scotia. All three keepers were officially commended.[^12] LighthouseFriends dates this wreck to August 4, 1919, but the Bulletin item is in the 1929 volume and names keepers who served in 1929.[^3][^12] In 1931 Fagonde and Fletcher answered distress signals from a small boat two miles off Nash Island and found an old man too tired to row.[^13]

## After the keepers

Oil lamps gave way in 1938, when electricity reached the station; the nomination records the new bulbs at 330,000 candlepower, then the second most powerful light in Maine.[^1] The nomination dates the replacement of the steam whistle by a diaphragm airhorn to the same year; LighthouseFriends dates the change from steam to air to 1917.[^1][^3] The Bulletin for October 1912 lists Petit Manan among stations whose illuminant had been changed to incandescent oil vapor, whereas the nomination puts kerosene vapor lamps in 1917.[^1][^14] Electric motors replaced the weights in 1965.[^1]

In 1972 the station was automated. The Fresnel lens was taken down and moved to Rockland, where the nomination says it went to the Shore Village Museum; LighthouseFriends calls the place the Maine Lighthouse Museum. A 2,000-watt rotating aircraft beacon took its place.[^1][^3] The Coast Guard turned the island over to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 1974.[^1][^4] LighthouseFriends says the surviving dwelling was rented to a lobster fisherman for a few years after automation and then sat empty until the fall of 1984, though the nomination, written in 1987, describes the dwelling as unoccupied since 1974. In 1984, when gull control made the island attractive to nesting terns again, a team from the College of the Atlantic fixed up the house for seabird research.[^1][^3] Campbell Construction repaired and restored the tower in 1997-98. In March 2001 a nor'easter damaged the power cable to the island and the light was out for two months; it was converted to solar power later that year.[^3]

## Today

Petit Manan Island is part of the Maine Coastal Islands National Wildlife Refuge. The Friends of Maine Coastal Islands say the Coast Guard still maintains the tower and the navigation aids, while the Fish and Wildlife Service maintains several historic buildings; LighthouseFriends reports that ownership of the lighthouse was transferred to the Fish and Wildlife Service in late 2006.[^3][^4] The island is closed to the public every year from April 1 to August 31 for seabird nesting, and seasonal technicians live on the island for 12 to 14 weeks each summer.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open September through March but the dwelling and tower are closed, that the keeper's quarters house refuge personnel, and that boat tours show the lighthouse and birds from the water even during the closure.[^3] The island lies offshore and can be reached only by boat, so anyone wishing to land should check with the refuge first.
