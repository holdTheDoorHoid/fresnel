At the southern tip of Prince Edward Island, where the ferry now crosses Northumberland Strait to Nova Scotia, a wooden lighthouse with the keeper's house built onto its side has stood since 1876. Two small range towers, put up in 1902 to guide vessels into the harbour, now stand beside it.

## The place

Wood Islands, once known as Port Woods, lies on the southernmost point of Prince Edward Island. A mail run between Wood Islands and Pictou, Nova Scotia, was the island's only link to the mainland until 1827.[^1] Visitors today reach Wood Islands by the ferry from Caribou, Nova Scotia, or by road from Charlottetown.[^1]

## Building the light

In its 1874 session Parliament set aside $6,000 for a lighthouse at Wood Islands.[^2] The contract went first to Archibald MacKay of Moncton, New Brunswick, but a year later he had made little headway and Donald MacMillan was given the job.[^2] Lighthouse Friends puts the contract at $3,000, says MacKay abandoned the work after receiving $900, and says the land cost $300, bought from Thomas Mahon.[^1] The station was put into service on 1 November 1876.[^1] (The provincial heritage record says only that the work was finished by November 1876.[^2])

The tower is square and wooden, 12.2 m (40 ft) high, with pedimented windows and a flared cornice under a wooden balustrade.[^1] The provincial record counts three storeys.[^2] The first lens was fourth-order, showing a fixed white light.[^1] The provincial record calls it a rare surviving tower with the keeper's dwelling attached, and says it resembles the one at Cape Egmont, in western Prince Edward Island.[^2]

In 1902 a pair of range lights was added to guide vessels into the harbour. The Department of Marine's report says they were put in operation on 6 September 1902. The report places the front tower on the breakwater at the harbour's east entrance, "50 feet from its outer end".[^3] That is about 15 m. It was a square wooden building with sloping sides, painted white, 19 feet (5.8 m) high, showing a fixed red light 18 feet above high water, seen 6 miles off.[^3] The back tower stood on the gravel beach 700 feet (213 m) away. It was of the same pattern, 33 feet (10 m) high, with a fixed red light seen 7 miles in the line of range.[^3] The report says day labour built them under the supervision of M. Walsh for $204.92.[^3]

## The keepers

James MacMillan, brother of the builder Donald, was the first keeper. He and his wife Lily had four sons, whom Lighthouse Friends calls the lighthouse boys.[^1] One of them, William, took over in 1887.[^1] In 1898 William McMillan was dismissed after being found guilty of partisan activity against the Liberal party. The dismissal was debated in the House of Commons. Alexander Martin complained that turning the keeper and his widowed mother (James had died in 1896) out of house and home in the fall was a "shameless transaction".[^1]

John McKay followed (1898 to 1912), then Levi Morrow (1912 to 1924), Thomas A. Smith (1924 to 1949) and George Stewart (1949 to 1972).[^1] Manson E. Murchison, Bernard Beaton and Leon Patton came after them.[^1] Patton, the last resident keeper, is listed as serving to 1990, the year the provincial record gives for full automation.[^1][^2] Wood Islands was one of the last three lighthouses on the island to have resident keepers.[^2]

Until the road was built in 1937 to 1940, the station was remote. The beach west of the lighthouse offered a route, but many keepers preferred to go to and from it by rowboat, and getting to school from the lighthouse was no small task for the children. Some of them found it lonely, while others relished the adventures close by.[^1]

The range lights had their own keepers. James Young served from 1902 until he resigned at the end of 1935, then Harold E. Young from 1936 to 1943.[^4] William Robert Brown took over in April 1943 and died in November 1944. His sister Hazel M. MacFarlane tended the lights until navigation closed, and after a cancelled appointment of Alexander J. Young in January 1945 she was reinstated.[^4] She was still keeper in 1963, when the range lights were electrified.[^4]

## After the keepers

Around 1941 the range lights were moved to the pier west of the lighthouse, when the ferry began running between Wood Islands and Caribou.[^4] A fog horn was set up at the lighthouse for the ferry in 1941.[^1] A shed was added to the front range tower in 1946 to hold fog alarm equipment for a trumpet through its seaward side.[^4] The lighthouse itself was electrified in 1958 and fully automated by 1990.[^2]

In the early 1980s the Coast Guard built a separate house for the keeper. The old dwelling was to be removed, but local residents intervened and the lighthouse was kept. The bungalow was taken away in 1991.[^1] The lighthouse opened to the public in 1998.[^1] The range lights were decommissioned in 2007.[^4]

Erosion had cut the distance from the shore from almost 150 m in 1876 to about 3 m. At first light on 10 March 2009 the lighthouse was jacked up, set on steel beams and wheels, and moved 70 m (230 ft) inland over frozen ground.[^1] The range towers were moved beside it in 2013.[^4] Wood Islands and Area Development Corporation became owner and operator of the museum in December 2009.[^1] The province designated the lighthouse a heritage place on 3 October 2012.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is a museum and interpretive centre, with the keeper's house open to visitors. Lighthouse Friends says the lighthouse is open daily from early June to late September, and reports the tower open in season.[^1] The two range towers, beside it, are listed as grounds and tower closed and can be viewed from Wood Islands Provincial Park.[^4] The Canadian Coast Guard's list of lights still carries Wood Islands as a private aid, an isophase white light every 10 seconds, 21.9 m above the water.[^5] The range lights were decommissioned in 2007.[^4] Check hours before travelling.
