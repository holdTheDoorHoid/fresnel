Killarney East Lighthouse stands on Red Rock Point at the eastern entrance to the town of Killarney, on the North Channel of Georgian Bay, about 75 km southwest of Sudbury.[^3][^1][^2] Its present square-tapered wooden tower, with an octagonal iron lantern, dates from 1909 and was recognized as a federal heritage building in 1991.[^2][^4]

## The place

Killarney sits at the tip of a granite peninsula that juts into Georgian Bay. A long, narrow channel separates the town from George Island, which shelters it from the open bay.[^1] The Ojibwa called the place Shebahonaning, "here is a safe canoe channel," Lighthouse Friends says.[^1] Etienne Augustin de la Morandière, who became the first white settler in the area, set up a trading post on the channel in 1820.[^1] Access to the town was limited to boats until Highway 637 was completed in 1962.[^1]

The lights were to mark the North Channel, which runs between the northern end of Georgian Bay and Lake Superior.[^1]

## Building the light

The North Channel lights were a long time coming. The contract that saw six "Imperial Towers" erected on Lake Huron and Georgian Bay in the 1850s was also to cover four North Channel lights, but cost overruns led to their postponement.[^1] In 1866 the government built five nearly identical lights along the North Channel, two of them at Killarney.[^1]

The two Killarney lights were first called Red Rock Lighthouse and Partridge Island Lighthouse. Each was a square wooden tower about 6.1 m (20 ft) from base to the top of its birdcage lantern. The Red Rock light stood on bare rock, with a focal plane of 24 m (80 ft).[^1] Philemon Proulx was appointed the first keeper of both.[^1]

In 1876 Darius Smith, superintendent of lights above Montreal, inspected the Killarney lights. Lighthouse Friends prints his report, which describes the upper light as having "two dual burner fountain lamps, and one 14-inch reflector on iron circle." The white square wooden building was 4.3 m (14 ft) high, and the light was 5.8 m (19 ft) from the ground.[^1] Smith also found the keeper of the lower light had been away from his post for over two years, and that "the lights have been kept by his two sons." Lighthouse Friends says Proulx was still the keeper at the time.[^1]

Proulx was superannuated in September 1880. Pierre Regis de Lamorandière, a grandson of the founder, replaced him.[^1]

The present towers date from 1909, according to Lighthouse Friends, which gives the cost as $782.29.[^1] The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1910 lists the erection of "a new 29-foot wooden lighthouse tower, surmounted by an octagonal iron lantern, at Killarney east," and a second of the same kind at Killarney west.[^4] The two sources date the work to different years, and the story does not choose between them.

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends gives the head keepers in order: Philemon Proulx (1867 to 1880), Pierre Regis de Lamorandière (1880 to 1904), Frank Roque (1904 to 1912), Joseph H. Burke (1912 to 1946), Frank J. Sinclair (1946 to 1950), Ferdinand Solomon (1950 to 1960), Basil Roque (1960 to 1961), Ferdinand Solomon again (1961 to 1979), Brent Skipen (1979 to 1981) and Alfred de Lamorandière (from 1982).[^1]

Lamorandière was also hired to help the fishery overseers against illegal fishing. Lighthouse Friends says this made enemies in the community, and that in 1893 he was threatened and the hay he used to feed his cattle was set on fire.[^1] Because the northwest light was risky to reach in bad weather, he built a shack in a hollow near the lighthouse and paid two men, Oliver Pilot and Henry Solomon, to look after the light when he could not reach the island.[^1] He retired in 1904 and died on 16 February 1923.[^1]

The Department's list of lightkeepers for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1909 gives Frank Roque's appointment at Killarney as 28 February 1905, a year later than Lighthouse Friends' start date for him.[^5] The Department's 1906 list shows him at the "Killarney Lights," and its 1911 list still names him there.[^7][^6]

In 1950 Ferdinand Solomon and Frank J. Sinclair exchanged stations, Lighthouse Friends reports.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On a windy morning in November 1956, Keeper Ferdinand Solomon set out early to tend the east light. Large breakers capsized his boat. He grabbed two life jackets and was washed about until a wave deposited him on Jackman Shoal, "as if it were a Godsend." A local game warden went into the water and brought him in, Lighthouse Friends says. Solomon made a full recovery and looked after the lights until his death in 1979.[^1]

## After the keepers

Before 1957 a hand foghorn served the east light. In 1957 a fog whistle was installed at the tower.[^1]

The Canadian Register of Historic Places records the tower as recognized by the Government of Canada on 5 September 1991, and says the simple timber design could be moved if water channels shifted or the coast eroded.[^2]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the lighthouse. Lighthouse Friends reports that the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] From Ontario Street, before the airport, a one-lane gravel road leads to the point. Parking is limited, and motor homes are not advised.[^1]

The Coast Guard list shows the light as seasonal, in place year round and operating 24 hours. Its characteristic is "Iso W 4s," from a focal height of 17.1 m, with a nominal range of 12 nautical miles. Mariners can have the foghorn switched on by keying a radio microphone five times on channel 19.[^3] The same list gives the second North Channel light at Killarney, "Killarney Northwest," on Partridge Island.[^3]
