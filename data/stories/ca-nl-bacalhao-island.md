Bacalhao Island Lighthouse stands on an island in Notre Dame Bay, Newfoundland and Labrador. It was built in 1894 and first lit that December, and it still shows a white flash every ten seconds, though the light of 1894 flashed every three.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Lighthouse Friends gives the Portuguese word for cod as *bacalhau*, and says a variant of that spelling is used for the island's name. Some features around the island are also named for the fish.[^2] The island lies just north of Herring Neck, on New World Island.[^2] The heritage character statement for the tower says the lighthouse stands out from the water because the site has little vegetation or other visual obstruction, and it asks that the site's barrenness be kept.[^3]

## Building the light

An iron tower was ordered in 1893 from Victoria Works, for Western Bay Head on Conception Bay. When it was ready the following year, it was shipped to Bacalhao Island instead.[^2] During 1894 a store went up at the landing, a road was built to a peak at the southwest end of the island, and the keeper's dwelling and the tower were erected.[^2] The dwelling stood 23 m from the tower and was joined to it by a covered passageway.[^2]

The heritage statement describes the tower as cast iron, and names Chance Bros. and Co., Lighthouse Engineers and Constructors, of Birmingham, England, as its manufacturer and designer.[^3] The Canadian Register of Historic Places counts it among about 26 lighthouses built of prefabricated iron and steel, which it believes are the only surviving examples of that type in Canada.[^4] A plaque on the lighthouse door carries the Chance Bros. name and the date 1884. Lighthouse Friends suggests that the date likely refers to the original lens, which it thinks had been used somewhere else before it reached the island in 1894.[^2]

The light was first lit on 6 December 1894.[^2] It ran on the Trotter-Lindberg system, in which the heat from the flame turned a slotted screen around the lamp, giving a white flash every three seconds.[^2] The tower was painted in red and white spirals to make the station easier to tell apart by day.[^2] Lighthouse Friends gives the focal plane as 108.8 m (357 ft).[^2] The Coast Guard's current list gives a focal height of 106.0 m.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends names Keeper William Howell as the man who placed the light in operation on 6 December 1894. Its roster spells the name Holwell and gives his service as 1894 to 1900.[^2] The roster continues with J. Kearley (1901 to 1904) and Edward Hussey (1906 to at least 1912).[^2] Four more names appear with no dates: Bennett Stuckely, Raply Hurley, Harvey Watkins and Leslie Powell. Barry Potter is listed for 1988 to 1992.[^2] These sources give little more than the names.

## After the keepers

The light changed to fixed white in 1923, and in 1924 its lens was upgraded from sixth order to fourth.[^2] In 1929, with an acetylene gas light installed, the characteristic became a white flash every nine seconds.[^2] In 1961 a few large shipping companies whose vessels passed the island asked for a fog alarm there.[^2] A fog-alarm building and a new dwelling followed in the early 1960s, and four keepers then worked the station in rotation.[^2] A fog horn sounds a three-second blast every thirty seconds when needed.[^2] The original dwelling was in poor condition in 1966, when it was sold and taken off the island.[^2]

Lighthouse Friends dates the lighthouse's classification as a Recognized Federal Heritage Building to 1989.[^2] The heritage statement gives three reasons for the designation: its historical association, which it ties to navigational aids in Newfoundland when Newfoundland was a British colony; its functional design; and its landmark value.[^3] In 2006 the Bacalhao Island Sector Light was established on a square skeleton tower near the lighthouse, replacing a pair of range lights that had operated on the island.[^1][^2]

## Today

The property is owned by Transport Canada, through the Canadian Coast Guard.[^3] Lighthouse Friends reports that the grounds are open and the tower closed, and that the island is best seen by boat or from the air.[^2] The Coast Guard's list of lights, updated on 23 June 2026, gives the main light as a flash every ten seconds, with a focal height of 106.0 m above the water and a nominal range of 17 nautical miles. It describes the main tower as a square skeleton tower with a red and white rectangular daymark. The heritage statement describes a cast-iron cylindrical tower with a spiralling daymark. The sources do not explain the difference.[^1][^3]
