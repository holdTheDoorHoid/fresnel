Goderich Main Lighthouse is a short, almost square stone tower on the bluff above the harbour at Goderich, Ontario, on Lake Huron.[^2][^3] It was erected in 1847 and is recognized as the first lighthouse on the Canadian shore of the lake.[^1][^2]

## The place

The harbour began as the mouth of the Maitland River. In the 1830s the Canada Company built two stone-filled wooden piers to give the anchorage some shelter.[^1] The tower was built to guide mariners near that river mouth, which the designation report describes as "prone to strong nor' westerly gales."[^2] Lighthouse Friends counts the station's start as a pair of range lights in the early 1830s and calls Goderich the oldest Canadian light station on Lake Huron.[^1]

The site on the bluff was a parcel of land overlooking the harbour. Lighthouse Friends says Thomas Mercer Jones, a land magnate with the Canada Company, sold it to Queen Victoria.[^1]

## Building the light

A contract for the tower was entered into in 1845, and it called for the tower to be finished by 1 July 1846.[^1] The designation report says the tower was "Erected in 1847" and that construction was overseen by the Department of Public Works.[^2] The sources do not reconcile the two dates. Lighthouse Friends also reports that severe gales in November 1846 damaged the lantern, and that the lighting apparatus was placed in the tower in 1847.[^1]

The stonemason was Adam MacVicar, who had worked on the Welland Canal before moving to Goderich. The designation report says he handled the work "in a very neat and competent manner."[^1][^2] The designation report calls the almost square plan "an uncommon design for Public Works." Unlike almost all masonry towers built before and after it, the report says, "it was not tall, round or tapered."[^2]

In 1847 Hamilton Killaly, a civil engineer, visited Goderich and inspected the new light. Lighthouse Friends quotes his report on the lantern: the glass was "much too light," and "three were lighted when I was there, the remainder having been boarded up."[^1] The Coast Guard list gives the tower as 10.1 m high, with its light 42.7 m above the water. Lighthouse Friends says the tower "stands just twenty feet tall to its lantern room," which is about 6 m.[^3][^1] The two heights are not reconciled.

In 1872 the government had a work built to protect the bank under the lighthouse from wave erosion. A Brantford firm completed it in the summer of 1873 at a cost of $3,850.[^1] In 1883 five panes of French plate glass were set in the lantern room, and the light was given ten "mammoth flat-wick burners." Lighthouse Friends reports, citing the Huron Signal, that the keeper was "exceedingly pleased" with the work.[^1]

The Department's report for the year ended 30 June 1909 records that at the main light "the catoptric light has been removed and a 4th order French lens" was installed. Lighthouse Friends dates the lens to 1908.[^4][^1]

Later works followed the breakwaters. The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1905 says that after a breakwater was built about 460 m (1,500 ft) outside the piers, a new back light was needed. It "was put in operation on 5th September, 1905," as a fixed red light on a mast "elevated 68 feet above the level of the lake."[^5] By the fiscal year ended 30 June 1909, the mast that had carried the back light was replaced by a "3-section steel skeleton tower."[^6] Lighthouse Friends dates the tower to 1908.[^1]

In 1914 the tower was heightened, and its gallery railing and stone lantern deck were replaced.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the light was "heightened five feet in 1914," and adds that a new lantern room and concrete deck were fitted.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists the head keepers in order: Thomas Brady (1847 to 1856), Humphrey Fidler (1856 to 1871), George N. Macdonald (1871 to 1886), Robert Campbell (1886 to 1910), William Robinson (1911 to 1926), Bert McDonald (1926 to 1939) and Reginald R. Needham (1939 to 1959).[^1] The keeper of the main light also looked after the pier lights. When the pier lights passed to the Department of Marine in 1875, George N. McDonald "was paid an extra $100 each year to look after the pier lights."[^1]

The Department's keeper list for the Ontario division in its 1911 report still gives "Robert Campbell" for the Goderich Lights, appointed 9 June 1886.[^7] Lighthouse Friends ends his service in 1910.[^1]

After Needham came Norman Comier (1959 to 1962), Walter Sheardown and Allan K. MacDonald (both 1962 to 1985), and Donald Bert MacAdam (1985 to 1992), according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1914 records a storm "which prevailed on lakes Huron and Superior, November 9 and 10, 1913." Three steamers foundered on Lake Huron in it, the James Carruthers of Toronto, the Regina and the Wexford, and "the entire crews of the three steamers (numbering about 50 persons) were drowned."[^8]

Lighthouse Friends says the Wexford "went down off Goderich with a load of grain." After bodies wearing life jackets from the ship washed ashore at Goderich, an inquest was held.[^1] A Perth Amboy newspaper of 14 November 1913 reported from Goderich that "the wreckage of the steamer Wexford has come ashore, besides bodies of several members of the crew bearing Wexford life preservers."[^9] The same paper reported that three bodies lashed to a raft stamped with the name John A. McGean "came ashore this morning near Goderich," and that a later report said twenty more bodies from that ship had washed ashore.[^9] A Grand Forks paper that day reported from Goderich that "The bodies of eight, men and one woman" from the Carruthers had been found on the shore.[^10]

Lighthouse Friends reports testimony at the inquest that "no one felt any particular responsibility in the matter of blowing the foghorn," and that on foggy or stormy days it was blown "whenever anyone happened to think about it."[^1] In 1914 an electric compressed-air diaphone fog alarm was set up at the western end of the north breakwater, and the life-saving station on the south pier was given the job of running it.[^1] The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1910 records the erection of a reinforced concrete gas-lighted beacon, 4.6 m (15 ft) high, on the western end of the breakwater.[^11] Lighthouse Friends dates it to 1909.[^1]

## After the keepers

The designation report says a park was created around the tower in 1941 and 1942.[^2] In 1945 a flashing red light went up on a small skeletal tower at the outer end of the south breakwater, and a radiobeacon was established at Goderich the same year. The present concrete tower on the southern breakwater was built in 1952.[^1]

The keeper's dwelling attached to the tower gave a different story in each source. The designation report says it was "removed about 1914, leaving the tower standing alone on the site for more than 75 years."[^2] Lighthouse Friends says it was sold off and moved to a private parcel to the northeast, and that it was "torn down in 1985."[^1]

On 29 August 2003 the Crown sold the lighthouse and the land around it to the Town of Goderich for one dollar.[^1] On 13 October 2009 the Town passed By-law No. 97 of 2009, designating the lighthouse under Section 29 of the Ontario Heritage Act, which is the Part IV process.[^2] After local marine enthusiasts and the Marine Heritage Committee approached the Town in 2009, the tower was stripped of paint, given new period mortar, a new door and windows, and repainted. Interpretive signs were installed, and a public opening was held in June 2010.[^1]

## Today

The Town of Goderich owns and maintains the lighthouse, and Lighthouse Friends reports that the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] The designation report places it at 280 Cobourg Street, in a residential area, with a public park around it.[^2] The Coast Guard list shows the light as a private aid to navigation, in place year round and operating 24 hours. It shows two white flashes every 25 seconds.[^3]
