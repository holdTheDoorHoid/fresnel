Southwest Reef Light was an iron tower on pilings in Atchafalaya Bay, Louisiana, first lit in 1859 and discontinued by the Lighthouse Service in 1916. Abandoned for decades, it was brought ashore in 1987 and set up in a riverside park in Berwick, where it still stands.[^1][^3]

## The place

Plantation crops once floated down Bayou Teche to Atchafalaya Bay. By the early 1800s the bay's shipping had grown enough to call for aids to navigation, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^3] A shoal about fifteen miles long reaches west from Point Au Fer. The bay's first lighthouse went up at Point Au Fer in 1827, but it did not mark the bay well enough, and in 1848 a lightship was stationed just off the end of the shoal.[^3]

The nomination places the tower about fifteen nautical miles off the coast of St. Mary Parish, in the Gulf of Mexico.[^1]

Running lightships was costly, and according to Lighthouse Friends that led the Lighthouse Board to approve a lighthouse here instead.[^3]

## Building the light

On 18 August 1856 Congress appropriated $20,000 for a lighthouse to replace the lightship and the Point Au Fer light.[^3]

The plan was for a square, pyramid-shaped iron tower, carried on four piles set into the shoal, with living space for the keeper inside.[^3] The nomination says it stood on a platform on cast-iron screw-pile legs, ten feet (3 m) above the water surface, and dates the tower to 1858.[^1]

The nomination lists the tower for its unusual Egyptian Revival styling. Quoting a Coast Guard inventory, it calls the design "unique among lighthouses of the Gulf Coast region," with most of the others round brick towers.[^1]

The light was red, shown from a fourth-order Fresnel lens with a forty-nine-foot (14.9 m) focal plane, and it was first lit on 1 September 1859.[^3][^6] A Lighthouse Board notice printed in the New Orleans Tribune in November 1865 gives the same lens and focal plane, and says the light could be seen for twelve nautical miles in clear weather.[^7]

On 3 March 1873 Congress appropriated $5,000 to elevate the tower against the seas, and $15,000 for a steam fog signal.[^3] The tower was detached from its foundation in 1875, bolted to four cast-iron columns and lowered back into place. Lighthouse Friends says this nearly doubled the buffer between the lighthouse and the bay.[^3] The twelve-inch steam whistle started on 31 March 1875.[^3]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists George Wright as the first keeper, from 1859 to 1860. Lighthouse Friends says he was transferred from the Point Au Fer light, which the new tower displaced.[^2][^3] Francis H. Clark served two stints as head keeper, 1865 to 1868 and 1869 to 1875.[^2] No keeper is named in the archive between 1861 and 1865, while the light was dark.[^2][^3]

Julius Herbert was first assistant from 1875 to 1877, then head keeper from 1877 to 1899, the longest head-keeper stint on the roster at twenty-two years.[^2] Samuel Jones served as an assistant, then as head keeper from 1900 to 1909.[^2] Edward L. Rollingson, who was first assistant from 1894 to 1898, was head keeper from 1909 to 1916.[^2] The archive's last first assistant is Louis Buras, Sr., who served from 1915 to 1916.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Confederates shut the light off in its third year of operation, and it stayed dark through the Civil War.[^3] Lighthouse Friends says the Lighthouse Board later reported that the tower had "sustained much damage from the rebels." Union forces found its lamps, lens and glass at St. Martinville, and Lighthouse Friends says these went back to the tower in the summer of 1865. The light was reactivated on the sixth anniversary of its first lighting, after repairs.[^3]

A Lighthouse Board notice printed in the New Orleans Tribune on 5 November 1865 gives a different date. Dated 18 October, it says that, "having been repaired and renovated," the light showed a fixed red light on the night of the 8th and "will be continued hereafter."[^7] The notice is dated later than the summer date Lighthouse Friends gives, so the two sources disagree on when the light came back.

In October 1867 a severe hurricane struck the bay and damaged the exposed tower. Lighthouse Friends says "the storm-tossed seas broke through the bottom of the dwelling and tore the gallery from the tower."[^3]

In January 1868 a Notice to Mariners said the red light then shown would be discontinued on 1 February and replaced by a white light "until further notice."[^8]

In its final year the steam whistle sounded for about 127 hours and used 26,830 pounds of coal, according to Lighthouse Friends. In January 1889 a 1,200-pound fog bell replaced it.[^3]

On 16 and 17 August 1915 a tropical hurricane struck the Gulf coast near Galveston, Texas. The Lighthouse Service's bulletin says that at Southwest Reef "the platform under dwelling and outbuildings were destroyed."[^4]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service completed the Point Au Fer Reef light in the fiscal year ended 30 June 1916, beside the new channel into the bay.[^5][^3] Lighthouse Friends gives 31 March 1916 as the day the new light was activated and Southwest Reef was discontinued.[^3]

For about seventy years the abandoned tower stood on its pilings, rusting.[^3] The nomination calls the move necessary. It says the tower "had been abandoned by the Coast Guard and would certainly have fallen victim to one storm or another within a few years."[^1][^3]

In 1987 the Atchafalaya Delta Tourist Commission, the town of Berwick and the group Save Our Coast combined to bring the tower ashore. Under House Bill 1080, ownership passed from the state to Berwick, and the tower was taken off its pilings on 8 October 1987.[^3]

The nomination says the tower sat in a warehouse until it was moved to its present site in April 1991. Lighthouse Friends and Cajun Coast both give April 1990 for the move.[^1][^3][^6]

The tower was sandblasted and partly restored in Berwick. New doors and windows went in, and a replica lantern room was set on top, in July 1996.[^3]

Louisiana's state historic preservation officer certified the nomination for the National Register on 29 July 1991.[^1]

## Today

The tower stands in the Everett S. Berry Lighthouse Park on the western bank of the Atchafalaya River in Berwick.[^3][^6] The town of Berwick owns it. Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^3] Cajun Coast lists the park as open daily to the public.[^6]

The nomination says the original cupola and lens are missing.[^1] Lighthouse Friends mentions a Fresnel lens of unknown origin kept at Berwick Town Hall on Third Street.[^3] No source cited here ties that lens to this tower.
