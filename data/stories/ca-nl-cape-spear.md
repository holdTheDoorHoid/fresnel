Cape Spear is a rocky headland just south of the entrance to St. John's harbour, and the lighthouse on it is the oldest surviving one in Newfoundland and Labrador.[^1][^2] For more than a century it was both a navigation light and a family home, kept almost without a break by one family, the Cantwells.[^1][^2]

## The place

Parks Canada calls Cape Spear the most easterly point of land in Canada,[^5] and the heritage statement says that for a long time the lighthouse was the first structure ships saw after crossing the Atlantic.[^4] Parks Canada's heritage statement describes the light as a landfall light that guided vessels heading for St. John's, the colony's chief centre of commerce and trade.[^4] A light had been placed at Fort Amherst, at the harbour entrance, in 1810; Cape Spear was the second light on any of Newfoundland's coasts.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1832 Newfoundland's newly created legislative assembly authorized lighthouses for the safety of coastal navigation, and Cape Spear was chosen for the first of them because it lay so close to St. John's.[^1] The Parks Canada heritage statement calls it the new government's first major public works project.[^4] According to Lighthouse Friends, an 1834 act authorized a loan of 1,000 pounds, the governor and the Commissioners of Lighthouses chose the site on a visit that year, and the contract went to two St. John's builders, Nicholas Croke and William Parker, working from plans supplied by the Northern Lighthouse Board in Scotland.[^2] Parks Canada says only that work began in 1834 or early in 1835.[^1]

The design joined house and light in one building: a square, two-storey wooden residence with the light tower in the middle of it.[^1][^2] The heritage statement says this arrangement suited the harsh conditions of the site, with the tower reached from the residence's upper storey.[^4]

The sources differ on the date. The heritage statements say the lighthouse was constructed in 1835,[^3][^4] while Parks Canada's history page says the light was operating by September 1836.[^1] Lighthouse Friends reconciles them: it says the wooden residence was completed in 1835 but the light could not be started until 1 September 1836, because the lantern and lighting apparatus had to arrive from Scotland and be mounted.[^2]

The light itself was second-hand. Parks Canada says it had been in use since 1815 at Inchkeith, on the east coast of Scotland, and that seven Argand burners with curved reflectors sat on a rotating frame, turned by clockwork, giving a 17-second flash of white light followed by 43 seconds of darkness.[^1] Lighthouse Friends adds that the Stevenson firm of Scotland supplied the copper-domed lantern.[^2]

Later changes to the light and its signals came in steps. The Parks Canada account has the light changing with technology: a glass dioptric system installed in 1912, oil first, acetylene from 1916 and electricity from 1930.[^1] Lighthouse Friends adds that seal oil lamps used from 1841 gave way to kerosene in 1874, and that the keeper at Ferryland, 58 km away, noticed the brighter light.[^2] It also dates a steam fog alarm to 1877 on the "bill of the cape", with a second trumpet added in 1896 because the first could seldom be heard to the south, and a diaphone in 1910.[^2] Parks Canada instead puts the first fog horn in 1878.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends names Emanuel Warren as the first keeper, serving until his death in 1846, and says the post then passed to James Cantwell.[^2] The same page tells how Cantwell came to have it: in August 1845, as the story is told there, his pilot boat found the frigate Rhine, carrying Prince Henry of the Netherlands, lost in fog and he piloted it in to St. John's; the prince offered him a reward, Cantwell asked to be keeper of Cape Spear, and the prince arranged it.[^2] The page does not say which of its listed references the account comes from, and no contemporary record of it is cited. What is firmly documented is the outcome: Parks Canada says the lighthouse was operated from 1846 to 1955 by several generations of the Cantwell family.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends gives this succession of head keepers, with its own spelling of the first name: Emanuel Warre (1836 to 1846), James Cantwell (1846 to 1879), Austin Sheppard (1880 to 1886), Dennis Cantwell (1886 to 1909), James Cantwell (1909 to 1918), William Cantwell (1918 to 1925), Jack Cantwell (1925 to 1939), Weston Cantwell (1939 to 1944), Frank Cantwell (1944 to 1965), Martin Hefferan (1965 to 1982) and Gerald Cantwell (1982 to 1997).[^2] The same page's text says James Cantwell died in 1880 after thirty-five years at Cape Spear, a year later than the list ends his term. It also says Sheppard was moved to Fort Amherst in 1887, a year after the list has Dennis taking over, and that James's son Dennis, who had been running the fog alarm, then took charge of the whole station.[^2] The page's text says Gerry Cantwell, son of Frank, took charge in 1982 (the list calls him Gerald), while a photograph on Parks Canada's page captions Gerry Cantwell as light keeper from 1969 to 1997.[^1][^2]

Parks Canada's heritage statement says Cantwell light-keepers served over a century, through the building's working life.[^3] Lighthouse Friends records that in 1931 seventeen-year-old Margaret Hefferan took a job teaching the eight children of the Cantwell brothers then serving as keepers, and that ten years later she married Frank Cantwell, the assistant keeper.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Lighthouse Friends reports a gale on 19 September 1846 that raised one side of the building several inches off its foundation and twisted the wooden framework under the lantern so badly that nearly every pane of glass broke. Each corner was then chained to the rock, and in 1850 a stone and brick tower was built inside the wooden building to carry the lantern.[^2] That is the circular stone tower which Parks Canada's heritage statement describes at the core of the residence.[^3]

In the early 1930s, the same source says, brothers Weston and Michael Cantwell were working the fog alarm with doors and windows shut, and carbon monoxide from the diesel engine overcame them. Weston's wife persuaded Jack Cantwell to go down the cliff with her; they found the brothers unconscious, and Jack dragged them to the door. Weston recovered within days; Michael died two years later, never having fully recovered.[^2]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends says two new keepers' dwellings were built a couple of years after Newfoundland joined Canada in 1949, and that in 1955 a new concrete octagonal tower, 13.7 m tall, went up near the old one; Parks Canada also gives 1955 for moving the lens into a new tower.[^1][^2] The Canadian Register's statement dates the newer lighthouse to 1953.[^4] Lighthouse Friends says the Department of Transport meant to demolish the old lighthouse but was met by public protest.[^2] The Cantwell line of keepers ended in 1997, when Cape Spear was de-staffed.[^2] Parks Canada says the family still speaks up for the lighthouse's preservation.[^1]

The old lighthouse was declared a national historic site in 1962, and Parks Canada began a five-year restoration in 1977. Additions were stripped away, the exterior was finished in 1981 and the interior early the next year, furnished to show a keeper's life in the late 1830s.[^2] The park was opened on 24 June 1983 by Prince Charles and Princess Diana.[^2]

## Today

The restored lighthouse stands in Cape Spear Lighthouse National Historic Site, run by Parks Canada, which also looks after the Second World War coastal battery on the cape.[^1][^5] The grounds are accessible year round, but visitor services are open only from June to October; the site is a 20-minute drive from downtown St. John's.[^6] The newer 1955 tower is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard, and Lighthouse Friends says its grounds are open but the tower is closed.[^2] Whether the old lighthouse's interior is open on a given day is a matter for the season's published hours.[^6]
