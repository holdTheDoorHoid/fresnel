Barbers Point Light stands at the southwest tip of Oahu, west of Honolulu, where a coral reef runs out from the point.[^1][^2] The present light is a 72-foot (22 m) concrete tower, first lit in December 1933 beside the coral-stone tower of 1888 that it replaced.[^1][^8]

## The place

LighthouseFriends says the brig *Arthur*, captained by Henry Barber, was sailing west from Honolulu to Canton when she struck the reef in 1796, and six of her twenty-two crew were lost. Since then, the point has been associated with Captain Barber.[^1] The French whaler *Marquis de Turenne* grounded on the same reef in 1855.[^1]

In 1880 the Hawaiian government's survey bureau chose a site for a light. William Alexander, its surveyor general, reported that he had selected a site and marked the spot with a pile of stones and a staff with a red and white flag.[^1] That year $2,500 was appropriated for a light there.[^1]

## Building the light

The French firm L. Sautter-Lemonnier was contracted for a fourth-order Fresnel lens, with its lamps and lantern room.[^1] The hardware arrived from France after the lighthouse funds were depleted, and the shipment was put into storage.[^1] It took six years to find $3,000, and the tower went up on land that James Campbell donated.[^1]

Peter High won the contract with the lowest of six bids: $1,892 for a stone lighthouse, and $309 for a keeper's dwelling.[^1] The coral-stone tower stood 42 feet (13 m) high, was painted white, and was topped by a red lantern room. It was first exhibited on 29 March 1888.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1934 dates the old tower to 1888, "during the reign of King" Kalakaua.[^8]

In 1912 the light was remodeled to show a fourth-order, double-flash lens lit by an incandescent oil-vapor lamp.[^1][^5] The 1912 Bulletin lists the change to incandescent oil vapor at Barbers Point.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the change was made so the light would stand apart from the steady plantation lights nearby.[^1] The Commissioner of Lighthouses later explained that the change "made it necessary to assign an assistant keeper to this station."[^10] A one-story frame dwelling was erected for the keeper on 26 June 1915, at a total cost of $3,198.96 to 30 June 1915.[^6]

By 1930 the old tower was deteriorating, and a new one was planned.[^1] An appropriation of $20,000 was secured in 1933 for a 72-foot reinforced-concrete tower beside it.[^1] The lens moved to the new tower, which was first lit on 29 December 1933.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that, with a crowd of interested spectators looking on, cuts were made in the coral stone on one side of the old tower, and it toppled over.[^1]

## The keepers

William Aalona Hatton was the first keeper, from 1888 to 1907. LighthouseFriends prints his name as William Hatton Aalona and says he was hired at $240 a year.[^1] After nearly twenty years in charge, he died in his sleep on 14 February 1907.[^1][^2]

Manuel Ferreira was an assistant keeper from 1914 to 1916 and head keeper from 1916 to 1925, and he was head keeper again from 1942 to 1946.[^2] When Ferreira and his assistant both fell ill with influenza, LighthouseFriends says Caroline Ferreira took on their responsibilities. Each night she minded the temperamental kerosene-vapor lamp, and every three hours she wound the clockwork.[^1]

Samuel Apolo Amalu kept the light in two stints, 1909 to 1913 and 1929 to 1941.[^2] The commissioner's efficiency flag for 1934 was awarded to him, a year when his wife was critically ill.[^9][^1] The same Bulletin records that the station became a one-keeper station on 18 January 1934.[^9]

Fred E. Robins was an assistant there in the 1920s and early 1930s and head keeper from 1953 until the light was automated in 1964.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Early on 31 August 1906 the army transport *Sheridan*, twenty-one days from Manila, struck the reef near the light.[^1][^4] The *Pacific Commercial Advertiser* reported that she "struck a coral reef" near the Barber's Point lighthouse, and that the Barber's Point beacon was "brightly shining" when she struck.[^4] The *Hawaiian Star*, reporting by wireless from Barbers Point, said the keeper's light "was burning brightly" and that the moon was shining when the vessel struck. The paper gave the time of the stranding as 1:48 a.m.[^3]

In 1919, LighthouseFriends says, Ferreira helped rescue seven Japanese fishermen whose sixty-foot (18 m) sampan had been crippled on the reef off the point. They were fed and given dry clothing at his home.[^1]

In January 1923 a storm with winds of 55 miles an hour disabled the four-masted schooner *Bianca*. On 14 January she anchored some distance off the point, with pumps and other machinery out of commission, and Ferreira picked up her distress signals.[^7] The Bulletin says the keeper had to make his way 3 miles "over a rough trail through a jungle of storm-torn algaroba trees" to the nearest telephone, to send the report to the superintendent at Honolulu.[^7][^1] LighthouseFriends says a Navy tug was sent and towed her to harbor.[^1]

On 7 December 1941 Keeper John M. Sweeney watched the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor.[^1] A few days later he wrote a letter describing the station, which LighthouseFriends prints in part.[^1] In it he records that two parachutists "were dropped close to the station," that they "prowled around the station all Sunday night" while "the Fort Kam. 55th C.A. boys" fired at them "with rifles and machine guns," and that one was wounded and later found on the beach.[^1]

## After the keepers

On 15 April 1964 a 36-inch airway beacon replaced the station's Fresnel lens. The light was automated, and the last keeper, Fred Robins, left the automated lighthouse later that year, on 7 December.[^1]

In 1985 a double-barreled rotating optic, the DCB-224, replaced the airway beacon, and LighthouseFriends says it raised the light's range to 24 nautical miles.[^1]

The lantern room and the lens that were removed at automation are on display at the Columbia River Maritime Museum in Astoria, Oregon.[^1]

## Today

LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard owns the light. The grounds are open, but the tower is closed.[^1] Visitors reach the light by Olai Street, which leads past Barbers Point Beach to the lighthouse.[^1]
