Drum Point Light is a white hexagonal cottage on iron screw-pile legs.[^1][^2] The Lighthouse Board built it in 1883 at the mouth of the Patuxent River in Maryland.[^1] It was decommissioned in 1962, and in 1975 it was barged up the river to the Calvert Marine Museum at Solomons, where it stands at the water's edge today.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The hazard was a low sandspit.[^3] LighthouseFriends, describing an 1838 report, says many vessels sheltered in the lee of Drum Point but often ran aground on the sandspit that projects from the point.[^3] The first proposal for a light came in that report.[^2][^3] Lieutenant William D. Porter urged the Treasury to place a beacon-light on Drum Point, at the mouth of the Patuxent River.[^2]

Nothing was built for a long time.[^2] An act of August 1854 included $5,000 for a lighthouse on Drum Point, but the state and federal governments argued over the land until 1856, and the transaction was never completed.[^2] The museum says the reason is probably lost for good.[^2] It notes that the land may have been owned by Richard B. Fitzgerald of Baltimore, who had plans for a railway to Drum Point, and that those plans may have complicated the sale.[^2]

Steamboat captains and agents petitioned Congress in 1874 for a light and a fog bell at Drum Point.[^2] That April the Maryland General Assembly passed an act of cession, which made it easier for the federal government to gain title to submarine sites.[^2] On 15 February 1883 the governor signed a deed conveying five acres of submerged land about one-sixteenth of a nautical mile south of Drum Point.[^2][^1]

## Building the light

Work on the white wooden tower and its iron base began on 17 July 1883, and the light was first shown on 20 August.[^2] Most of the parts were made ahead and barged to the site, and LighthouseFriends says the beacon went up in about a month.[^3] The wrought-iron piles came from the Allentown Rolling Mills in Philadelphia.[^3]

The lens was a fourth-order Fresnel, shipped from the Lighthouse Engineer's office on Staten Island.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the French firm of Henry-Lepaute made the lens to shine over only 270 degrees, so that no light would fall on the land.[^3]

The two sources disagree on the range. The museum gives the red light's range in clear weather as thirteen nautical miles; LighthouseFriends gives eleven.[^2][^3]

The fog bell weighed 1,400 pounds.[^3] A hammer struck it a double blow four times a minute in poor visibility, and the keeper had to wind its 600-pound weights every two hours.[^3]

The tower did not stay on the water.[^1] The National Register nomination says it was originally placed one-sixteenth of a nautical mile from shore, and that from 1965 on the water had receded until the lighthouse was "marooned on the beach."[^1] LighthouseFriends puts the silting earlier: by 1900 the bay came only three feet (0.9 m) up the pilings, and in the 1970s the lighthouse stood above dry land.[^3] The sources do not agree on when the shore moved.

## The keepers

The first keeper was Benjamin N. Gray, who came from the assistant's post at Cove Point.[^2] The Lighthouse Society lists him as principal keeper from 1883 to 1890, and LighthouseFriends prints his middle initial as M.[^4][^3]

James L. Weems, Sr., is listed for 1891 to 1918, the longest stint on the Society's roster.[^4] LighthouseFriends says Anna Weems Ewalt was born at the lighthouse in 1903, and that she gave the museum some family heirlooms and detailed recollections of the furnishings.[^3] The sources do not say how she was related to the keepers. The principal keeper in 1903 was James L. Weems, Sr.[^4]

William Yeatman Jr. was keeper for 1918 and 1919.[^4] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index lists "Yeatman, William, keeper Drum Point, rescue," and the Commissioner's report for 1919 names him as "keeper, Drum Point Light Station, Md."[^6][^7]

John W. Hansen, the station's last civilian keeper, served from 1942 to 1959 on the Society's roster.[^4] LighthouseFriends says he recalled the light being turned off during military training exercises on the bay.[^3] Its account of his retirement gives 1960, a year later than the roster.[^3][^4]

The Society's roster lists "Beatrice E. Goeshy" as a laborer for 1931, the year William M. Goeshy was principal keeper.[^4] The sources do not say how they were connected.

From 1944 the Coast Guard appears on the roster beside the civilian keepers.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the light was semi-automated in 1960, when Hansen retired, and was then staffed by three Coast Guardsmen working twelve days on and six days off.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The station's logbooks survive from 1883 to 1943 and are kept at the National Archives.[^2] The museum uses them to list a few unusual events.[^2]

On 12 March 1885 a sloop dragged its anchor and hit the lighthouse.[^2] The museum says its crew was apparently drunk, and one of them fell overboard and was rescued.[^2]

On 31 August 1886 the Charleston earthquake shook the tower.[^2] The museum says the tremors slammed doors, rattled the bell machinery and woke the children, and that the keeper had his family out at the light.[^2]

On 1 March 1887 an extremely low tide let the keeper walk completely around the tower.[^2] On 24 February 1895 ice came over the south side of the river, and one floe hit the station and shook it, overturning several chairs.[^2]

LighthouseFriends counts 1933 as the station's worst misfortune.[^3] A severe gale raised the water level, flooded the lower floor of the cottage, swept away the Weather Bureau's instruments, and sank the station's motorboat after a gaff gave way.[^3] Keeper John J. Daley swam ashore and filed a damage report.[^3]

The Bulletin index also lists a rescue at Drum Point under Yeatman's name.[^6] The Bulletin for 1926 reports that on 20 September the keeper, James L. Lewis, helped six people in a disabled boat in the vicinity of the station.[^8][^4]

## After the keepers

The light was changed in the 1890s.[^5] The Board's report for fiscal 1900 records a dark sector inserted in the light on 10 December 1899.[^5] LighthouseFriends says steamboat captains had asked for it, to cover the Sandy Point bar off Solomons Island.[^3] The museum gives the same month.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says the light's characteristic changed again in 1911, to a fixed white light with three red sectors over the shoals near the river's entrance.[^3]

Electricity reached the station in 1944, but the light was often out during the war.[^3] The nomination says the original light was replaced with an automatic light after World War II, and that the original light is on exhibit at the Chesapeake Bay Maritime Museum in St. Michaels.[^1]

The light was decommissioned in 1962.[^2] The museum says the lighthouse fell victim to vandals until it was moved to its present site in 1975.[^2] The nomination says that after its April 1973 nomination, "all twenty-two doors and twelve windows were broken."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the vandalism lasted about a decade and a half.[^3]

LighthouseFriends says the Calvert County Historical Society had hoped to restore the station as early as 1966, but red tape and private land-access problems held it up for almost a decade.[^3] The station was listed on the National Register in 1973.[^3][^1] The nomination says the General Services Administration disposed of the lighthouse after the state could not keep it up, and that an easement written into the deed protects it.[^1]

A Savannah, Georgia company moved the 41-ton tower.[^3] LighthouseFriends says a $25,000 grant from the Maryland Historical Trust went toward the cost of the move, and on 27 March 1975 the tower was barged to its new home at the museum.[^3] The lighthouse was opened to the public on 24 June 1978.[^3] After Anna Weems Ewalt died in 1995, $45,000 from her estate went into an endowment for the lighthouse.[^3]

## Today

The Drum Point Light is open to the public at the Calvert Marine Museum, weather and conditions permitting, all year.[^2] Visitation is included with paid museum admission.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the museum owns the lighthouse and that tours run almost every day.[^3] The tower has not stood on its 1883 site since it was moved in 1975. That site lay on submerged land about one-sixteenth of a nautical mile due south of Drum Point.[^2]
