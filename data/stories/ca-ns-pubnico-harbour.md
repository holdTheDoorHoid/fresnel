Pubnico Harbour light stands on Beach Point, a sand spit on the eastern side of the harbour mouth, on Nova Scotia's south shore between Cape Sable and Yarmouth.[^5][^6][^8] For about eighty years three generations of one family kept it, and LighthouseFriends names them as the Amiros.[^8][^5] By LighthouseFriends' account the first lighthouse was finished in 1853; Rowlett dates the station to 1854.[^5][^6] The light there now is a fibreglass tower of 1984.[^5][^6]

## The place

Pubnico Harbour is a long, deep inlet with the town of Pubnico at its head, and the light marks its mouth.[^5][^8] In 1848 the Nova Scotia lighthouse commissioners considered a light there. The commissioners judged that it would help local trade but do nothing for general navigation in the Bay of Fundy, so none was built then.[^5] In early 1852 Simon d'Entremont and others petitioned for a light at Pubnico Beach, and the petition was read in the House of Assembly. The light was on a list of five to be built that year, but the bids came in too high and the work waited another year.[^5]

The harbour mouth had other marks by the turn of the century. On 1 February 1901 the Department set a whistling buoy in 15 fathoms of water, six miles south-west by west of the harbour light.[^1] In 1902 a tide gauge was set at D'Entremont's wharf at Lower East Pubnico, "about half a mile above the lighthouse".[^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says Maturin Amiro was appointed the first keeper, at £35 a year with a £15 fuel allowance.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the tower was much like the Fort Point light at Liverpool, with a lantern room glazed on three sides.[^5]

The earliest inspection described in these sources is William Condon's visit of 1857, as LighthouseFriends quotes his report. He found the building "entirely too small", and "not more than large enough for a beacon, not to speak of a family of eight to live in it."[^5] The keeper had put up a small kitchen at his own expense. The oil had to be kept in the house, because there was no cellar. At high tide the water came within a few feet of the door, and all the household water had to be brought by boat from nearly a mile away.[^5]

Later works, as LighthouseFriends lists them, were an addition to the dwelling in 1858, a new freshwater tank and an oil store in 1872, and in 1889 a ten-foot addition to the tower with an iron lantern room moved from Peggy's Point and a sixth-order Fresnel lens.[^5] The Department's report for 1909 describes the fixed light that the new lantern replaced as a "white, 7th order dioptric light".[^3] The two sources disagree about the lens order, and the Department's report does not say when that light was installed.

## The keepers

Maturin Amiro kept the light from 1853 until 1893.[^5] He was a carpenter. LighthouseFriends says he kept enlarging the house until it had twenty-two rooms.[^5]

In 1893 his son George took over, and kept the light until 1916.[^5] The Department's keeper table for 1907 prints "Amero, Geo. D Pubnico" against the station, with an appointment date of 6 February 1893.[^4] LighthouseFriends spells the name Amiro. In 1898 George was given a hand-operated foghorn, which he had to sound in answer to the signals of vessels in poor visibility.[^5]

Austin J. Amiro followed, from 1916 to at least 1937.[^5] LighthouseFriends also names Elmer Larkin (at least 1949) and Merle Thomas, the last keeper, who still lived in the dwelling when the station was de-staffed on 7 September 1987.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends says storm waves reached the dwelling, which ruled out a garden or livestock on the narrow spit.[^5] In the winter of 1890 and 1891 the breakwater around the light was damaged. A 150-foot stretch was rebuilt under Keeper Amiro at a cost of $183.[^5] On 19 January 1926 fire destroyed the lantern room. A fixed white light was shown until repairs could be made.[^5]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends dates a new lantern and a fourth-order lens to 1908, after which the light showed an occulting white, six seconds on and four off.[^5] The Department's 1909 report, which covers the fiscal year 1908–09, lists the same change among its improvements, as a light "visible for six seconds and eclipsed for four seconds."[^3]

In 1967 the old tower was removed. A fog-alarm building took its place, with the light on a skeletal tower above it.[^5][^6] In 1984 a freestanding fibreglass tower went up near the end of the spit. Rowlett gives it as 11 m (36 ft) tall and LighthouseFriends as 11.5 m (38 ft).[^5][^6] The light and fog alarm went out of service in 2000; only the light came back in 2005.[^5][^6] The Coast Guard list shows the light as "Flash 1 s; eclipse 2 s", and Rowlett describes it as a red flash every three seconds.[^6][^7]

## Today

Pubnico Harbour light is Canadian Coast Guard property. The grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^5][^6] To reach the light, turn east off Route 3, just north of Charlesville, onto Lighthouse Road. It is a gravel road, 0.3 km long, that ends near the light. No parking is provided, but there is room at the end of the road to turn round, and visitors walk the last stretch out to the light at the tip of the spit.[^5]
