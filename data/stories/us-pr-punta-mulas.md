Punta Mulas, also called the Faro de Vieques, stands on a point near Isabel II, the main town on Vieques.[^1][^8] It is one of the lighthouses the Spanish colonial authorities built across Puerto Rico and its islands at the end of the nineteenth century.[^1] Work began in 1895, and the light began operating on 1 June 1896.[^5][^7]

## The place

The nomination says the island was first occupied by the French, who were "quickly replaced by the Spanish colonial powers in 1647," though it adds that French and English settlers stayed on "without trouble."[^1]

The light was put up to guide ships through a passage of reefs.[^2] The nomination calls it "a very dangerous passage formed by a chain of reefs," and names one reef in particular, "the renowned Caballo Blanco," in the Vieques Sound.[^2] It also says the light "was also of key importance for the sea traffic going through San Juan Passage."[^2]

The sources do not agree on where the light stands. The 1901 Lighthouse Board report puts it on the northerly shore of Vieques, which it also calls Crab Island, and about ten miles to the east of Puerto Rico.[^5] The nomination says it stands on the "easterly side of Isabel II Harbor, westerly shore of Vieques Island," and the HAER data pages also give the western shore.[^2][^9] EnciclopediaPR places it west of the port of Isabel II.[^7] LighthouseFriends places it just east of the town.[^8]

## Building the light

The 1901 report says the light "was begun in 1895 and was finished in 1896 by the Spanish," at a cost of 14,500 pesos. It does not name a designer.[^5] The 1976 nomination calls the light "one of the smaller models," "designated model no. 6 in the Folio showing plans for lighthouses," and gives the builder as "Eng. Jose M. Sanz," one of "the military engineers of the time, Jose Sanz."[^1] A Spanish-language encyclopedia article gives another name, Francisco de Albacete.[^7] The sources do not agree, and this story does not choose between them.

The tower is brick and stone, with a cast-iron stair and a cylindrical lantern.[^2] The nomination says the building was "originally painted light blue with white trimmings."[^2] The 1901 report describes an octagonal tower and gives its height as "27 feet high from its base" (about 8 m). The nomination says the tower "rises 50' including the lantern section" (about 15 m).[^5][^1]

The original lens was a sixth-order fixed red Barbier & Bernard lens, which the nomination gives as having "an 8 mile range."[^2] The 1901 report also calls it a fixed red light of the sixth order, gives a shorter range, and puts the light "about 68 feet above high water" (about 21 m).[^5]

## The keepers

The 1901 report says the islands it covers were "ceded to the United States by the Government of Spain" by a treaty of 10 December 1898. It says that on 1 May 1900 the Board "was directed to take charge of the Porto Rican light-house service."[^3] The report also says the Porto Rican light stations were inspected in January 1901, and that "at that time their condition was good to excellent."[^4]

The USLHS roster lists seven stints at the station. The earliest is Alfredo Vicente, shown as "at least 1901-at least 1915."[^10] Jose P. Castillo served from 1916 to 1917, and Jose F. Carrero is listed from at least 1921.[^10] Cayetano Valle held the station from 1930 to 1932 and again from 1933 to 1934, with Hermogenes Coffie in between, in 1932 and 1933.[^10] Andres Rivera is listed from 1935 to at least 1941.[^10] LighthouseFriends says only a head keeper was assigned, because an assistant was not required for the station's sixth-order lens.[^8] The sources say little about most of these men beyond their names and years.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's 1931 list under the heading "Saving of life and property" includes "Cayetano Valle, keeper, Point Mulas," and dates his rescue to 17 August.[^6]

LighthouseFriends says that in 1931 Valle was commended for rescuing two men in danger off the north coast of Vieques, near the light.[^8]

After Hurricane Georges struck in 1998, the building was restored again, according to the encyclopedia article.[^7]

## After the keepers

In the 1940s the brick roof was rebuilt in reinforced concrete, and the interior was rearranged.[^2] The nomination says that in 1944 "further alterations were introduced," and that "the 1940 pantry became a power plant."[^2] In 1949 the light was "converted into an unattended automatic light and boarded up."[^2]

LighthouseFriends says the light was left unattended from 1949, and that a caretaker began living in the dwelling in the 1960s.[^8] The HAER record says that "Since the 1960's, the lighthouse has been reoccupied."[^9] The nomination adds that "the light characteristics went also through several changes, at least seven, since 1902."[^2]

The building was restored in 1992.[^7][^8] LighthouseFriends says it opened to the public as a museum on 12 October 1992, to mark the 500th anniversary of Columbus's first voyage to America.[^8] The encyclopedia article says the municipal government of Vieques bought the structure and restored it that year.[^7] By 2005, LighthouseFriends says, water damage had led to its closure to the public.[^8] The encyclopedia article says it was reopened in 2015.[^7]

The lighthouse also appeared in the 1986 film *Heartbreak Ridge*, according to LighthouseFriends.[^8]

## Today

The sources disagree about who owns the light. LighthouseFriends says the U.S. Coast Guard owns it.[^8] The HAER data pages name the U.S. Department of Commerce and the U.S. Coast Guard as present owners.[^9] EnciclopediaPR says the municipal government of Vieques bought the structure in 1992.[^7]

The sources also disagree about access. LighthouseFriends says a small museum once occupied the lighthouse, and its visitor notes list the grounds and dwelling as open.[^8] Its writer, who visited in 2009, found the grounds closed.[^8] A tourism page says it is now a small museum.[^11] The 1976 nomination lists its access as "Restricted access."[^2]

None of these sources settles the question for today. Check locally before going, and stay out of any closed building or grounds.
