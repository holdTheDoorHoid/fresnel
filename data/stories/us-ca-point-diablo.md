Point Diablo Light is a small light and fog signal on a steep headland on the north side of the Golden Gate, about halfway between Point Bonita and Lime Point in Marin County.[^2][^7] The Lighthouse Service set it up in 1923 as an unattended station, looked after from the Lime Point light station.[^2][^3][^4]

## The place

The point is a spur of the Marin Headlands that runs about 600 feet (180 m) out into the water.[^2] The 1923 Lighthouse Service Bulletin calls it a point that was "a menace to vessels entering" the bay in fog.[^3]

## Building the light

The 1923 Lighthouse Service Bulletin describes "a new unattended light and fog signal station," placed at Point Diablo in the eighteenth lighthouse district, "approximately halfway between Lime Point and the Heads."[^3] It says the building stands "on a precipitous ridge" and is 50 feet (15 m) above low water.[^3] LighthouseFriends puts the shack about 80 feet (24 m) above the water, and describes a small white shack with a red roof.[^2] The two heights disagree, and the sources do not settle which is right.

The Bulletin describes the light as electric and incandescent, in a 300-millimeter lantern.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the building held two lens lanterns and a 12-inch (30 cm) electric siren.[^2] The Bulletin gives the siren a copper cowl, and says it "has been reported as having been heard a distance of 5 miles." It calls the signal "highly spoken of by mariners."[^3]

Both light and siren were controlled from Lime Point Light Station over a two-wire control line.[^3] Telephones at both Lime Point and Point Diablo let the keepers listen in on the light and siren, and let the two stations talk to each other.[^3]

No keeper of the point itself is named in the sources. The USLHS place record calls the point a "Minor Aid to Navigation" and names no one.[^1] The Bulletin says the station "is visited once a week by the keepers of Lime Point Light Station" for cleaning and oiling the apparatus.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the same, that the Lime Point keepers had to make the trip each week.[^2] The Rowlett directory says keepers from Lime Point first maintained this beacon.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The point was dangerous before the light stood on it. In February 1891 the American ship Elizabeth, of Belfast, Maine, bound for San Francisco, drifted in a gale outside the north head near Point Bonita while tugs tried to tow her. The hawser parted. A lifeboat from the Fort Point station, launched to help, was driven toward "the rocks off Point Diablo." When the boat reached the westerly arm of the point, "it was found to be impossible to weather it."[^6]

When the lifeboat "took a broad sheer, filled with water," Keeper Charles Henry, "who was steering, was washed overboard." The report says that eleven of the twenty-nine people aboard the ship "were saved." Henry's body was recovered in the bay near San Rafael, California, on 22 February, "the day after his death."[^6]

On 11 December 1932 a heavy snowfall brought the fog signals at the entrance to San Francisco Bay into use. The Bulletin says the signals at "Bonita Point, Mile Rocks, Lime Point, Point Diabolo, and Fort Point" ran for a few hours. It says that "It is believed that this is the first time that snow has fallen in the vicinity of San Francisco Bay" heavy enough to need them, and that "No previous entry of this nature is found in the records of these stations."[^5] The Bulletin spells the point "Diabolo."

## Today

In 2007 the Coast Guard was seeking to transfer this light and four other area light stations to the National Park Service, according to the Rowlett directory.[^7] LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard owns the light and that it is being transferred to the Golden Gate National Recreation Area. It also says the grounds and tower are closed.[^2] The Rowlett directory names the Coast Guard as owner and the National Park Service as site manager, and says the site is "accessible only by boat."[^7] Neither source is dated to the present day, so the sources do not establish the transfer's current state.

Solar panels now power the modern beacon, and the light flashes every six seconds, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] LighthouseFriends also says the light is best viewed from the water, though views can be had from the coastal road in the Marin Headlands.[^2] Do not land on the point or climb the closed tower.
