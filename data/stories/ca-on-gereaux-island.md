Gereaux Island light stands on a small rocky island at the south side of the entrance to Byng Inlet, on the north-east shore of Georgian Bay in Ontario's Parry Sound District.[^1][^2] The station is dated to 1870, though a first tower had been put up on the island in 1869; the square wooden tower with its attached dwelling, built in 1880, still stands.[^1][^3] It is now a Coast Guard station reached only by boat, and the tower is closed to visitors.[^4]

## The place

The Magnetawan River reaches Georgian Bay at Byng Inlet. LighthouseFriends says the river's name is an Ojibwa word meaning "swiftly flowing waters", and that it carried timber from the lumber country to Byng Inlet to be milled.[^1] The town was established at the river mouth in 1869 as a lumber town, and at its peak, LighthouseFriends says, it had the second largest sawmill operation in Ontario.[^1]

The channel into the inlet was the hazard the lights were meant for. The Department's description of the range lights, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, says the two lights in range "lead into the mouth of Byng Inlet, with nowhere less than 4 fathoms water, clearing Magantawan Ledges on the south and Burton Bank on the north side."[^1]

## Building the light

In 1869 the sawmill companies at Byng Inlet offered to pay half the cost of "a lighthouse on a small island at the entrance to the inlet" if the government paid the rest. Without waiting for plans or instructions, they built it, and officers of the Department of Marine found it unsafe in high winds. After the tower was improved, the government paid its share, $357.69. The sawmill company ran the light until 1 May 1875, when the Department took over.[^1]

Two years after the 1876 report, the superintendent of lighthouses above Montreal found the first tower in poor condition, its main posts partly rotten, and not worth extensive repairs. The keeper's dwelling, 5.5 by 6.1 m (18 by 20 ft), was too cold in winter because it was built of single boards.[^1]

Parliament voted money for a new light in 1879. P.J. Sylvester built the present square, pyramidal tower with an attached dwelling, and its fixed white light, 14 m (47 ft) above the water, was placed in operation in October 1880.[^1] The federal heritage character statement says the tower was built to designs by the Department of Marine and Fisheries, under Chief Lighthouse Engineer Joseph Tomlinson, and that it replaces an earlier tower built by local lumber interests.[^2]

Staff Commander John G. Boulton of the Royal Navy surveyed Georgian Bay between 1883 and 1893 and suggested a pair of range lights to mark the channel more exactly. Charles L. White won a $895 contract in 1899 to build the two towers.[^1] The range lights were lit shortly after navigation opened in 1900. LighthouseFriends quotes the front light as fixed red, 10 m (34 ft) above the water and visible 8 miles, and the back light as fixed white, 18 m (60 ft) up and visible 13 miles.[^1]

Later works changed the range again. The Department's list of new aids for the year ended 31 March 1912 gives the Byng Inlet work as a "combined dwelling and tower, forming the front light", and a steel skeleton tower for the back light.[^5] LighthouseFriends dates the skeletal back tower to 1936 and says the wooden one had served since 1890. The sources do not reconcile these dates.[^1]

The Department's lights list for the year ended 31 March 1911 records that the island light's five mammoth lamps and reflectors had been replaced by a 4th order lens, with petroleum vapour burned under an incandescent mantle.[^6]

## The keepers

Joseph Lamondin is the first keeper LighthouseFriends names, from 1873 to 1877. The Department's 1876 report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, names "Joseph Lamondet, Keeper", who had been in charge for three years.[^1] The Maganettawan Lumber Company was paid to keep the light from 1877 to 1883. James Milne was keeper for a season, and Lamondin came back in 1884 and kept the light until 1901.[^1]

Louis Lamondin, one of his two sons, was appointed on 20 July 1901, according to the Department's list of keepers in its report for the year ended 31 March 1911.[^7] By 1914 the list shows "L. Lamondin" at Byng Inlet, with three lights.[^8] LighthouseFriends says the father and both sons kept the light for sixty-two years. Charles Lamondin took over from Louis in 1918, and Louis returned in 1925 and stayed until 1946.[^1]

After 1946 Joseph G. Barron kept the light until 1967, and the roster then lists a run of shorter stints. Art Niederhumer, the last resident keeper, served from 1978 to 1989.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The 1876 report, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, called the position "a very exposed one". It noted that "in stormy weather there is no shelter for man or boat at the lighthouse", and said a small room was much needed at the base of the tower to shelter the keeper in stormy weather.[^1]

In December 1924 the Dominion steamer *Greenville*, a lighthouse supply vessel that had served the Parry Sound agency since 1918, was bound for Byng Inlet when she ran aground on Burton Bank in a snowstorm.[^1] The Department's report for the year ended 31 March 1923 lists a Dominion steamer, *C.G.S. Grenville*, in Georgian Bay lighthouse and buoy service. It may be the same vessel, but the spelling differs and the sources do not connect the two.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the captain could not see the range lights, searched for the island light, and found that it was out. Its keeper, who lived alone on the island and had run out of food, had gone to the nearest town for supplies. The steamer was holed and stayed stranded until spring. The keeper was dismissed afterwards.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1936 a skeletal tower replaced the wooden back light of the range.[^1] The island light was then shown from a skeletal tower near the old lighthouse for at least twenty years, but LighthouseFriends says it is now back inside the historic tower.[^1]

A detached keeper's bungalow was added in 1966 to house the extra keeper needed for a new foghorn and radiobeacon.[^1] The lighthouse and range lights were automated in 1989, when Niederhumer's service ended.[^1]

The federal heritage character statement (FHBRO report 90-190) says the light was no longer operational and that the tower served as a daymark. The windows were boarded, and the exterior was clad in metal.[^2] The Heritage Guide, reproducing the federal register, gives the recognition date as 5 September 1991.[^10]

## Today

The Canadian Coast Guard is the custodian of the lighthouse.[^2][^4] The keeper's bungalow now houses a Coast Guard Inshore Rescue Boat station, which patrols between Point au Baril and the mouth of the Bad River.[^1] The Coast Guard's list gives the light as "Oc W 10s", with a focal height of 15.9 m, a nominal range of 13 nautical miles and a tower 15.1 m high.[^3] That list shows the light operating, which differs from the heritage statement's view that it was no longer operational. The site and tower are closed, and the island is reachable only by boat.[^4]
