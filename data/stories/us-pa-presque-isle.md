Presque Isle Light stands on the north shore of the Presque Isle peninsula, which arches out into Lake Erie and makes a natural harbor for the city of Erie, Pennsylvania.[^2] Its brick tower was first lit on 12 July 1873, and it now stands inside Presque Isle State Park. A modern beacon replaced the Fresnel lens in 1962, and the state has owned the light since 1998. A nonprofit now opens the tower to visitors.[^2][^12][^10]

## The place

The name means "almost an island" in French.[^2] In 1753 the French built Fort Presque Isle on the mainland near the harbor entrance.[^2] The first lighthouse at Erie was built in 1818 on a mainland bluff, the Erie Land Lighthouse.[^2] In 1870 plans were begun for a light on the north shore of the peninsula, nearer the lake, which would "better mark that navigational hazard."[^2] The Erie Land light was discontinued in 1880, and it was re-established in 1885 "after mariners protested the decision to extinguish it."[^2]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated money for the new light on June 10, 1872, and construction on the peninsula began that September.[^2] The tower was planned in limestone, but the cost was too high, and "bricks were used instead."[^2] The light was attached to the keeper's brick dwelling, and it was "first exhibited on July 12, 1873." LighthouseFriends gives the cost as $15,000.[^2] Landing the material was hard. LighthouseFriends reports the loss of "a scow carrying 6,000 bricks" in the attempt.[^2]

Until 1882 the station showed "a fixed white light punctuated each minute by a red flash." That year, LighthouseFriends says, the tower received a "revolving fourth-order Fresnel lens."[^2] The shoreline was receding.[^2] The Board's annual report in the 1891 to 1892 volume records that a jetty "which extends 400 feet into the lake" had been built in 1886, and that "the beach has formed to the westward for a length of 290 feet" since.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the Board had noted in 1886 that the shore in front of the light had "receded thirty feet" in the two years before.[^2] A board fence was built in 1894 "to protect the station buildings" from sand.[^2]

The tower was raised in 1896 to lift the light to a focal plane of 73 feet (22 m).[^2][^12] LighthouseFriends says the tower was raised "seventeen feet, four inches," while Rowlett's directory gives the increase as 33 feet (10 m).[^2][^12] Kerosene was adopted as the fuel in 1898, and the tower was painted white a year later.[^2]

## The keepers

Charles T. Waldo was the first keeper, with an annual salary of $520.[^2][^1] On the day of the first lighting, Waldo wrote that "there was one visitor."[^2] His wife Mary gave birth to a daughter at the lighthouse in 1876, LighthouseFriends says, and the child was "the first child to be born on Presque Isle."[^2] The road to the peninsula was not finished until 1927. Until then the keepers walked a 1.5-mile pathway to a boathouse on Misery Bay, and then rowed across the bay to reach a school or a store.[^2]

The roster continues with Orrin J. McAllister, George E. Town, Clark McCole, Lewis Van Natta, Louis Walrose and Thomas L. Wilkins, through 1901.[^1] Andrew W. Shaw, Jr., became keeper in 1901 and held the post until 1927.[^1][^2] The Bulletin's report of 1916 calls him "Andrew Shaw, jr.," and LighthouseFriends says he "was recognized multiple times by the Lighthouse Service for saving life and property."[^5][^2]

Frank S. Huntington followed Shaw from 1927 to 1933 on the roster.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Shaw "abruptly retired" after the road opened, because too many visitors were coming to the lighthouse.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's account of 13 November gives the date without a year.[^5] It appears in the January 1916 issue, and it says Shaw "used all possible means to obtain assistance for the tug," the *Henry E. Gillen*, which had stranded on a bar near the station.[^5] LighthouseFriends places the same stranding in 1916.[^2]

On 8 November, the Bulletin reports, Shaw "rendered valuable service in preventing the spreading of a fire," which was burning near the station and in protecting government property.[^6] The Bulletin gives only the day and month. LighthouseFriends dates Shaw's firefighting near the station to 1917.[^2] In July, the Bulletin says, Shaw furnished food and shelter to the crew of a "loop yacht" that had stranded near the light; the Bulletin's 1917 volume places this in July 1917.[^7] LighthouseFriends puts a yacht stranding near the station two years after the tug incident, and says Shaw gave food, shelter and clothing to its three passengers.[^2]

In 1925 a fire burned on the peninsula for several days.[^8] The Bulletin says that Shaw and Robert Allen, "keeper of Presque Isle Pierhead Lighthouse," both "rendered efficient service" in extinguishing it.[^8] Allen's pierhead light was a separate station with its own keeper.

On January 8, 1928, Frank Huntington, his wife and son "rescued two boys who had fallen through the ice near the station," according to LighthouseFriends, which quotes Huntington's own report.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1924 to 1929 lists a rescue by "Huntington, Frank, keeper Presque Isle," with the account at page 234.[^9]

## After the keepers

In 1921 the Commissioner of Lighthouses proposed to extend the power line to the Presque Isle Light Station, which "will permit a great increase in intensity of the flashlight."[^4] Electricity reached the lighthouse in 1924, LighthouseFriends says, and an oil-engine generator was installed in case of a power failure.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says that in 1933 the lighthouse was consolidated with the Presque Isle North Pierhead Lighthouse, and that "the position of keeper at Presque Isle Lighthouse was discontinued." Huntington was "transferred to the pierhead station as first assistant."[^2] The same account also says he "served until 1944," which conflicts with the 1933 change.[^2] A modern beacon replaced the lens in 1962, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The National Park Service's NPGallery record for the station is dated 4 August 1983.[^11]

The state took over the property in 1998. LighthouseFriends says the light was "officially transferred to the Pennsylvania Department of Conservation and Natural Resources," and Rowlett gives the same year for the transfer to state ownership.[^2][^12] In 2006 the tower was opened for two days during the Discover Presque Isle Weekend, and "More than 750 people paid $2 to make the climb."[^2] A Historic Structures Report was published in June 2007.[^2] In 2014 a rule requiring the park manager to live in the park was changed, and the light was "leased to the non-profit Presque Isle Light Station Board," which opened the station to the public in 2015.[^2] LighthouseFriends says "30,000 people toured the keeper's dwelling" in the first full season, 2016.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse stands in Presque Isle State Park, on the north shore of the peninsula.[^2] LighthouseFriends says it is owned by the Pennsylvania Department of Conservation and Natural Resources and that the dwelling and tower are open.[^2] The nonprofit's hours page gives the tower as "Open Fri/Sat/Sun/Mon from 10 am - 5 pm" from Labor Day to 28 September, with a last climb at 4:40 p.m. From 2 to 26 October the same days are open from 10 a.m. to 3 p.m., and the station is "Closed for the season after October 27." The page says all hours are weather permitting, so check before you go.[^10]
