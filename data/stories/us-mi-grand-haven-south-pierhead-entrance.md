The Grand Haven South Pierhead Entrance Light is the outer of two red lights at the end of the south pier, where the Grand River meets Lake Michigan. The outer light is a red fog-signal house built in 1875, with a lantern on its gable end. The inner light is a cast-iron tower on the same pier, which Lighthouse Friends places nearly 600 feet (183 m) back from the end. The two were set up as a range, so that a ship lined them up on the way in. The Park Service inventory dates the first lighting of both to 1905.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

Grand Haven sits where the Grand River enters Lake Michigan. The Conservancy's history says the port had its first lighthouse in 1839, on the beach.[^4] Vessels coming in made the entrance between the two piers. In a 1879 wreck narrative, the Life-Saving Service describes that entrance as "about 400 feet wide" (about 122 m).[^5]

The pier light came later. The Detroit and Milwaukee Railroad kept a light of its own on a pier for its ferries. After the federal government extended the pier between 1867 and 1870, the light moved to the new pierhead, but it was later discontinued because it could not be reached in heavy gales.[^1] The Lighthouse Board asked $8,000 in 1870 for a wooden tower, a bell and 1,200 feet (366 m) of elevated walkway to the pier. Lighthouse Friends says these went in during 1871.[^1] The USLHS record gives the range's year of establishment as 1871.[^6]

## Building the light

The first light was a stone dwelling and tower near the river mouth, begun in 1838 by contractors from Milwaukee.[^1] In 1838 Lieutenant James T. Homans reported that inferior materials were being used. Stephen Pleasonton, the overseer of the country's lighthouses, sent Trueman H. Lyon to examine the work, and Lyon found Homans' report incorrect. Leading citizens had written in October 1838 that the materials were of good quality.[^1] The first keeper, Nehemiah Merritt, was hired in August 1839.[^1]

A replacement light was built on the bluff above the old site. Work began in 1854, and the light was placed in operation the following year.[^1]

The pier lights were then built out in stages. The Lighthouse Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1894 records a conduit on the south pier, begun that June, "for running in and out the lantern shown from its outer end."[^7] The next year's report says the front range light was moved 278 feet (85 m) nearer the outer end of the south pier on 6 July 1894.[^8] The pierhead light moved about 500 feet (152 m) to the outer end of the pier and was re-established on 4 May 1895, "without change of characteristic."[^9]

The inner light's cast-iron tower is the type that Heap's 1889 handbook describes as the practice of the time: once a pier was finished, a separate foundation was built and a conical cast-iron tower set on it.[^10] That is general period practice, not a record of this tower's builder, which none of the sources cited here names. The National Park Service inventory dates the inner light's first lighting to 1905, gives its tower as cast iron and conical, 51 feet (15.5 m) high, with a focal plane of 52 feet (15.8 m).[^2]

The lens moved in the same year. The Conservancy says a 52-foot (16 m) steel tower was placed on the end of the south pier in the fall of 1904, and that on 12 January 1905 the Fresnel lens was moved from the bluff light to it.[^4] The Park Service's figures for that tower (cast iron, 51 feet) differ from the Conservancy's on both points.[^2] The Conservancy adds that the ship captains had not been told of the switch, and that the captains of the Crosby and Goodrich steamers that came in that night were totally confused.[^4]

The towers moved again, and the sources do not agree on when or which structure moved. Lighthouse Friends says that over the year after April 1906 the cylindrical tower was moved nearly 600 feet (183 m) shoreward onto a new concrete foundation, while the fog-signal house was moved out to the pierhead and given a lantern on its outer gable.[^1] The Conservancy dates the move of the tower to 1907.[^4] The Park Service inventory says the outer tower was moved in 1905, and that the inner tower has not been moved.[^2] This is the least certain stretch of the station's history.

## The keepers

The USLHS roster for the bluff light starts with Jeremiah O. Balch in 1839, and then lists Nehemiah Merritt as head keeper from 1839 to 1847.[^11] Lighthouse Friends says Merritt was hired as the light's first keeper in August 1839.[^1] Harry Miller followed as head keeper at the bluff light from 1861 to 1875.[^11]

A woman also served at the pier lights. The pierhead roster lists Mary Miller as first assistant keeper from 1872 to 1875.[^6] Lighthouse Friends lists a "Mrs. Harry Miller" in the same post for the same years.[^1] Neither source says outright that the two names belong to one woman, and the roster does not say how she was related to the keeper.

Frank Fraga's career shows how a pier station's staff rose through the ranks. The roster has him as second assistant from 1886 to 1892, first assistant to 1900, and head keeper from 1900 to 1911.[^6] Joseph Kilgore was head keeper from 1911 to 1920.[^6] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' keeper list for that period names Kilgore as keeper of the Grand Haven Pierhead Range Light Station.[^12]

Charles Bavry was head keeper from 1920 to 1926.[^6] Rolla J. Paris was first assistant from 1918, and head keeper from 1947.[^6] Carl K. Nafus was second assistant in the early 1920s.[^6] Those three names are the crew the Commissioner listed in 1922, when the station was named in a rescue record (see below).[^13]

The roster lists 24 names under the Coast Guard heading, with no rank given, for the years from about 1959 onward. Its last entries, from 1969, include Michael F. Wickman as head keeper.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The fog signal of 1895 was a steam siren. The Conservancy says that in 1895 the fog alarm at Grand Haven "blasted in the key of F for 5 seconds at intervals of 35 seconds," with steam raised by a coal-fired boiler on the site.[^4] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1894 says the pierhead's first-class steam sirens ran some 435 hours that year.[^14]

The first light, on the beach, was lost in a storm in 1852. The Conservancy says a late-fall storm took out the seawall on 6 December, and that the tower and cottage collapsed on 17 December, leaving the port without a light for about three years.[^4] Lighthouse Friends gives the same month but puts the wall's failure on the night of 17 December, with the tower falling later that day.[^1]

On 9 December 1886 a gale drove the pierhead beacon twelve feet (3.7 m) backward and crushed the elevated walk behind it. The outer end of the crib settled below lake level, and the fog-signal house was slightly damaged, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1]

On 25 January 1916 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reports that Joseph Kilgore, keeper of the Grand Haven Pierhead Range light, rescued two men of the Coast Guard who were in danger of being carried out into Lake Michigan by the ice.[^15] In 1922 the Commissioner's annual report lists the station for rescuing two young men whose canoe capsized near it.[^13]

On 14 September 1961, during the remnants of Hurricane Carla, two young men went out on the pier to watch waves "stirred up by the remnants of Hurricane Carla." One was swept off. Coast Guard crewman David E. Sparks, on duty at the light, threw a life ring and went into the water after him. Lighthouse Friends says the waves defeated the rescue, and Sparks hung on to the ring until Coast Guard men reached him. In 1962 the Coast Guard awarded him the gold lifesaving medal for that day.[^1]

## After the keepers

The bluff light's tower was removed in 1910, and its keeper's house was enlarged for the pier keepers.[^1] The house was sold at auction in 1956 and converted to apartments.[^1][^4]

The fog signals changed several times. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1918 lists the rear pierhead range light as changed from oil to electric incandescent.[^16] In 1921 the Commissioner's report called the air-siren fog signal "the subject of constant complaint from the vessel interests using this port" and asked $12,700 for improvements.[^17] The Conservancy says that in 1922 the end of the south pier was rebuilt with a new concrete head, and that the 1875 fog-signal house received a diaphone horn.[^4]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletins also recorded fog hours at the station. The 1930 Bulletin lists the Grand Haven Pierhead Light Station at 1,531 hours of fog for fiscal 1929.[^18] The 1931 Bulletin records 1,554 hours of fog at the Grand Haven Light Station "during 12 months," and notes that earlier reports had covered fewer months.[^19]

The National Park Service inventory records both lights as automated in 1969.[^2] In 2009 the Coast Guard declared the pierhead lights excess and offered them under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act.[^1] Spectrum News says the city took possession of the structures in 2009.[^3] The City of Grand Haven received a deed to the two lights in December 2012, according to Lighthouse Friends, and a volunteer conservancy was formed to care for them.[^1] The council approved $259,577 to paint the structures, and Lighthouse Friends says the painting was carried out in late spring and early summer of 2013.[^1]

The opening followed a 17-year restoration effort by the Conservancy. In June 2026 the outer light opened to the public for interior tours for the first time in its history, and more than 1,000 people visited on the first day.[^3]

## Today

The Park Service inventory, as archived in 2007, gave the Coast Guard as owner and manager, and listed both lights as active aids to navigation.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the City of Grand Haven owns the lights.[^1] The Conservancy's home page, which is undated, says needed repairs to the west wall of the fog-horn house will be handled by the Coast Guard.[^20]

The 2026 tours of the outer light were scheduled through Labor Day, on Tuesdays, Thursdays, Saturdays and Sundays, from 10 a.m. to 5 p.m. They were free, and the climb to the lantern room cost $5 and was optional. The lighthouse opens only when the pier is dry and waves are not crashing over it; the source says to look for an "OPEN" sign near the pier entrance gate.[^3] That schedule ended with Labor Day 2026, so check before going.

The bluff light's keeper's house has been private apartments since 1956.[^4] Lighthouse Friends' visitor note, which does not mention the 2026 tours, says the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^1] The sources describe tours of the pier lights only, and none of them describes the inner light as open to visitors.[^4][^3]
