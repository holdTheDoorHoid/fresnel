Seguin Island sits about two and a half miles off the mouth of the Kennebec River in Maine, and its light station is the second oldest on the Maine coast.[^1][^2] The granite tower is only 53 feet (16 m) tall, but it stands on the island's summit, so the first-order Fresnel lens in its lantern burns some 180 feet (55 m) above the sea by the National Register's figure; LighthouseFriends gives 186 feet.[^2][^3] It is also known for fog, which the Lighthouse Service once counted at 2,734 hours in a single year.[^4]

## The place

The name is said by some to come from an Indian word meaning, roughly, "place where the sea vomits"; others derive it from a word for a bump.[^5] The island lies about two and a half miles off the mouth of the Kennebec River, and a light there was meant to serve both the coast and the river entrance.[^3] In June 1786 fifty-five local merchants petitioned the General Court of Massachusetts for one, arguing that "if there was a Light upon this Island many Vessels would be saved from Shipwreck."[^6] President Washington approved a light station in May 1793.[^6] Massachusetts ceded part of the island to the federal government in 1794; the National Register nomination says the remainder followed in 1797.[^2]

Fog is the island's signature. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin of 1912 gave 2,734 hours of fog at Seguin in 1907, about 30 percent of the year, as the highest record at any station it counted.[^4] The National Register nomination repeats the figure and calls Seguin the foggiest light station in the United States.[^2] Another history gives the figure as 2,374 hours, which looks like a transposition.[^6]

## Building the light

The first tower was to be an octagonal wooden structure on a stone foundation, advertised for bids in the local newspapers in April 1795.[^6] A newspaper notice in late October 1796 announced the buildings finished under the supervision of Henry Dearborn.[^6] One account says Dearborn was the only bidder.[^3] The sources disagree on the dates and the cost. George Putnam's 1917 history says the first lighthouse was built in 1795 for $6,300; the National Register nomination says construction began in 1790 and the wooden tower was completed in 1797 at $6,300.[^7][^2] Putnam adds that the keeper's salary was fixed at $200 and that the Commissioner of the Revenue wrote to the Treasury Secretary about it in 1797.[^8]

The wooden tower did not last. It was rebuilt in stone in 1819, according to one history, at about 20 feet (6 m) to the base of the lantern; the National Register nomination dates the stone tower to 1820 and gives its cost as $2,500.[^6][^2] Another history says that Congress allocated the $2,500 for a 21-foot tower and a stone dwelling, and that fourteen lamps with reflectors made a fixed white light.[^3] A fog bell was added in 1837.[^6][^3]

By 1842 the keeper, Nathaniel S. Todd, was describing a station in poor repair. In a report quoted by Kraig Anderson's LighthouseFriends, he said the stone tower was "very leaky," that the well ran dry for two months of the summer, and that he had to bring water for the household from the mainland.[^3] The Lighthouse Board decided in 1852 that a new tower was needed.[^3] Congress appropriated $35,000 in 1856, and the present granite tower and brick keeper's duplex were finished in 1857, with a first-order Fresnel lens made by Henry-Lepaute.[^3][^2] The nomination lists the tower itself at $26,000.[^2] One history says the lens was nine feet high, large enough for a keeper to step inside to light the lamp.[^6]

Fog signals followed. A new fog bell with striking machinery arrived in 1854.[^6] A 10-inch steam whistle was in place by 1873, sounding one eight-second blast every minute.[^6][^3] The brick fog-signal building that stands today dates to 1889 according to the US Lighthouse Society's archive and LighthouseFriends; the National Register nomination lists a fog signal building under 1857, which conflicts with them.[^1][^3][^2] The Light-House Board's report on fog signals described the whistle as sitting on the roof of that building, about 250 feet (76 m) south of the tower, and noted that the tower, dwelling and rocks north of it muffled the sound in that direction. It called Seguin "a guide to the Kennebec River and a most important station."[^9] In the Board's fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 the whistles ran for some 1,405 hours.[^10]

A brick oil house was added about 1891 (LighthouseFriends) or 1892 (the archive); the Board's report covering those years describes a new brick oil house 11 feet by 16 feet, while the National Register nomination lists an oil house of the same size under 1857.[^3][^1][^11][^2] In the Board's fiscal year ended 30 June 1895 a railroad was built from the boathouse at the landing to the signal house, with a car and a hoisting engine for coal and supplies.[^12]

## The keepers

The first keeper, Major John Polereczky, was appointed in March 1796 at $200 a year.[^6] He had served with French troops in the American Revolution, and in November 1796 he asked for a raise to $300, writing that he had "but one Cow here for my family."[^6] The request was denied.[^6] The archive lists him as head keeper from 1796 to 1804, with Christopher Pushard as assistant.[^1] A 1932 Lighthouse Service Bulletin article on papers from Washington's administration mentions an extra allowance to the Seguin keeper for clearing the adjacent land.[^13]

Jonathan Delano followed in 1804. In 1809 he wrote that the dwelling leaked badly.[^6] In 1825 Collector Isaac Ilsley reported, on the strength of an affidavit, that Delano had fraudulently disposed of lamp wicks; LighthouseFriends reads the episode as a trade of wicks for tinware. John Salter took over on 2 March 1825.[^3] Salter's tenure ended in 1839. In his 1842 report Todd said he had paid Salter, "the late keeper," $540 for the buildings and improvements Salter had made over fifteen years, and that his own family numbered seven.[^3] Thomas Day, keeper from 1875 to 1886, had been captain of a Black Ball liner before the lights and bought a farm in Bowdoinham when he retired.[^1][^6] In 1880 he wrote to the inspector that he and his two assistants had been asked for $79 for Republican campaign expenses.[^3]

The archive's roster shows women among the assistants. Rachel Spinney served as second assistant in 1865-66, Jane Morrill as first assistant in 1867-68, and Louisa N. Crane as second assistant in 1871-72.[^1] The roster gives only their names and years.

Herbert L. Spinney, a native of nearby Georgetown, was assistant from 1893 and head keeper from 1903 to 1907.[^3][^6] He collected and mounted birds, many of them found dead after striking the lantern glass.[^6][^3] A visitor trade grew up around his collection, and he began charging a dime to keep the crowds manageable.[^6] His journal entry for 3 September 1899 recorded a night that was "very dark, not a star to be seen," with air "so impregnated with smoke as to make my throat smart in breathing," and birds landing on the lantern glass.[^6] He counted either 275 dead birds on one morning (New England Lighthouses) or 500 (LighthouseFriends).[^6][^3] In November 1904 the Daily Kennebec Journal listed H. L. Spinney of Seguin Light Station among the members to give illustrated lectures at a coming meeting, his titled "Birds from a Lighthouse." The notice, which calls him captain and says he had kept the light fifteen years, does not give his first name, and the roster puts his time on the island at eleven years by then.[^14]

Elson L. Small kept the light from 1926 to 1930; his wife Connie later wrote a book about life there.[^1][^6] She recalled sing-alongs with visiting work crews.[^6] Frank Bracey, an assistant and later head keeper who had been a crewman on the Portland Lightship, claimed to have seen gulls knocked from the air by the fog horn, which was once heard at Bath, 14 miles away.[^6] Seguin became a men-only station in 1963, and the last keeper whose family lived there was George F. Johns.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

No named wreck or rescue at Seguin appears in the sources used for this story. What they record is fog, the fog signals, and accidents on the tramway. The tramway that carried supplies up from the landing ran away in 1937, killing the keeper's dog.[^3] In September 1949 Joyce Irvine, wife of Coast Guardsman Daniel Irvine, was riding the car with their two small children when it broke loose near the top of the hill. Both children came through on the grass; she stayed aboard until the car crashed at the bottom, and was badly hurt, with a broken thigh bone according to LighthouseFriends. The two accounts differ on which parent got which child out, and both say the family dog died. Passengers were no longer allowed to ride the tramway.[^3][^6]

In 1936 the Bureau of Lighthouses granted Archie Lane of Northeast Harbor a license to dig on the island for pirate treasure that local folklore said was buried there. The hunt ended unsuccessfully that November.[^2]

## After the keepers

Electricity reached the island in 1955.[^3] The Coast Guard automated the light in 1985, and the last Coast Guard keeper on the roster is Edward T. Brown.[^1][^6] Local people formed the Friends of Seguin Island; the Coast Guard leased the station to them in 1989, and in 1998 they received ownership under the Maine Lights Program.[^3] The same year the Coast Guard announced a plan to replace the Fresnel lens with a plastic solar-powered optic. The Friends gathered more than 7,200 signatures, and in March 2000 Senator Olympia Snowe announced that the lens would stay in operation.[^3] In April 2019 the Coast Guard cut the island's power after judging the submarine cable unsafe; a generator flown in by helicopter ran the station that season, and the Friends installed a solar system in 2020.[^3]

## Today

The Friends of Seguin Island Light Station own the island, and the light is still an active aid to navigation with its first-order Fresnel lens.[^1][^3] Visitors reach the island by boat, and the Friends staff it with volunteer keepers from Memorial Day through Columbus Day.[^3][^15] According to LighthouseFriends, the grounds are open, and the tower and the museum in the keeper's duplex are open from Memorial Day through Labor Day.[^3] Overnight stays in the guest quarters are limited to Friends members.[^16]
