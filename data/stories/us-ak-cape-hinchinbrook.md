Cape Hinchinbrook is the southwest corner of Hinchinbrook Island, on the east side of the entrance to Prince William Sound, Alaska. A light and fog signal went up there in 1909 and 1910 after a steamer ran aground nearby, and the Art Deco concrete station that replaced it in 1934 still stands, is still owned by the Coast Guard, and still marks the way in from the Gulf of Alaska.[^1]

## The place

The station is on the east side of the entrance to Prince William Sound; the nomination describes the light as marking that entrance and the turn toward Valdez for ships crossing the Gulf of Alaska to Cordova and Valdez.[^1] Those two ports had grown quickly: the National Register nomination ties the light to the Fairbanks strike of 1902, the Copper River mines, and the railroad out of Cordova, and says the cape had been the scene of some of Alaska's worst wrecks, including the loss of a fleet of Russian bidarkas in 1799 and the steamer *Aloha* on the southeast side of the island in 1901.[^1] Cape Hinchinbrook was not on the list of sites the Light-House Board put to Congress in 1901; shipping, in the nomination's words, soon created the need.[^1]

The wreck that settled the question was the steamer *Oregon* in 1906. Lighthouse Friends dates it to the night of 13 September and says the ship was about three miles off course in heavy fog when it ran onto rocks fifty yards off the island, with fifty passengers aboard.[^2] The nomination says only that she grounded three miles from the cape and that the lighthouse tender *Columbine* happened to be nearby and took off the passengers and crew.[^1] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1930, recalling the rescue, gives "126 passengers and crew."[^3] Sources therefore disagree on how many people were aboard, and this account does not choose.

## Building the light

Congress had authorised the station on 20 June 1906, and on 27 November 1906 President Theodore Roosevelt signed Executive Order 525 setting aside the lighthouse reserve.[^1][^2] The estimate was $125,000, but only $25,000 was appropriated at first, and full funding did not arrive until 1908.[^1]

Construction began in April 1909 under A. B. Lewis of the Standard Building Company of Seattle, with a crew the nomination puts at forty men.[^1] They cleared the site and built a tramway and a supply landing. Bad weather made the work hard: one supply scow broke loose and drifted west to Montague Island before it was recovered.[^1] By the end of September work stopped, with only the tram and part of the shell of the fog-signal building done.[^1] A temporary fixed light was shown through the winter, and the nomination says a keeper stayed behind "under strained conditions"; Lighthouse Friends adds that he had his wife with him.[^1][^2] Neither source names them.

The contractors returned in June 1910 and finished the concrete, the oil house, the shop and the tram house. The light was first lit on 15 November 1910, and the total cost was $100,323, well under the original estimate.[^1] The first building was a two-storey octagonal concrete block that combined the fog signal, the light and the keepers' quarters, which the nomination describes as unusual for the time because most stations had separate dwellings. It had rooms for four men.[^1] The lens was a rotating third-order Fresnel lens made in Paris by Barbier, Bénard & Turenne, and the fog signal was a pair of compressed-air sirens driven by engines.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says the lens made a white flash every five seconds.[^2]

Mariners complained about the air siren, and in 1918 the Commissioner of Lighthouses reported that it was "not effective" and asked for a diaphone.[^7] The Bulletin for 1922 records the change from air siren to air diaphone;[^8] the nomination and Lighthouse Friends give 1923.[^1][^2] The nomination also records a Navy radio direction-finding outpost, begun in 1923, which it calls the earliest station with electronic radio equipment; the equipment was built and operated by the Navy.[^1]

Earthquakes in 1927 and 1928 broke away sections of the cliff in front of the building.[^1] By 1931 the Bulletin was calling the situation an emergency: "Conditions at Cape Hinchinbrook constitute an emergency, and construction of a new station must begin immediately."[^9] Lighthouse Friends says the old building had been cracked by the quakes and its concrete judged poor, so it was replaced rather than moved.[^2] Work began in May 1931; funds ran out the next year, and after a further appropriation it resumed in May 1933, finishing in spring 1934 at a total cost of $91,793.[^2]

The new building was designed in the Lighthouse Service's Sixteenth District office in Ketchikan; the nomination credits Dwight A. Chase and Edward W. Laird, and quotes Laird, writing in 1991, claiming the engineering as his own.[^1] It was the first of six Art Deco and Art Moderne stations in Alaska in the 1930s, the most ornate of them, and the only one to put the living quarters inside the main building. It was also the only one given extra diagonal reinforcing bars against earthquakes.[^1] The old lantern was moved to the new tower; the old building was demolished and, in the nomination's words, portions dumped over the cliff, though its foundation remains, partly eaten by the eroding bluff.[^1] A radio beacon came with the new station.[^1]

## The keepers

The first station's quarters were meant for single men, and the nomination notes that, unlike most stations, Cape Hinchinbrook was set up for men without families.[^1] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists the first principal keeper as Harry F. McBride (1910-1911), followed by Sigvart G. Olsen (1911-1918), William A. Shoemaker (1918-1921) and John W. Barnes (1921-1922).[^4] The archive's assistants for the first years include Charles E. Louks (1910-1913) as first assistant and Oscar H. Sheppard (1910-1911) as second.[^4] Lighthouse Friends lists Shoemaker as returning as head keeper in 1922-1925 and 1927-1929, which the archive's roster does not show; the two lists differ on several dates.[^2][^4] Edward C. Hope appears as an assistant in 1940 and as principal keeper from 1941; the archive then lists Coast Guard crews from 1959 to 1971.[^4]

One story comes from Ralph Tinkham, the district superintendent, as summarised by Lighthouse Friends from a *Keeper's Log* article of 2001 (it gives no year). On a surprise visit he found all three keepers asleep, the pantry bare apart from a very long shelf of pancake flour, and the men, after a quarrel over a missing ring, not on speaking terms for six months. They still did their work and shared what they hunted and fished. The stalemate ended when the tender *Cedar* brought supplies and a relief keeper.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 20 May 1912 the lighthouse tender *Armeria*, the first tender permanently assigned to the new Sixteenth Lighthouse District, struck an uncharted rock while delivering supplies to the station.[^1][^2] The Valdez *Daily Prospector* reported that she was "driven by heavy swells upon an uncharted rock" with a hole punched in her hull, and that her captain ran her to the beach, where she lay full of water; all aboard got away in small boats and were picked up by the steamer *Sampson*.[^5] The nomination instead says the station's keepers launched their dinghies and rescued the crew.[^1] Lighthouse Friends agrees with the newspaper that the *Admiral Sampson* took the crew from the boats.[^2] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1912 also reported the loss.[^6]

On 27 March 1964, Lighthouse Friends says, a man at the station radioed that tremors were coming every 10 to 15 minutes, the building was shaking badly and he had to leave it again.[^2]

## After the keepers

Under the Coast Guard, which took over the lighthouses in 1939, Lighthouse Friends says duty became a one-year tour with staggered arrivals.[^2] Coast Guardsman Tim Escher arrived in 1968 and later shared photographs of the station; Lighthouse Friends says the crew stood radio watch round the clock and that supplies came ashore by crane and cargo net.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the old third-order lens was replaced in 1967 by a two-headed aerobeacon, while the nomination says the lens stayed in use until 1971, when a DCB-224 beacon replaced it.[^2][^1] Helicopter-pad work for automation began in 1972, and the station was automated in 1974.[^2][^1] The last entries of the visitors' register, as quoted there, record the crew's distress at having to put down the station's two dogs before the men left.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the station and the light remains in service; the nomination, written in the early 2000s, says it still serves an active role in navigation, with a helicopter pad and radio masts around the building.[^1] It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2005.[^2] Lighthouse Friends advises that it is best seen from a boat or plane, with Cordova the nearest town offering either, and that the grounds and tower are closed.[^2] The original third-order lens was transferred to the State of Alaska in 1975 on condition that it be displayed, and has been at the Valdez Museum since 1976.[^1][^2]
