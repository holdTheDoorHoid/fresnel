Colchester Reef Light was a wooden lighthouse on a reef in Lake Champlain, built in 1871. In 1952 it was taken down, carried ashore and rebuilt on the grounds of the Shelburne Museum in Vermont, where it still stands.[^3][^4]

## The place

The reef lies about a mile out from Colchester Point, roughly seven miles northwest of Burlington.[^2] The light stood on a rock called Middle Bunch, which had seven feet (2.1 m) of water over it at low water.[^2] In 1869 the Lighthouse Board recommended a light, and New England Lighthouses quotes the Board's report: "It is recommended that an appropriation be made for the erection of a light-house on Colchester Reef, South Hero Island, or in the vicinity."[^5] The museum says the light was built to mark three reefs between Vermont and New York.[^3]

## Building the light

The Lighthouse Service ran a national design contest in 1869. Albert R. Dow, a graduate engineer from the University of Vermont, won it.[^2][^5] New England Lighthouses gives the cost as $20,000.[^5] Luther Whitney built it, and the USLHS archive lists him as the contractor.[^1][^2]

Workers first built a wooden crib in Burlington and hauled it out to the reef, where it was sunk and then filled with concrete and stone.[^2] At the end of the 1870 season two courses of cut stone sat on top of it, and the spring ice tore some of that away. More stone had to be procured, and LighthouseFriends gives the final cost as $24,500, with Congress providing $4,500 on top of the $20,000 appropriated in 1870.[^2] The foundation pier was finished by the end of June 1871, and the lighthouse followed a few months later.[^2]

The light had a sixth-order Fresnel lens, installed in 1871.[^1] The two histories describe the tower differently. LighthouseFriends describes a Second Empire style dwelling with an octagonal tower on a mansard roof, and the USLHS archive records a square tower on the dwelling.[^1][^2] The height is also given two ways: the USLHS archive gives 35 feet (10.7 m) above mean high water, and LighthouseFriends gives a focal plane of 51 feet (15.5 m).[^1][^2]

## The keepers

Heman L. Malaney was the first keeper, from 1871 to 1882.[^1] New England Lighthouses calls him the first of nine keepers at the reef.[^5] LighthouseFriends says five assistants served under him in those years.[^2] When he retired in 1882, his assistant, Walter M. Button, took over and moved his family into the station.[^2] To help feed the family, Button kept a garden and a cow on Sunset Island, about half a mile away.[^5]

On 29 January 1888 Button's wife, Harriet, went into labor, and Button rang the fog bell as the signal for the doctor on shore.[^2] The doctor and his assistant decided to risk crossing the ice on foot. When they were about halfway to their patient, a large floe cracked clear of the main section, leaving the two men stranded. They reached South Hero Island, four miles from where they had started.[^2] Myrtle Edna Button was born that night, without the doctor.[^2][^5]

In 1909 August Lorenz became keeper.[^1] LighthouseFriends says he had struck up a correspondence with William H. Howard, the keeper at the reef, who longed for the city. The two decided to switch jobs and met halfway between to trade their tools.[^2] Lorenz rowed across the lake for supplies.[^2] One late autumn, spray froze on his back until he was frozen fast to his dory, and he chipped himself free with a knife.[^2] One night the pressure of ice split the southwest corner of the lighthouse.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says Lorenz kept the light until 1931. The roster gives 1909 to 1930.[^1][^2]

The last keeper is also disputed. LighthouseFriends names Joseph Aubin, who turned off the light in August 1933.[^2] The roster ends with George Doig (Doige) in 1931 to 1933 and does not list Aubin for this station.[^1] New England Lighthouses gives Aubin as keeper from 1931 to 1933.[^5]

The number of keepers is also disputed. The museum says the lighthouse was home to "eleven successive lighthouse keepers and their families."[^3] New England Lighthouses says nine.[^5] The roster lists nine different principal keepers by name.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In mid-January 1873 a storm struck the lighthouse so hard that the lens was thrown from its pedestal, and Keeper Malaney feared for his safety.[^2] New England Lighthouses says the storm damaged the lens.[^5]

Ice threatened the foundation that winter.[^2] Congress provided $5,000 for riprap in 1874, and it was placed in 1875. Another 300 tons went on in 1895.[^2]

The foundation needed more work in the 1920s.[^6][^7] The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1920 said, "Contract has been let and riprap is now being placed at Colchester Reef Light Station."[^6] The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1920 reported the placing complete.[^8] The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1922 listed work "to protect and strengthen the foundations from further damage at Colchester Reef."[^7] The 1923 Bulletin reported the work at the reef completed.[^9]

A photograph from April 1923, credited to the U.S. Coast Guard and reproduced by New England Lighthouses, shows ice damage.[^5]

For the fiscal year 1929, the Bulletin reports the least fog at any station with a fog signal as 25 hours, recorded at Colchester Reef.[^10]

In June 1961 lightning struck the lighthouse and caused only minor damage.[^2]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says Aubin closed the station in August 1933, after a battery-powered beacon was fixed to the outside of the lighthouse.[^2] The USLHS archive gives 1933 as the year the light was discontinued.[^1]

The empty lighthouse fell into disrepair.[^5] In June 1952 it was auctioned, on condition that it be removed from the pier within thirty days.[^2] A young couple bid $50, planning to take the building down and use its lumber to build a house.[^2] Electra Webb, founder of the Shelburne Museum, bought it for $1,300 plus building materials.[^2] New England Lighthouses gives a different sequence: the couple had bought the lighthouse from the Coast Guard for $50, and Webb bought it from them in July 1952.[^5] The two histories disagree on how the lighthouse changed hands.

Before the building came down, every part was photographed and numbered, and the timbers were numbered too.[^2][^5] The museum says the abandoned lighthouse was dismantled from its site on the lake and re-erected at the museum in 1952.[^3] The marker names six men and credits a crew with bringing the lighthouse ashore under hazardous conditions.[^4] LighthouseFriends also refers to six men, from a plaque on the lighthouse.[^2] New England Lighthouses says five men dismantled it, took it to Shelburne by barge, and reassembled it in less than a month.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the rebuilding was done in the fall of 1952.[^2]

Later the Coast Guard gave the museum a Fresnel lens, a bronze fog bell and the clockwork that struck the bell.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse stands near the center of the Shelburne Museum's campus. Visitors can learn about Lake Champlain's history there.[^3] The USLHS archive lists the site as open to the public, with the museum as owner.[^1] The marker and the lighthouse are on the museum grounds, which can be reached from Shelburne Road.[^4]
