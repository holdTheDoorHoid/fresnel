Cape Ray is the southwestern tip of Newfoundland, facing Cabot Strait, the passage between Newfoundland and Cape Breton that ships between the St. Lawrence and Europe typically used.[^1] Its light was built by Canada, not by Newfoundland, and its towers have been lost or damaged by fire twice.[^1][^10]

## The place

The Cape Ray Lighthouse stands on the southernmost tip of Newfoundland, on the highest point of land within the lightstation.[^2] Lighthouse Friends quotes an 1860 report by John Page, chief engineer of the Province of Canada's Department of Public Works, who described the extreme point of the cape as flat and bare apart from dwarf spruce "closely matted together", with a conical hill, the "sugar loaf", at least 180 m (600 ft) high about two miles inland.[^1] Page marked out the site for a tower at about the centre and highest part of the cape, 26 m (85 ft) above the sea and 300 m (1,000 ft) north of the water mark.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends gives the background to a Canadian light on Newfoundland's coast: the shore from Cape Ray to Cape St. John was reserved for French fishermen until 1904, which it takes to explain Newfoundland's lack of interest in lighting it. Newfoundland gave Canada the land free, but because the site was still part of the French Shore the British government also had to sanction the work.[^1] Parks Canada's heritage statement says several attempts to build here were made before confederation before one finally succeeded in 1871, and that the light also strengthened the relationship between Canada and Newfoundland.[^2]

## Building the light

Work began in 1870 and the tower was finished by the end of that year. The first lighthouse was a hexagonal wooden tower, 12.5 m (41 ft) high, showing a white flash every ten seconds from a revolving apparatus of twelve lamps and reflectors. It went into operation on 13 July 1871 at a total cost of $11,347.76, and Robert Rennie was hired as keeper at $600 a year.[^1]

In 1873 a steam fog whistle was added, first sounding on 20 October; Rennie's salary rose to $1,000, but he had to use $400 of it to hire an engineer to run the whistle.[^1] After an inspector concluded that the whistle could hardly be heard from the west, he recommended a new alarm at the tip of the cape, 550 yards from it; in 1877 a "cheap and simple" steam fog horn invented by N. S. Woodward of Sherbrooke, Quebec, was put into operation, and the original horn was shipped to Point Amour. By 1878 the inspector reported the Woodward horn could be heard only at a short distance, and it was replaced that year by a Champion horn.[^1]

In April 1885 the lighthouse was destroyed by fire after the "explosion of a lamp"; the fire stayed within the tower. Materials came down from Quebec on the steamship *Napoleon*, and a new, octagonal wooden tower was lit on 6 August 1885, the flash now coming every twenty seconds.[^1] Newfoundland's inspector, John Nevill, took the loss as an argument for cast-iron towers, noting that "one or two losses by fire would soon more than cover the difference of first cost for several stations."[^1]

The Department of Marine's report published in 1906 recorded a 15 m (50 ft) brick chimney built on a concrete foundation at the fog alarm station.[^3] The next report says the steam horn was replaced by a compressed-air diaphone giving one blast of five seconds every minute in thick or foggy weather.[^4] Lighthouse Friends dates the new lantern room and first-order Fresnel lens, giving a group of three flashes every fifteen seconds, to 1906, but the Department's report for 1907-08 says a first-order triple-flashing light had been provided and was "now in process of erection", so the date of the new light is unsettled.[^1][^5]

## The keepers

Keepers named Rennie held Cape Ray for most of its first sixty years; the sources read do not say how they were related. Lighthouse Friends lists Robert Rennie from 1871 to 1884, then Edward H. Rennie from 1884 to 1925, Burnett A. Rennie from 1925 to 1933, and Edward H. Rennie again in 1933 and 1934.[^1] Edward's name appears in the Department of Marine's report for the year ended 30 June 1900, which says repairs "under the supervision of the light keeper, Mr. E. H. Rennie" included wainscotting rooms in the dwelling and a new floor in the oil store.[^6] Theodore Lohnes followed (1934 to 1945), then F. G. Osborne (1946) and Hiram D. Osborne, who stayed until the late 1970s, and Gordon Thomas from the late 1970s to 1991.[^1] These dates come from a single secondary list and are not checked against appointment records.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Parks Canada says the light helped guide mariners through the hazards of Cabot Strait,[^2] and the Department of Marine's reports note ships in trouble nearby. One wreck return, in the report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1916 (published 1917), lists the London steamer *Cheltonian* stranded off Cape Ray on 23 May (so May 1915), with a preliminary inquiry held at Sydney on 5 June.[^7] Another, for 16 June to 3 July 1918, records going to the assistance of the British steamer *Ascania*, stranded 32 miles east of Cape Ray; a gale and heavy sea prevented salvage and the steamer became a total wreck.[^8]

The station was also a communications post. A telegraph cable linked Cape Ray to Cape North in 1856, and Lighthouse Friends says a Marconi wireless station was there by 1906.[^1] The Department's report published in 1906 describes the enlargement of the Cape Ray wireless station, among three Gulf stations it says had an effective range of fully 250 miles.[^9]

## After the keepers

The wooden lighthouse was damaged by fire in 1958 according to Lighthouse Friends, which says a temporary light on a pole served until the concrete tower was completed in 1960. Parks Canada gives the second fire as 1959 and the building of the present tower as 1959 to 1960.[^1][^10] The tower is a tapered octagonal concrete design that Parks Canada says originated in 1913 and was used widely in the 1950s and 1960s.[^2] A red daymark band was painted on the shaft in 1998.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends says the light was automated in 1991, while Parks Canada's statement describes the lighthouse as staffed, so the present staffing is unsettled in the sources read.[^1][^2]

## Today

Parks Canada's register entry for the light tower, based on a Federal Heritage Buildings Review Office report, gives its range as 17 nautical miles.[^10] Lighthouse Friends says a small building beside the tower was recognised as a Federal Heritage Building in 2007.[^1] The community has turned the former keeper's dwelling into a museum and interpretation centre that displays Dorset Paleoeskimo artifacts found at Cape Ray, and a large shed into a craft store.[^2] Lighthouse Friends' visitor notes say the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] Lighthouse Friends gives the owner as the Canadian Coast Guard.[^1] Check the museum's current hours before you go.
