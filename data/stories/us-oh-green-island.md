Green Island Light stands on a small island in western Lake Erie, off Put-in-Bay, Ohio.[^1] Its first lighthouse burned on a night that an 1898 account and LighthouseFriends both remember as bitterly cold, at the turn of 1863 and 1864, and the keeper's family sheltered in an outbuilding until the next morning.[^1][^4] The light now shows from a steel skeleton tower put up in 1939.[^4][^15] The limestone lighthouse that stood before it is a ruin.[^3]

## The place

Green Island is one of twenty-one small islands in the Erie Archipelago, according to LighthouseFriends.[^4] Gora counts it as one of fourteen islands in Ohio's Bass Islands archipelago.[^3]

The sources also give three different distances from Put-in-Bay. Thorndale puts Green Island "two miles distant" from the village.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it lies "three miles southwest of Put-in-Bay."[^4] An 1822 letter by Dr. John J. Bigsby, reprinted by Gora, describes a strontian deposit on the island's east side and places the islet "Four miles from Put-in-Bay harbour."[^3]

The sources do not name a shoal, reef or channel that the light was built to mark.

In October 1820 Major Joseph Delafield, an agent of the International Boundary Commission, visited the island. LighthouseFriends says he discovered the celestite there, a source of strontium, in cliffs along the east side, and that Green Island soon became the main American source of the element.[^4] Gora says one of Delafield's topographers, Major Louis G. de Russy, had found the mineral the year before, in 1819.[^3]

The 1864 newspaper says only that the light was "very important to lake navigation."[^2] An "original appropriation of $5,000" had been made for it in 1851.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the government bought the site at the western end of the island in December 1851, for $500, from Alfred P. Edwards.[^4]

## Building the light

The 1864 newspaper gives the light's building date as 1854.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the work ran late because of "the prevailing epidemic," likely an outbreak of cholera. The contractor finished in late November 1854, and a fourth-order revolving lens was installed. A defect in the revolving machinery and the lateness of the season meant the light was not shown until 1855.[^4]

The newspaper and LighthouseFriends describe the light differently. The newspaper calls it a "flash light" with "a costly French mirror," and says the structure was "somewhat like the one on Cedar Point, being a light tower and dwelling conjoined."[^2] LighthouseFriends describes a brick tower attached to a one-and-a-half-story keeper's house, with a fixed white light and a bright flash every 150 seconds, shown from a focal plane of 43 feet (13 m).[^4]

The second light, built after the fire, was a limestone tower nine feet (2.7 m) square with an adjoining keeper's house. LighthouseFriends says the new two-story lighthouse went into operation on 1 July 1865, and Gora's history says it had been built by July 1865.[^4][^3]

Later work was small and steady. In 1889 a boathouse went up on the east end of the island, with boatways into the water and an iron winch for launching and retrieving the station's boat.[^4] The Light-House Board's report for 1892 says the station's landing "was damaged by ice during last winter" and was repaired.[^6] The Board's report for 1894 says the wharf was lengthened by 30 feet (9 m) and widened to 16 feet (5 m), so that the lighthouse tender could land supplies.[^7] The report for 1896 says the station received "a carriage and iron track" to haul the keeper's large boat out of the water.[^8] The report for 1901 records that the light's characteristic had been changed from "fixed white varied by a red flash every minute" to flashes alternately red and white, with thirty-second intervals.[^9] LighthouseFriends dates the earlier change, to a red flash every minute, to 1887.[^4]

## The keepers

The archive's roster of keepers lists the principal keepers and their assistants in order.[^5] The first was Colonel Charles F. Drake, whom LighthouseFriends calls the light's first keeper.[^4] The 1864 newspaper calls him "Col. Chas. F. Drake, formerly of this city."[^2] The roster gives him as principal keeper from 1854 to 1871.[^5] Thorndale, writing in 1898, describes his wife and daughter, and a son, Pitt, who was at Put-in-Bay on the night of the fire.[^1] Gora's history says that Drake's granddaughter, Mrs. John K'Burg of Catawba Island, was a small child that night, and that she is now the only one still living of the six people who were on the island during the 1864 fire.[^3]

The roster also lists a Mary V. Drake as first assistant from 1865 to 1871, and a Maria M. Van Epp as first assistant from 1872 to 1882. The sources do not say how these women were related to the principal keepers.[^5]

Nicholas Van Epp was principal keeper from 1871 to 1882.[^5] Joseph A. Gibaut followed from 1882 to 1899, and LighthouseFriends and Gora spell the name Gibeaut.[^3][^4] The roster lists Elizabeth "Lizzie" A. Gibaut as first assistant from 1882 to 1898.[^5] John Safe was principal keeper from 1899 to 1903.[^5] LighthouseFriends reports a 1901 newspaper story about his team of Italian greyhounds, which pulled a sled across the frozen lake to take his children to and from school at Put-in-Bay.[^4] Gora gives the team to the keeper who came after Ferguson, which would put it after 1914, so the two sources disagree on who owned the dogs.[^3]

Robert P. Waterfield was principal keeper from 1903 to 1905, and George F. Ferguson from 1905 to 1914.[^5] Lydia Ryall's 1913 story, reprinted in Gora's history, says Ferguson had served three years at the Ashtabula Life-Saving Station, was appointed assistant keeper of the Detroit River light in 1901, was promoted a year later to keeper of the Black River light at Lorain, and moved to Green Island after two years there.[^18] The same story says he had held the Green Island post "since June, 1905," the date LighthouseFriends also gives.[^18][^4] Two years at Lorain, counted from 1902, would end about 1904, so Ryall's own account does not quite agree with itself. This story uses June 1905.[^18] LighthouseFriends also says his wife, Bertha, rowed to Put-in-Bay for a doctor when he was ill.[^4] Ryall's story says the couple had arranged a code of flag signals with the Put-in-Bay light keeper, and that lights were used at night instead of flags.[^18]

Peter L. Diffley was principal keeper from 1914 to 1917.[^5] William L. Gordon held the post from 1917 to 1926.[^5] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for fiscal 1920 names him as keeper of the Green Island Light Station, in a table of lighthouse service that saved life or property.[^12] In a letter printed in the 1922 Lighthouse Service Bulletin, William L. Gordon, keeper, described a small homemade radio set that picked up news and concerts from Detroit stations.[^11]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Sandusky Daily Commercial Register reported on 5 January 1864 that the light "was burned between 7 and 8 o'clock on Friday evening last." Colonel Drake had "saved only two beds, a marine clock and some small articles," and the paper put the government's loss at $6,000 to $8,000.[^2]

Read literally, "Friday evening last" in a paper dated Tuesday, 5 January, means Friday, 1 January. Thorndale places the fire on the night of the outgoing of 1863 and the incoming of 1864, and LighthouseFriends says it went up in flames on New Year's Day 1864.[^2][^1][^4]

Thorndale's account of 1898, reproduced in Gora's history, says the temperature fell "from 60 degrees above to 25 degrees below zero" within a few hours.[^1] When Pitt Drake reached the island the next morning, "a shout was heard from the outbuilding, the only one which the island now contained," and the family were found alive. "Col. Drake also suffered both from the cold and from burns received."[^1][^4]

On 25 July 1919 William L. Gordon, keeper, "towed a disabled launch, containing two men and two women, to Put in Bay, Lake Erie."[^10]

In April 1974, LighthouseFriends reports, two fishermen landed on the island and started a fire that burned for a full day, leaving only a shell of the old limestone lighthouse.[^4]

## After the keepers

The Lighthouse Service automated Green Island Light in 1926 and transferred Keeper Gordon to the South Bass Island Light, which also looked after the automated lights on Green Island and Ballast Island.[^4] The old light stayed active until 1939.[^4] Work went on at the station while Gordon was keeper. The Commissioner's fiscal-1921 report lists $1,030 for "removed sheds and built inclosed veranda, cistern, etc." at Green Island, and the fiscal-1922 report lists $1,843 for "rebuilding superstructure of wharf."[^13][^14]

In 1939 the Coast Guard replaced the light with an automated light on a steel skeletal tower about 960 feet (290 m) southwest of the old one.[^4] Gora gives that tower as 67 feet (20.5 m), with its focal plane at 80 feet (24 m), where LighthouseFriends calls the tower eighty feet (24 m).[^3][^4] In 1961 the federal government transferred the lighthouse property to the State of Ohio.[^4] Gora says the island is now managed by the Ohio Division of Wildlife as a wildlife refuge.[^3]

## Today

The 2025 Light List describes Green Island Light (LLNR 5535) as a white light flashing every 2.5 seconds, 80 feet (24 m) above the water, on a skeleton tower.[^15] Gora says that light is still in service.[^3] The old limestone lighthouse survives only as a ruin.[^3]

The island is a state wildlife refuge. Ohio's administrative code makes it unlawful to enter without authorization from the chief of the Division of Wildlife or their designee.[^16] The archive's place record lists the station as not open to the public.[^5] Visitors should not land on the island without that permission. The National Park Service's register record for the light, published 18 December 1990, places it in the Light Stations of Ohio multiple listing.[^17]
