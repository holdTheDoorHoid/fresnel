Lahaina Light stands on the government wharf in Lahaina Harbor, on the western end of Maui, Hawaii.[^3][^4] Its present tower is a concrete pyramid, and the Coast Guard owns it.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the first light at this harbor to 1840, when whale ships were using the port.[^1] The Lighthouse Service's own 1936 review says Hawaii's first light was a private aid at Kawaihae in 1859, with Lahaina's light coming after it.[^2]

## The place

Lahaina was a favored port for whale ships. LighthouseFriends says whalers favored the islands between 1820 and 1860, and that Lahaina became their port of choice for refitting and resupply. It adds that the town was the kingdom's capital until 1845.[^1] The 1936 review calls Lahaina "the early capital of the islands."[^2]

The landing lay at a narrow break through the coral reef.[^1] The first light was meant to mark the landing for boats coming into port at night. A letter of the time, which LighthouseFriends quotes, says there were quite a number of boats wrecked by the waves there.[^1]

Reefs were a danger across the islands. The 1936 review says "Many wrecks occurred throughout the islands" in the early days, because the coral reefs and rocky headlands lacked proper lighting. That is a general statement about the islands, not a record of wrecks at Lahaina.[^2]

The 1936 review says Hawaii's first light was "a feeble, fixed white light," established in 1859 as a private aid at Kawaihae on the island of Hawaii. It says Lahaina "was next provided with a light, this being merely an oil-wick lamp placed behind a plain window glass."[^2] LighthouseFriends gives 1840 for the first Lahaina light and calls it the first light in the islands.[^1] The two accounts cannot both be right about the first light in Hawaii, and the sources do not settle which is correct.

The lights changed hands with the government. The 1936 review says the monarchy kept the aids up to 1893, a provisional government kept them in 1893 and 1894, the Republic of Hawaii ran them to 1900, and the territory's Public Works Department ran them from 1900 to 1904. "Since 1904, lighthouses in the Hawaiian Islands have been maintained by the Federal Government."[^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says the first Lahaina light was built in 1840 on a stretch of waterfront called Keawaiki.[^1] A tower plaque, quoted by LighthouseFriends, says King Kamehameha III ordered a nine-foot (2.7 m) wooden tower built on the site in 1840, with whale-oil lamps kept burning at night by a Hawaiian caretaker paid $20 a year.[^1]

LighthouseFriends calls the 1866 light the third at Lahaina and says little is known of the second.[^1] According to the *Hawaiian Gazette*, as quoted by LighthouseFriends, the old house was pulled down and a new one erected on the old site, and the lamps were altered to burn kerosene instead of whale oil.[^1]

In 1904 the federal Lighthouse Board took over the aids in the islands. The Board's description, as quoted by LighthouseFriends, gives a fixed white light made from two ordinary kitchen lamps of small power, with red and green sectors. It was shown from a white wooden pyramid built on top of a storehouse.[^1]

Lieutenant John R. Slattery had been sent to Hawaii in 1904 to help improve harbors and aids to navigation. In 1905 he prepared a new light to serve the wharf where more than 500 steamers called each year.[^1]

The Light-House Board's notice, dated 15 June 1905 and printed in the *Maui News* on 1 July, said "On 20 June 1905 this light will be discontinued." A new light would be shown from a tower east of the old location. It was described as "fixed white," with "2 red sectors" marking the reef line.[^3]

A Board bulletin in the *Pacific Commercial Advertiser* of 26 August 1905 says the light was "moved to and established on the structure recently erected." The light stood 55 feet (17 m) above the base of the structure and 60 feet (18 m) above the water. Its characteristic was changed to fixed white between two fixed red sectors, instead of one red and one green sector.[^4]

The start date is in dispute. LighthouseFriends, quoting the *Maui News*, gives 13 May 1905 for the day the new light was placed in operation. The Board's notice gives 20 June 1905 for the switch.[^1][^3]

In 1917 a 39-foot (11.9 m) concrete pyramid replaced the wooden tower. LighthouseFriends gives its cost as $1,549 and says a fixed red light is shown from it.[^1] A plaque on the tower says the concrete structure was dedicated by the Coast Guard in 1916.[^1] The sources give the year two different ways.

## The keepers

The archive's roster of principal keepers reads "Kaukaia (-1893)," then "Captain David Taylor (1893-1903)," "George H. Dunn (1904-1907)" and "Samuel Ako (1907-1922)."[^5] Only the one name, Kaukaia, covers the years before 1893.

LighthouseFriends says Taylor took charge of the light in 1893, after the previous keeper refused to take an oath to support the provincial government. That government followed an overthrow of the monarchy by armed businessmen.[^1] LighthouseFriends does not name that keeper, and the sources here do not say whether he was Kaukaia.

Taylor was the harbormaster at Lahaina, and he was also given the light's care.[^1] In 1904 George H. Dunn, an agent for two steamship companies, replaced him and kept the light until 1907.[^1][^5]

Samuel Ako was placed in charge of the light in 1907.[^1][^5] LighthouseFriends reports that in February 1917 Ako sued the Pioneer Hotel Company for $10,000. He said the company had removed furniture from the room he and his family occupied, which left them sleeping on the floor for four days and nights.[^1] It adds that the 1930 census lists him as a lawyer.[^1]

## After the keepers

The roster ends Ako's tenure in 1922.[^5] In the same year the Lighthouse Service's bulletin lists the Lahaina light as changed from oil to acetylene, and from "fixed red to flashing white."[^7] That entry places the light on the "east coast" of Maui, which the other sources contradict. The sources read here do not say whether a keeper stayed on after 1922.[^7]

In 2009 the Coast Guard's Aids to Navigation Team in Honolulu converted the light to solar power, using light-emitting diode (LED) technology. LighthouseFriends says the change ended years of reliance on Maui Electric Co.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light, and the Lahaina Restoration Foundation maintains it.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's archive lists the light as not open to the public.[^6] The two sources differ on public access.

A plaque placed at the tower in 1984 by the Lahaina Restoration Foundation gives the 1840 history. On that account, LighthouseFriends says, the light was the first in the Hawaiian Islands.[^1]

On 8 August 2023 a wildfire destroyed most of Lahaina's historic district. LighthouseFriends says the light was damaged but survived.[^1]

LighthouseFriends places the light at the western end of the harbor, reached from Front Street.[^1]
