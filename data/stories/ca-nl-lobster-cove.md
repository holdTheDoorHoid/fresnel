Lobster Cove Head Light stands on a headland at the north side of the entrance to Bonne Bay, on Newfoundland's west coast, inside Gros Morne National Park. Its round cast-iron tower was first lit in the spring of 1898, about nine years after a local member of the legislative assembly publicised residents' concern over the bay's lack of navigation aids in 1889.[^1][^2][^4]

## The place

A few kilometres inside the entrance, Bonne Bay divides into two fjords, the East Arm and the South Arm.[^1]

In 1889 G. C. Fearn, the member of the legislative assembly for the district that included Lobster Cove, drew attention to the lack of navigation aids in Bonne Bay. Residents of Rocky Harbour were each giving a pint of oil a week to keep a light burning in a local fisherman's house.[^1] Fearn reported that an older woman had offered to keep the lighthouse for no salary. As LighthouseFriends prints her words, she said, "You may be sure, it will always be burning, for I have my three boys on the banks."[^1]

## Building the light

Inspector J. T. Nevill chose Lobster Cove Head in 1889 as one of four new light sites on the west coast of Newfoundland.[^1] Four years later an iron tower was ordered from the Victoria Iron Works in St. John's. The lantern and illuminating apparatus was ordered from Chance Brothers of Birmingham, England.[^1] The register describes the shaft as locally cast iron.[^2]

The lantern and apparatus arrived in 1894, but the light was not lit until spring 1898, when the buildings were finished.[^1] The Lighthouse Directory gives 1897 as the station's date.[^3] The lamp burned kerosene vapour inside a fifth-order dioptric lens. The lens turned twelve times a minute and flashed every 2.5 seconds.[^1] The round iron tower was painted white. A covered way led from it to the keeper's house, 6 m (20 ft) to the east. The house and the covered way were painted white with black roofs.[^1]

In the federal register's reading, the tower marks the colony's late acceptance of its obligations to the west coast.[^2]

## The keepers

Robert Lewis was appointed the first keeper, at an annual salary of $504.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates his service from 1897, a year before the light was lit.[^1]

The station also became a signal station in 1898. Flags raised up a pole passed messages between ships and the shore.[^1]

William Young succeeded Lewis in 1902 and kept the light until 1941. That year his son George Young became the third and last keeper, and he served until 1970.[^1]

## After the keepers

Automated equipment was installed in 1969. George Young retired the next year, and the contract to service the light was awarded to a contractor.[^1] In 1970 the keeper's house went on a five-year lease to a private tenant. When the lease ran out, the property passed to Parks Canada.[^1]

In 1990 George Young raised the flag at the official opening of the keeper's house to the public. The lighthouse was designated a Recognized Federal Heritage Building that year.[^1]

The Lighthouse Directory says ownership of the lighthouse itself passed to Parks Canada in 2014.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard owns the lighthouse, and that the property passed to Parks Canada when the lease expired.[^1] The two accounts disagree about who owns the tower.

## Today

The lightstation is run by Parks Canada as part of Gros Morne National Park. Its exhibit season in 2026 ran daily from 15 May to 12 October, from 9:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m.[^4] Parks Canada's page invites visitors to "step inside our century old lighthouse" for the exhibit.[^4] The directory calls the keeper's house a visitor centre and museum, LighthouseFriends puts the exhibits inside the dwelling, and both list the tower as closed.[^3][^1]

The directory lists the keeper's house as open daily from mid-May to mid-October, and the tower as closed.[^3] The Coast Guard's list gives the light as an isophase white light every four seconds, with a focal height of 35.0 m and a range of 12 nautical miles.[^5] The directory describes it as a white flash every four seconds, so the two sources describe the rhythm in different terms.[^3]
