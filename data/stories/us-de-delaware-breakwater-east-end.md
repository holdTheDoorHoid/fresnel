Delaware Breakwater East End Light is a brown cast-iron tower at the far end of the inner breakwater at Lewes, Delaware. It was built in 1885 to mark the entrance to Delaware Bay, its keepers were withdrawn in 1950 when the light was automated, and the State of Delaware now owns it.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5]

## The place

The breakwater was a federal project of the 1820s. Congress authorized it in 1825, William Strickland was appointed chief engineer, and work began in 1828 with the first stone shipments.[^1] The result was a harbor of refuge behind the breakwater. Its National Register nomination credits the harbor with saving "countless ships and hundreds of lives."[^1]

The harbor had lights before this tower. In 1833 the Army asked Strickland to design a lighthouse for it, and it was placed on the west end of the breakwater.[^1] The nomination's description places a light on the northeast end of the breakwater in 1848.[^1] Its history section says the 1848 lighthouse "that had stood on the western end of the breakwater" was made obsolete when the gap was closed.[^1] The two passages do not agree on where the 1848 light stood. The 1885 light was meant to warn mariners off the dangerous shoals off Cape Henlopen, with a red sector aimed seaward.[^2]

## Building the light

The present tower was built in 1885. It is a small conical tower of four tiers of cast-iron plates, bolted together and lined inside with two feet (0.6 m) of brick, on a circular concrete foundation.[^1][^2] The state marker gives the base as twenty-two feet (6.7 m) across.[^2] The sources disagree on the height. The nomination gives 49 feet (15 m), and the U.S. Lighthouse Society's record gives 56 feet (17 m) from the base to the center of the lantern.[^1][^3]

The first lens was a fourth-order Fresnel.[^2][^3] The light was first lit in October 1885, and a steam-powered Daboll fog trumpet was added that November.[^2][^4] A reinforced concrete oil shed went up 100 feet (30 m) west of the tower in 1912 and held the fuel for the light until it was electrified.[^1]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society roster lists 39 entries for principal keepers and assistants from 1885 to 1950, counting repeat stints.[^3] Willard H. Hall was the first principal keeper, from 1885 to 1896.[^3] Several men moved up from assistant to head keeper. William J. Salmons was first assistant from 1885 to 1891 and principal keeper from 1896 to 1899. Frank M. Saulsbury was first assistant in 1901 and 1902 and principal keeper from 1902 to 1913.[^3] Henry C. Wingate was second assistant from 1907 to 1911 and principal keeper from 1914 to 1919.[^3]

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' table of life-saving assistance by Lighthouse Service employees for fiscal 1915 names Wingate, keeper, and H. P. Marshall, first assistant keeper, at the Range Front station.[^9] This account does not say what they did.[^9] The marker says this tower was the Range front light from 1903 to 1918.[^2]

Later men followed the same path. John C. Gray was first assistant from 1930 to 1935 and principal keeper from 1935 to 1947. Burgan N. Brittingham was second assistant from 1927 to 1933 and first assistant from 1935 to 1950.[^3] The last principal keeper on the roster is Henry C. Stoehr, from 1947 to 1950.[^3] The keepers were still on board until 1950, when the light was automated.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The fog trumpet was the light's signal in thick weather.[^2][^7] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1893 gives its running time for that year as 673 hours, and the report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1899 gives about 607 hours.[^6][^8] The trumpet did not always carry. In its report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1894, the Board says that mariners had come within half a mile of the breakwater with the trumpet running and could not hear it.[^7]

## After the keepers

In May 1903 the tower became the front light of the new Delaware Breakwater Range, replacing the West End light.[^4] The marker says it held that role from 1903 until 1918.[^2] The light was automated on 11 July 1950.[^2] For a time during the 1960s and 1970s, the structure was used as a base of operations by the Pilots' Association of the Bay and River Delaware.[^2]

The light was decommissioned in 1996. On 5 February 1999 the federal government conveyed it to the State of Delaware.[^2] In 2004 the Delaware River & Bay Authority and the Delaware River & Bay Lighthouse Foundation partnered to preserve it, and the state marker was installed that year.[^4][^2]

## Today

The State of Delaware lists the light among its historic sites, at the east end of the inner breakwater in Lewes. Access is prohibited except for accompanied visits conducted by Cape Water Tours, and the light can be viewed from Cape Henlopen State Park.[^5] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record says the light is open to the public, which conflicts with the state's listing.[^3] Visitors should not walk out to it on their own. It is not the 1926 light at the east end of the Harbor of Refuge breakwater, which the nomination describes as a replacement for an earlier light damaged in 1920.[^1]
