Lehua Rock Light stands on the summit of Lehua, a small rocky islet 18 miles west of Kaua‘i and about 0.75 mile north of Ni‘ihau.[^1][^2] The Lighthouse Service set up an automatic gas light there on 24 April 1931, according to the 2009 archaeological survey, which says the light holds the record as the highest beacon in the U.S. Lighthouse Service.[^3]

## The place

The Lighthouse Service's *Bulletin* of 1931 described Lehua as "little more than a rock, with vegetation scant due to lack of water." It said great numbers of seabirds nest in holes on the faces of its cliffs.[^4] The 2009 survey calls the island dry and windy, with steep slopes, little soil, and no reef, no beach and nowhere for a canoe to land.[^5] The landing is on the south side, where the survey finds an eroded terrace at sea level. Most of the south coast is vertical sea cliffs averaging 50 to 60 feet (15 to 18 m) high.[^6]

The sources do not say which ships the light was built to guide. The Bulletin calls it "the newest lighthouse in the waters of the central Pacific Ocean," and explains that a site this high was possible because low cloud and fog are rare in Hawaii.[^4]

## Building the light

The Lighthouse Service was proposing a light on Lehua a decade before it was lit. The Commissioner's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1921 mentions a "proposed acetylene light" at Lehua Island, "where the ascent to the summit is exceedingly difficult," and says it was "found practicable and safe" to pipe the gas up at high pressure "to any distance."[^7] The report does not say that the light was built at that time.

The Bulletin says Governor Farrington set the island aside for lighthouse purposes by executive order on 10 August 1928, and that President Coolidge confirmed it.[^4] The 2009 survey, citing Love Dean's 1991 book on Hawaiian lighthouses, gives the transfer to federal control for a lighthouse reservation as 1928 as well.[^3]

Surveys of the island followed. The Bulletin says the crest of the island was chosen for the light, and that a sheltered landing for small cargo boats was found in the channel between Lehua and Ni‘ihau.[^4] The survey says the work used that south landing to unload materials from boats. On a 60-foot bluff to the west of it the crew built a derrick, a small building for the gasoline engine that ran the hoist, and an acetylene gas supply house, and a pipeline carried the gas up to the light.[^3]

The Bulletin says the apparatus was prepared and assembled at the depot before it was erected on the island.[^4] The survey's photograph caption places the tender Kukui at anchor off the island during the 1931 construction.[^3] The Bulletin said that the problems of getting materials for the small tower, and of putting the lantern and its fittings on the top of the island, were "comparable to an ascent to the crest of Diamond Head" from the shore near the Diamond Head light.[^4]

The survey gives the summit's elevation as 702 feet (213 m) above sea level, and the Bulletin gives the same figure.[^8][^4] The Washington Times, reporting the new light in May 1931, put it at "Seven hundred and seven feet above the Pacific Ocean" and called it "the highest light maintained by the U.S. Lighthouse Service."[^9] A Bulletin article of 1936 gives 709 feet.[^10] The sources do not agree on the height, and this story does not choose between them.

The Bulletin describes a 375-millimeter lens on a short concrete tower, flashing white every 10 seconds. It rated the light at 15 miles, but said its range would be about 20 miles in clear weather.[^4] The light was to be entirely automatic and unwatched. The Bulletin said it would burn for a year without attention, with the apparatus inspected every six months, weather permitting.[^4]

## After the keepers

No source here names a keeper on Lehua. The Bulletin says the light was designed to run unwatched.[^4] In 1989 the unit was changed to a solar-powered light on a fiberglass pole, according to the survey.[^3]

A report published on 16 April 2025 says a Coast Guard MH-65 Dolphin helicopter crew, facing "challenging wind conditions and severe updrafts," hoisted out an outdated, inoperable aid and installed a new, upgraded Carmanah light in its place. The two crew members were then hoisted back aboard and flown back to O‘ahu.[^1]

## Today

Lehua is federal property under the U.S. Coast Guard, which operates the light, and a state seabird sanctuary managed by Hawai‘i's Department of Land and Natural Resources.[^2] The survey says the Coast Guard now maintains the light by helicopter, rather than by boat.[^3]

The survey says the original light, its supporting structures and its outbuildings no longer exist, and that the 1989 light replaced them. Remnants of the original foundation at the summit, and of foundations for the light's support structures on the south coast, still remain. The survey records these under the name "Lehua Rock Light Ruins," and describes six concrete platform structures near the landing as associated with the construction and maintenance of the original light.[^6]

The survey recommends preserving the cultural sites on Lehua. It says the historic lighthouse features "possess potential for adaptive re-use" in a proposed ecosystem restoration project.[^11]

None of these sources says the island is open to visitors. It is Coast Guard property and a seabird sanctuary, so this story does not suggest landing on it.
