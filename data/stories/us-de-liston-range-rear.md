Liston Range Rear Light stands about three miles (5 km) inland from the Delaware River, near Biddle's Corner in New Castle County, Delaware.[^1][^2] It is a wrought-iron tower, measured by the National Register nomination at 120 feet (37 m) from its base to the focal plane,[^1] built to be the rear mark of a range that lined ships up the river's channel. It has stood on its present site since 1906, after being taken down and moved from a farm at Port Penn.[^1][^2]

## The place

The Delaware River is wide but not very deep, and its main channel "passes alongside numerous shoals and islands, and these hazards sometimes made navigation of the Delaware a dangerous proposition."[^1] To help ships keep to that channel, the Light House Establishment set up range lights: a front light near the water and a taller rear light set well back from it.[^1] A mariner who saw one light directly above the other, the nomination explains, "knew that his vessel was on the line of the river's channel and passing clear of islands and shoals."[^1]

Congress approved the work in 1875 and "appropriated $55,000 for their construction."[^1] The lights were meant to mark the channel "from Reedy Point south to Ship John Shoal."[^1] The rear light at Port Penn was first lit on 2 April 1877, on a farm called Hazel Glen. "It stood on a farm called Hazel Glen, west of Port Penn."[^1][^2]

## Building the light

When the tower was built, a circular stair with 152 steps and five landings wound around a central cast-iron column up to the watchroom. Its first light was an oil-fired burner with a double wick, and its range lens was 29 inches (74 cm) across, with a prismatic reflector.[^1]

The tower is wrought iron.[^1] The nomination calls that unusual: "It is constructed of wrought, rather than cast, iron," because the Establishment "most often resorted to cast-iron" for its other towers.[^1] LighthouseFriends credits the Kellogg Bridge Company of Buffalo, New York, with building it.[^2] Towers of this kind were erected and inspected at the builder's works before they were shipped out to their sites.[^1]

About 1904 a new ship channel was dredged in the Delaware, and "Some of the range lights were abandoned, others moved."[^1] On 25 October 1904 the Port Penn rear light was discontinued and temporary lights went up on the Liston range.[^2] The temporary rear light was "a 100-foot-tall lamp post" with a daymark on it.[^2]

The tower was taken apart and re-erected here.[^1] The nomination says a Philadelphia contractor did the work "at a cost of $5,345, or about one-third the cost of a new tower."[^1] LighthouseFriends names the contractor, John L. Grim of Philadelphia.[^2] The light went back into the tower on 15 May 1906. "The tower was re-lit on May 15, 1906."[^1][^2] On 10 November 1906 a second-order range lens replaced the reflector light; LighthouseFriends says it was made by Barbier, Benard & Turenne of Paris, and that the lantern room's walls had to be extended outward to give the keeper room to move around the lens.[^2]

The houses came next. By the end of August 1907 a new dwelling, barn, oil house and privy had been finished at the station.[^2] The assistant keeper's house is dated two ways. LighthouseFriends puts a second dwelling at 1910, "at a cost of $5,851,"[^2] and the nomination says "In about 1913, it erected an assistant keeper's house."[^1] The two sources disagree on the date.

The Service's bulletin index for 1912 to 1917 lists "Listons Range Rear, change in illuminant" at page 70 of the bulletin. The index does not say what the light was changed to.[^4]

## The keepers

Daniel Bailey, appointed keeper of the Port Penn rear light on 1 July 1904, stayed in the old station while he tended the temporary light at Liston.[^2] The archive's roster gives four head keepers for the station: Daniel Bailey (1904–1905), William Harrington (1905–1913), Harry E. Spencer (1913–1928) and Charles R. Manlove (1928–1933).[^7] Its roster also lists thirteen first-assistant stints between 1906 and 1930, several of them only a few months long.[^7]

The head keeper and his assistant did not always get along. In October 1919 the assistant, Robert B. Davidson, was suspended after a scuffle at the foot of the tower with the head keeper, Harry E. Spencer.[^2] The quarrel began over a boy who had been friends with Davidson's son, whom Spencer ordered off the reservation.[^2] The district office called Spencer "one of the most efficient keepers in this District" and said he could not get along with his assistants.[^2] Davidson was reprimanded and kept off duty without pay for close to a month, and Spencer was counseled to treat assistants with more consideration. Both men went on working at the station for close to another decade.[^2]

Both men were later among the keepers the Service sent to its exhibit at the Philadelphia exposition. "During the course of the exposition the following lighthouse keepers were detailed for duty as attendants," the Service's bulletin reports, and the index lists Davidson and Spencer as the keepers of Liston Rear for that duty.[^5][^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources record no wreck at the station. They record one emergency there, a fire. In its report for the fiscal year 1921, the Commissioner of Lighthouses notes that Spencer and Davidson "extinguished fire near the station, which threatened to destroy several near-by buildings."[^3] The report gives no date beyond the fiscal year, which ran from July 1920 to June 1921.

## After the keepers

Up until about 1930 the fixed light, which shone twenty-four hours a day, was tended by a keeper.[^1] The date the light was electrified is disputed. LighthouseFriends says the station was electrified "sometime in 1930," which ended the assistant keeper's post.[^2] The nomination puts the change in the mid-1930s.[^1] Manlove retired in 1933, and the station was then fully automated.[^2] The dwellings were sold.[^1][^2] The nomination says they went "to private individuals" after manning ended in the 1930s,[^1] and LighthouseFriends says only the small parcel at the foot of the tower stayed with the government.[^2]

The lamp received an automatic bulb changer in 1976, which moved a fresh bulb into place when one burned out.[^1] By the time of the 1978 nomination the Coast Guard station at Gloucester, New Jersey, serviced the light "only once or twice a month."[^1]

In 2004 the Delaware River & Bay Lighthouse Foundation took a thirty-year lease from the Coast Guard and became responsible for the tower and its brick oil house.[^2] It gave up the lease in 2007.[^2] On 10 April 2012 the tower was offered under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act to eligible government agencies and non-profit groups.[^2] When none took it, the General Services Administration held an online auction that closed on 20 May 2013 with a high bid of $22,003, won by a private buyer.[^2] In 2020 that buyer sold the tower to a private owner who also held the two dwellings, so the tower and dwellings were in one ownership again.[^2]

## Today

The tower and dwellings are privately owned, and the grounds, dwellings and tower are closed.[^2] Visitors should not go onto the property or try to climb the tower.[^2] The Archive's place record marks the light as not an active aid to navigation and not open to the public.[^7] A Delaware Bay association page, last updated in 2018, says the Coast Guard owns the small piece of land under the tower.[^8] The sources do not agree on who owns the ground, and they do not establish whether the rear light still shows.
