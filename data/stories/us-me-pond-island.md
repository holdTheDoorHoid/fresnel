Pond Island Light stands on a ten-acre island at the western side of the mouth of the Kennebec River, in Maine, beside Popham Beach.[^2][^3] It was first lit in 1821.[^3] The brick tower that stands there now dates from 1855 and is still an active aid to navigation, but the keepers' buildings are gone and the island has been a federal wildlife refuge since 1973.[^1][^3]

## The place

Pond Island lies on the western side of the river's entrance.[^2] Its ocean side has cliffs of sixty feet (18 m) or more, and the island tapers down to the west.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that how the island got its name is not known.[^2]

The river mouth already had a light on Seguin Island, two miles farther out, which had been built about a quarter century before. LighthouseFriends says an additional beacon was needed to mark the mouth more clearly.[^2] The refuge's history says the Pond Island light, put in operation in 1821, made "the hazardous entrance to the Kennebec River safer for mariners."[^3]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $10,500 on 31 March 1821 for three lighthouses in Maine, one of them on Pond Island.[^2] The first tower was of rubble masonry.[^2] After a visit in 1842, the inspector I. W. P. Lewis described the tower, as LighthouseFriends quotes his report, as "thirteen feet high," with cracked walls and loose stones. His conclusion was: "Whole structure requires rebuilding."[^2]

An 1850 inspection found the tower still cracked and the lantern room "very rusty." It recommended a new tower and lantern room, and noted that reflectors from the West Lighthouse at Cape Elizabeth had been installed on Pond Island.[^2]

The present tower was built in 1855: a brick cylinder 20 feet (6.1 m) tall, fitted with a fifth-order Fresnel lens and joined by a work shed to a wooden keeper's dwelling.[^1][^2]

## The keepers

The Lighthouse Society's archive lists twenty-two principal keepers for Pond Island, from Samuel L. Rogers in 1821 to Ronald D. Howard, whose entry begins in 1960.[^1]

Rogers served until 1832.[^1] In 1823 he petitioned for a well or a cistern, because the island had no fresh water.[^2] LighthouseFriends quotes his words: "I suffer great inconvenience on account of having no means to obtain fresh water but by transporting it from the mainland." Stephen Pleasonton, overseer of the Lighthouse Establishment, ordered that a cistern be built if no well could be dug.[^2]

The archive lists Samuel Gill (1861-1864) and then Hannah Gill (1864-1867), a woman listed as principal keeper.[^1]

Isaac W. Morrison followed, with the archive giving 1889 to 1903 and LighthouseFriends giving 1904 as the end.[^1][^2] A notice in the Augusta *Kennebec Journal* of 26 April 1902 reported the death of a Mrs. Morrison, aged 70, wife of the keeper of Pond Island light, from a stroke of paralysis. It said the couple had kept the island for the past thirteen years.[^9] The notice gives the keeper only by his surname. Thirteen years fits the roster's 1889 start for Isaac W. Morrison, but the notice does not name him.

Ezra W. Morrison is listed from 1904 to 1926.[^1] He died in June 1926.[^8] The paper reported that when the big lamp "failed to reflect," men went out in a power surf boat to investigate, and found that he had died suddenly of heart disease.[^8] The paper said he had been keeper for 29 years, which does not fit the archive's 1904 start.[^8][^1] Napoleon B. Fickett followed in 1926 and served until 1948.[^1] The archive then lists six more keepers, the last two being Douglas Frewin (at least 1958 to 1960) and Ronald D. Howard (from 1960).[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 8 September 1869 a hurricane swept along the Maine coast. On Pond Island it blew down the pyramidal fog-bell tower and damaged the striking machinery, but the bell escaped uninjured.[^2] In 1890 a new bell of 1,200 pounds replaced the old steel one, which was badly corroded.[^2]

In January 1884 the schooner *Electric Light*, of Bath, Maine, bound from Baltimore with a cargo of coal and seven men aboard, anchored "under the lee of Pond Island" on the night of Friday, 18 January.[^6] The next morning, in a thick snow-storm, her anchor broke out while she had a long scope of cable out, and she began dragging toward the shore. The revenue steamer Woodbury tried to get a line to her. Keeper Haley of the Hunnewell's Beach station went off with his crew and offered help; they helped furl the sails and heave the anchor up, and the Woodbury towed her into the river.[^6]

Eight years later, on the evening of 25 September 1892, the tug Battler was caught in a dense fog while coming down the river with the whaleback barge 202 in tow.[^7] The barge's anchor fetched up on the island.[^7] The tug got ashore on Wood Island bar in the cross tide, and her crew blew for assistance, which was "promptly answered by the Hunniwells Beach life-saving crew."[^7]

The Lighthouse Board's report for 1894 recorded fog-signal tests on the Kennebec and the view of the Pond Island keeper: with any strong wind, the Seguin whistle could hardly be heard at Pond Island.[^4] In June 1895 the Board changed Pond Island's fog signal from a blow every 8½ seconds to a blow every 10 seconds.[^5]

## After the keepers

According to LighthouseFriends, the last resident keeper, Ronald D. Howard, left the island on 11 September 1960, once the light had been automated.[^2] The Lighthouse Society gives 1960 as the year of automation.[^1] The refuge says the Coast Guard automated the station in 1963 and removed the buildings, leaving only the lighthouse.[^3] Of the station's buildings, only the brick tower survived; the rest were later razed, LighthouseFriends reports.[^2]

The island was transferred from the Coast Guard to the Fish and Wildlife Service in 1973.[^3] Terns had nested on the island until 1937, when a rise in gulls drove them away.[^2] In 1996 the Service, the Maine Department of Inland Fisheries and Wildlife and the National Audubon Society began a tern restoration.[^3] In 1999 the island produced its first tern chick in more than sixty years, from ten pairs of common terns that nested there.[^3]

## Today

Pond Island National Wildlife Refuge is a ten-acre island at the mouth of the Kennebec River, next to Popham Beach.[^3] The light is still in service.[^1] The refuge lists a 250 mm optic, a focal plane of 52 feet (15.8 m) and a fog signal of two blasts every 30 seconds.[^3][^1]

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^1] LighthouseFriends, which gives the grounds as open from September through March with the tower closed, says the light is best seen from the water or from Popham Beach State Park.[^2]
