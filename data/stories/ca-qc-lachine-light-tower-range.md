The Lachine range lights are two steel towers in Parc Saint-Louis, at the upper entrance to the Lachine Canal on the south-west shore of the island of Montreal. The front light stands at the end of a railway wharf, and the rear light stands in shoal water in the bay east of the wharf, so a ship that keeps the two lights in line is steering down the dredged channel.[^1][^3]

## The place

The canal exists because of the Lachine Rapids.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the rapids stopped early vessels, which had to unload their cargo and haul it overland around them.[^1] The canal opened to navigation in 1825 with seven locks, and it was enlarged between 1843 and 1848 to five locks of greater size.[^1]

In 1849 a lighthouse went up on a pier at the canal's western entrance, to help vessels pass from the canal into Lake St. Louis. LighthouseFriends describes it as a wooden tower 6 m (20 ft) high, with one lamp in a reflector.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1889 two new pole lights were put up at Lachine, LighthouseFriends says, and the keeper's salary was raised fifty dollars to pay for keeping them.[^1] In 1900 these were replaced by the present pair, built for the Department of Railways and Canals.[^4] The Department of Marine's annual report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1903 says the old mast light was "replaced by a permanent lighthouse forming the front light of a range established by the Department of Railways and Canals to indicate the axis of the dredged cut".[^3]

The towers are steel, shaped as cones and riveted from curved plates, the method shipbuilders and boiler-makers used in the early twentieth century.[^5] The Canadian Register of Historic Places calls the front tower "an extremely rare example of a circular metal tower outside of Newfoundland."[^5] The same report gives the front light as "an occulting white acetylene light", 9 m (30 ft) above the summer level of the lake, which "should be visible 10 miles in".[^3] The Coast Guard's list now shows both lights as fixed green (see Today).[^6]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends names John Norton as the first keeper of the pier light, and says he served roughly thirty years before he was replaced at the opening of navigation in 1880. Its head-keeper list gives his span as "at least 1851 – 1880", so the start date is not fixed by the sources.[^1] It says he was seventy-eight when he was superannuated in 1880, and that he died later that year.[^1]

Charles Leger took charge in 1880, and in 1889 his pay rose by fifty dollars for the two new pole lights.[^1] Alphonse Blondin replaced him in 1891, but LighthouseFriends says he was dismissed in 1897, after an investigation found that he had let others keep two of the lights in his charge.[^1] Thomas Leger followed from 1897 to 1900.[^1]

From 1910 the head keepers were Arthur Lizotte (1910 to 1917) and then the Malo family.[^1] The Department's keepers' table for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1914 lists "Lachine ranges" against "A. Lizotte, Jr", with his appointment on 27 April 1910 and a salary of $305.[^7][^1] J. B. Malo kept the lights from 1917 to 1921. LighthouseFriends then names "Eugunie Malo" as head keeper from 1921 to 1923, and J. B. Malo returned from 1924 to 1934; H. Malo followed to 1935.[^1] LighthouseFriends is the only source read that names Eugunie Malo, and it prints her first name as "Eugunie", so the spelling and her role rest on that one source.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 10 October 1878 a violent gale sank the Lake St. Louis lightship, which stood near the Lachine light. LighthouseFriends says three men were paid $1,000 to raise her, and she was repaired over the winter and back on station for the 1879 season.[^1]

## After the keepers

The canal closed to shipping in November 1970, LighthouseFriends says, and was transferred to Parks Canada in 1978 for tourism development. It opened to pleasure boating in 2002.[^2] The rear tower gained an equipment platform above its cupola in 1939.[^4] The two towers were designated Recognized Federal Heritage Buildings in 1991.[^2] The sources do not say when the last resident keeper left or when the lights were automated.

## Today

Both towers stand in Parc Saint-Louis. LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the towers are closed, and that the front light's wharf has paid parking.[^1][^2] The 1989 heritage statement records ice damage to the front tower and says its base is severely corroded.[^4]

The Coast Guard's March 2025 List of Lights still shows both ranges, as fixed green, visible in line of range, operating at night only and seasonal, with a range of 12 nautical miles.[^6]
