Point Lookout Light stands at the southern tip of Point Lookout State Park in St. Mary's County, Maryland, where the Potomac River meets Chesapeake Bay. It was built in 1830, its light was put out for the last time in January 1966, and it is now a historic site open for tours.[^1][^2] The park's complex manager calls it "the oldest integral lighthouse standing in the country."[^2]

## The place

The lighthouse was used to guide ships away from the shallow waters that extend off the point.[^2] The point was first considered for a small beacon in 1825. Congress appropriated $1,800 to help mark the river's entrance, but Jenifer Taylor, who owned the land on the point, was not satisfied with the $500 he was offered. A group of freeholders set $1,150 as a fair price.[^3]

## Building the light

John Donahoo built the lighthouse in 1830 for $3,350. The first building was a story and a half, about a third the size of the building that stands today.[^1] Donahoo was also the contractor for eleven other Maryland lights, the state says.[^2] The Archive gives 1831 as the year the light station was established, and 1830 for the tower.[^4]

The first keeper, James Davis, lit the light on 20 September 1830, according to the state. LighthouseFriends gives 30 September 1830 as the date of the first exhibition.[^1][^3] A fourth-order Fresnel lens with a 300-degree arc was placed in the lantern in 1857.[^3] An inspection in 1858 noted that the bricks of the house had been painted yellow, the tower was white, and the shingle roof remained red.[^3]

The station grew in the 1880s. In 1883 the house was given a second full story, and the light was raised to 41 feet (12 m) above sea level, according to the state.[^1] The Archive gives the height as 37 feet (11 m) above water, so the two sources disagree.[^4] That year the station also became a buoy depot, with coal and buoy sheds and a wharf.[^3] The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1891 says the funds for rebuilding the wharf at the depot had been provided.[^8] In April 1893 the Board's report says thirty-eight of the wharf's piles were covered with yellow metal against the shipworm, and "Chafing by the ice during the past severe winter" made the work necessary.[^5]

In the year ended 30 June 1894 the Board bought an iron oil house, able to hold about 1,000 gallons of mineral oil, to be erected at the station.[^6] The light also had a red sector, which the Board discontinued on 30 November 1895.[^7]

## The keepers

The state's account says keeper positions "were highly political and sometimes subject to the whims of an administration."[^1]

James Davis died on 3 December 1830, just over two months after he lit the light.[^1][^3] His daughter Ann took over his duties until 1847.[^1][^4] The roster lists William Wood from 1847 to 1849 and William P. Baxter from 1849 to 1853.[^4] Richard Edwards was installed in 1853 and died soon after. His daughter Martha kept the light until 1855, when she turned it over to her sister Pamelia, who served until 1869.[^1][^4] The roster lists Ann Davis, Martha A. Edwards and Pamelia Edwards each as a head keeper, so three women held the principal post in the years between 1830 and 1869.[^4] The state says Pamelia was removed in 1869 with no reason given, and that most of the records of her term were destroyed in the Commerce fire of 1921.[^1]

William Yeatman served from 1871 until his death in 1908, the longest term at the station, according to the state.[^1] His son William Yeatman Jr. was an assistant from 1931.[^1][^4]

Thomas Jacobson was keeper from 1912 to 1917. In 1917 he was promoted from keeper to mate of Cape Lookout Shoals Light Vessel No. 80, off North Carolina.[^10]

George M. Willis Sr. was keeper from 1917 to 1939, and he and his assistant retired on disability in 1939.[^1][^4] Robert W. Fulcher took the station after Willis, served until a stroke in 1942, and retired on disability in 1943.[^1] Herman F. Metivier was an assistant from 1939 and keeper from 1943 to 1954, according to the state; the roster gives 1942 to 1954 for his keepership.[^1][^4] George Gatton served from 1957 until 31 October 1965 according to the state, and the roster gives 1956 to 1965.[^1][^4] Raymond E. Hartzel darkened the light for the last time on 11 January 1966.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In 1888 the Board reported that water had encroached so far upon the river front that it covered one end of a depot building's foundation. An emergency breakwater of hundreds of barrels of broken cement was thrown together, and the Board asked for a permanent bulwark of large timbers and stone, which was built the next year.[^3]

On 25 July 1913 Keeper Thomas Jacobson went to the assistance of the occupants of a U.S. Navy hydroplane, C-1, which had been disabled in the water, and towed the boat to shore.[^9]

On 29 January 1919 Keeper G. M. Willis Sr. assisted the captain of a schooner that was beached near the station. He helped store the schooner's property in his barn, and furnished lodging for the night to the captain and his crew.[^11][^16]

On 15 March 1923 Willis rendered assistance to the occupants of a house that was destroyed by fire in the vicinity of the light station.[^12] The index for 1924 to 1929 lists "Willis, George M., keeper Point Lookout, assisted airplane 96."[^13] LighthouseFriends dates a disabled plane that landed near the station to 1925, and says its occupants spent the night at the lighthouse.[^3]

## After the keepers

The Bulletin's 1930 article describes the remodeling of the old dwelling for the station's centennial, with a wind-electric generating plant added.[^14] The state dates a 1927 expansion of the dwelling into a duplex, and LighthouseFriends places the remodel into two apartments in 1930.[^1][^3] In 1934 new electrical equipment replaced that plant, which had become inadequate for the station's increased current demands.[^15] LighthouseFriends says the station was transferred to the Coast Guard in 1939, and that a visit by the Commissioner of Lighthouses in May 1939 recommended the retirement of the keeper and his assistant.[^3]

The Navy began buying land around the lighthouse in 1951, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] Late in 1965 a skeletal structure was built off the point, and it worked with the lighthouse until 11 January 1966, when Raymond Hartzel darkened the lighthouse for the last time.[^1] The Archive gives 1965 as the year the light was discontinued.[^4]

The state acquired the lighthouse from the Navy in a land swap in 2006 and began renovations in 2017. It estimates the renovation and exhibits at about $5 million.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is part of Point Lookout State Park. Tours began on 15 May 2025 and run from Thursday through Monday, five days a week; the park planned that schedule until September, with hours depending on seasonal staffing.[^2] Inside are period furniture, an early radio and exhibits about the keepers and their families. The renovated coal and buoy sheds beside the lighthouse can also be shared with the public.[^2] The park is also known for its Civil War history.[^2] LighthouseFriends notes a per-person fee to enter the park.[^3]
