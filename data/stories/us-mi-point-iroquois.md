Point Iroquois Light stands on the highest ground on the point at the eastern end of Lake Superior, where Whitefish Bay meets the St. Marys River, near Brimley, Michigan. The first station there was built in 1855. The brick tower visitors climb today dates from 1870, and the light was shut down in the early 1960s. The Forest Service runs the site and lists a Gnoozhekaaning (Bay Mills Indian Community) exhibit, which its page shows as closed for renovations.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

## The place

The point takes its name from a battle. Terry Pepper's history says "Iroquois Point had received its name in 1662 after the local Ojibwa encountered a band of intruding Iroquois encamped on the Point."[^1] The Forest Service gives the same event as "a battle that took place in 1662 between the local Ojibwa."[^2]

Ships needed a mark at the river mouth. Terry Pepper says the location of the entrance "remained unmarked," and that with the planned opening of the new lock at the Soo in 1855, "the increase in maritime commerce would be both dramatic and immediate."[^1] Congress "appropriated $5,000 for the project on March 3, 1853."[^1]

## Building the light

"Construction began in 1855, and was completed the following year," Terry Pepper writes. The first tower was a "45 foot tall rubble stone tower with a wooden lantern deck" (about 14 m).[^1] LighthouseFriends says the first tower had an iron lantern and a "sixth-order Fresnel lens."[^4] Terry Pepper says it was a "Fourth Order Fresnel lens."[^1] The sources disagree on the order.

The first keeper was Charles Caldwell. Terry Pepper says he "had served for a year as the assistant keeper at Whitefish Point," and was "transferred-in as the station's first keeper."[^1] Terry Pepper dates the first lighting to the night of 8 June 1856. The Forest Service gives 20 September 1857.[^1][^2]

By 1867 the tower and dwelling were in poor shape. Terry Pepper says the wooden lantern deck "was in danger of collapse." In 1868 an inspector judged that the only viable remedy was "the complete replacement of both structures."[^1] Terry Pepper says the estimate was that "the two new structures could be built for $18,000." LighthouseFriends says Congress "allocated the requested amount on July 15, 1870," for a new lighthouse estimated at $18,000.[^1][^4]

The new tower went up in 1870. Terry Pepper describes it as "50 feet in height from the limestone foundation to the bottom of the iron gallery" (about 15 m).[^1] The Forest Service says "The tower is 65 feet high" (about 20 m).[^2] LighthouseFriends gives "nearly sixty-two feet tall" (about 19 m).[^4] Terry Pepper says the lantern housed "the Fourth Order Fresnel from the original tower." LighthouseFriends says the 1870 lantern held a fourth-order lens by L. Sautter & Cie. The two accounts differ on where the lens came from.[^1][^4] The dwelling was "two-story," with a covered walkway to the tower.[^1][^4]

A fog bell, "struck a single blow every twelve seconds," was established at the station on 25 October 1884, LighthouseFriends says.[^4] Terry Pepper dates a stand-alone bell tower to 1885.[^1] The Lighthouse Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 says "The fog-signal house and duplicate machinery were built," and Terry Pepper says the new signal was "officially placed into service on October 31, 1890."[^6][^1] The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1894 says the whistle "was in operation some 414 hours" that year. The same report lists a circular iron oil house built at the station.[^7] In 1896 the old bell tower was taken down and moved to Grand Marais.[^8]

## The keepers

Simon Teeple, who kept the light from 1857 to 1862, and Hosea Smith, who kept it from 1869 to 1887, both had wives who kept house at the lighthouse. A historical marker at the site says Jane Teeple and Martha Smith "kept house and raised families at the lighthouse when their Euro-American husbands served as Keepers."[^3][^5]

In November 1890 the head keeper was Edward Chambers. James Lasley Jr. was appointed acting assistant keeper that month and moved into the cramped dwelling with Chambers and his family.[^1] The fog signal had brought more work. Terry Pepper says that in 1901 keeper Joseph Bishop and his assistant Otto Bufe fed coal into the boilers for "a total of 534 hours, an all-time record for the station."[^1]

Terry Pepper says Inspector Edward H. Gheen "finally took up the interests of the Point Iroquois keepers and their families" in 1902. A six-room addition to the dwelling was finished on 11 November 1905.[^1][^4] LighthouseFriends says the addition created a duplex, with the head keeper in the original half and the assistant in the new one.[^4]

In May 1914 a forest fire threatened the station. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin names "William B. Campbell, keeper, and Lewis B. Curtis, second assistant keeper," who worked with the crew of the tender Aspen to fight it.[^9] The USLHS roster gives the keeper as "William L. Campbell," so the two sources disagree on his middle initial.[^5]

Keeper Elmer Byrnes served through the station's electrification. LighthouseFriends says he "petitioned for a school at Point Iroquois for some time before one was finally approved," and that the one-room school taught six children from the lighthouse families and three from a nearby fisherman's family.[^4] John Soldenski replaced Byrnes as head keeper in 1935. LighthouseFriends attributes the station's stone walls to him, and the marker gives the date as 1937: "Stone walls around yard completed by Chief John Soldenski and friends."[^4][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Ice could trap shipping near the point. The Detroit Times reported from Sault Ste. Marie on 29 April 1909 that "Forty boats were fast in the ice" the night before, between Point Iroquois and a second point that the scanned text reads as "Point Aux Plus."[^10] LighthouseFriends says Keeper Byrnes and his assistants would take horses out on the winter ice to fish for herring or gather blocks of ice for the ice house, and that "on two occasions, the horses broke through."[^4]

The L'Anse Sentinel printed a short item in January 1893 that its scanned text credits to a "Democrat." It says residents at Point Iroquois had picked up several barrels of oil thrown off the "ill fated Northerner." The item does not say when or where the ship was lost.[^11]

The marker says that after late fall storms keepers "even walked the beach searching for wreckage and frozen bodies."[^3]

## After the keepers

The fog signal changed more than once. Terry Pepper says the steam whistles were removed in 1926 and replaced with diaphone fog signals.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates an air siren to 1927 and a diaphone to 1933, and says "Electricity, provided by electric generators, was also extended to the tower and dwelling in 1933."[^4]

Terry Pepper says the light's characteristic was changed in 1922 "to only 4 seconds, with a flash of only 0.8 seconds duration."[^1]

The Coast Guard closed the station in 1962. LighthouseFriends says Gerald V. Cousins "served as the last Officer-in-Charge," and left at the end of July 1962, "when the station was boarded up and closed."[^4] The marker dates the decommissioning to 1962, and adds "Out buildings removed."[^3] Terry Pepper says the light was discontinued after a lighted buoy was placed offshore in 1962.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the light was deactivated in 1963, months after Canada's Gros Cap light was established.[^4]

The fourth-order lens came out of the lantern. Terry Pepper dates its removal to 1965 and says it "was placed on display at the Smithsonian Institution."[^1] LighthouseFriends says it was "removed from the lantern room and shipped to the Smithsonian Institution."[^4]

Ownership passed to the Forest Service in 1965. LighthouseFriends says the Forest Service "applied for the lighthouse reservation in 1964 and was awarded the property in 1965."[^4] The marker agrees: "Hiawatha National Forest takes over management of site."[^3] Terry Pepper names the U.S. Park Service as the new owner instead.[^1]

The station buildings were leased to the Bay Mills-Brimley Historical Research Society, which Terry Pepper says "completed a total restoration of the building in 1983."[^1] The marker dates the partnership to 1982, and gives the museum's grand opening as 1988.[^3] The light was placed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1975.[^2]

## Today

The Forest Service says "Climb the spiral staircase to the top of the 65-foot light tower." It lists the tower's hours as "Tuesday- Saturday 11am-4pm," and says the operating season runs "June 9th through October 16th."[^2] The Forest Service lists a Gnoozhekaaning (Bay Mills Indian Community) exhibit as "Currently closed for renovations."[^2] Its directions say to turn west onto Lake Shore Drive and "drive 6.5 miles to the lighthouse."[^2] The page shows "Site Closed," after the close of the season on 16 October.[^2]
