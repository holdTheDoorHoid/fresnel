Cape Meares Lighthouse stands on a headland above Tillamook Bay on the Oregon coast.[^1] It is a squat octagonal tower of brick inside and iron outside, and it is the shortest lighthouse on the Oregon coast. The National Register nomination gives its height as 40 feet, while the Oregon Encyclopedia and the Friends of Cape Meares Lighthouse give 38.[^1][^2][^3] The light was first shown on 1 January 1890. The Coast Guard automated it in 1963, and the state has managed the lighthouse since 1968.[^1][^2][^12]

## The place

The headland has changed names. John Meares, a British trader who sailed the Northwest Coast in 1788, named the northern headland Cape Lookout.[^2] Charts in 1850 and 1853 put that name on a cape ten miles south, and in 1857 George Davidson of the Coast Survey gave the northern headland the name Cape Meares.[^2]

Commerce on Tillamook Bay grew. The nomination says that "shipping by sea increased substantially, thus necessitating the installation of navigational aids to mark" the entrance.[^1] The Army engineer James S. Polhemus surveyed Cape Meares and the neighboring Cape Lookout in 1886 and spent two days on Cape Meares. He found the site hard to supply: "There is no beach or convenient landing place on the Cape where construction materials could be landed."[^1] The Friends' history gives the same survey to a "Captain Powell" of the Corps of Engineers, and says the southern point was free of timber and had a spring that could supply construction water.[^3] The nomination says the southern finger was "noted as a more suitable site," and the Friends' account gives the same point's lack of timber and its spring as reasons.[^1][^3]

A bill introduced by John H. Mitchell in the Senate on 25 January 1886 asked for money to buy a site and build a light at Cape Meares.[^1] The Oregon Encyclopedia says Congress authorized the lighthouse in 1887 and approved $60,000 for it.[^2]

## Building the light

The nomination says that the plans, drawn by Corps of Engineers officer C. F. Powell, were approved in January 1888. Bids were advertised, and Charles B. Duhrkoop of Portland was chosen to build the tower for $2,900.[^1] A kiln was built on the site to fire bricks from local clay, and "Local Native Americans were hired to dig the clay and cut timber for the project."[^1] The outer iron shell came from Willamette Iron Works of Portland, and it reached the site on 1 March 1889 at a cost of $7,800.[^1]

The lens came from Paris. The nomination says it was "hand-ground in 1887 by Henry LaPaute in Paris," and that it was "reportedly only one of two eight-sided lenses in the United States."[^1] It came around Cape Horn, and a hand-operated crane made of spruce lifted it up the cliff to the lantern room.[^1] The lens was so large "that the keeper could walk around in the lens to conduct daily cleaning."[^1]

The dwellings, oil houses, barn and cisterns were built by a second contractor. The nomination calls him "Richard Seaman, a Portland contractor," and says the buildings were finished at a cost of $26,000.[^1] The Oregon Encyclopedia gives the name as Richard Leaman and says he won the $26,000 contract for those buildings.[^2] The Friends name him Robert Seaman and give $33,829 as the total for grading and buildings.[^3] The three sources spell the contractor's name differently and give different totals. The nomination and the Oregon Encyclopedia both name Duhrkoop and his $2,900 tower contract; the Friends do not name him.

Duhrkoop completed the tower, with the lens in place, on 23 December 1889.[^2] The Lighthouse Board's notice to mariners, as the nomination quotes it, gave the focal plane as "223 feet above the mean sea-level." The nomination's own figure is 217 feet above sea level.[^1] The light was first lit on 1 January 1890, with Anthony Miller as head keeper.[^1][^2]

The sources do not agree on what the light showed. The nomination says the signal "alternated between a two second red flash and 60 second white flash." The Board's list of lights in 1899 gives the characteristic as "First order, fixed white, varied by a red flash every minute."[^1][^7] The Friends describe about 30 seconds of fixed white followed by a five-second red flash once a minute.[^3] The story reports all three and does not choose between them.

## The keepers

The nomination says the first keepers "were appointed on November 26, 1889": Anthony W. Miller as head keeper, Andrew Hold as first assistant and Henry York as second assistant. The three men "watched the lamp 24 hours a day on rotating, eight hour shifts."[^1] The roster spells the first assistant's name Andrew P.C. Hald and lists him for 1889 to 1894, and the roster lists Miller as acting principal keeper for 1889 to 1891.[^11]

The clockwork that turned the lens had to be wound "every two and one-half hours," and when it failed "the keeper manually turned the lens until the clockworks could be repaired in the daylight hours."[^1] The Oregon Encyclopedia gives the winding interval as four hours.[^2] The Friends give two and a half hours for a single winding.[^3]

George Hunt is listed as principal keeper from 1892 to 1903 on the roster, and the nomination lists him among early keepers for 1892.[^11][^1] The nomination also names "Mrs. Augusta Hunt" as acting keeper in 1903, and the roster lists her as principal for that year.[^1][^11] The sources agree that a woman had charge of the light in 1903 but differ on her title. Neither says how she was related to George Hunt.

The nomination names a keeper Thomas Ford for 1909.[^1] The roster lists William P. Ford as principal from 1909 to 1918. The two names are most likely the same man, but neither source says so.[^1][^11]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Board's reports of the early 1890s show what the station needed before it had a road.[^4] In its report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891, the Board recorded picket fences around the dwellings and wrote that "A wagon road leading from this station to some public highway is now urgently needed."[^4] The nomination says the road was appropriated in August 1892 and that bids came in too high. The Board's fiscal 1894 report says work "was commenced on a wagon road" to the Tillamook River, and that it was "pushed vigorously until November 20, when funds were exhausted."[^5][^1] The nomination also says that by 20 November 1892 a mile and a half of the road was complete, which cannot be squared with its own statement that work began in 1894. This story reports the Board's version.

The Board's annual report dated 9 November 1895 says that "A work room attached to the tower, built of brick, was partly finished." It adds that "it was impossible, on account of bad roads, to deliver all the materials at this station in time."[^6] The nomination dates the workroom to 1895, says the original was demolished in 1960, and gives 1978 as the year of its reconstruction.[^1]

In March 1894 the Daily Astorian reported on a walk from Tillamook.[^10] A. P. Hald, "first assistant lighthouse keeper at Cape Meares," was one of three men who walked from Tillamook and then came into Astoria by steamer. "They had a terrible experience at Hug Point, and all three narrowly escaped death."[^10]

The gale of 29 January 1921 was, in the Lighthouse Service Bulletin's words, a "record-breaking gale" that damaged stations "from Yaquina Head to Cape Flattery." At Yaquina Head the damage was "blowing off the shingles" from the dwelling, and the Bulletin says the same damage happened at Cape Meares.[^9]

By 1922 the station still had no fog signal.[^8] The Commissioner of Lighthouses, recommending one for Cape Lookout, wrote: "Though there is a light at Cape Meares, 8 miles distant, there is no fog signal."[^8]

## After the keepers

An incandescent oil-vapor lamp replaced the five-wick kerosene lamp in 1910.[^1][^2] The station was electrified in 1934, when two generators were installed, one at the base of the tower and one near the workshop.[^1][^2]

The Coast Guard built a new automated light on the bench above the old tower in 1963, and the old light was decommissioned.[^1][^2] The nomination says that after the new beacon was in place "the two keepers at Cape Meares were assigned duties elsewhere, bringing an end to the station's 73 years of service."[^1] The Oregon Encyclopedia gives the date of automation as 1 April 1963.[^2]

The Coast Guard discussed demolishing the tower.[^2] Local residents resisted, and in 1964 the Coast Guard leased the tower to Tillamook County.[^2][^1] The Oregon Encyclopedia reports that the empty lighthouse was badly vandalized; the four ruby bullseye lenses were stolen, and it says that one was recovered in a Portland drug raid in 1984, one was left at the Tillamook County Pioneer Museum in 1986, and a third turned up a week later at a park manager's house.[^2]

In 1968 the lease passed to the Oregon State Parks and Recreation Department, which tore down the dilapidated keepers' dwellings and renovated the tower.[^2] The Oregon Encyclopedia says the department also built a replica of the 1895 workroom. The nomination and the Friends' history date the workroom's reconstruction to 1978.[^1][^2][^3] The lighthouse reopened to the public on Memorial Day in 1980.[^2]

Gunshots damaged the lantern room and lens in January 2010.[^2] The Oregon Encyclopedia says the lenses were glued and stabilized, and that 2017 replacements of the red panels were made of red plastic glass.[^2] The Coast Guard turned off the modern light on 25 June 2014, leaving the tower dark for the first time in 124 years.[^2] The state office signed the National Register nomination on 31 March 1993.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is part of Cape Meares State Scenic Viewpoint, a park of about 240 acres that the Oregon Parks and Recreation Department runs with the volunteer Friends of Cape Meares Lighthouse.[^2][^3] The park is surrounded by the Cape Meares National Wildlife Refuge.[^1][^12]

The state's park page says the lighthouse and its gift shop are open from 11 a.m. to 4 p.m. daily between Memorial Day and 30 September.[^12] In winter the lighthouse is closed but the grounds stay open. Tower tours are limited to five visitors at a time, with no reservations.[^12] The Friends say the working light is now in a building next to the historic tower.[^3]
