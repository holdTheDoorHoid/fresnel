
Red Island Light (phare de l'Île-Rouge) stands on a low islet of pebbles in the middle of the St. Lawrence, opposite the mouth of the Saguenay, where the river divides around a long reef.[^1][^45] Its round cut-stone tower was built in 1847 and 1848 and has shown a light since November 1848.[^2][^3] Keepers lived on the island until the light was automated in 1988.[^4] The light still flashes, but the keepers' houses were dismantled in 2020.[^5]

## The place

The islet is about 500 metres long and 200 metres wide.[^6] Its highest point stands 5.2 m (17 ft) above the water. East of it lies the reef, the first obstacle that splits the river into a north and a south channel.[^1][^45]

The Amis des phares describe a place that was hard on ships. Fog banks near the Saguenay could hide the reef, and navigators in the fog had trouble telling the bow of their ship from its stern.[^7] A cold current from the Labrador side rises onto the shoals, so the water stays close to freezing even in midsummer.[^7] Trinity House laid a black buoy on the east end of the reef in 1833, and lit the tower in 1848.[^7]

Pelletier and Dionne compiled 160 groundings, collisions and incidents near the island. Of these, 133 happened in the nineteenth century, and 138 of the 160 involved sailing vessels.[^8][^9]

In late 1846 Charles-Eusèbe Casgrain, the deputy commissioner of public works, wrote to Trinity House about the losses on the lower river and asked for more lights there.[^10][^11]

## Building the light

Trinity House of Quebec, formed in May 1805 on the model of Britain's Trinity House, chose the site.[^10][^6] Its three stone towers of the 1840s on the lower river, Pilier-de-Pierre, Île Bicquette and Red Island, were designed by Charles Atherton, an English-born civil engineer who was in Canada only in 1842 and 1843.[^10] The national register calls Red Island "one of the first cylindrical stone towers built before Confederation".[^12]

The contract went to Joseph Archer Sr, a master carpenter, mason and general contractor who had built the southwest point light on Anticosti in 1831. It was signed on 20 February 1847.[^11] A widely repeated story that the stone came from Scottish ship's ballast is, the Amis des phares say, wrong. The contract specified stone from the Deschambault quarries.[^11]

On 2 July 1847 the superintendent, William Smith, reported that the workmen had dug 5.2 m (17 ft) without reaching rock. Smith ordered 1.8 m (6 ft) more, and the workmen found only water. The tower therefore stands on a stone foundation more than 6 m (20 ft) deep.[^13]

The lantern and the optical apparatus came from England and reached the island on 12 October 1847.[^13] Work stopped for the winter with only the base course laid. Almost all of the Deschambault stone for the tower had been landed on the island before the next spring.[^13][^2]

Work resumed in June 1848. On 11 August Smith wrote that the tower would soon reach its third storey, and on 24 October the red lantern was set on top.[^2] A lighting specialist and Augustin Dufour, the future keeper, installed a catoptric system of 24 oil lamps behind reflectors 53 cm (21 in) across.[^2][^14]

The tower is 15.5 m (51 ft) of masonry with a 4.6 m (15 ft) lantern, 20.1 m (66 ft) in all. Its walls are lined inside with firebrick, and three projecting stone courses were meant to keep water off the walls.[^2]

In 1848 the tower was the only building on the site. It served as light, store and living quarters, although the contract had also called for a house for the keeper.[^3] The light was officially shown from 11 November 1848.[^3]

The bulletin dates a brick-and-wood house, joined to the tower by a covered passage, to 1850.[^4][^15] The Québec heritage record gives 1848 for the original house, a date the bulletin does not share. It says the keeper's house built in 1965 replaced that original residence.[^6]

## The keepers

The board first considered a man from Les Éboulements, Épiphane Tremblay. It dropped him when it found that he could not walk without help.[^14] The governor then proposed Augustin Dufour. Dufour's salary was 100 pounds a year, part of which had to pay an assistant, and he received 40 pounds a year for water and firewood.[^14] There was no school for keepers. The bulletin says the only way to learn was beside an experienced keeper, so the board suggested he spend eight to ten days on Île Verte first.[^14]

Dufour's first season went badly. Smith and the lamp specialist left the island on 7 November 1848. When they reached the Rivière-du-Loup landing, they found the light had gone out, and the board sent them back with Gilbert Lindsay, a 20-year-old from Île Verte.[^14][^16] Dufour's health was also poor, and Lindsay fell ill and left the island by the end of November.[^16]

On 10 December 1848 Dufour wrote that he had put the light out as instructed and had left the next day, leaving the assistant, Walter, and a young man, Nicolas Pelletier, in charge. He complained that the lamps leaked oil, and asked that Lindsay take his post. The board accepted his resignation. Dufour came back on 4 January 1849 and kept the post through that year.[^17] He left in 1850, and Hubert Fraser, who came from Berthier with a large family, took over.[^17][^18]

Fraser's first winter in the new house was hard. He wrote that in cold weather the water froze within seven or eight feet of the stove. On 16 December the family of thirteen gave up the house and took refuge in the tower.[^15]

On 11 October 1860 Hubert Fraser and his son Daniel, aged 22, drowned on a boat trip to the south shore for supplies. Such trips were a routine part of the keeper's work.[^15][^19][^18]

Gilbert Lindsay, son of Robert-Noël Lindsay, the keeper at Île Verte, then took the post. The bulletin calls him the station's third keeper and says he had been an assistant on Île Verte.[^15] It does not say whether he is the young Gilbert Lindsay of Île Verte whom the board named as Dufour's assistant in 1848, and this story does not assume that he is.[^17] The bulletin says he stayed through 1861 and then went back to Île Verte. LighthouseFriends has him at Red Island until 1867, when he replaced his father at Green Island (Île Verte).[^15][^18]

Pierre-Elzéar Fraser, another son of Hubert, replaced Lindsay as the fourth keeper of the island. He kept the light until he died in December 1889.[^15][^20]

Pierre-Théodore Fraser took the post in April 1890. LighthouseFriends gives 1889 as his start.[^20][^18] In 1891 he married Angèle Caroline, known as Cary, a daughter of Gilbert Lindsay. She died on the island in April 1903. He retired in 1927 and died in 1931 at 65.[^20]

Théobald Fraser, of Île Verte, succeeded him and kept the light until 1942.[^20][^18]

In 1945 a veteran took the post. Gilbert Fraser had been wounded on 5 July 1944 in Normandy and had lost his right forearm.[^21][^22] His family lived on the island each year from April to December.[^22] A radiobeacon with a 38 m (124 ft) antenna was added to his duties.[^22]

Armand Lafrance, who worked under Fraser, recalled that there were only two men on the island. Among their work he named the radiobeacon, the foghorn, two generators and the fuel drums, which he said he moved himself.[^23] Fraser moved to the Cap-de-Bon-Désir light, on the north shore, in 1958.[^23]

Maurice Thibault became keeper in August 1958 and left after the 1959 holiday season. His brother Patrice was appointed assistant at the same time.[^24] Antoine Therrien, who came from the Île Blanche pillar, became keeper in spring 1960.[^24] He left the station in 1978, at 65.[^25]

Patrice Thibault joined in 1972 as a keeper on the second crew, became principal keeper in 1978, and resigned in 1987.[^25]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A lightship also marked the reef. In 1871 an iron vessel painted red, with RED ISLAND LIGHT SHIP lettered on each side, was moored northeast of the island.[^18][^26] LighthouseFriends dates her mooring to 1 July; the bulletin gives 9 July as the day her light first showed.[^18][^26] Its six-reflector light stood on the foremast, 10 m above the deck, and its steam whistle sounded for ten seconds every minute in fog.[^26]

In a gale on 18 November 1873, the lightship was driven aground on White Island Reef. LighthouseFriends says the captain and crew spent twenty-four hours on the wreck before they boarded a boat and made their way through ice to the south shore.[^18] Captain Joseph Lévesque drowned on 16 October 1876 while crossing from the shore to his ship.[^26][^18] His brother David took command for the rest of that season.[^18] Another lightship master, Silvio Petitgrew, drowned on 9 September 1899.[^26]

The keepers also helped ships. In November 1853 ice pushed the waterlogged barque Electric onto the reef. Hubert Fraser, with his sons Daniel and Elzéar, freed her, and the two young men took her to Tadoussac, where she wintered safely.[^27]

In September 1872 the Sailor's Home, loaded with timber, ran onto the reef in a gale. Captain Lévesque of the lightship helped her get free.[^28]

In April 1858 the Pride of Canada was lost on the reef in a snowstorm. The bulletin says that thirteen men left in a boat and perished, since only the boat was found. At each low tide the rest of the crew unloaded cargo to lighten the iron-hulled ship, and she reached Quebec on 14 June.[^27][^28]

On 6 July 1901 the collier Louisburg, towing a barge, struck the Red Island shoal and was badly damaged. She was patched at Quebec and then repaired in dry dock at Halifax.[^29][^28]

On 10 October 1901 the lightship was moved about a mile south, to the 20-fathom line on the south edge of the bank. St. Lawrence pilots claimed that she would serve heavy ships better there in fog.[^30]

On 30 October 1902 the steamer Iberian went ashore on Red Island Reef on her way to Quebec. The report blamed the pilot for mistaking the Red Island lightship for the White Island lightship, and suspended his certificate for eighteen months.[^31]

In 1905 the lightship was fitted with a submarine bell that struck its number, 3, every fourteen seconds.[^32]

In November 1906 the barque Magda was destroyed in a storm, and all her crew were lost.[^19]

On 2 July 1907 the steamer Montrose went aground on the reef in fog.[^33][^34] A court of investigation later found that the pilot had continued at full speed, and on too fine a course, after the lightship's fog signal was heard ahead. It fined him $100, and it admonished the master.[^35] The accident list and the court both place the grounding at Red Island, in fog.[^33][^35]

In 1999 the cruise ship Norwegian Sky, leaving the Saguenay, was pushed onto the reef by the strong current called the bœuf.[^8]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1988. Keeper Luc Caron, at the Prince Shoal light, asked for Charles-Hector Fraser from Île Verte to help shut the station, and Fraser then went on to Red Island.[^36] The bulletin notes that one more light remained to be closed, and Red Island was closed after the shoal light.[^36]

The gulls took over the island. Empty houses without residents or upkeep deteriorated quickly, the bulletin says, especially in the salt air. After people had deserted the island for about a decade, life returned to the old buildings.[^37] Three enthusiasts from Tadoussac, interested in maritime anthropology, formed an association to save the site.[^37] Under agreements with Fisheries and Oceans Canada, it set up the CIMEVIR, a centre for the interpretation and enhancement of Île Rouge. Its first task was to restore and furnish the houses. The chronology dates the centre to 1990.[^37][^38] Its first visitors came in 1999.[^37] LighthouseFriends says overnight stays in the keepers' houses were offered for a few years in the late 1990s.[^18] The venture ended after the 2000 season. The bulletin says that supply problems, weather and cancelled crossings had defeated it.[^37]

Other changes came in the 1960s and later. An electronic fog detector replaced the manned fog signal in 1964. In 1965 the 1850 house was replaced by two new houses. In 1966 the lamp went electric, supplied by diesel generators that the bulletin says used 400 to 500 barrels a year.[^39]

Under the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act, in force from 2010, the federal government offered unwanted lighthouses to municipalities and non-profit groups.[^38][^40] The Amis des phares argue that the Act was not really designed to save heritage lights. No other body applied for Red Island, and by 2015 the light was, in their words, an orphan.[^40]

In 2018 the government announced that all the buildings except the light would be destroyed.[^38] A 2019 tender called for their demolition. Its 177-page specification called for a simple fence to limit access to the light, whose stated purpose was to warn visitors of the danger of falling debris from the structure.[^41][^40] The Amis des phares say the buildings were dismantled in 2020.[^5]

## Today

The light still shows. The Coast Guard list gives a white flash every ten seconds, with one second of light and nine of darkness, from a cylindrical tower.[^42]

The Amis des phares name Fisheries and Oceans Canada as owner. The Lighthouse Directory and LighthouseFriends name the Canadian Coast Guard.[^43][^44][^18] The island lies inside the Saguenay–St. Lawrence Marine Park.[^18] It can be reached only by boat, and LighthouseFriends lists the grounds as open and the tower as closed.[^44][^18] The Amis des phares describe the island as a refuge for birds.[^5] The tower is closed to visitors.[^44]
