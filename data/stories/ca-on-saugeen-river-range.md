Two small wooden towers mark the mouth of the Saugeen River at Southampton, Ontario, on Lake Huron: a front light at the end of a pier and a rear light up on a ridge on the river's north bank. Lined up, they once led fishing boats through a narrow dredged channel to the wharfs, and in 2012 they were among the first lighthouses designated under Canada's Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act.[^1][^2]

## The place

The Saugeen River enters Lake Huron at Southampton, in Bruce County. The Department of Marine and Fisheries' report for the fiscal year 1900-01 describes a narrow dredged channel with 2.4 m (8 feet) of water at most, running up to the end of the pier; vessels had to leave the pier on the port hand when entering and then steer gradually for the south bank and the wharfs.[^2] The first light here was meant for fishing boats: it was set up in 1883 to guide them into the river.[^1]

## Building the light

In 1883 a lantern was hoisted on a mast set on a crib built on the north breakwater pier, fifty feet (15 m) from its outer end. Murdoch McLeod was appointed keeper at $80 a year, and the light was green.[^1] In 1893 a seventh-order lantern replaced the original small lens, and the light became white.[^1]

Around 1900 local fishermen put up a second light of their own. The 1900-01 report says it had been kept "for one or two years" by the local fishermen on the north bank, 213 m (700 feet) from the government light, burning a fixed green light 14 m (45 feet) above the water on a mast over a white slatwork beacon. Together the two lights, in line, led vessels up the channel. The report added that it was shown only in the fishing season and that the government took no responsibility for it.[^2]

In the fiscal year 1902-03 the Department took over the rear light and replaced both mast lanterns with enclosed towers; Lighthouse Friends dates this to 1903.[^3][^1] The report calls them "wooden buildings, square in plan, with sloping sides", painted white, each 9.4 m (31 feet) from base to the top of the lantern ventilator. The front tower stood on a cribwork block on the breakwater, 12 m (40 feet) from the pier's end, showing a fixed green light 11 m (36 feet) above the lake that the report expected to be visible 4 miles from all points of approach by water. The rear tower stood where the mast had been, 213 m (700 feet) inside the front light. John McAulay built both under contract for $1,085.[^3]

The Department's report for 1906-07 records the rear lighthouse being moved 503 m (1,650 feet) back along the line of range onto a stone foundation on the crest of the ridge on the river's north bank, so that it stood 716 m (2,350 feet) from the front tower with its light 19 m (61 feet) above the lake. The work was done by day labour and cost $110.35.[^4] Lighthouse Friends places the move in 1907.[^1] At the opening of navigation in 1913 the lights changed from fixed green to fixed red, and later that year a fifth-order lens was fitted in the front tower and a reflector in the rear.[^1]

A fog signal came to the river mouth in 1905, though not on the range lights: a steam whistle was fitted to the town waterworks building, which stood on the lake shore a short distance south-west of the river mouth, and the municipality was to maintain it. It gave two blasts of five seconds every minute.[^5][^1] The whistle was discontinued in 1936, when the range keeper was given a hand horn to sound in answer to a vessel's signal.[^1] The range lights were electrified in 1939, and an electric tyfon horn sounding a two-second blast every ten seconds was fitted in the front tower.[^1]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends, citing the Department of Marine's annual reports, lists the head keepers as Murdoch McLeod (1883 to 1899), Donald McAulay (1899 to 1909), Angus McAulay (1909 to 1928), Joseph Granville (1928 to 1941), Lydia Granville (1941 to 1954), John Angus McLean (1954 to 1958) and John MacDonald (1958 to 1977).[^1]

McLeod's tenure had its troubles. He bought a house and outbuildings near the light, only to find the land belonged to the Department of Indian Affairs; in 1886 the Department of Marine stepped in and helped him buy it for fifty dollars.[^1] In December 1897 the Department recommended that his services be dispensed with for insubordination and refusal to obey the Inspector of Lights. Lighthouse Friends infers the matter was settled, since he seems to have stayed until his death in February 1899, when his widow received the customary two months' salary, $13.33.[^1]

## After the keepers

In 1989 the Coast Guard began, without notice, to strip the wooden shingles from the rear tower and cover it in aluminum siding. Townspeople held a protective vigil around the clock for three days, and the plan was dropped.[^1]

On 3 August 2012 the two towers were designated heritage lighthouses, among the first under the 2008 Act.[^1] The statement of significance for the front light in the Canadian Register of Historic Places calls it a very good example of the "pepper pot" design, and notes it was built a year before Southampton was incorporated as a town.[^6]

## Today

Both lights still appear in the Canadian Coast Guard List of Lights, the front light on a cribwork block near the outer end of the breakwater.[^7] Lighthouse Friends gives the owner as the Town of Saugeen Shores and reports the grounds open and the tower closed, with the pier reached from a parking area off South Rankin Street.[^1] The towers date from 1903, as the Department's report shows,[^3] though the station itself was first lit in 1883.[^1]
