St. Andrews North Point Lighthouse, usually called the Pendlebury Lighthouse after the family that kept it for almost a century, is a wooden octagonal tower built in 1833 on Indian Point, at the entrance to St. Andrews harbour in New Brunswick. It stopped being the working light in 1938. A modern light on a skeletal tower now shows about 75 m (250 ft) to the south.[^1][^2][^4]

## The place

St. Andrews was founded in 1783 by Loyalists who left the United States after the Revolutionary War. It stands at the tip of a peninsula in Passamaquoddy Bay, at the entrance to the St. Croix River, and it was an important seaport in the early days of the Colony of New Brunswick.[^1]

Duties levied on vessels that called at New Brunswick ports helped pay for lighthouses. Lighthouse Friends reports that £784 was collected at Saint Andrew in 1832, and that £196 of the resulting fund was spent in 1833 on "erecting a Light House on Indian Point, at the Eastern entrance of Saint Andrew's Harbour."[^1] Indian Point lies beside a busy wharf on the north-east side of the harbour.[^2]

## Building the light

The first tower was a wooden octagon topped by a lantern room, and its interior was left unfinished, which gave little insulation in winter. Its original lantern was judged "utterly unfit." Four lamps in 15-inch reflectors were hard to tend in so small a room, and the tower was not well secured to the wharf: shaking the railing could make the whole tower totter.[^1] Surveyor L. Donaldson visited in 1839 and recommended plastering or limewashing the interior, anchoring the tower to the wharf and moving the lantern from Head Harbour Lighthouse to St. Andrews. The foundation was secured in 1840, and in 1841 the Head Harbour lantern was placed on top.[^1]

The light burned oil. In 1857 its four lamps consumed 179 gallons of seal oil and 32 gallons of porpoise oil. Seal oil was the cheaper of the two, but porpoise oil had to be burned for roughly four months in the coldest part of the year.[^1]

## The keepers

James Smith, the first keeper, served for nearly a decade until he was removed in 1842 for misconduct. The Commissioners of Lighthouses at Saint Andrews, as Lighthouse Friends quotes them, wrote that "his confirmed habits of intoxication left us no course to pursue."[^1]

John Pendlebury transferred from Machias Seal Island in 1842 to become second keeper, and the family kept the light for nearly a century.[^1] The two sources differ on the dates. Lighthouse Friends gives John Pendlebury until 1855 and his son George A. Pendlebury from 1855 to 1889.[^1] The Canadian Register of Historic Places says John "served as the keeper until his death in 1853," after which his son George operated the light.[^2] The sources do not settle the question.

William J. Pendlebury kept the light from 1889 to 1916, and the Department's list of lightkeepers for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1905 names "Pendlebury, Wm. J" at St. Andrews.[^1][^6] Mary A. Pendlebury is listed for 1916, and Emma Pendlebury for 1917 to 1938.[^1] Lighthouse Friends says Emma had been keeper for twenty-one years when the light was closed, and the Register calls her the last lightkeeper.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

A storehouse was built near the light in 1843, and a small boat was provided so the keeper could help people in distress. Lighthouse Friends quotes the official reason: there had been "loss of life by the upsetting of boats near the station" in daylight, which "might have been averted had a boat been available."[^1]

The Saxby Gale of October 1869 "carried away a portion of the foundation block" of the wharf the light stood on, and repairs were needed at once.[^1] On 23 November 1874 a heavy gale blew the tower over. It was not seriously damaged, and after the old block was repaired and raised, the tower was returned to its place in 1875. In the interval it had been moved to the public wharf, where the Marine Department was subject to rent.[^1]

## After the keepers

The light was deactivated in 1938.[^1][^2][^4] Until the Second World War the St. Andrews Yacht Club used it as a clubroom.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends says the Department's annual report for 1876, the year the Sand Reef light was built offshore, included the remark that "the inhabitants of St. Andrews still cling to the old buildings and appear unwilling to let them go."[^1]

In the early 1960s part of the seawall collapsed and the tower tilted sharply. After an appeal from the Charlotte County Historical Society, the owner shored up the foundation.[^1] A private owner had held the tower for years, and in 2002 he transferred it to the St. Andrews Civic Trust.[^1] In July 2002 the tower was moved 15 m (50 ft) away from the wharf, which was deteriorating, and a storm the following winter severely damaged the wharf.[^1][^4]

The lantern room was removed in 2003 and sent to a local community college, where it could be copied, since it was too far gone to repair.[^1] In 2010 the seawall was rebuilt and the tower was restored and returned to its original location, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1] The Register says it now sits only a few metres from its original site.[^2] A newly made lantern room was placed on top on 24 October 2011.[^1] The Civic Trust describes the tower as designated a Local Historic Place for its architecture and its link to the family.[^3] It is also on the Canadian Register of Historic Places.[^2]

## Today

The St. Andrews Civic Trust owns the tower, and the Town of St. Andrews manages the site.[^4] The grounds are open, but the tower is closed.[^1][^4] There is no parking at the lighthouse. Lighthouse Friends advises parking near the corner of Augustus and Patrick streets and walking south along Patrick Street.[^1]

The light that shows now is a separate structure. The Coast Guard's List of Lights gives St. Andrews (North Point) as a red flash every four seconds, from a square skeleton tower with a red and white rectangular daymark, with a focal height of 7.0 m and a nominal range of four nautical miles. The tower is 12.1 m high, and the light operates all year.[^5] Rowlett's directory places it on a skeletal tower offshore, about 75 m (250 ft) south of the old lighthouse.[^4]
