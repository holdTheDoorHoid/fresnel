The Ponce de Leon Inlet Light stands on the north side of Ponce de Leon Inlet in Volusia County, Florida, with the Atlantic on one side of the property and the Halifax River on the other.[^1] Its brick tower, about 176 feet (54 m) high, was described in its National Historic Landmark nomination as the second tallest brick light tower in the United States.[^2] It is the second tower to serve this inlet: the first, built on the opposite shore in 1835, fell about a year after it was finished.

## The place

The inlet was Mosquito Inlet, and the light the Mosquito Inlet Light, until the name changed in 1927 (the nomination and the museum say 1927; Lighthouse Friends says 1926).[^2][^4][^6] Oranges, rice, cotton, hides, lumber and other goods were shipped from plantations through the inlet, and in 1830 thirty-eight ship owners and plantation owners of Mosquito County and New Smyrna signed a memorial to Congress saying they were "suffering in considerable privations, and difficulties, in the trade to this quarter in consequence of there being no Light House at Mosquito Inlet."[^2]

The harbour itself was treacherous. The Light-House Board wrote in 1870 that "no soundings can be relied on" and that the inlet might open or close once or more each year, and that "the wrecks lying on or near the bar give a practical illustration of the uncertainty of the channel."[^2] The Board's engineer judged that the trade through the inlet did not justify a major light, but the coast needed one somewhere in the 95 miles between Cape Canaveral and St. Augustine, and Mosquito Inlet could serve as both a coastal and a harbour light.[^2]

## Building the light

**The first tower.** Congress approved $11,000 on 30 June 1834.[^3] Winslow Lewis, the Boston builder, was awarded the contract, and the station was finished in February 1835 at a cost of $7,494: a 45-foot (14 m) round brick tower with eleven lamps and reflectors, and a one-storey brick keeper's house.[^3][^2] John Rodman, the customs collector at St. Augustine, did not trust the contractor, and after he inspected the tower in August 1835 he reported, according to the museum's account, that it was the worst workmanship he had ever seen.[^3]

William H. Williams, the son of a local plantation owner, was appointed keeper on 10 March 1835.[^3] The nomination gives his salary as $450.[^2] The light was never lit: oil for the lamps was never sent, and Williams kept the silvered reflectors in a trunk indoors to stop them tarnishing.[^3][^2] In May he complained to Rodman that the well had gone dry, the kitchen chimney smoked, and the house had neither porch nor shutters, though gales were, as he put it, peculiar to the place.[^3]

On 21 October 1835 a storm that the museum says lasted seven days struck while Williams was away serving on a grand jury in St. Augustine; his wife and children were in the house. On 4 November he wrote that he had "found myself a beggar having lost everything in the house," the family escaping with their lives, and that where the house had stood was now beach.[^3] The nomination says the washed-out ground also undermined the tower, which began to lean.[^2]

Then came war. The museum dates the Seminole raid led by Coacoochee to 25 December 1835, the nomination to 26 December.[^3][^2] The raiders burned open the door and smashed windows on their way up the stairs, but found no oil with which to set the tower alight.[^3] The nomination says the raiders took at least one lamp reflector, and records a tradition that Coacoochee wore a reflector as a headdress at the Battle of Dunlawton; Lighthouse Friends doubts this, since the reflectors were thought to have been lost in the October storm.[^2][^6] With the area at war nobody could repair or shore up the tower, and it fell in April 1836.[^3][^2] Congress appropriated $7,000 in March 1837 to rebuild on a new site, but the money returned to the Treasury in 1839; a request from the Florida legislature in 1847 went unanswered.[^6][^2]

Williams joined a band of citizen soldiers, the Mosquito Roarers, later sat on the territory's Legislative Council, became the first sheriff of the county (by then renamed Orange County), and died in 1845.[^3]

**The second tower.** The Light-House Board asked for $60,000 for a 150-foot tower in 1870 and repeated the request each year through 1874.[^2] By 1882 the estimate had risen to $200,000. Congress authorized $30,000 for study and plans on 7 August 1882 and a further $30,000 on 3 March 1883.[^2] The site chosen was on the north side of the inlet, because the inlet was moving south and a tower there should avoid the fate of the first; ten acres were bought for $400 from Bartola C. Pacetti and his wife Martha.[^2]

Work began in 1884 with a landing, workmen's quarters, workshops and a mule-drawn tramway.[^2] Orville E. Babcock, engineer of the fifth and sixth lighthouse districts, was drowned off the inlet with three others while supervising the new light; Lighthouse Friends and the museum both record it.[^6][^4] The date is not settled: Lighthouse Friends gives 2 June 1884, while a telegram datelined 2 June and printed in the *Indianapolis Journal* said the men were drowned on Saturday, which would be 31 May.[^6][^15] The *New-York Tribune* of 4 June named Babcock, his clerk Levi P. Luckey, Benjamin P. Suter of Washington and a seaman as the dead, and said that the *Pharos* had met a north-east gale, and that the "probability" was that the party had tried to go ashore in a small boat that was swamped in the breakers; only Babcock's body had been recovered.[^14] The foundation was begun on 7 June.[^2]

Money arrived in instalments, $40,000 in March 1885, $50,000 in August 1886 and $20,000 in March 1887, and the 1885 grant came so late that work waited until after the summer heat.[^2] By the end of 1886 the tower stood 51 feet (16 m) high with 470,000 bricks laid, and five of the schooners carrying supplies had been wrecked.[^2] The nomination also credits the district's construction superintendent with an adjustable working platform, held in holes left in the outer wall every 10 feet and filled in when the platform came down, and says the method became standard for later brick towers.[^2]

The tower is a double wall of brick, an outer wall tapering from 32 feet (9.8 m) at the base and an inner wall holding a constant 12-foot (3.7 m) diameter, joined by cross-walls like the spokes of a wheel. A cast-iron stair of 194 steps reaches the watch room and ten more iron steps lead to the lantern.[^2] The brickwork was thought so fine that the tower was left red-washed instead of painted.[^2] Cracks in the entrance steps and the west wall may date from the earthquake of 31 August 1886, the nomination says, judging by old photographs.[^2] The designer is given as F. Hopkinson Smith.[^2] The station also received three keepers' dwellings and an oil house, among the first and largest kerosene stores in the country.[^2]

The first-order Fresnel lens, made by Barbier and Fenestre of Paris in 1867, was lit for the first time on 1 November 1887.[^4][^2]

## The keepers

The first keeper of the new light was William Rowlinski, born in Russia in 1833, who had settled in Charleston, South Carolina, and fought for the Confederacy. The museum says he was named acting keeper on 21 October 1887 and principal keeper on the last day of the year at $720 a year; the nomination's keeper list gives 13 October 1887 as his start.[^5][^2] The first assistant was Hardie Bryan.[^2] Rowlinski found it hard to get on with his assistants, and in 1893 he was swapped with the keeper of Georgetown, South Carolina.[^5][^6] The archive roster lists six different men in the two assistant posts between 1887 and 1890.[^1] Rowlinski resigned in 1902 and moved to a house near his old lighthouse, where he lived until his death in 1914.[^5][^4]

Thomas Patrick O'Hagan arrived on 1 December 1893 with his wife Julia and four children, and seven more were born at the station.[^5] He once admitted that many of his log entries were in Julia's hand, because her writing was far better than his.[^5] He left for Amelia Island in 1905.[^5]

John Lindquist, born in Sweden in 1861, who had gone to sea at fifteen, kept the light from 1905 to 1924.[^5] The museum says he built his own lifesaving boat and made important rescues, and won the efficiency pennant and star in 1914 for the best station in the district.[^5] The Bureau of Lighthouses' bulletin lists Mosquito Inlet among the stations holding an efficiency flag for the 1915 fiscal year.[^8] On 20 February 1915 the bulletin has Lindquist and the station's first assistant helping save the yacht *Niagara*, which had run ashore a mile south of the light and was pounding in the breakers.[^8] On 10 May 1923 he and first assistant Richard Heisser were officially commended for rescuing five people from the disabled motor boat *Hyrandale*.[^10] Lighthouse Friends says that on 26 October 1919 the first assistant, Joseph B. Davis, died of a heart attack on the stairs while climbing to light the lamp. When the light did not come on, the second assistant, Benjamin F. Stone, went up, found him, carried him down and then lit the light.[^6]

Charles L. Sisson followed in 1924. In autumn that year the Bureau commended him for keeping watch for smugglers with the Coast Guard and for putting up the crew of the cutter *Yamacraw*, whose launch had engine trouble, for a night at the station.[^11][^5] The museum's history credits Sisson with bringing electricity to the keepers' houses in 1923, but the same museum and the archive roster both start his term in 1924, so the date is uncertain.[^4][^5][^1] John Belton Butler took over in 1926, and is described by the museum as often commended for rescuing small boats; in December 1932 his family watched President Hoover pass on the yacht *Sequoia*.[^5] Edward Lockwood Meyer, who had been first assistant here from 1926 to 1930 according to the archive roster, returned as principal keeper in 1937. When the Lighthouse Service merged into the Coast Guard in 1939 he enlisted and stayed as officer in charge until December 1943.[^5][^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

At the start of January 1897 the writer Stephen Crane, bound for Cuba on the filibustering tug *Commodore*, survived her sinking in a dinghy. In his account for the *New York Press* he wrote: "The lighthouse of Mosquito Inlet stuck up above the horizon like the point of a pin. We turned our dinghy toward the shore."[^12] The museum says the principal keeper at the time was O'Hagan, and that Crane's newspaper report became the basis of his short story "The Open Boat."[^4]

Lightning struck more than once. A report of the Light-House Board from the mid-1890s records that a lightning stroke destroyed the electric apparatus for calling the relief keeper and a new one was installed.[^7] In 1919 the Bulletin said the tower had lately been struck twice, putting the telephone and call-bell systems out of order and making a small hole through a wall of one dwelling.[^9] The museum adds that an October 1927 storm damaged the oil house and its new tanks.[^5]

## After the keepers

On 31 August 1933 the lamp was electrified and the first-order lens was replaced by a third-order lens from Sapelo Island, Georgia; the light began flashing.[^2] A radiobeacon was set up in the first assistant's house in 1940, and in 1941 the keepers' families moved out and Coast Guard crews took the quarters.[^2] The nomination says the station was fully automated in 1952, while the museum and Lighthouse Friends say 1953.[^2][^4][^6] In 1958 an experimental solar sea-water distillation plant was built on the grounds, and in 1963 the new Town of Ponce Inlet used the second assistant's dwelling as its town hall.[^2]

In March 1970 the Coast Guard put out the tower's light and exhibited a new one on a 50-foot (15 m) steel skeleton tower on the south side of the inlet, removing the lens and fencing the property against vandals.[^2] The property was declared surplus and deeded to the Town of Ponce Inlet in 1972, and listed in the National Register on 22 September 1972; the Preservation Association was founded to restore it.[^2][^4] In 1978 a planned condominium on the south shore was found to hide the Coast Guard's new light, and on 15 December 1982 the brick tower was relit.[^2] It was named a National Historic Landmark in 1998.[^4]

## Today

The light station is run as a museum by the Ponce de Leon Inlet Lighthouse Preservation Association, which restored the three keepers' dwellings as exhibits; the first-order lens from Cape Canaveral went on display in 1995, the tower's own first-order lens in 2003, and the restored third-order lens went back into the lantern in April 2004.[^4] The light is listed as a private aid to navigation.[^4] The museum is open daily at 10 a.m. except Thanksgiving and Christmas, with hours that vary by season; it says it tries to keep the tower open for climbing during normal hours but may close it for maintenance or special programs, so check its hours before travelling.[^13]
