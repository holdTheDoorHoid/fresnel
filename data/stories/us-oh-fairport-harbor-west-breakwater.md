Fairport Harbor West Breakwater Light stands at the end of the west breakwater that shelters the harbor at Fairport Harbor, Ohio, where the Grand River meets Lake Erie.[^1][^2] Its square tower of brick in a steel frame was first lit on 9 June 1925.[^1][^2] The light has been privately owned since a 2011 sale, and the lighthouse is closed to visitors.[^1][^2]

## The place

Fairport Harbor sits at the mouth of the Grand River, a waterway that LighthouseFriends says has long served regional commerce.[^2] A harbor plan adopted in 1896 would bring two breakwaters together at the harbor mouth. The west breakwater was extended by 1,000 feet (305 m), finished in 1910.[^2] Range lights were already on the east pier.[^2] The Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1894, records that the rear light there was first shown on 15 October 1893.[^8] The old stone lighthouse, on a hill above the harbor, was already standing.[^2]

## Building the light

The first light at the breakwater's end was meant to be temporary, LighthouseFriends says. In 1911 a 25-foot (7.6 m) steel skeletal tower carried an automatic acetylene light on the pierhead. The next year a range light went up on a 65-foot (19.8 m) steel tower at the west pier's end, and the east pier's rear range was dropped.[^2]

Congress voted $42,000 in 1917 for a major light and a compressed-air fog signal. Wartime conditions and rising prices meant the money fell short.[^2] The station's own website gives June 1917 for the appropriation.[^10] Designs were finished in 1919. In 1920 the concrete foundation was readied at the breakwater, and the steel shell began to take shape at the Buffalo Lighthouse Depot.[^2][^3]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin describes the method as "an interesting method of economically building a new lighthouse structure ashore for an exposed location."[^3] The shell measured 28 feet (8.5 m) on each side and was two stories and an attic high, with a 38½-foot (11.7 m) tower at one corner. It weighed about 65 tons.[^2][^3] A steamer carried it on deck 147 miles along the lake to Fairport Harbor, where a derrick scow took it and set it on the breakwater pierhead. The Bulletin estimates the saving at about $10,000 and puts the transfer cost at about $1,350.[^3] By 1921 the steel shell was on site, with interior concrete walls poured up to the top of the first story.[^4]

Work stopped through 1922 and 1923 for lack of money. On 28 May 1924 Congress added $15,000, and building resumed.[^2] The fourth-order Fresnel lens began operating on 9 June 1925. It showed an occulting white light every five seconds, three seconds on and two off. Small generators driven by oil engines supplied the current, and a diaphone sounded three blasts every thirty seconds.[^2][^5] The 1925 Bulletin records that the light changed from oil to electric lighting and from fixed to occulting, and that a fog signal was established.[^5] Its 1933 issue says the old stone light on the hill was discontinued on 9 June 1925, though its tower still remains.[^6]

Later works extended the breakwater. In 1935 the west breakwater gained a 500-foot (152 m) steel pile extension, with a light at its outer end. A diesel generator was installed in 1939, a submarine cable reached the lighthouse in 1940, and a marker radiobeacon was established in 1948.[^2]

## The keepers

The Digital Archive's roster gives five head keepers: Daniel I. Babcock (1925–1926), Frank S. Huntington (1926–1927), Frank LaRose (1927–1937), John E. Brophy (1937–1944) and Herman N. Barr (1947–1948). Assistants served beside them, among them Karl H. Jackson, Thomas R. Dowling, Eugene E. Liebel and Robert E. McCarthy.[^1]

Frank LaRose kept the light for a decade from 1927.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the schedule kept two of the station's three men at the light at all times. On sunny days the keepers draped cloth around the lens, since sunlight focused through it could scorch the paint in the lantern room. In winter they walked out to the light in "ice shoes," metal soles fitted over their everyday shoes.[^2] The Digital Archive's record of LaRose lists him as first assistant at the Conneaut Harbor West Breakwater light from 1917 to 1927. For 1927 to 1937 it lists him as principal keeper at the old Fairport Harbor light as well as at this breakwater light; LighthouseFriends mentions only this light for those years.[^11][^2] After this light he served at South Bass Island from 1937 to 1941.[^11] The record gives his dates as 1874 to 1957.[^11]

LighthouseFriends says John E. Brophy took over as head keeper here in 1937, after five years running the Ashtabula light. Aged sixteen, in 1910, he shipped on the whaler *Ellen A. Swift*, which sailed from New Bedford.[^2] Herman N. Barr is the last keeper on the roster, for 1947 and 1948.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 21 November 1892 the steamer *Canisteo*, towing the schooner *Stewart*, entered the harbor in a southwest gale and slightly damaged the beacon on the outer end of the east pier.[^7] That was the east pier, not the breakwater light. No wreck or rescue at the breakwater light itself is recorded in the sources used here.

## After the keepers

The keepers' service ended in 1948. The Digital Archive's roster ends that year, and the restoration website says the house has been uninhabited since 1948.[^1][^10]

In 2005 the Coast Guard judged the light surplus to its needs and offered it free to qualifying public and nonprofit groups. No qualified applicant came forward, so the General Services Administration auctioned the light in 2009.[^2] Two early sales fell through when the winning bidders defaulted. In a third auction in 2011 the winner backed out, and the second-highest bidder took the light. The buyer, a marketing executive from Virginia, planned to use the house as a personal summer home.[^2] Restoration began in the spring of 2012.[^2]

## Today

The Digital Archive records the light as an active aid to navigation, with a 300 mm optic.[^1] The light has been on the National Register since 1992.[^9] The restoration website describes a top-to-bottom renovation of the building, with the first-floor windows stripped, refinished and reglazed, the walls repaired and painted, the floors refinished, the whole structure rewired, and power supplied by a generator.[^10]

The light and house are private property, and the lighthouse is closed to visitors. LighthouseFriends describes the grounds as open, but treat the house and its land as private. The old stone lighthouse on the hill, a separate station, now serves as a marine museum.[^2][^10]
