Wolf Trap Light stands in about 16 feet (4.9 m) of water on the east end of Wolf Trap Spit, in the lower Chesapeake Bay between the York and Rappahannock rivers. The brick house on its cast-iron pier, the one standing there now, was built in 1894. It replaced a screw-pile light of 1870, which the ice carried away on 22 January 1893.[^1][^4][^5][^8][^9]

## The place

The spit is a shoal between the York and Rappahannock rivers.[^4] The engineer who rebuilt the light described its setting in 1894. The site lies about 12 miles (19 km) northwest of Cape Charles City, Virginia, and under two miles (3 km) northeast of New Point Comfort light. It has 15 feet (4.6 m) of water at mean low tide, and "large masses of ice pass over it in severe winters."[^6]

The name has a story. A Norfolk interview printed in 1893 repeated the tradition that the shoal took its name from the stranding of the British man-of-war Wolf "during the revolution."[^2] A lightship was stationed on the shoal in 1821, according to a Chesapeake Chapter timeline, and the archive's place record gives 1821 as the station's establishment.[^8][^9]

## Building the light

The site had been marked since 1870 by a light on iron piles. The engineer's report calls that structure "an iron skeleton structure," but the Light-House Board's 1894 narrative calls it "a wooden building on iron screw piles."[^5][^6] Either way, the ice carried it off. The Board reports that the light "was carried away by the ice on January 22, 1893," and that the lighting apparatus and most of the movable equipment were salvaged from the floating wreck and kept at the Portsmouth depot.[^4]

A Richmond newspaper gave a different account. On 1 February 1893 it reported that a revenue cutter had "picked up the Wolf Trap light-house at the capes and towed it to the south shore to drift on the beach, as it was dangerous to navigation."[^3] A third account, the chapter timeline, says the wreck was swept 20 miles (32 km) down the bay and settled near Thimble Shoal.[^8]

Congress, by act approved 3 March 1893, appropriated $70,000 for the new light, and the Board decided to replace the old structure "by a structure erected upon a solid pier."[^6] The engineer, Captain Eric Bergland of the Corps of Engineers, described the pier. Its shell is 210 cast-iron plates forming a cylinder 30 feet (9.1 m) across and 42 feet (12.8 m) tall, set on a wooden caisson of long-leaf yellow pine. A steel air shaft 5 feet (1.5 m) across, with an air lock at its top, gave access to the caisson's working chamber while the foundation was sunk. The pier was then filled with concrete to a level 30 feet 2 inches (9.2 m) above the caisson roof. On top sits "a keeper's dwelling of brick surmounted by a fourth-order lantern."[^6]

The metal cost $6,950 and the erection $31,150. Bergland reported that the pier was completed in April 1894, without a single mishap.[^6] The archive gives the light's height as 52 feet (15.8 m) above mean high water and lists a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^9]

While the new light was built, the Board kept a temporary light on the spot. A gas buoy was placed there in March 1893, and on 30 June 1893 "the lighthouse tender Holly" was moored there to serve as a light-vessel.[^4] Light-vessel No. 46 was moored off Wolf Trap Shoal on 31 July.[^7] On 28 August 1893, during a heavy gale, an explosion in her port boiler killed "the engineer in charge and one seaman."[^7]

## The keepers

The archive's roster for the station runs from 1894 to 1971 and lists 114 keeper stints.[^9] It starts after the loss, so the 1893 men are not on it. The Roanoke interview names John William Thomas as the assistant keeper who was at the station when the ice came.[^2] Charles B. Prosperi is the first principal keeper on the roster, for 1894, and Peter W. Jarvis held the post from 1894 to 1907.[^9]

The keepers' names change with the decades. The timeline credits an "Assistant Keeper John Morgan" with rescuing the crew of a disabled plane in 1918, but gives no middle initial. The roster's only Morgan is John E. Morgan, a second assistant in 1918 and 1919. No source here connects the two.[^8][^9]

Floyd E. Crewe appears twice as principal keeper, in 1944 to 1946 and again in 1956 to 1968.[^9] The chapter timeline calls him the last Lighthouse Service civilian keeper, who retired in 1968 after 38 years, but dates his start to 1954. The roster lists him as first assistant from 1954 to 1956.[^8][^9]

The archive lists a keeper, Ismael Torres, Jr., for 1970 and 1971.[^9] The roster also has 55 lines under a "USCG" heading, which means Coast Guard personnel. The earliest dated one is 1947, and five give only an end year, 1971.[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The January 1893 ice is the story's main event, and the Roanoke interview has the most detail. The paper says the assistant keeper, John William Thomas, was alone at the station as the ice piled up against the doomed structure.[^2] He prayed, the paper says, and then saw the smoke of a steamer battling with the ice. He went out on the ice, waving his hat, and was told to come aboard. On the way to shore he broke through the ice in eight feet (2.4 m) of water and came near being drowned.[^2]

Later events came from the chapter timeline, which is a compilation and not a primary record. It lists a schooner, the Dorothy Palmer, running aground at the light in 1904, and a schooner, the Cary Ann Bell, sinking near the light in 1909. In 1919 it says the keeper and his assistant helped a family of six stranded by a storm.[^8]

## After the keepers

The chapter timeline dates the automation of the light to 1971, the year the archive's roster ends.[^8][^9] The Virginia Department of Historic Resources describes a wooden caisson that supports a cast-iron foundation cylinder and a brick dwelling. It gives the National Register listing date as 2 December 2002, and the Virginia Landmarks Register date as 10 September 2003.[^1]

The timeline records the light being made available for ownership under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act in 2004 and 2005, when it was sold at auction to a buyer who wanted to turn it into a bed and breakfast. It was resold privately in 2006, and offered for sale again in 2012.[^8] In 2017 the Coast Guard declared the light unsafe for personnel to access and maintain, and the timeline says the light was discontinued.[^8]

## Today

The archive lists the light as not open to the public. It names a private owner, with The Lighthouse Centers, and the timeline says that owner bought it in 2023.[^8][^9] The sources disagree on the light's present status. The timeline says it was discontinued in 2017, but the archive still lists it as an active aid to navigation.[^8][^9] The tower is closed and privately owned, so nobody should climb it or land on the spit.
