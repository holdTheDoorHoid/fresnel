Pomham Rocks Light stands on a small rock in the Providence River, off the East Providence shore and about three miles from downtown Providence. It is a keeper's house with a short wooden tower rising from its roof, built in 1871 and first lit that December.[^1][^2]

## The place

Pomham Rocks, also spelled Pumham Rocks, take their name from a Narragansett sachem. The two rocky islets are joined at low water by a bar.[^2] The National Register nomination puts the light station on less than an acre, and gives its distance from the East Providence shore as roughly 200 yards in one place and about 1,000 feet in another.[^3] A stone daybeacon, with a vane and black ball, marked the rocks before any light was built.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress provided $20,000 on 15 July 1870 for three lights in the Providence River, one of them on the westernmost islet at Pomham Rocks.[^2] The U.S. Government acquired the site and the Lighthouse Board built the station in 1871.[^3] The dwelling is a wood-frame house, 28 feet by 28 feet, on a granite base, with the tower attached.[^3] Sources differ on the tower: the nomination gives 34 feet, while Lighthouse Friends gives a forty-foot tower.[^3][^2] The light first showed on 1 December 1871, a fixed white light from a sixth-order Fresnel lens, 69 feet (21 m) above the water by Lighthouse Friends' figure; the Lighthouse Society's archive gives 67 feet.[^2][^1] On 1 October 1872 the light was changed from white to red.[^2] A wharf and boathouse followed in 1873, with $1,200 from Congress.[^2]

## The keepers

Charles H. Salisbury was the first keeper and held the post until 1893; the archive's roster prints his term as 1871-1893.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends says his wife was made assistant keeper in November 1872 and the appointment was revoked the next month. When he died in May 1893, Mary A. Salisbury was appointed acting keeper and resigned before the year was out.[^2] Nathaniel Dodge (1893-1898) and Thomas S. Fishburne (1898-1908) followed.[^1]

Adolph H. Aronson kept the light from 1908 to 1937, the longest stay.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Friends says he began on the Hog Shoal lightship in 1899, then served at Hog Island Shoal Lighthouse before coming to Pomham Rocks.[^2] The station had no electricity, telephone or running water; rain from the roof filled a cistern.[^2] When Aronson's daughter had appendicitis and was too ill to move by boat, the doctor came out and operated in the lighthouse.[^2] Nellie Aronson wrote to her representative in 1950, while Congress was considering benefits for keepers' widows, that she had learned to tend the fog bell by hand. When the machinery failed she rang it with a sledge hammer, and she wrote that "winding a fog signal and winding a clock are entirely different."[^2] Lighthouse Friends adds that Aronson hurt a foot in an accident on a trip between lighthouse and shore, that it had to be amputated, and that he returned to work as soon as he could.[^2]

William J. Howard kept the light from 1937 to 1951.[^1] On retiring he called the station his "Little Alcatraz," and said he looked forward to electricity, running water and a bathtub.[^2] Howard B. Beebe (1951-1956) and later Coast Guard keepers and crew are in the archive roster.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In winter the river sometimes froze solid enough to walk to shore. When the ice was too thin to walk on, the keeper had to cut his way through it in a small rowboat.[^2]

The fog signal drew complaints. A blower siren, run by an oil engine, was established in the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900, and the Board's report for the next year records that the siren's characteristic was changed on 15 June 1901.[^4][^5] Lighthouse Friends quotes a local newspaper calling it "THE GREATEST NUISANCE IN THE HISTORY OF THE STATE." It adds that the siren sounded for only 79 hours in the twelve months to 30 June 1901, and that a fog bell replaced it on 1 October 1902.[^2]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin names Pomham Rock among the stations where damage was experienced in the New England hurricane of September 1938, but gives no detail of what happened there.[^6]

## After the keepers

The Coast Guard brought electric power to the station in 1956, Lighthouse Friends says, and a 1939 fourth-order lens replaced the original.[^2] The nomination, which counts 107 years since 1871 and so dates from about 1978, says electricity had come to the island "15 years ago", which does not obviously match 1956.[^3] The light was retired on 5 June 1974 and replaced by a skeleton tower showing a fixed red light.[^2] The nomination says the Coast Guard vacated the dwelling and the Rhode Island Historical Society maintained the property.[^3] The National Park Service catalogues the nomination with a publication date of 9 July 1979.[^8]

In 1980 the station was auctioned, and Mobil Oil bid $40,100. A caretaker employed by Mobil lived on site to maintain it, but it gradually fell into disrepair.[^2] In 2005 Exxon Mobil leased it to the American Lighthouse Foundation for restoration by the Friends of Pomham Rocks Lighthouse, and the light was relit in 2006.[^2] Exxon Mobil gave the lighthouse to the Friends in 2010, according to Lighthouse Friends; the Lighthouse Society's archive says instead that the American Lighthouse Foundation owns it and the Friends manage it.[^2][^1] Commercial power returned in January 2017, and interior restoration ran from October 2017 to March 2018.[^2] The fourth-order lens returned to the island in 2021 for the lighthouse's 150th birthday.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is reachable only on escorted boat tours run by the Friends of Pomham Rocks Lighthouse. Private boats, canoes and kayaks are not permitted.[^7] Lighthouse Friends says the tours began in 2020.[^2]
