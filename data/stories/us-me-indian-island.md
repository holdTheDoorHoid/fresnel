Indian Island Light stands on a small island at the entrance to Rockport Harbor, in Knox County, Maine.[^1] The tower no longer carries a navigational aid.[^1] The island is privately owned, and the grounds, dwelling and tower are closed to visitors.[^1][^2]

## The place

Rockport's harbor was sheltered and deep, and that was a principal reason the community grew.[^1] Shipbuilding made Rockport known in the first half of the nineteenth century, and the town later became a center of lime production.[^1] The light, on the west side of Indian Island, marked the entrance to the harbor.[^1]

On 3 March 1849 Congress appropriated $3,500 for a light at Beauchamp Point or on the other side of the harbor entrance.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the request came from E. K. Smart.[^2] The light was built on Indian Island, just off the tip of Beauchamp Point, and the station has been known by both names.[^2]

## Building the light

The station was established in 1850.[^1] Its first light was shown from a lantern on the roof of the brick dwelling.[^1]

Collector Luther Jewett recommended Rufus Dunning for the post, a man long at sea who did not drink spirits.[^2] The appointment went instead to David Sargent, who served about a month.[^2] The archive spells the name Sargeant.[^3] His replacement, Silas Piper, had sold the plot for the lighthouse to the government for $25.[^2]

A fourth-order Fresnel lens was installed in 1856, taking over from the station's several lamps and reflectors.[^2]

In November 1857 the Lighthouse Board recommended discontinuing the light.[^2] LighthouseFriends reports the Board's reasons: the light stood two miles south of Negro Island light, and it was of no use to the general navigation of the bay.[^2] The light was discontinued in 1859, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The nomination says only that the station was deactivated before the 1861 Light List, for a reason it does not know.[^1]

On 23 June 1874 Congress allocated $9,000 to restore the station.[^2] As LighthouseFriends reports the Board's plan, the one-and-a-half-story brick dwelling would be renewed, its walls and foundation repaired, a 16-foot-square (4.9 m) frame addition built, and a brick tower erected in place of the lantern on the roof.[^2]

The tower was built in 1874 and 1875.[^1] The 1850 brick block of the keeper's house survives, with a square frame ell added in 1874–75.[^1]

The nomination gives 1850 as the year the station was established, and 15 January 1875 as the day the rebuilt complex went back into service.[^1]

LighthouseFriends also reports that in 1902 a fifth-order lens was exchanged for a fourth-order lens to increase the light's power.[^2] It does not say when that fifth-order lens was installed, so its 1856 fourth-order lens and its 1902 exchange leave a change in between that is never dated.[^2]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1894 lists Indian Island among the stations where topographical surveys, showing the contours and buildings, were made that year.[^4] The report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1900 records about 130 feet (40 m) of boat slip built, with minor repairs.[^5] LighthouseFriends dates a 150-foot boat slip to 1885 instead, so the two accounts differ.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive's roster covers the station from 1850 to 1933.[^3] Joseph Small kept the light from 1875 to 1881.[^3] Knott Crockett Perry followed in 1881 and served until his death in 1894.[^2] A local newspaper's notice of his death, as LighthouseFriends prints it, said that in fourteen years he had not been absent from the light for more than two nights.[^2]

On 25 June 1894 the Portland daily press reported that the Secretary of the Treasury had appointed David S. Arey keeper of the light station at Indian Island, "vice Knott C. Perry, deceased."[^6] Arey served until 1902.[^3] The roster then lists Edmund Coffin (1902–1914), Charles E.B. Stanley (1914–1921), LeRoy S. Elwell (1921–1925) and William F. Reed (1925–1933).[^3] Reed, whose full name was William Foster Reed, had served earlier at Saddleback Ledge, Halfway Rock and the Cuckolds.[^2]

A granddaughter of the keeper recalled the route in a 2001 Lighthouse Digest article, as LighthouseFriends reports it.[^2] From Beauchamp Point it ran through Orchard Farm and then through some woods to the shore, where a long horn was kept to summon the keeper's household on Indian Island.[^2] When Keeper Reed heard the horn, he rowed over from the island to fetch her.[^2]

The furniture belonged to Reed and his wife and had been brought from the mainland.[^2] The coal and wood for heating also came by boat, and once unloaded had to be hauled up to the house in wheelbarrows.[^2] The granddaughter remembered that the kerosene lamp often smoked, so its glass needed frequent cleaning.[^2]

In 1928 Reed described the station as a seven-room house with a light tower, an oil house, a boathouse, a barn, a woodshed and other small buildings.[^2] The light, he wrote, "has to be lighted at sunset and extinguished at sunrise every day in the year."[^2] As LighthouseFriends reports it, a 1932 letter by Reed, printed in the Rockland Courier-Gazette, described a station with no telephone and little boat traffic in winter.[^2] Getting the mail meant a daily trip to Rockport.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Commissioner of Lighthouses' list of rescues for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1921 names C. E. B. Stanley, keeper of the Indian Island Light Station, who towed a disabled power boat with one man aboard to port.[^7] The Bulletin's index for 1918 to 1923 has a matching entry, "Stanley, Charles E.B., keeper Indian Island, tows boat." It does not name the state, so it is read here only as a second record of the same keeper.[^8] The sources read here record no storm or wreck at this station, and the 1921 tow is the only rescue they name.[^7][^8]

## After the keepers

The light was discontinued on 30 June 1933, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] Reed retired after that and moved to nearby Camden.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that as Rockport's lime trade slowed in the 1920s, the light came to be seen as minor and missed out on renovation work.[^2]

The dates differ between sources.[^1][^2][^3] The nomination says the station was sold in 1932.[^1] LighthouseFriends says it was one of nine abandoned Maine lights offered for sale in 1934, and that this one sold at auction for $1,025 that September.[^2] The archive gives 1934 as the year the light was discontinued, where LighthouseFriends has 1933.[^2][^3]

The island and light have stayed in one family since the sale, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] An automated light was established on nearby Lowell Rock in 1949.[^2]

## Today

The island and tower are private.[^2] The grounds, dwelling and tower are closed, and the archive lists the station as not open to the public.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends says the light is best seen from the water.[^2]

The nomination lists the contributing buildings: the brick tower of 1874–75, the brick keeper's house with its 1874–75 frame ell, a stone oil house built in 1904, and a shed of two connected parts.[^1] The nomination's boundary covers 4.5 acres, which it describes as the entire land mass of the island.[^1] LighthouseFriends gives the island as seven acres.[^2] Do not land on the island or try to climb the tower.
