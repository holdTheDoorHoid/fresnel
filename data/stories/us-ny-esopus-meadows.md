Esopus Meadows Light stands on the mud flats of the Hudson, off the west bank in the Town of Esopus, Ulster County, New York.[^5] Its first light, a stone house, is dated 1839 by LighthouseFriends and 1837 by the Hudson River Lighthouses site.[^1][^5] The wooden house that stands now was completed in 1871 and first lit in 1872.[^1][^5] The light was put out in 1965 and relit in 2003 after restoration, and the station is reached only by boat.[^1][^5]

## The place

The flats were once a meadow, big enough for cows to graze on at low tide.[^1] The river changed, and the meadow is now underwater at any tide.[^1] Water chestnut now grows in thick beds over the submerged flats, and the beds shelter young striped bass.[^5] The Hudson River Lighthouses site says the flats are still a navigation hazard.[^5]

The light was meant to warn mariners of the submerged mudflat and to steer river traffic toward the east side, clear of the shallows.[^1]

## Building the light

On 3 March 1831 Congress appropriated $3,000 for a lighthouse at Esopus Meadows.[^1] Work had not begun six years later.[^1] The Fifth Auditor of the Treasury, Stephen Pleasonton, noted that the site was flooded at high tides, and that a pier strong enough to resist floating ice would cost twice the sum appropriated.[^1] Congress provided $3,000 on 3 March 1837 to begin construction, and another $3,000 on 7 July 1838 to finish the work.[^1][^5] The Hudson River Lighthouses page dates the first light's span from 1837.[^5]

The first house was stone, 34 by 20 feet (10 by 6 m), with an octagonal tower and lantern, on a wooden pier 41 by 50 feet (12 by 15 m).[^1][^5] Four lamps and reflectors gave a fixed light, and Jeremiah Teerpenning was the first keeper.[^1][^2] A sixth-order lens replaced the lamps in 1854.[^1]

By 1869 the keeper's dwelling was no longer fit to live in during the winter, and the pier had decayed so badly that the ice and freshets could carry the lighthouse away.[^1][^5] Congress provided $25,000 on 15 July 1870 to rebuild, but the money came too late in the season for work to start before the spring.[^1] The new light was built 100 feet (30 m) southeast of the old one, on 250 piles driven into the river bed, with a round granite pier.[^1][^5]

Its wooden frame was clad in clapboard, and its design is credited to Albert Dow, a Vermont architect.[^1][^5] LighthouseFriends describes a rectangular two-story dwelling with a tower at one end of its mansard roof; the Hudson River Lighthouses site calls the keeper's residence square.[^1][^5] The second light first showed from a fifth-order Fresnel lens on 26 August 1872.[^1]

The Light-House Board's report for 1891 says that a fog bell worked by machinery had been in operation since the opening of navigation that year.[^3]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster has thirty-six lines for the station, from 1839 to 1965.[^2] The last eight are Coast Guard crews from 1959 to 1965.[^2]

In September 1903 an inspector found Keeper George R. Humphrey away from the station and wrote that he was neglecting his duty by being frequently absent.[^1] Humphrey resigned that same day, LighthouseFriends reports.[^1] Thomas J. Murray was keeper from 1907 to 1916.[^2]

Emanuel Weber was transferred to Esopus Meadows in January 1933 from Race Rock Light, where he had been second assistant keeper.[^1] In May 1934 an inspector found the station dirty and in disorder, and Weber was reprimanded.[^1] By the next inspection, six months later, he had made considerable improvement.[^1]

Sylvia Weber took over after her husband's death in April 1936.[^1][^2] The archive lists her as principal keeper from 21 April to 9 June 1936, about seven weeks.[^2] LighthouseFriends calls her a temporary keeper who served for nearly two months.[^2][^1]

The Hudson River Lighthouses page lists Anna Warren as "de facto" keeper from 1886 to 1891, a span the archive roster gives to Omar Warren (1885 to 1891).[^5][^2] The page does not say how she was connected to him.[^5][^2]

LighthouseFriends describes the river's lights in general, not this station alone.[^1] When the river froze over, they were shut down and many keepers took part-time shore jobs.[^1] In summer, if a station was in order and no inspector was due, keepers would join the fruit harvest for extra cash.[^1] The Hudson River Lighthouses page adds that the keeper kept a daily log of the weather, events and the ships passing on the river.[^5]

John J. Kerr, who kept the light from 1944 to 1959, kept a barnyard of pets at the station.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends mentions a pet rooster that sat in the bow of his rowboat when he rowed to town.[^1][^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin for 1915 reports that on 14 January keeper Thomas J. Murray rescued two men and a woman whose ice boat had broken through the ice near the light.[^4] He took them to the station and gave them dry clothing and hot food.[^4]

On 14 March 1936 a cutter passed the station, breaking up the channel ice, and the ice around the riprap moved.[^1] The station's boat broke away on a cake of ice, and Keeper Weber went down with a pike pole to try to retrieve it.[^1] He fell into the icy water.[^1] LighthouseFriends says his wife, Sylvia, saw him go over, scrambled back onto the riprap with the boat, and helped raise it to the pier.[^1]

Weber kept on with the light while he had a severe cold.[^1] He developed a fever on 15 April and died on 20 April 1936.[^1][^2]

## After the keepers

The light was automated and put out on 18 August 1965, when the roster's last keeper line ends.[^1][^2] Boards were placed over the windows and painted to look like curtains.[^1] Left unattended, the lighthouse deteriorated quickly.[^1] Barges struck the granite pier, and ice and flood tides weakened it until it cracked.[^1]

Settling caused the east side of the lighthouse to droop sixteen inches (41 cm), LighthouseFriends says.[^1] The Hudson River Lighthouses site gives eighteen inches.[^1][^5]

The Hudson River Lighthouses site says the Save Esopus Lighthouse Commission leased the lighthouse from the Coast Guard in 1990 to begin restoration.[^5] LighthouseFriends says the commission was relaunched in 1997, and the Hudson River Lighthouses site says it was reorganised that year.[^1][^5] Title passed to the commission on 22 September 2002 under a federal pilot disposal program.[^1][^5] The Coast Guard reactivated the light on 31 May 2003, and it shone again.[^1][^5]

In May 2010 a floating dock was installed, and in July the lighthouse was opened to visitors who came by boat.[^1]

## Today

The Hudson River Lighthouses page gives the owning organization as the Save Esopus Lighthouse Commission, and describes the lighthouse as a not-for-profit organization under section 501(c)(3).[^5]

The station is reached only by boat.[^5] Special tours can be arranged, and boaters may stop by when the open flag is flying.[^5] Views from shore are available from Lighthouse Park in the Esopus Meadows Preserve on the west bank, and from the east bank at Rhinebeck and Staatsburgh.[^5]
