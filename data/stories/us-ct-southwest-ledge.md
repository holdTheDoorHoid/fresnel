Southwest Ledge Light is a small white cast-iron house, with a lantern on its mansard roof, sitting on an iron cylinder in the water at the mouth of New Haven Harbor, in New Haven County, Connecticut. Its builders made two of the same house, and the first one went to a world's fair.[^1]

## The place

Southwest Ledge is a rock formation on the east side of the main channel into New Haven Harbor.[^1] LighthouseFriends puts it about a mile offshore, covered by only seven and a half feet (2.3 m) of water at low tide.[^2] In 1868 Y. C. Houston recommended that the ledge, which intruded on the shipping channel, be blasted away rather than marked with a lighthouse.[^1]

When Congress asked about a light on the ledge in 1872, the Lighthouse Board answered that the ledge "lies in the middle of the main ship-channel into New Haven Harbor," that it had "7 feet 6 inches of water on the rock at mean low water," and that a light and fog signal would serve commerce better than removing it. The Board warned that a lighthouse there would be very expensive because of its underwater foundation and the ice that would attack it, and estimated the cost at $117,800 (this is the Board's reply as reproduced on LighthouseFriends, not read in the original report).[^2] The harbour's existing light then stood at Five Mile Point, on the mainland shore.[^2]

## Building the light

The design was by Major George H. Elliot of the Lighthouse Board,[^2] and the National Register nomination says the iron-tube foundation was meant for northern underwater sites where floating ice menaced screw-pile lighthouses.[^1] The foundation was a cylinder of curved cast-iron plates bolted together, set on a poured concrete base inside a ring of riprap and filled with stone and concrete.[^1] Ramsey and Carter of Baltimore were given the contract for the ironwork in June 1875.[^1]

LighthouseFriends reports that about 3,000 tons of riprap were laid for the foundation ring, that a severe storm in the winter of 1873-74 threw some of the stones into the empty ring, and that the last section of the iron tube was placed in the summer of 1876.[^2] The house on top is a Second Empire design in cast iron, with a mansard roof and an octagonal lantern.[^1]

The first house made for the ledge never reached it. In 1876 it was set up on the grounds of the Centennial Exposition in Philadelphia, where a keeper lived in it and showed the light each night from 4 July until the exposition closed.[^1] In June 1876 it was agreed that an identical superstructure being made for Ship John Shoal would go to New Haven instead, so work on the ledge could go on, and the exhibition house went to Ship John Shoal when the fair ended.[^1] The New England Lighthouses guide says the same.[^3]

The light was first shown on 1 January 1877, and on the same day the old light at Five Mile Point was put out.[^3] The Lighthouse Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1897 says the old Five Mile Point station "was abandoned in 1877 upon the establishment of the Southwest Ledge light-station" and was transferred to the War Department on 13 November 1896.[^8] The lens was a fourth-order Fresnel.[^4]

The Lighthouse Board's reports show how hard it was to give the ledge a working fog signal. A hot-air engine had been installed, but the Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1892 says the station was built in 1877 "on a caisson in deep water" and that the space was too small "to give the engine proper draft."[^6] That year a second-class Daboll trumpet ran about 250 hours.[^6] On 7 January 1893 the engines and trumpet were discontinued and a bell struck by machinery, one blow every fifteen seconds, took over; the Board asked for a $12,500 steam signal.[^7] A second-class Daboll trumpet worked by oil engines in duplicate was established on 30 March 1897; the Board's report gives no hours of operation, "the apparatus having remained imperfect."[^8] The sounding was stopped from 20 May to 15 September 1898.[^9]

Other work in these years included a crane for hoisting boats and materials (fiscal 1891), a boat-hoisting winch and an oil house outside the landing gallery (fiscal 1892), and about 400 tons of riprap to protect the boat landing and pier (fiscal 1896).[^5][^6][^16]

The tower's foundation joints had opened by 1911 and were bound with straps and bands. During the Depression, Congress appropriated more than $13,000 for work here, riprap was added around the tower, and two boat cranes were installed.[^1] The ledge became the southwestern end of one of the harbour's riprap breakwaters.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive of the U.S. Lighthouse Society lists Elizur Thompson as head keeper from 1877 to 1881, and then Henry C. Thompson from 1881 to 1898, after serving as his assistant.[^4] LighthouseFriends calls Elizur the last keeper of Five Mile Point, and says Henry's twenty-one years may be the longest term at the station. It also says he was forced to retire at sixty-five by an investigating committee for "intoxication and neglect of duty."[^2] Sidney B. Thompson, the assistant in 1876-77, is credited by the same source with saving four people soon after the light was lit.[^2]

Frank C. Hall followed (1898-1905), then Jorgen Tonnesen (1905-1914), who according to LighthouseFriends died at the lighthouse in 1914 at seventy-one.[^2][^4] The roster lists a long run of assistants across the station's years, many of them for only weeks or months.[^4]

LighthouseFriends says keepers complained of constant damp, cockroaches and foul water collected from the roof in the cistern.[^2] The New England Lighthouses guide says the damp remained a problem, and that in 1916 Keeper Edward Grime resigned over the dampness, the lack of drinkable water and the cockroaches.[^3] The roster shows an Edward Grime as first assistant only from 26 to 28 April 1916.[^4] LighthouseFriends adds that winter storms sometimes drove riprap stones into the landing space, making it hard to reach the house.[^2]

Both guides also tell of Assistant Keeper Nils Nelson (spelled Nilson in one), who is said to have won a lifesaving medal at Sakonnet Point before being sent here.[^2][^3] They say he became violent and chased Keeper Tonnesen around the tower with a fire axe, and that Tonnesen's brother-in-law came to the station for protection.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends adds that Nelson later pinned Tonnesen to a wall with a butcher knife until the brother-in-law stepped in, and that he took his own life ashore in January 1908.[^2] The roster lists Nils Nelson as first assistant in 1907-08.[^4] These accounts have not been checked against a primary record.

Albert Wilkinson was head keeper from 1920 to 1926 and George Petzolt from 1926 to 1929. John Ostman was head keeper from 1929 to 1942, having been assistant here since 1924.[^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin recorded several rescues. On 8 November 1914 Keeper John Petterson and Assistant Arthur Herne brought two men whose motor boat had broken down to the station and kept them overnight, as they were in danger of being wrecked in a storm.[^10] On 10 May 1917 Petterson rescued two men who were clinging to wreckage of a towboat that had capsized and sunk near the station.[^11] On 25 August 1921 Keeper Wilkinson and Second Assistant J. Eliot Dudley (the archive roster has a James E. Dudley in that post) rescued from drowning a man, a woman and a child, occupants of a boat swept onto the breakwater in a high wind.[^12][^4] The Commissioner of Lighthouses' report for fiscal 1921 also noted that the keepers gave food and lodging to the occupants of a U.S. barge cast up on the New Haven Breakwater.[^13] LighthouseFriends puts the total at at least twenty lives saved between 1914 and 1924.[^2]

A storm on 24 October 1917 damaged several lights in the Third District, and Southwest Ledge was among them; the Bulletin gives no detail for it.[^14]

LighthouseFriends also reports that Wilkinson was fatally shot in a hunting accident on 14 January 1926 on the breakwater, that Keeper Ostman fell from a ladder in November 1942 and died the next day, and that on 17 May 1943 First Assistant Harold J. Burbine was badly burned when a fog-signal motor exploded.[^2]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the light was automated in 1953.[^2] The New England Lighthouses guide gives the date as 15 August 1953 and says four Coast Guard keepers were removed, with power supplied by a cable from shore.[^3] In 1989 the National Register paperwork noted that the windows were boarded with plywood.[^1] The lighthouse was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 29 May 1990.[^1]

In May 2015 the Coast Guard declared the lighthouse excess, and after no suitable organization was found under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, the General Services Administration auctioned it. The auction closed on 2 September 2016 with a high bid of $180,002 from two groups teaming up, the Werth Family Foundation and Beacon Preservation; ownership passed in March 2018.[^2]

## Today

Southwest Ledge Light is privately owned by Beacon Preservation, Inc., and is still an active aid to navigation.[^2][^15] Beacon Preservation's plans include a marine laboratory for Southern Connecticut State University's Werth Science Center and a workspace for its Green Light Academy; in 2022 Preservation Connecticut and The 1772 Foundation gave $9,000 for repainting.[^2][^15] The tower is closed to the public. It is best seen from a boat, and distant views are possible from Lighthouse Point Park in New Haven.[^2]
