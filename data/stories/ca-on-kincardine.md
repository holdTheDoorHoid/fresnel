The Kincardine Lighthouse stands on the bank of the Penetangore River, where the river meets Lake Huron at the town of Kincardine, Bruce County, Ontario. It is an octagonal tower rising above a keeper's house. It was built as the rear half of a pair of range lights that brought ships into the harbour, and the keepers' house now holds a museum.[^1][^3]

## The place

The town was first called Penetangore. LighthouseFriends translates the name as "the river with the sand on one side."[^3] In 1856 parallel piers were built to straighten the last few metres of the winding river and to control where it flowed into Lake Huron.[^3]

The harbour served two trades. By 1866 six fishing boats were putting out each morning to set their nets in Lake Huron. Salt deposits were found at Kincardine in 1868, and in 1875 two salt companies made 187,000 barrels, a good part of it exported to the United States.[^3] The entrances were a hazard. A government harbour survey of 1900 listed Saugeen River, Port Elgin and Kincardine first, and said these three harbours were "small with shallow entrances and cannot be entered in stormy weather."[^5]

## Building the light

In 1874 the government set a light near the end of the north pier, in a square wooden building on a low open frame.[^3] Ross Robertson was appointed keeper on 30 April 1874 and died seven months later; William Kay took charge.[^3]

In 1877 the superintendent of lights, Darius Smith, inspected the pier light and its companion range light. He wrote that "The range light is a very slight building, the foundation of which is very rotten." The range light's lantern had to be carried by hand from the outer light.[^3] Parliament appropriated $4,000 in 1878 for a new inner range light.[^3]

The contract went to Joseph White. When he failed to finish on time, the contract was annulled, and the local inspector completed the work so that the light began operating in 1881.[^3] The municipality gives a different date for the building: "1880" is cut into the cornerstone above the rear door, and it calls the tower 24.4 m (74 ft) tall.[^1] The sources disagree on the year. The Canadian Register describes the attached dwelling as one-and-a-half storeys, where the municipality and LighthouseFriends say two.[^2][^1][^3]

The original lamp burned kerosene, and the municipality says the keepers hoisted its barrels up the stairs. Its shutters turned on weighted chains to make the light flash.[^1]

In 1885 Keeper Kay wrote to his superiors offering to terrace and sod the station grounds. He also asked for a fence to keep his wood from being stolen. When he was asked whether he could build the fence himself, he replied that "I have to sleep half the day."[^3]

The front light on the north pier was built twice. The first tower burned in 1902 (see below). Its steel-framed replacement was lit in May 1903. The Chief Engineer's report gives its height as 10 m (33 ft) to the vane, with a fixed red light 11 m (35 ft) above the lake and a seventh-order dioptric lens.[^7]

## The keepers

Kay kept the light from 1874 until 1899. The superintendent wrote in 1877 that "The lights are well kept," and noted that "Mr. Wm. Kay, Keeper, has a family of nine."[^3] Kay's successor, Thomas McGaw, took over in 1899.[^3]

McGaw was also the coxswain of the new life-saving station. The station was set up during the 1903 season: "Thomas McGaw is coxswain of the crew and there are six men with him, making a total of seven."[^7] A life-boat built at Collingwood was sent to Kincardine for the station.[^7]

Keepers came and went after McGaw. William G. Temple served from 1913 to 1928. Oran Westell kept the light from 1929 to 1955, and Alonzo Burley from 1956 to 1978. LighthouseFriends' roster ends Burley's service in 1978, but its text says the keeper's job was discontinued in 1977.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 9 July 1902 the front range light on the north pier burned. The government's report records that it "was burned down on July 9, 1902."[^6] LighthouseFriends says it was struck by lightning first, and that keeper McGaw then showed a fixed red light from a lantern on a pole 8.5 m (28 ft) high until the new tower lit in May 1903.[^3][^7]

The schooner Anna Maria cost five lives, the only deaths in the Kincardine wrecks these sources record. A Cleveland paper's Kincardine dispatch, dated 9 October 1902, says she was "wrecked here Tuesday night," loaded with coal and bound for the harbour from Cleveland. That places the wreck on the night of 7 October, which LighthouseFriends also gives.[^12][^3] The rescue party reached the schooner and got her crew off, but a huge wave upset the boat as it was about to start for the shore. The dispatch says "Five were lost."[^12] LighthouseFriends names the dead as the captain, a female cook, two other crew and one rescuer. The dispatch names the captain, three of the crew and a rescuer, Mr. Ferguson.[^3][^12]

The Department's list of rewards for saving life records the rescue of crew from "the American schooner Anne Maria wrecked off Kincardine, Ont." (the list spells the name Anne Maria).[^8] In April 1903 medals for the rescue were presented in Kincardine. A St. Paul paper's report from Kincardine, dated 15 April, says the presentation was made the night before, on the president's behalf, by the United States consul, to the rescuers of "the crew of the American schooner Anna Maria."[^13]

In the year to 30 June 1904 the steamer Singapore went water-logged off Kincardine in a storm and drifted onto the beach. "The captain, his wife and three children, with three seamen were lashed to the rigging." The Kincardine life-saving crew went to their help and rescued all hands. "The vessel was a total loss."[^9]

The report for the year to 30 June 1910 records "No wrecks or casualties" at Kincardine.[^10]

On 1 May 1903 a steam fog siren, "established in connection with the town waterworks machinery," was put in operation. In thick weather it gave a blast of 2½ seconds every 45 seconds.[^7]

## After the keepers

In 1910 the light received a fourth-order lens and a vapour lamp in place of its old catoptric apparatus.[^10] In 1911 the alternating red and white light was changed to a flashing white light, "showing one bright flash every 20 seconds."[^11]

The sources give different dates for electric power. The municipality says the lamp became electric in 1922, while LighthouseFriends dates the electrification of the range lights to 1925.[^1][^3]

In 1931 an electric fog alarm was set up at the front range, and LighthouseFriends says one still runs there.[^3] The keeper's post ended in 1977, and in 1980 the lighthouse was leased to the Kincardine Yacht Club.[^3]

In December 2008 the town council designated the lighthouse a heritage building, partly in response to an outcry that year over vinyl siding and modern windows added to it.[^3] The Canadian Register's statement of significance calls the tower "very closely associated with the ongoing development of a Great Lakes coastal navigational system."[^2]

## Today

The Town of Kincardine owns the lighthouse, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3] The Kincardine Yacht Club administers the lighthouse and museum. The municipality says they open daily from 11 a.m. to 5 p.m. from 1 July to Labour Day, and that admission is $5 per person.[^1] LighthouseFriends notes that the tower is open in season.[^3]

The Coast Guard's list gives the light as a flashing red, one second on and four off, every five seconds ("Fl R 5s"). It classes the light as a private aid, with its light 24.4 m above the water and the structure 19.2 m above ground.[^4] The municipality gives the tower as 24.4 m (74 ft) tall. LighthouseFriends gives it as 19.2 m (63 ft), which matches the Coast Guard's figure.[^1][^3][^4]

The 1903 steel front tower is no longer the front light. According to a LighthouseFriends caption, it served until the mid-1920s. The Coast Guard now lists a 6.1 m cylindrical mast with a sector light near the west end of the north pier.[^3][^4]

On evenings in July and August, members of a pipe band take turns playing from the top of the tower at sunset, in memory of an early Kincardine piper, Donald Sinclair. The municipality says the story "has been passed along through generations by word of mouth."[^1]
