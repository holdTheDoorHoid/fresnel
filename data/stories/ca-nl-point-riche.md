Point Riche Light stands at the tip of a peninsula near Port au Choix, on the west coast of Newfoundland's Great Northern Peninsula. The station was first lit in 1871. The wooden tower standing there now dates from 1892.[^1][^4][^11]

## The place

The light was put up to help steamers through the Strait of Belle Isle. Ships on the run between the St. Lawrence and Europe used the strait, and the first light, completed by the Canadian government in 1871, was meant to assist them.[^1]

The coast here had been part of the French Shore. France held fishing and occupancy rights from Cape Bonavista to Point Riche from 1713 until 1763, when the region was redefined. LighthouseFriends says France gave up its rights in 1904.[^1]

The federal register links the tower to safe navigation in important shipping lanes through the Strait of Belle Isle.[^2]

## Building the light

The first station's buildings were completed in 1870. The lamps, twelve burners with 20-inch (51 cm) reflectors, were installed the following year. The whole lighthouse cost $10,157.50, and it was hexagonal, about 12 m (40 ft) from base to vane.[^1] The light, a white flash every fifteen seconds, was first shown on 26 August 1871.[^1]

The register credits the tower's design to the Department of Marine and Fisheries, from the years when William Anderson was its Chief Engineer.[^2][^11]

On 15 August 1890 fire destroyed the lighthouse.[^1][^4] Before anything was rebuilt, the opinions of masters of vessels using the strait were asked. A majority wanted the light rebuilt on its old site, and tenders were invited.[^1] Daniel McDonald of Pictou, Nova Scotia, submitted the lowest tender, $1,750. In the summer of 1892 he built the present wooden tower, which carried a mirror (catoptric) light. It was put into service on 20 October 1892.[^1]

In 1908 concrete foundations were built up around the tower to carry the heavier new optic. The work cost $8,170.[^1][^6] The Department's report for the fiscal year ending 31 March 1909 describes the new light as a third-order dioptric double flash, with two flashes every five seconds.[^5]

## The keepers

Eugene Roy was the first keeper, at $500 a year. In 1873 his pay was raised to $600, so that he could hire an assistant. Roy felt he could not attend the revolving light without one.[^1]

Roy died in 1881. That year Ferdinand Lemieux was hired as keeper of the lighthouse and the fog gun, at $400 a year.[^1]

Narcisse Breton kept the light from 1896 to 1926.[^1] The Department's list of light-keepers for 1901 has "Breton, Narcisse" on the same line as Point Rich.[^8]

After Breton came Joseph N. Romeo Breton (1926 to 1927), then Joseph Narcisse Gaudias Breton (1927 to 1959), and John Lawrence Rumbolt, who is listed as keeper from 1959 and at least until 1962.[^1]

In 1902 the Meteorological Service inspector, H. V. Payne, visited six stations. At Point Rich the instruments were well cared for, but the station needed a wind vane and a sun-dial.[^9]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 25 October 1874 William Barbour, the district engineer, arrived off Point Riche on the steamer Lady Head. A gale kept him from landing, and the ship took shelter at Port Saunders.[^1] There he heard that the light had not turned for six weeks. He walked eleven miles to the station, arriving at 5 p.m., and found the revolving gear out of order. With the steamer's second engineer he had the lens working by 1 a.m. The next night the keeper reported that the light had stopped again. This time Barbour found that all it needed was a little oil.[^1]

After two wrecks off Point Riche in 1876, mariners and J. U. Gregory, the Marine Agent at Quebec, recommended a trial of a Neptune fog horn. It began operating on 10 October 1877, sounding an eight-second blast every ten minutes. The Inspector of Lights found it could be heard for two miles, though the sound was slight.[^1] The horn proved too weak for steamers and was discontinued in 1879.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1905 says that, under contract with the government, the Marconi company had erected a wireless station at Point Rich that year.[^7]

The Department's report for 1922–23 says that two power sets "taken from the Point Riche and Cape Ray Stations" were installed at Point Amour.[^10] The report does not say which kind of station it means. The 1905 report lists Point Rich and Cape Ray among the Marconi wireless stations, so these were probably wireless stations rather than the light.[^7]

Diesel generators were installed at the station in 1968. Commercial power reached it in 1970, which allowed the station to be automated and de-staffed.[^1]

LighthouseFriends says the keeper's house built in 1908 burned, leaving only its foundation. It dates the fire to 1998 in its text.[^1] The lens has gone. The lantern room remains, with a modern beacon that shows a white flash every five seconds.[^1]

The register recognized the tower as a Recognized Federal Heritage Building on 17 October 1991.[^11]

## Today

The Coast Guard's list gives the light as a white flash every five seconds, with a focal height of 29.3 m and a range of 10 nautical miles. It describes the tower as white and octagonal, with a red upper part.[^3]

The federal register describes the tower's massing as hexagonal.[^2] The Coast Guard list, the Lighthouse Directory and LighthouseFriends all call it octagonal.[^3][^4][^1]

The Canadian Coast Guard owns the light, and Parks Canada manages the site through Port au Choix National Historic Site.[^4][^1]

The grounds are open and the tower is closed. The last part of the road, beyond the Port au Choix visitor centre, is not paved. Visitors can also walk in along Philip's Garden Coastal Trail.[^1]
