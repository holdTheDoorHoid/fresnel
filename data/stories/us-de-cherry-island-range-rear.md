The Cherry Island Range was a pair of range lights on the western shore of the Delaware River, just north of the mouth of the Christina River in Delaware.[^3] The front light stood on the pier of the Edgemoor depot beside the water, and the rear light stood on a rise 1,456 yards (1,331 m) north of it.[^3] Both were lit in 1880 to guide ships past Cherry Island Flats, a shoal in the river.[^3] The rear tower was torn down around 1970 or 1971, and what is left today is an oil house and a skeletal tower that carries a red light.[^3]

## The place

The land by the river had been low marsh.[^3] A Lighthouse Board report for 1878, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, records an appropriation of $10,000, made by an act approved 20 June 1878, "for a light or lights to guide past Cherry Island flats."[^3] The board wrote that range lights, if they could be used, would be more effective and "will probably not cost more than $50,000", while a light on the shoal "will not cost less than $100,000."[^3]

The front light's site was a three-acre riverfront parcel that the Edgemoor Iron Company sold to the government in July 1879 for $3,000, and the Lighthouse Board built its Edgemoor depot on the same ground.[^3]

## Building the light

The range lights were lit for the first time on the night of 1 April 1880. The Wilmington *Daily Gazette* of 3 April reported range lights "above the mouth of Christiana creek" for the new channel west of Cherry Island Flats, lit "the night of April 1st."[^4] LighthouseFriends gives the same first lighting.[^3]

The sources disagree on the front light's height. LighthouseFriends describes a white tower 21 feet (6.4 m) tall from its base to the lantern; the USLHS record gives 13 feet (4 m).[^3][^2]

The rear light was a two-story keeper's dwelling with a square tower on one corner, topped by a pyramidal roof and a ventilator ball. The USLHS record gives the height of the light as 120 feet (37 m) above mean high water, and LighthouseFriends says it was exhibited from 120 feet (37 m) above sea level.[^1][^3] The USLHS record gives the rear tower as steel, 40 feet (12 m) from base to lantern, with a reflector as its original optic. For the front light it gives a fifth-order Fresnel lens.[^1][^2]

Work on the rear station went on for years. An attempt to dig a well there in about 1885 failed, LighthouseFriends says, and a well was dug and fitted with a pump in 1891.[^3][^5] In 1894 the Board's report says the boundary and other fences on the reservation were rebuilt.[^9] By 1897 the dwelling, "including its small tower," had been thoroughly repaired, and a detached brick oil house had been built.[^7] In 1899 a kitchen addition was begun.[^8]

## The keepers

William W. Simmons was the front light's keeper from 1880 to 1890, and William H.C. Knowles followed him until 1912.[^2] John A. Patterson was the first keeper of the rear light and served from 1880 until 1910.[^1][^3]

In June 1906 Patterson was hurt on his way home. A Wilmington paper reported that he broke through a boardwalk near Shellpot Park, that one leg was badly sprained, and that "no bones were broken."[^10]

Lawson Holland kept the rear light from 1910 to 1915. Joseph Cofield followed from 1916 to 1918, and Charles E. Marshall from 1919 to 1930.[^1] The USLHS roster and LighthouseFriends both list Emma J. Holland as principal keeper from 1915 to 1916. Neither describes her work.[^1][^3]

In 1930 Julian Bacon was transferred to the rear light from the Elbow of Cross Ledge light. He arrived with his wife, his daughter and his mother-in-law, Lydia Burton. In December 1930 he was moved on to Marcus Hook Rear Range.[^3] John E. Collins served from 1931 to 1933, when he retired for disability; his wife was then made custodian of the reservation, and the family kept living at the lighthouse.[^1][^3]

In 1953 the keeper was Arthur W. Davis, a Coast Guard chief boatswain's mate. LighthouseFriends, describing a Wilmington *Sunday Star* article of 15 February 1953, says he had a wife and five youngsters with him and fifteen lights along the river to look after.[^3] The roster then lists Emil J. Herzog from 1958 to at least 1960 and Jim Coffey from at least 1965.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In August 1891 the keeper's dwelling at the front light was destroyed by fire. LighthouseFriends gives the date as 8 August 1891, and the Board's next annual report says the dwelling "destroyed by fire in August of last year, was rebuilt."[^3][^6] In 1925 a fire at the Edgemoor depot, beside the front light, destroyed the depot warehouse.[^3]

## After the keepers

By 1953 the rear light had a keeper again.[^3] As other lights on the river were automated, the keeper took charge of several of them.[^3] The USLHS record gives 1971 as the year the rear light was discontinued, and the front light's discontinuance in 1949.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard decided, around 1970 or 1971, to "tear the historic structure down" rather than refurbish it or move it.[^3]

## Today

The front light's site is now occupied by a DuPont plant. A modern range light stands on a skeleton tower near the plant's southern pier.[^3]

The rear station is Coast Guard property.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open and the tower closed; the USLHS record says the light is not open to the public.[^3][^1] The USLHS record still lists the rear light as existing, while LighthouseFriends says the tower came down in 1970 or 1971.[^1][^3] Do not climb or enter the tower, and check with the Coast Guard before going onto the grounds. What remains is a historic oil house built in 1897 and a skeletal tower supporting a red light.[^3]
