Middle Bay Light stands on screw piles near the middle of Mobile Bay, south of the city.[^1][^3][^5] It is a wooden hexagonal house with a light on top, built to mark the bend of a dredged ship channel.[^1][^5] The light was first lit on 1 December 1885, according to the Keeper's Log and LighthouseFriends, though a 2026 newspaper article says it went into service in September 1885.[^2][^5][^6]

## The place

Mobile Bay is shallow.[^5] Before the channel was dredged, the bay was marked only by wooden stakes and copper-clad barrels.[^5] A channel 17 feet (5 m) deep was dredged in the 1880s, and an act of Congress of 3 March 1883 appropriated $19,000 to mark "the recently completed Mobile ship channel."[^1][^5] The light stands at the bend of the channel, where it turns like a dogleg.[^5] The nomination places it "near the middle of Mobile Bay."[^1]

The sources measure its position from different points. A 1905 newspaper puts it eighteen miles below Mobile.[^3] The Keeper's Log puts it about 15 miles south of the harbor.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives 14.3 miles north of Mobile Point.[^5]

The light's position also made it a quarantine stop. In July 1905 the Quarantine Board of Mobile Bay authorized its executive officer to require vessels arriving with suspicious illness aboard "to be stopped at the middle light," where the ship would stay and discharge its cargo onto a lighter.[^3] In June 1903 the Ladies' Aid Society of Government Street Presbyterian Church held an excursion on the bay steamer Heroine out to the Middle Bay light and back.[^4]

## Building the light

The contract for the lighthouse was let in 1884.[^2] The pre-fabricated ironwork reached Fort Morgan, at Mobile Point, the following year.[^2][^5]

Storms delayed the work. On 12 September 1885, after the piles had been screwed into the bay bottom, the nearly finished structure settled, "sinking some seven and a half feet" (about 2.3 m) into the bay.[^2][^5] The Keeper's Log says no structural damage was incurred, and that the light still towered almost 60 feet (18 m) above the bay.[^2]

The first lamp burned kerosene, and the Keeper's Log describes its light as white with red flashes every thirty seconds.[^2] The nomination gives the flash as "varied by a red flash every 30 seconds."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the first beam came from a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^5] The house was built as both a residence and a light.[^1] The nomination calls it "reputedly" a replica of the Hooper's Strait light off Maryland, and the Keeper's Log says the same.[^1][^2] Five similar lights were planned for the channel, but none was built.[^2]

The house changed in 1905.[^1] The nomination says the light was rebuilt that year, and that "it is believed that it is a duplicate of the original light."[^1] LighthouseFriends gives a different account: the lens and lantern room were removed, and two acetylene lights were mounted on a pole protruding from the roof.[^5] The two accounts do not agree, and the sources do not settle which is right.

## The keepers

The archive's roster names Joseph P. Groux (1885–1889) as the first head keeper, followed by Lee D. Cuthbert (1889–1893) and James Terreson (1893–1905).[^7] The roster also lists twelve first-assistant stints.[^7] LighthouseFriends dates the change of head keeper later, with Cuthbert to 1903 and Terreson from 1903.[^5]

The Keeper's Log says the keeper initially earned $600 a year for his duties, and the assistant $400.[^2] Pay rose in 1893 to $640 and $440.[^2] The keeper and his family lived in the dwelling, and food and supplies were shuttled out to them from the Eastern Shore.[^2]

LighthouseFriends tells a family story. During World War I the keeper and his wife had a baby at the station. When the mother could not nurse, a small corral was built on the gallery and a milk cow was brought to the station, rather than send mother and child ashore.[^5] The account names no keeper for that period.

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Keeper's Log says the station was manned on a continual basis until the hurricane of 1916.[^2] The Log, published in spring 1986, says the light had been buffeted by the winds of three hurricanes in the past year, and that Hurricane Elena, in September 1985, had swept other navigation aids away.[^2] In 2013 a shrimp boat ran into the lighthouse, bending a metal support piling.[^5]

## After the keepers

The nomination says the light was automated in 1935, and that the structure has been vacant since.[^1] LighthouseFriends also says the light was electrified in 1935.[^5] The Keeper's Log dates the conversion of the lantern from kerosene to electricity to just prior to World War II.[^2]

No keeper tended the light from the mid-1930s until the late 1960s, and the lighthouse fell into disrepair.[^6][^5] A former Coast Guard crewman, quoted by LighthouseFriends, knew it as the Half Way House. He remembered it from the early 1960s as in poor shape, with no windows and bird droppings all through the inside.[^5]

In 1961 the Coast Guard declared the structure a hazard to navigation, and in 1967 the General Services Administration gave permission to demolish it.[^2][^5] The Log reports that the Mobile Bar Pilots Association argued the light was still an important navigation aid. Ships' radar picked up the light, although it could not record the smaller, more modern buoys.[^2] The demolition was dropped, and "the lighthouse was spared."[^5] The nomination says the light was saved by concerned citizens.[^1]

The state's nomination form is dated September 1974.[^1] That year Unit 11 of the Navy Reserve's Mobile Construction Battalion 14, the SeaBees, began restoration work.[^2] Dr. Sam Eichold, president of the Mobile County Historical Society, formed the Middle Bay Light Preservation Society.[^2]

For the centennial in 1985, volunteers worked on weekends in October and November to restore the lighthouse by 1 December.[^2] Captain Hal Pierce, the centennial commission's chairman, supervised the work, and the Propeller Club of Mobile spearheaded the effort. The Coast Guard provided transport. The day-long celebration was held on 1 December, the anniversary of the light's first lighting.[^2]

The Alabama Historical Commission took responsibility for the lighthouse, and a $349,400 restoration contract followed in 2002.[^5] The Alabama Lighthouse Association proposed moving the light ashore to Battleship Park.[^5][^6] LighthouseFriends dates the proposal to June 2008 and the commission's vote against it to December 2009. A 2026 newspaper article dates the proposal to 2009.[^5][^6] A $270,000 restoration followed in 2010.[^5]

Crews began repairs to the roof and walkway in late June 2026.[^6] The site director said the commission had solicited bids for the work in August and September 2025.[^6] A Facebook post by the Coast Guard Auxiliary's Eastern Shore flotilla put the price of the repair work and paint job at $1.2 million.[^6]

## Today

The Alabama Historical Commission has protected the lighthouse as historic property since 2003.[^6] The lighthouse is not open to the public.[^6] It stands offshore in the bay, and LighthouseFriends advises that it is best seen by boat.[^5] Visitors should not try to land on it or climb it. The Alabama Lighthouse Association had new exterior doors installed around 2005, and it is working to raise money for handrails so that public access can be allowed.[^5]

The 2002 work replaced a roughly fifteen-foot (4.6 m) pyramidal structure, which had carried a red flashing light, with a six-foot (1.8 m) pole supporting a solar-powered red light.[^5]

A Fresnel lens thought to be from the light was on display at the Fort Morgan Museum until 2003, and LighthouseFriends says it is now with the Coast Guard in Mobile.[^5] A note with the lens says it replaced the original fourth-order lens.[^5] Other sources claim the lens that was in the museum is the original.[^5] A 2026 article says the lighthouse's original Fresnel lens is on display at the Fort Morgan Museum.[^6]

A lantern room similar to the one used on the light was restored by the Alabama Lighthouse Association and can be seen at the National Maritime Museum of the Gulf of Mexico in Mobile.[^5] A 2026 article also places a lantern-room exhibit, donated by the association, at the GulfQuest Maritime Museum.[^6]
