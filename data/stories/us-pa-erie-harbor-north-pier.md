The Erie Harbor North Pier Light, also called the Presque Isle North Pierhead Light, stands at the outer end of the north pier that guards the entrance to Presque Isle Bay at Erie, Pennsylvania.[^2] Its white iron tower, with a black band, has stood on the pier since 1857 or 1858, depending on the source, and it has been moved along the pier at least three times.[^2][^3][^19][^20]

## The place

The harbor lies behind Presque Isle, a sand peninsula that bends out into Lake Erie.[^2] By 1837 it had two marks: the Erie Land light, put up on a bluff in 1818, and a beacon at the entrance to the bay that had only just been established.[^2] An inspection report of that year said ships coming down the lake would not see the beacon until they were close to it. It advised them to use the bluff light to find the upper entrance, then follow the beacon in.[^2] On 3 March 1837 Congress appropriated $674 "for completing the beacon-light at the end of the pier which forms the entrance into the harbor of Erie," in the words LighthouseFriends gives.[^2]

## Building the light

The first beacon was a wooden tower. In 1855 a vessel entering the harbor during a gale struck it, and the beacon and its new sixth-order lens were destroyed. A lantern hung from a gallows frame on the pier until a new tower could be put up.[^2] The Erie marker and the Presque Isle Light Station's own page date that loss to 1857 instead.[^20][^19]

An 1855 cost sheet, as LighthouseFriends reports it, priced a new pierhead and beacon at about $5,250, with a sixth-order Fresnel lens.[^2] The tower was cast iron, and the marker says the iron was forged in France.[^20] The USLHS place record gives the lens as a sixth-order Fresnel installed in 1857.[^1] At first the lower part of the tower stood open, so its spiral stair showed, and only the watch room under the lantern had walls.[^2]

The pier kept growing, and the tower kept moving with it. LighthouseFriends dates the move of the iron tower to the outer end of the pier to 1882, when its light was changed from fixed white to fixed red; the station's own page says the tower was moved closer to the end of the pier in 1883.[^2][^19] A 934-foot (285 m) elevated walk to the keeper's dwelling was built in 1883.[^2] In 1891 the north pier was extended, and the Lighthouse Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 says the pier "will be extended 450 feet during this fall, the beacon must also be moved" (about 137 m).[^3] The Board's list of changes records the move to within 30 feet (9 m) of the outer end of the pier on 10 October 1891.[^4] In 1894 the beacon moved again, to about 20 feet (6 m) from the outer end of the new extension, and about 313 running feet (95 m) of elevated walk was built to reach it.[^7]

The harbor also needed a fog signal. The Board's 1893 report says a steam fog signal costing not more than $4,300 "was authorized by the act approved February 15, 1893, but no appropriation therefor has yet been made."[^6] A fog bell had been set up at the eastern end of the pier in 1880, and LighthouseFriends says it was considered ineffective.[^2] In 1895 a tight board fence 360 feet (110 m) long was built partly in the water "to keep out floating debris," and a well was drilled.[^8]

## The keepers

The USLHS roster lists seventeen head-keeper terms, held by sixteen men (Leonard Vaughn served twice), from Samuel Foster (1835-1836) to Walter J. Korwek (1935-1946), with assistants and a laborer in the same list.[^1] The roster's earliest entry, Samuel Foster (1835-1836), predates the beacon that LighthouseFriends describes as only just established by 1837. No source checked for this story explains the earlier date.[^1][^2]

Robert Hunter was first assistant from 1886 to 1889 and head keeper from 1889 to 1901.[^1] Benjamin Franklin Henry was head keeper from 1869 to 1884. LighthouseFriends and the marker both call him "Frank Henry."[^1][^2][^20]

Robert Allen came to the station as second assistant in 1903 and became head keeper in 1909, serving until 1930. LighthouseFriends says he had "the longest tenure as head keeper."[^1][^2] Frank S. Huntington was second assistant from 1917 to 1926 and first assistant from 1933 to 1944. Patrick J. Owens was first assistant from 1917 to 1933.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In the year ended 30 June 1892 the Board recorded that Erie Range No. 1, a range light on the same north pier, had one pane of its lantern "broken by a sailing scow while entering the bay during a prevailing storm."[^5]

In 1913 the Life-Saving Service awarded Robert Allen a silver medal of honor.[^13] LighthouseFriends says it was for rescuing two people who were about to drown while swimming near the pier. Allen tied a line to a mop handle and tossed it to one man, then swam out for the woman who had gone under.[^2]

On the night of 27 August 1918 the steamer Tempest, loaded with coal, sank in the channel about 200 feet (61 m) south of the Erie Coast Guard station after her boilers blew up.[^2][^14] LighthouseFriends prints Allen's report of the sinking.[^2] The Bulletin's index ties Allen, Owens and Huntington to a rescue on page 43 of the 1918 volume.[^15] The Bulletin's account, printed in its 1918 issue, places the wreck in the channel of Erie Harbor near the light station and names the keepers and their wives among the rescuers.[^14] Allen's report names the keepers' wives, his own among them, who pulled men onto the pier. Owens rolled a log into the water, jumped in after it and shoved it toward the cries for help, and Huntington rowed out and picked up a man who had no lifebelt. The second engineer was missing, and the Coast Guard found him the next morning.[^2]

In 1925 a fire burned on the peninsula for several days. The Bulletin says Andrew Shaw, jr., keeper of the Presque Isle Light, and Robert Allen, keeper of the Pierhead Light, "rendered efficient service" in fighting it.[^18]

## After the keepers

The fog signal came in 1899. The Board's report for that year describes a new fog-signal station, "just established," on the peninsula, about 1½ miles (2.4 km) "westerly from the beacon on the outer end" of the pier.[^10] LighthouseFriends puts the building about a mile and a half north of the pierhead instead, so the two accounts disagree on its direction.[^2] LighthouseFriends says a ten-inch steam whistle "commenced operation on August 1 of that year."[^2] The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1901 says the whistle "was operated some 48 hours, and consumed about 2 tons of coal."[^12]

The Board's report for 1900 says the keeper's dwelling was rebuilt and enlarged "so as to accommodate the families of the two keepers." LighthouseFriends describes a new duplex built that year for the head keeper and first assistant, with the second assistant moved to the old dwelling.[^11][^2] The same Board report says an open framework tower, which had held a light at Dunkirk, New York, was moved to the north pier to serve as a fog-bell tower.[^11] Earlier, the 1896 report had recorded that the woodshed was rebuilt and enlarged.[^9]

The lights were electrified in 1924, on 18 July according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The Bulletin's 1924 list gives the Pierhead light as changed "fixed to occulting, oil to electric incandescent."[^16] The same Bulletin list names only "Presque Isle, Pa." among fog signals changed from bell operated by machinery to an air diaphone.[^17] LighthouseFriends instead says the diaphone replaced the station's steam whistle, beginning operation on 6 August 1924, so the two accounts do not match.[^2] A tyfon air signal replaced the fog bell on the pier in 1933.[^2] The tower moved to its present spot in 1940 and was encased in steel plates. A radiobeacon followed in 1941.[^2][^19]

The nonprofit page says lighthouse keepers "were no longer needed after 1945," although the roster above runs to 1946.[^19][^1] In 1995 the Coast Guard removed the fourth-order Fresnel lens and installed an automated, solar-powered red flashing light.[^19] The lens is now at the Erie Maritime Museum.[^2] In January 2023 the Coast Guard offered the tower, which it had deemed excess, at no cost to eligible groups. The nonprofit Presque Isle Light Station, Inc. applied for ownership, LighthouseFriends reports.[^2]

## Today

The Coast Guard still runs the light, which is now an automated red flasher.[^20][^19] The tower is not open to the public. The grounds can be reached during Presque Isle State Park hours, and LighthouseFriends says you can walk to the pierhead from a parking area on Coast Guard Road.[^19][^2] The fourth-order lens is on display at the Erie Maritime Museum.[^2]
