Michigan Island Light stands on a bluff at the southern point of Michigan Island, one of the Apostle Islands in Lake Superior, about seventeen miles northeast of Bayfield, Wisconsin.[^1][^4] Two towers stand a short distance apart there.[^1] The first, a stone tower built in 1856 and 1857, has been dark since 1929.[^2][^4] The second, a 112-foot (34 m) cast-iron skeleton tower moved to the island in 1929, still carries a light according to LighthouseFriends.[^3]

## The place

In 1853 Congress set aside $5,000 for a light to guide boats to La Pointe, the fur-trading settlement on Madeline Island.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the light was meant for Long Island, but when workmen arrived in 1856 a local Lighthouse Board representative sent them to Michigan Island instead.[^2] The nomination says the light "marked both the north and south channels into La Pointe and Bayfield for boats coming from the east."[^1] The sources differ on the distance from Bayfield. LighthouseFriends quotes an 1870 newspaper at 15 miles east, and the Park Service says 17 miles northeast.[^2][^4]

## Building the light

The Milwaukee firm of Sweet, Ransom and Smith took a $4,500 contract for "a sixty-five-foot rubblestone tower connected to a keeper's dwelling." LighthouseFriends says the work was finished by October 1856.[^2] The district inspector, Lorenzo Sitgreaves, rejected the tower as "not built in conformity to the terms of the contract." The contractors then built a frame light on Long Island, reluctantly, in 1857.[^2] The Park Service says the finished tower stood fifty-two feet (16 m) tall, and that its final cost topped out at more than $12,000, about $7,600 over the first estimate.[^4] The sources do not agree on the height.[^2][^4]

The Park Service says the first lens was a third-and-a-half-order Fresnel lens, "manufactured by the prestigious Henri Lapaute Company of Paris, France."[^4] The first light did not last. The Park Service says its lamp went out after about a year, operations stopped in 1858, and the valuable lens was sent to the La Pointe lighthouse on Long Island.[^4] In 1867 the Board asked for the station to be brought back into use. LighthouseFriends quotes its report that it "seems to be desirable to re-establish it." Congress provided $6,000 in 1868, and a third-and-a-half-order Fresnel lens began operating in September 1869.[^2][^4]

The brick oil house was built in 1895, seventy-five feet (23 m) west of the dwelling, to hold the kerosene the light burned.[^9][^2]

In 1910 the Board asked for $100,000 for a new light and fog signal on the island, a request it repeated for years.[^2] The estimate is printed in the Commissioner's report as "For establishing a light and fog-signal station on Michigan Island (Lake Superior), Wis."[^10] In 1918 the plan changed to raising the old light, adding a fog signal and setting an acetylene light on Gull Island. Funds came in 1928, by which time the plan had become a radiobeacon.[^2] In the spring of 1928 a survey crew "floated helium balloons to the appropriate height" to test visibility from the water at possible tower sites.[^2] A tramway from the station's dock up to the bluff was built that year.[^2][^4]

The new tower was a 112-foot (34 m) cast-iron skeletal tower that had served at Schooner Ledge on the Delaware River from 1880 to 1918, according to LighthouseFriends.[^2] The Park Service says the Lighthouse Board had used a cast-iron, 112-foot (34 m) skeletal tower "lying upon the banks of the Delaware River," and installed it on the island's highest ground.[^4] The nomination dates the tower to 1880.[^1] The total cost of the 1928 and 1929 improvements, the new dwelling and powerhouse included, was $61,041.[^2] On 29 October 1929 the Fresnel lens moved from the old tower into the new one.[^2] LighthouseFriends quotes the keeper's log: "Started up new tower at sunset," with the station looking odd, "the old tower being dark for the first time in navigation in 72 years."[^2]

## The keepers

Roswell H. Pendergrast was keeper from 1869 to 1874, at $560 a year.[^2] He and his wife Helen planted more than a thousand trees and shrubs to learn what would thrive on the island, and by 1872 they had sold more than $3,000 in nursery stock.[^2] The roster names Helen L. Pendergrast as first assistant from 1872 to 1874, and Matilda Rumrill as first assistant from 1874 to 1882, with Pliny F. Rumrill as head keeper.[^6]

Robert Carlson, who had been an assistant at Outer Island, became head keeper in 1893.[^2] His wife, Anna Maria Carlson, came with him, with their young daughter, and twin boys were born in May 1894. The Carlsons chose to spend the winter on the island, and his brother was his assistant there. Early one morning the two men went out fishing on the ice, and the ice broke away and drifted them to Madeline Island. Anna, left alone with the children, later remembered "the stark loneliness of it." The men got back on the fourth day.[^2] The Board's report for fiscal 1894 says the walk and stairway from the dwelling to the boathouse, 446 feet (136 m) long, was rebuilt that year.[^8]

Edward J. Lane was head keeper from 1902 until 1939, when he retired after 37 years on the island. LighthouseFriends calls him the longest-serving keeper in the history of the Apostle Islands.[^2][^6][^7] In 1916, LighthouseFriends says, he shot a lynx that had come to the back door of the dwelling.[^2]

Between 1929 and 1937, most first and second assistants stayed a year or less, the Park Service says.[^4] By 1939 the station was run by one keeper, Robert E. Westveld.[^4][^7] One year, while closing the station for the season, Westveld fell two stories from an icy roof and broke both feet. The radio was out and ice sealed the boathouse doors, so the men could only wait until someone on the mainland missed them. Three days later Mrs. Westveld mentioned her worry to a supply-boat captain, who went out and brought Westveld to Bayfield for treatment.[^2] The last resident keeper left in 1943.[^2][^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 5 May 1889 lightning struck the old tower and ran down its metal stairway, causing no serious injury, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The Park Service says the damage was moderate, and that copper poles on the tower kept the building from being destroyed.[^4]

The Lake Carriers' Association, in its 1908 annual report, wrote that "A number of wrecks have occurred in this vicinity in recent years," and that the light could not be seen by vessels approaching from the east, as LighthouseFriends quotes it.[^2] The Park Service says ships passing north of the island could not pick up the beacon.[^4] The Board's answer was to ask for a replacement light on higher ground, a request it repeated for years.[^2][^4]

On 3 September 1914 Keeper Lane and his assistant, Lawrence T. Pedersen, took in a man whose motor boat had been disabled during a gale. They furnished food and shelter at the Michigan Island Light Station.[^11][^12]

## After the keepers

After the First World War the Schroeder Lumber Company bought most of the island outside the lighthouse reservation and logged it from 1919 to 1923.[^2] The Park Service says logging continued until 1968.[^4]

The Coast Guard assumed responsibility for the lighthouse in 1939.[^2] The lamp became fully automated in 1943, when Westveld was relieved, and Coast Guardsmen stationed on nearby Devils Island took over the station's operation and made periodic visits.[^4] The old tower was no longer lit.[^2]

In 1970 the National Park Service created Apostle Islands National Lakeshore and included the island.[^4] In 1972, LighthouseFriends says, the Fresnel lens came out of the new tower and went on display at the park's visitor center in Bayfield.[^2][^4] A DCB-224 aerobeacon replaced it.[^4] The lighthouses on the island were listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1977.[^1][^4] A 2011 cultural landscape report guides the Park Service's work to partly restore the island's 1920s landscape.[^4]

## Today

LighthouseFriends describes the new tower's light as a solar-powered LED beacon, and the old tower is dark.[^2][^3] The archive's record for the new tower, by contrast, says the light is not an active aid to navigation and lists no current optic.[^7] The two sources conflict, and this story does not settle which is current. The nomination gives the new light's focal plane as 160 feet (49 m) above the lake.[^1]

Behind the old lighthouse, the station's privy and barn still stand.[^2] The Park Service says it has improved the station's grounds and buildings to ensure visitor safety.[^4] The island is reached by boat.[^2] LighthouseFriends names a boat operator, Apostle Islands Cruise Service, for trips to the island, and says the grounds are open while the tower and dwelling are closed.[^2] The Park Service's visit page describes a one-mile trail from the lighthouse to a campsite on the sandspit at the island's west end.[^5] LighthouseFriends notes that it takes some effort to get out to the island.[^2]
