Sheringham Point Light stands at Sheringham Point, near Shirley, British Columbia, on the Strait of Juan de Fuca.[^5][^6] Its six-sided reinforced-concrete tower was completed in 1912 to light a stretch of the coast that had been dark.[^1][^2] The light has been unstaffed since 1989.[^2] The site is now owned and run by a non-profit society, and the Canadian Coast Guard still lists the light as an active aid to navigation.[^5][^7]

## The place

The point was first mapped in 1790 by the Spanish navigator Manuel Quimper, who named it Punta de San Eusivio. In 1846, Captain Henry Kellett renamed it for Commander William Louis Sheringham of the Royal Navy, LighthouseFriends says.[^6]

The light was for the approaches to the Strait of Juan de Fuca, which by the early twentieth century "had become a strategic shipping channel," in the words of Parks Canada's statement of significance.[^1] The Society says the new light was meant to give aid "along the dark stretch of coastline between the light stations at Carmanah Point and Race Rocks."[^2]

The land was bought in 1911. The Society says four acres came from Edwin Clark, an early settler and the community's first postmaster.[^2] LighthouseFriends gives the sale as $196.85 for 3.937 acres.[^6]

## Building the light

The tower was designed by William P. Anderson, then chief engineer of the Department of Marine and Fisheries.[^1] Parks Canada says that design "was only used from 1907 until 1912," which makes Sheringham one of its last towers.[^1]

The tower is hexagonal, with six buttresses that flare out to carry the gallery.[^1] The sources give different heights. The Society says the tower is "12.5 metres high," while LighthouseFriends says it "stands twenty metres (64 ft) high."[^2][^6] This story does not choose between them.

LighthouseFriends quotes the Department's figures for the work: $8,745 for the tower, dwelling, boathouse and oil shed, $7,609.50 for the lantern room, optic and freight, and $4,898.49 for installation. It names the contractor as Thomas Stedham.[^6]

The original lens was a third-order Fresnel, floated on a bed of mercury and turned by weights that had to be "re-wound every 3 hours."[^2] The Society says the light could be seen "almost 25 km out to sea," while the Coast Guard's nominal range is 9 nautical miles.[^2][^7]

Eustace Arden lit the light for the first time on September 30, 1912.[^5][^6] The Department of Marine and Fisheries' lightkeepers table, in its annual report for the fiscal year 1914-15 (printed in 1916), lists "E. T. Arden" in the column that lines up with Sheringham Point.[^8] The site also had a boathouse and "a residence for the lightkeeper and his family."[^2]

## The keepers

The Society says seven principal lightkeepers served the station from 1912.[^3] The first was Eustace Arden. He was born in Toronto and moved to British Columbia in the early 1890s. Before he came to the light, he drove the mail stage between Victoria and Sooke and ran a store at Otter Point.[^3] The Society says he and his wife spent "34 years at Sheringham Point."[^3]

Supplies came by sea. The Sooke Region Museum's historian writes that, "there being no access by road in the early days," the family depended on "the monthly visit of the supply ship Estevan as it brought oil for the lighthouse."[^9]

Thomas Charles Cross kept the light as a temporary keeper for about four months in 1946, the Society says.[^3] Alfred Dickenson followed, from 1946 until his retirement in 1948.[^3] LighthouseFriends says that on his first night of service "the light stopped at 12:30 and again at 1:10 a.m."[^6]

Thomas Westhead kept the light from 1948 to 1959.[^3] Frederick Mountain was head keeper from 1959, and the Society records his "sudden, tragic death in 1967."[^3]

James D. Bruton's start is dated differently. The Society puts him at the station from 1967 to 1987.[^3] LighthouseFriends says he was transferred from Lennard Island "in 1968, after Keeper Frederick Mountain passed away at the station," and the Sooke historian says it was "in 1968 that the Jim Bruton family came to take over the Light."[^6][^9] During Bruton's time the lens was replaced and the fog-alarm building rebuilt.[^3]

Kurt Cehak and his wife Erika served at the station from 1987 to 1989. The Society says that labour rules stopped Kurt being rehired for the last six months, so "it was Erika who was officially listed as Sheringham Point's last Head Lightkeeper during this period." It calls her the only woman to hold that post.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Before the light, the bark *Anna Barnard*, bound from San Francisco to Sooke with a load of lumber, struck rocks a few miles from Sheringham Point on the night of February 26, 1862, according to LighthouseFriends. Its captain swam to shore, but the cook and a seaman who were with him drowned.[^6] The five men left aboard climbed the mast and waited for the tide to go out.[^6] The survivors were, LighthouseFriends says, "assisted by First Nation people" and taken to Victoria.[^6]

The wreck that prompted the light was the steamship *Valencia*. On January 22, 1906, it missed the turn into the Strait of Juan de Fuca and "steamed directly on to the rocks near Pachena Point."[^2] The Society says that "137 men, women and children were lost."[^2] Parks Canada's statement says the wreck, which resulted in "over 100 deaths," prompted the Department to improve the aids along this coast.[^1] The Society says the government then ordered twelve more lighthouses, and that one of them was at Sheringham Point.[^2]

In 1942 the site was enlarged by 1.7 acres, and a surveillance building went up. "This housed a small contingent of armed forces," the Society says, watching for enemy incursions.[^2]

## After the keepers

In 1925 a two-tone diaphone foghorn was added, powered by a diesel engine.[^2] LighthouseFriends describes it differently: the original steam fog horn "was replaced around 1925" by a diaphone "powered by compressed air from oil engines."[^6] The fog-alarm building was replaced in 1976 with a flat-roofed concrete-block structure.[^2] The lens went the same year. It was replaced by an electric rotating lens, and the original third-order Fresnel went to the Sooke Region Museum.[^2][^6]

The assistant keeper's post was abolished in the mid-1980s.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the original keeper's dwelling "was intentionally burned down on January 9, 1986," after the post went.[^6] The modern bungalow was also burned in the mid-1990s to stop vandalism, the Society says.[^2]

The light was automated during the 1980s and "finally de-staffed in 1989," the Society says. LighthouseFriends gives the month as August 1989.[^2][^6]

In January 2003 the federal government declared the site "excess to its program requirements," and the Society was formed that year to protect and conserve the site.[^2][^6] In 2010 the government declared most lighthouses surplus and passed the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act.[^2] In June 2015 the government designated Sheringham Point a National Heritage Lighthouse, and in October 2015 "legal Title to the Lighthouse and its surrounding lands was transferred to the Society."[^2] The Society calls it the first lighthouse in Canada designated under the Act.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the designation came in 2015, and records a multi-year commitment of $550,000 from the Westaway Charitable Foundation to the Society in 2017.[^6]

## Today

The lighthouse is "owned, operated and cared for by the Sheringham Point Lighthouse Preservation Society, a non-profit charity based in Shirley."[^5] The site is open to the public every day. The Society's visit page gives its current hours as 9 a.m. to 7 p.m., with seasonal changes.[^4] A site-hours box on another Society page reads 9:00 am to 6:00 pm.[^3]

Access is on foot, from a parking lot at the end of Sheringham Point Road. The Society says the trail is "approximately 200 metres from the parking lot to the Lighthouse Gate," and a further 300 metres to the tower, which is steeper. A viewing platform at the top of the site gives a view for visitors who cannot walk the trail.[^4] The Society says "this is the ONLY legal access to the Lighthouse," and that there is no legal access or parking on the roads nearby.[^4]

The interior of the tower and the engine room are closed "due to safety considerations."[^4] The Coast Guard list gives the light's characteristic as Fl G 15s, a focal height of 21.9 metres, and a nominal range of 9 nautical miles, and describes the tower as a "White hexagonal tower with red top."[^7]
