Conimicut Light is a six-story cast-iron tower standing in open water at the mouth of the Providence River in Rhode Island, built to warn ships off a long sandbar that reaches out from Conimicut Point.[^1] The first lighthouse built offshore in Narragansett Bay, it is still an active aid to navigation and now belongs to the City of Warwick.[^1][^3]

## The place

The Providence River is the approach to Providence, and the National Register nomination describes the light as one mile offshore, midway between Conimicut Point and Nayatt Point.[^1] It warns of a shoal, a sandbar that extends from Conimicut Point out toward the light.[^1] A light had been lit at Nayatt Point, on the east side of the river's entrance, in 1828, but the nomination says it did not warn ships of that bar.[^1] Before Conimicut, the nomination adds, only unlighted day markers and buoys marked the offshore reefs and shoals within the bay.[^1]

## Building the light

Sources disagree on when the first structure went up. The National Register nomination says an unlit granite day beacon was placed on the sandbar's eastern edge in 1857; LighthouseFriends says a granite tower was built on the shoal in 1866 as a daymark.[^1][^3] Both agree on what came next. According to LighthouseFriends, vessel captains and owners petitioned the Lighthouse Board for a light before the tower was even finished, and Congress voted $15,000 in March 1867 to fit it for one.[^3] The tower was raised, a store and watchroom were added, and a lantern and lens went on top. The nomination says the light was first lit on 1 November 1868.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that the fourth-order Fresnel lens came from the Nayatt Point lighthouse, which was then discontinued, and that the new station also had a fog bell.[^3]

The granite tower had no living quarters. The nomination says the keeper used the old Nayatt Point lighthouse as his quarters and rowed across the river to the new tower from a landing built there.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the keeper and his assistant made that mile-long trip in a small rowboat.[^3]

A dwelling built on the landing pier in 1874 did not last. The nomination says an ice floe destroyed it the following spring and that it was never rebuilt.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the congressional appropriation for the dwelling to March 1873, and says the assistant keeper's post, abolished when the dwelling went up, was restored once the keepers had to row again.[^3] By 1880 both the tower and the Nayatt Point dwelling were in disrepair, and the government chose to replace the old tower with a new iron one that held living quarters.[^1] The old tower came down in 1883.[^1][^3]

The present tower is a prefabricated cast-iron cylinder, 60 feet (18 m) high and about 18 feet (5.5 m) across, standing on a cement cylinder sunk about 10 feet into the bay bottom.[^1] Loose boulders around the base were placed to deflect ice floes.[^1] Inside, the nomination lists a kitchen on the first floor, a living room on the second, a bedroom on the third and a tool room on the fourth, with the watchroom above.[^1] In 1896 iron davits were added for lifting a small boat out of the water, and in 1898 a larger pier with a covered storm porch replaced the earlier landing.[^1] The Light-House Board reported that the characteristic of the light was changed on 10 September 1898 by adding a red sector.[^4]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive, working from the keepers' registers, lists 22 terms as principal keeper between 1868 and 1965, Jensen holding two of them.[^2] Davis Perry was the first, followed by Fernando Healy, John Walker and Samuel S. Beaumont; Horace W. Arnold held the post from 1874 to 1886.[^2]

Arnold was keeper on the night the ice took the dwelling. LighthouseFriends dates the event to March 1875 and quotes the Daily Star of 9 March: the building toppled onto its side with Arnold and his nine-year-old son asleep in the upper story, and Arnold "swam about for upwards of an hour with him upon his back, inside the building" before he left the boy on a mattress and went to ring the tower's bell and set a distress signal.[^3] The paper as quoted calls the station "Conanicut", which LighthouseFriends treats as Conimicut. The two were taken off by a tug and a schooner, and the family lost everything they owned; LighthouseFriends adds that Arnold was awarded $319 four years later.[^3]

Daniel McDonald kept the light from 1895 to 1906, and Nicolai Jensen from 1909 to 1920 and again from 1923 to 1925.[^2] In its report for fiscal 1921 the Commissioner of Lighthouses noted that E. J. Smith, keeper of Conimicut, had given food and lodging to the crew of a Providence motor boat that ran aground near the station and later sank.[^5] Nelson H. Powell then held the post for 24 years, from 1931 to 1955.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Providence News reported that the steamer *Pilgrim*, on her run up from New York, ran aground on the Conimicut shoals in September 1904. She was not damaged, and floated off as the tide rose.[^7]

On 8 June 1922 the keeper's family suffered a tragedy at the station. According to the Associated Press, Ellsworth Smith's wife Nellie, thirty, who had grown despondent in the isolation of the light, gave poison tablets to her two sons and took one herself.[^6] Smith, returning to the light that evening, found that she and their younger child were dead, and he took the older boy ashore, where he was given an antidote.[^3][^6] LighthouseFriends reports that the light was dark that night, and that mariners were told of the outage by broadcast.[^3]

LighthouseFriends records that by the mid-1930s as many as forty to sixty people at a time were fishing from the riprap around the base, until the Coast Guard helped have the station posted off-limits.[^3]

## After the keepers

The light was converted from kerosene to electricity in July 1960, which the nomination says made it the last lighthouse in the United States to be electrified.[^1] Automation followed. The nomination says 1963; LighthouseFriends says Officer-in-Charge Russell R. Barnhart and his crew left for the last time on 23 June 1965, and the archive's roster ends in 1965.[^1][^2][^3]

The City of Warwick acquired the lighthouse in 2004 under the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act, with two former keepers at the ceremony.[^3] A Conimicut Lighthouse Foundation formed the next year, but LighthouseFriends reports that a decade after the transfer little had been done, and that a 2014 lease proposal stalled.[^3] A federal grant, given as $775,000 by LighthouseFriends and as a $750,000 congressional earmark by the mayor's office, paid for exterior restoration, which Warwick's mayor announced complete in August 2024.[^3][^8]

## Today

Conimicut Light is an active aid to navigation, owned by the City of Warwick, which is responsible for the structure; the Coast Guard looks after the light and fog signal.[^3] The tower is closed to visitors and stands offshore; the best views from land are from Conimicut Point Park.[^3] The mayor has said he is planning to restore the interior as well.[^8]
