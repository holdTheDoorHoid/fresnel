Love Point Light was a six-sided iron screwpile lighthouse in Chesapeake Bay, off the north end of Kent Island, Maryland, at the mouth of the Chester River. It was lit in 1872. Its house was dismantled in 1964, and a small flashing light and a fog bell now stand on the old foundation.[^1][^13]

## The place

Signed petitions asking for lights at Bloody Point and at Love Point reached the Lighthouse Board in 1868.[^4] The idea was older than that. In 1837 the Navy's Board of Commissioners accepted Captain Alexander Claxton's objection to a light at Love Point, which he judged "uncalled for by the exigencies of commerce."[^2] An 1854 appropriation of $15,000 for a light there had still not been begun by 1857. The Board said a light on the point would do little for navigation, partly because of the long shoal that runs out from it.[^3]

The light was set on the shoal, not on the point. The Board's report for 1871 places the site in ten feet of water about one mile from Love Point, and the report for 1872 says about one and a quarter miles, to the northeast.[^6][^7]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $15,000 on 15 July 1870 for a light-house on Love Point.[^5] The plan was an iron screw-pile structure, to stand in ten feet (3 m) of water a little over a mile from the north end of Kent Island.[^5] The chapter of the U.S. Lighthouse Society says the light was built in 1872 at a cost of $15,000.[^13]

A Pennsylvania firm, Bigler, Young & Co., held a claim for the erection of the light. In 1874 Congress authorized the Treasury to settle that claim, up to $1,694.55.[^9] The light was lit on 15 August 1872.[^8]

The Board's annual reports record routine work in later years. In the year ended 30 June 1899 new model fifth-order lamps were installed at Love Point.[^14] In the year ended 30 June 1901 some 954 feet (291 m) of new decking was laid, and soundings were taken around the lighthouse.[^15]

The Chesapeake Chapter describes the house as hexagonal and says it was a duplicate of the Choptank River light.[^13] The lens is the first point where the sources disagree. The Archive's record gives a fifth-order Fresnel lens installed in 1872.[^1] The Board's report for 1876 says the light had been reduced on 15 November 1875 from a three-and-a-half-order lens, flashing red, to a fifth-order lens, fixed white.[^11]

## The keepers

The Archive's roster for Love Point has 84 lines, running from 1872 to 1945 and after.[^1] William B. Reed was the first head keeper, from 1872 to 1876, and Charles H. Erdman was the first assistant in 1872 and 1873.[^1] Between 1876 and 1886 eight different men were listed as head keeper, several of them for a year or two.[^1]

Anders Simonsen was head keeper from 1908 to 1919, and Arthur Midgett from 1922 to 1930.[^1] William M. Midgett was an assistant keeper from 1916 to 1917, and he appears in the 1916 rescue described below.[^1][^17] Otho Bounds (1930 to 1937), Nelson H. Lewis (1937 to 1939) and Samuel C. Dawson (1939 to 1945) followed Arthur Midgett as head keepers. The roster's last head keeper, C. C. Dowdy, is listed from 1945 with no end date.[^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The first winter was hard on the light. The Board's report for 1873 describes the ice of winter 1872 to 1873: two of the ice-breaker piles were carried away and two of the main columns were broken, so that the light had to be discontinued for a time.[^8]

In 1874 Congress added $5,000 for riprap stone to protect the base.[^9] The report for 1875 says that after the riprap was placed, the light stood through the winter of 1874 to 1875 "without damage or material displacement."[^10] In January 1879 ice banked up against the platform without doing damage, and the Board had 100 cubic yards of heavy stone placed on the riprap.[^12]

Keepers also made rescues. The Bulletin for 1916 says the tender *Jessamine*, commanded by Rufus A. Brooks and assisted by William M. Midgett, assistant keeper of the Love Point station, rescued an oysterman "who had fallen overboard near Love Point Light Station."[^17] Second officer Norman C. Manyon took part. In 1927 the Department cited the rescue of 20 November 1916 among Captain Manyon's commendations.[^18]

In September 1926 Arthur Midgett, the keeper, gave help to the occupants of a motor boat disabled near the station.[^19] On 5 July 1927 he assisted a disabled motor boat with three men aboard.[^20] The Bulletin's index for 1924 to 1929 lists "Midgett, Arthur, keeper Love Point" among those who assisted vessels.[^21] LighthouseFriends also records that on 21 October 1928 the keeper helped a man whose fishing boat had turned over near the station.[^23]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the light was automated in 1953.[^22] In 1964 the house was taken down, and an automatic light was erected on the screwpile foundation.[^22] The Archive's record says the same: the house was dismantled in 1964, and the foundation now serves as a base for a small flashing light and fog bell.[^1] In the report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1919 the Commissioner of Lighthouses listed ironwork repaired at "Love Point Shoal Light Station."[^16] LighthouseFriends gives the same repair for this light under 1919.[^22] The Board's reports of 1870 to 1873 call the light "Love Point Shoal" too,[^5][^8] so the entry is taken to be this light, though the report gives no other detail.

## Today

The old iron foundation carries a small flashing light and a fog bell.[^1] The Archive's record says the station is not open to the public.[^1] The station is not open to the public, and visitors should not try to land near the foundation.[^1]
