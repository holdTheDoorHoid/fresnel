Wood End Light stands at the southern tip of the sandy arm that curls around Provincetown Harbor, on Cape Cod, Massachusetts. A light has shone there since the early 1870s, from a brick tower the Coast Guard still owns.[^1][^3] The light was automated in 1961, and the tower is closed to visitors.[^3]

## The place

The station lies "across the Harbor from Provincetown," the 1981 survey form says, about a mile and a half away.[^1] LighthouseFriends says Wood End lies "between Race Point and Long Point," the two other lights on the same sandy spit.[^3] Keepers at Wood End and Long Point both complained to the Lighthouse Board about the salt works and fish oil works between them, "the stench and flies" that came with them.[^3]

A wreck helped bring the light. LighthouseFriends says the November 1871 sinking of the schooner William H. Atwood, with the loss of all aboard, "contributed to the erection of a lighthouse at Wood End."[^3] Before that there was a day beacon: "A white, pyramidal day beacon constructed there in 1864," LighthouseFriends says.[^3] The 1981 form says the land was acquired in 1864 and a white pyramidal tower erected then.[^1]

Congress appropriated $15,000 for the light in June 1872, according to LighthouseFriends.[^3]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says the new light first shone on 20 November 1872 through "its fifth-order Fresnel lens."[^3] The 1981 form dates the present tower to 1873.[^1] "The present 38' tower was erected," it says, and the tower still serves as an aid to navigation.[^1] The sources do not reconcile the two dates.

The tower is "square, pyramidal, brick," and LighthouseFriends says it is painted brown.[^3] The 1981 form says its exterior "has been painted" white.[^1] Its focal plane is "thirty-four feet above ground" (about 10 m) and "forty-five feet above sea level" (about 14 m).[^3] LighthouseFriends says the red light "flashed every fifteen seconds" and "could be seen eleven nautical miles in good weather."[^3]

The keeper's dwelling was built in 1896, "one-and-a-half-story, six-room, wooden keepers house" for $29,000.[^3] A stone breakwater across the upper harbor followed in 1911, "so that at low tide one could cross from Provincetown directly to the light."[^3]

In 1916 the Bureau changed the light's illuminant. The 1916 Bulletin lists "Wood End, Cape Cod Bay" among the lights changed from oil to incandescent oil vapor.[^5] LighthouseFriends quotes the 1917 Bulletin on the lens: "In January, 1916, the installation of a 35 mm, type B, single-tank, incandescent oil vapor lamp" was recommended for "the eight panel fifth-order revolving lens at Wood End Light Station," but "this lamp had never been used in any but a fourth-order lens."[^3][^6] LighthouseFriends says a metal deflector was fitted, and that "an installation of this kind was made on August 17, 1916."[^3]

## The keepers

Thomas Lowe lit the light and "served at the station for the next twenty-five years," from 1872 to 1897.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends prints the account Lowe gave a Boston Globe reporter in 1889, when "Keeper Lowe demonstrated how the clockwork mechanism turned the lens." As a boy, LighthouseFriends says, Lowe "shipped out on a fishing schooner from Wellfleet," later "saw action in the Mexican War aboard the frigate Ohio," and tried his luck as a gold rush '49er. Poor health brought him ashore, and he became a keeper. "His first assignment was in 1868 as an assistant at Cape Cod Lighthouse."[^3]

Philip R. Smith replaced Lowe in 1897 and kept the light until 1924.[^2][^3] The efficiency pennant went to the station under him: "From 1912 to at least 1920, Keeper Smith was awarded the efficiency pennant."[^3] The Bulletin defined the award as going to "the light station showing the highest general efficiency" in each district.[^4] In the 1915 list for the second district, the flag goes to "Wood End, Mass" and "Philip R. Smith."[^4] The Bulletin's flag lists name him again in 1917, and in 1921 for the calendar year 1920.[^6][^7]

Douglas H. Shepherd kept the light from 1924 to 1940.[^2] In a severe ice storm in February 1935 he was cut off. The Boston Globe, quoted by LighthouseFriends, reported that he "has been ice-bound and imprisoned at his station the past two weeks."[^3] George H. Fitzpatrick (1945) and Clarence E. Allison (1950 to 1952) complete the roster.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The 1981 form's chronology for 1875 says more than fifty ships were wrecked along this shore.[^1] LighthouseFriends says there have been over fifty shipwrecks since 1875.[^3] Because of the wrecks, LighthouseFriends says, Lowe at times had to rouse nearby residents to help with rescues.[^3]

The Life-Saving Service opened its own station at Wood End in 1896, "one located at Wood End, near Provincetown," in the service's annual report for that year.[^3][^8]

In October 1930 the station's water supply ran out. LighthouseFriends says the state forester ran hose "from a hydrant in Provincetown out to the lighthouse," and the water "was allowed to run for forty hours to replenish the supply."[^3]

## After the keepers

In 1960 the lens was converted "from a 4th Order Fresnel" to a modern light, the 1981 form says.[^1] The 1917 Bulletin's lens note, as LighthouseFriends quotes it, and the USLHS record describe a fifth-order lens, which disagrees with that account.[^3][^2] In 1961 the lighthouse was automated, with a modern aerobeacon in the lantern room, and the keeper's dwelling and storage shed came down: "Dwelling and storage shed were razed."[^1][^3]

The station was unmanned by the time of the 1981 form, which lists its status as "Unmanned/Active aid."[^1] In 1979 the Coast Guard considered the tower for "conversion to a solar power"; in 1981 it was converted, and LighthouseFriends says it was "one of the first solar-powered lights in the country."[^1][^3]

A 1998 license lets the American Lighthouse Foundation maintain the structure, and its Cape Cod chapter has teamed up with New England Lighthouse Lovers "to paint the oil house and lighthouse."[^3]

## Today

The Coast Guard still owns Wood End Lighthouse, and the American Lighthouse Foundation holds the license to maintain it.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends lists the grounds as open and the tower as closed: "Grounds open, tower closed."[^3] The U.S. Lighthouse Society's record lists the station as not open to the public.[^2]

LighthouseFriends describes the walk: "walk the 1/2 mile breakwater" from Provincetown and then "trudge another 1/2 mile through sand."[^3] The 1981 form describes a different route. "The nearest road ends at Herring Cove Beach," it says. Access is by four-wheel-drive vehicle only, and the National Park Service restricts it.[^1]
