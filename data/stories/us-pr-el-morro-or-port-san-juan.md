The Faro del Morro (El Morro Lighthouse), also called the Puerto San Juan Light, is a three-story brick tower with a crenellated parapet and ornamental guardhouses at its corners, set on the walls of the fort at the entrance to San Juan harbor in Puerto Rico. Sources date the first light on this fortress to 1846 or 1853, and the tower there now, built in 1908, was raised around the brick base of an earlier tower.[^1][^2][^3]

## The place

The light sits on Castillo San Felipe del Morro, the six-level fort on the headland at the harbor mouth. The National Park Service says its foundation was laid in 1539 and that it was not considered complete until 1787.[^4] The lighthouse is on the east side of the harbor entrance, atop the fort's walls.[^2] The US Lighthouse Society archive describes a square tower on the castle, with its light 180.75 feet (55 m) above high water. The Lighthouse Board's 1901 report gave the height as 171 feet (52 m).[^1][^5]

## Building the light

Sources disagree on when the first light was lit. LighthouseFriends gives 1 January 1846, with a lantern holding five parabolic reflectors on the fort's Austria Bastion.[^2] The National Register nomination also says 1846 and also mentions five reflectors.[^3] A National Park Planner page gives August 1846 and says the iron tower was made by a New York company and shipped to San Juan.[^6] The US Lighthouse Society archive and the Lighthouse Board's 1901 report instead say the station was established in 1853 "by the Spanish."[^1][^5] The same disagreement appears in George R. Putnam's 1917 book.[^7] The sources read do not explain the difference.

The light moved in 1876 to the Ochoa Bastion, the bastion closest to the sea, where LighthouseFriends says it could be better seen by ships approaching from the northeast.[^2] The nomination says the new installation had an exposed brick octagonal base carrying an octagonal cast-iron tower, painted gray and white, and that it had no dwelling "although it was serviced by three keepers."[^3]

The tower was a casualty of the Spanish-American War. LighthouseFriends links the damage to Admiral Sampson's bombardment of San Juan in May 1898.[^2] The Lighthouse Board, which took over the Puerto Rican lights on 1 May 1900, reported that "Extensive repairs were made to Morro light, which had been rendered unserviceable by the American bombardment."[^8][^7]

The sources differ again on the 1899 replacement. LighthouseFriends says Ensign W. R. Gherardi of the US Navy took charge of the island's lighthouses on 24 January 1899 and began an octagonal reinforced-concrete tower, and that the light burned again on 22 March 1899.[^2] The nomination also says the Navy Department built a concrete octagon.[^3] The Lighthouse Board's 1901 report says instead that the present tower "was buflt by the insular government in 1899" (the spelling is the report's OCR text) and describes a gray octagonal iron tower 46 feet (14 m) high from its base.[^5] The National Park Planner page describes an octagonal brick tower on the 1876 base.[^6] No source reconciles the three descriptions.

That tower did not last. The nomination says that by 1905-1906 a crack ran through and around the top, directly under the lantern, and the tower was demolished; LighthouseFriends says it had a serious crack by 1905.[^2][^3] The present three-story tower was built in 1908 around the old brick octagonal base.[^3] It has a crenellated parapet with ornamental guardhouses on its corners, similar to those on the fort, in a design LighthouseFriends calls Spanish-influenced.[^2] The lens is a third-order Fresnel by Sautter, Lemonnier & Cie. LighthouseFriends dates it to 1908, while the nomination calls it an 1875 lens installed in 1908.[^2][^3] The National Park Planner page says a new Fresnel lens arrived in 1880.[^6] It turned by clockwork driven by a 200-pound (91 kg) weight hanging in the stair column. An electric motor replaced the clockwork in 1932.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends gives the cost of the new lighthouse as $3,961.47.[^2]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society archive, working from the keeper registers, names head keepers at Port San Juan from at least 1901: Eugenio Fiol, then Severo del Olmo, Jose Prieto, and from 1925 Manuel Perez Castillo, who had served as first assistant in 1924-1925 and appears as head keeper until at least 1941. Elias Muskus followed him in 1945-1948.[^1] Assistants in those years include Esteban Parra Furniz, Francisco Garcia, Arturo M. Ferrer, Jose P. Castillo and Alfredo Vicente.[^1] The archive's "at least" dates mark documented spans, not full terms.

In 1920 a single-story dwelling, 52 by 24 feet (16 by 7 m) and covered in stucco on metal lath, was built for the head keeper and the assistant.[^2] On Puerto Rican stations of the American period, Putnam notes in general that "the lightkeepers are natives of the island."[^7] One keeper's career is documented in the Lighthouse Service Bulletin. Domingo Suarez Rosa retired on 1 July 1925, aged 62, with 26 years in the US Lighthouse Service and 12 in the Spanish service. His obituary lists Port San Juan among six lighthouses where he served; it gives no years for any of them.[^9]

From 1946 the archive lists Coast Guard crew names, rather than civilian keepers, for the station.[^1] The nomination says the light was automated in 1962 and the resident personnel discontinued.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

No wreck or rescue is tied to this station in the sources read. Two earthquakes touched it. After the earthquake of 11 October 1918 the Lighthouse Service reported damage as inconsequential except at Point Borinquen and Point Jiguero; a follow-up item added "minor cra cks are r eported fr om Gu anica, Cape San Juan, and P ort Sa n .Junn Light Stations" (OCR text).[^10] On the night of 18 August 1925 the keeper reported a severe shock at about 12.03, lasting one to two seconds, and "No damage was caused to the station."[^11]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends reports the tower was painted gray at first, and that by 1937 it was cream with brown trimmings.[^2][^3] The nomination of 1981 records the tower as altered and in deteriorated condition, with restricted access.[^3] The Park Planner page says the 1908 bricks suffered from water seeping into the mortar, that repair funds from the Coast Guard in 1979 were returned unspent after a recommendation to tear the tower down, and that the National Park Service repaired it in the early 1990s.[^6] LighthouseFriends says surrounding parking lots and roads were removed before the quincentennial of Columbus's arrival in the New World and the tower was restored to its original appearance.[^2]

## Today

LighthouseFriends lists the tower as owned by the National Park Service, with grounds open and tower closed.[^2] In 2009 the lantern room was under restoration and wrapped in scaffolding, and public tours were not being offered; a ranger took the site's author up the stairs by special arrangement.[^2] The Park Planner page, last updated in 2024, says the brick tower again shows multiple cracks.[^6] The fort is part of San Juan National Historic Site, and the park says both its forts can be explored daily from 9:00 am to 5:00 pm.[^4] Anyone planning a visit should check with the park about the tower.
