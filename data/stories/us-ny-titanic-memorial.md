The Titanic Memorial Lighthouse is a lantern tower with a fixed green light and a time ball, built to remember the people who died when the Titanic sank in April 1912.[^1] It first stood on the roof of the Seamen's Church Institute, at South Street and Coenties Slip in Lower Manhattan, New York City.[^1] Since 1976 it has stood in a park at Fulton and Pearl Streets, at the entrance to the South Street Seaport.[^1][^4]

## The place

The Seamen's Church Institute was established in 1834.[^1] Its new twelve-story headquarters rose above South Street at Coenties Slip.[^1][^2] The museum's account links the cornerstone ceremony to 25 South Street.[^4] The tower's fixed green light could be seen across New York Harbor and down as far as Sandy Hook.[^1]

## Building the light

The cornerstone of the new building was laid one day after the sinking.[^1] A week later the institute announced that the lighthouse on top of the building would be a memorial to the victims.[^1] The memorial was paid for by public subscription.[^4] The museum says that wealthy donors wrote checks and schoolchildren gave pennies and nickels.[^4]

The memorial was dedicated on April 15, 1913, a year to the day after the sinking.[^1][^4] The Evening World reported that the ceremony had been planned for the roof, but rain moved it to the fourth-floor auditorium, where Bishop David H. Greer officiated.[^1][^2] Greer said that the light "shall guide pilgrims and seafaring men from every clime into this port."[^1] The New-York Tribune's report carried the line "MANY SURVIVORS ATTEND," and a further line, "Time Ball and Light To Be Ready Soon."[^3]

The time ball was put into operation on November 1, 1913.[^4] It was a bronze frame four feet (1.2 m) across, covered in canvas painted black for maximum visibility.[^4] It dropped at noon, when a telegraphic signal from the Naval Observatory activated it, and residents and workers in Lower Manhattan used it to set their watches.[^4]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the time ball dropped at noon from 1913 until 1967.[^1] The museum says the structure stayed in its original place until 1968.[^4] In 1965 the institute put its building up for sale.[^1] It moved to 15 State Street in July 1968, and the old building, with the lighthouse on its roof, was set to be demolished.[^1][^4]

A group of preservationists persuaded the demolition company to give the lighthouse to the Seaport Museum.[^4] The museum's founding president, Peter Stanford, wrote to the company that "this monument should not be doomed as part of the obsolete building to which it happens to be attached."[^4] The Kaiser-Nelson Steel & Salvage Corporation donated the top of the tower to the museum in July 1968.[^1][^4] In May 1976 it was re-erected on a lighthouse-inspired concrete podium at the corner of Fulton and Pearl Streets, with funds from the Exxon Corporation.[^1][^4]

In 2020 descendants of people connected with the Titanic called for its restoration, and a group called Friends of the Titanic Lighthouse Restoration formed to refurbish and relight it, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] The museum selected the preservation architects Jan Hird Pokorny Associates in 2023.[^4] The restoration, which the New York City Landmarks Preservation Commission reviewed and approved, included the restoration and replacement of structural steel members, glass windows and copper components, and upgrades to the electrical system and the hoist.[^4] The museum says the restoration was completed in summer 2026.[^4]

## Today

The tower stands in Titanic Memorial Park at Fulton and Pearl Streets, at the entrance to the South Street Seaport Historic District.[^1][^4] The South Street Seaport Museum owns it.[^1][^4] After the restoration, green lights glow inside the lantern.[^5] Around noon each day the restored time ball is raised and lowered on a new rigging system, and when it is not in use it rests in a receiver at the top of the lantern.[^5]

LighthouseFriends lists the grounds as open and the tower as closed, so visitors should expect to see the tower from the park rather than climb it.[^1] A plaque on the tower reads "LATITUDE 41°46' NORTH" and "LONGITUDE 50°14' WEST" above the date "APRIL 15, 1912."[^1] Those coordinates lie in the Atlantic, far from the site.[^1]
