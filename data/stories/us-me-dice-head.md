Dice Head Light stands on the southernmost point of the Castine peninsula, at the entrance to Castine Harbor.[^2][^4] Congress appropriated $5,000 for a light there in May 1828,[^1] and the first tower was a conical rubblestone tower with a dwelling beside it.[^2] In 1937 the navigation light moved to a skeleton tower nearer the shore.[^2] When a windstorm toppled that tower in 2007, the Coast Guard put a new optic in the old tower, which has carried the light since 1 January 2008.[^2]

## The place

Lumber and shipbuilding were busy on the Penobscot River when Congress acted in 1828.[^2] The site was Dice Head, almost two miles east of the northern end of Islesboro, on land that had once belonged to a family named Dyce.[^2] The 1828 Act spells the name "Dice's Head";[^1] the Lighthouse Board's report for 1895 calls the station "Dice Head, entrance to Castine Harbor, Maine."[^4]

Its position was criticised early on. In October 1835 Henry D. Hunter of the revenue cutter *Jackson* wrote that the light was poorly situated and suggested that it be located on the northern head of Holbrook Island, at the eastern entrance to Castine Harbor, as New England Lighthouses quotes him.[^2]

## Building the light

The 1828 appropriation reads: "For building a lighthouse on Dice's Head, five thousand dollars."[^1] The tower that was built stood 42 feet (12.8 m) from its base to the focal plane, beside a rubblestone dwelling. Its octagonal wrought-iron lantern held ten lamps and fourteen-inch reflectors, showing a fixed white light 129 feet (39 m) above mean high water.[^2] The USLHS station record measures the tower differently, from its base to the centre of the lantern, as 51 feet (15.5 m).[^8] The same record describes the keeper's quarters as a wooden colonial cape dated 1829, where New England Lighthouses describes a rubblestone dwelling.[^8][^2]

New England Lighthouses reports a newspaper notice of 5 November 1828 saying the light would go into service that evening.[^2] An unsigned clippings file of extracts from Lighthouse Service reports gives 1829 as the year the light was established, and says it was "rebuilt in 1858."[^3] The USLHS record also gives 1829.[^8] The sources disagree on the first year, and this story does not choose between them.

New England Lighthouses reports that an 1838 inspection found the station "in good order," but that I. W. P. Lewis's 1843 report to Congress described the tower as "laid up in lime mortar of bad quality," with walls cracked by frost and a dwelling "out of repair altogether."[^2]

A law of 18 August 1856 authorised the Treasury to discontinue the light if it decided the light was unnecessary.[^3] The Lighthouse Board considered discontinuing the light around 1857, but major repairs were carried out in 1858. The work included a six-sided wooden sheath around the tower and a fourth-order Fresnel lens in place of the lamps and reflectors.[^2] An unsigned clippings file also reports that "Important repairs have been made" at Dice's Head and four other Maine light-houses, and that "lenses have been placed in them."[^3]

## The keepers

The USLHS place record lists fifteen keeper lines for the station, from 1828 to 1937.[^8] The first keeper was Jacob Sherburne, as the roster spells the name. New England Lighthouses calls him Jacob Shelburne, a former sea captain, and lists his service as 1829 to 1837.[^2] In his autobiography, written while he was keeper, he recalled that in June 1828 he went to New York and then sought the appointment as keeper of the light then being built.[^2]

George H. Webb served from 1865 to 1887, Charles A. Gott from 1887 to 1911, and Edward T. Spurling from 1911 to 1930, each for about two decades.[^8][^2] Adelbert H. Gott is listed for the single year 1911.[^8] New England Lighthouses reports that the Spurling family had six children. Beatrice Spurling, one of them, recalled life at Dice Head in a 1974 interview. Then eleven, she attended the grammar school in Castine, a short walk from the light station.[^2]

The station's upkeep shows in the Board's reports. Its 1895 report records that "An oil house was built."[^4] The 1896–97 report says "A hand striker for the fog bell was set up."[^5] The 1899 report records that "About 150 running feet of stone wall was replaced by a wire fence."[^6] The Board's report for the fiscal year from July 1890 to June 1891 records that a 1,000-pound bell was provided and hung at the station, to be rung by hand in answer to signals.[^10] New England Lighthouses dates the bell to 1890.[^2]

The last keeper on the roster was Vurney L. King, who served from 1930 to 1937.[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In its issue of 1 September 1931 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin lists the Dice Head keeper among those officially commended for saving life and property.[^7] The Bulletin says that Vurney L. King, keeper, Dice Head Light Station, "on July 23, rescued a woman who had become marooned on a rock when the tide came up." She "had been in this situation five or six hours, and was unable to swim."[^7]

## After the keepers

New England Lighthouses says the light was electrified in 1935.[^2] Two years later the navigational light was moved to a skeleton tower closer to the shore.[^2] The keeper's house and the land around it became the property of the Town of Castine a short time later, and in 1956 the town took over the old tower.[^2]

In 1997 Castine voters approved $98,000 to repair the lighthouse, and another $25,000 followed in March 1998.[^2] In April 1999 a late-night fire burned through the roof of the keeper's house, having apparently started in a faulty chimney.[^2] Nobody was home, and the tower was not damaged.[^2] The repairs were completed by September 2000.[^2]

In September 2007 a windstorm, which New England Lighthouses calls a microburst, toppled the skeleton tower.[^2] In late October 2007 the Coast Guard announced that it would install a new optic in the old tower.[^2] A 250 mm optic went into service on 1 January 2008, showing a white flash every six seconds.[^2]

## Today

The Town of Castine owns the tower and the keeper's house, and rents the house to help pay for the property's upkeep.[^2][^8] The light is active again.[^2] New England Lighthouses says the grounds are open to the public daily until sunset, and that a path leads around the tower.[^2] The sources do not say whether the tower itself is open to visitors.

The Castine Historic District nomination says that the only thing Castine needed to make its setting perfect was "a lighthouse, and it has one."[^9]
