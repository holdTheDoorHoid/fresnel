Windmill Point Light stands where the Detroit River runs into Lake St. Clair, in Michigan.[^1] The first light there went into service in 1838. The tower that stands today dates from 1933.[^1]

## The place

The point was marshy ground with a French name that meant something like "almost an island." A stone windmill about thirty feet (9 m) tall stood there and gave the place its later name.[^1]

On 3 March 1837 Congress appropriated $5,000 for a light on Windmill Island, at the outlet of Lake St. Clair.[^1] That same year Andrew Mack, the customs collector at Detroit, bought nearly four acres at the point for the light.[^1]

In its 1874 report the Lighthouse Board called the station "a guide for the whole commerce of the lakes," as LighthouseFriends quotes it.[^1]

A road to the point was added in the 1870s. Congress provided $2,000 for it in July 1876, and it was finished by 1879.[^1]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says the light was placed in operation the year after the appropriation. The first tower was built of stone and stood about forty-four feet (13 m) high, with a brick dwelling for the keeper.[^1] By 1848 its lantern held five lamps with fourteen-inch reflectors, giving a fixed white light.[^1]

A sixth-order lens came in 1856. In 1867 a larger fifth-order lens replaced it. The light was fixed white, with a red flash every ninety seconds.[^1]

The Board asked for a new station in 1873 and again in its 1874 report, which said: "An entirely new station is imperatively demanded here."[^1] Congress gave $18,000 in March 1874. The new conical brick tower was finished in October 1875. LighthouseFriends gives it as close to fifty-seven feet (17 m) tall, but the archive's place record gives the height from base to the center of the lantern as 51 feet, so the two sources do not agree.[^1][^3]

In 1890 the grounds were reworked. The old wooden bulkhead and jetties were taken out, and a concrete wall 567 feet (173 m) long went in along the shore. A six-inch sewer pipe was laid from the dwelling to the lake, and a circular iron oil house was built on the northeast side.[^1]

On 7 December 1890 a fourth-order lens changed the light. The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1891 records the new character as a fixed white light "varied by a red flash every fifteen seconds."[^2]

The same report records the range lights. The act of 3 March 1891 "appropriated $3,000 for the establishment of range lights at this point," work began on 8 April and finished in May, and the lights were "exhibited for the first time" on the night of 20 May 1891.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the range lights brought in a first assistant keeper, Hamilton Martin, in 1891.[^1]

In 1907 the wooden range towers gave way to two steel towers. The towers carried a locomotive headlight that was raised and lowered in a wooden cage and gave a fixed red light.[^1] Eight rooms were added to the dwelling in 1908. In 1919 the range towers were raised and electric lights were installed.[^1]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists 29 keepers and assistants from 1838 to 1930, beginning with John M. Martin, who served from 1838 to 1842.[^3]

LighthouseFriends says John Martin, the first keeper, had been a soldier in the War of 1812, later sat for Michigan as a representative, and died in Washington in 1848.[^1]

Nathan James Roach Kennedy was the principal keeper from 1889 to 1907, according to the archive.[^3] The archive then lists Mattie Kennedy as acting principal keeper for 1907, and gives no further detail about her post.[^3] LighthouseFriends lists her among the head keepers for that year.[^1]

Walter G. Marshall came to Windmill Point in 1910 after about twelve years as head keeper of Spectacle Reef Light, LighthouseFriends says. He was about sixty on arrival, and in 1917 his station was awarded the lighthouse efficiency flag for the best general efficiency in its district that year. He retired from the station in 1919.[^1]

The archive lists Otto Redman as principal keeper from 1919 to 1927, and James T. Story as first assistant from 1920 to 1924.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends says that in 1915 Marshall and his assistant pulled a boat to safety near the seawall, in heavy seas. The next year the two keepers towed a disabled boat ashore, and Marshall rescued a man from drowning.[^1]

For the year ended 30 June 1921 the Commissioner of Lighthouses recorded that Redman, the keeper, and J. T. Story, the assistant, of Windmill Point, rescued four men whose boat had capsized.[^4]

For the year ended 30 June 1922 Redman and Story rescued two persons who had become exhausted.[^5]

## After the keepers

In the late 1920s the Public Health Service took over the reservation in a land swap, to build a marine hospital on it. The old main tower was kept on as an unwatched light. Its main lamp ran on commercial power and was switched on and off by a sun relay. If the power failed, a reserve acetylene light lit itself.[^1]

The range lights were discontinued in 1930, and their towers were moved to the St. Marys River.[^1]

The new light began operating on 1 April 1933, and the old tower was torn down.[^1] The Lighthouse Service's Bulletin for 1934 printed a note headed "NEW WINDMILL POINT LIGHT, DETROIT RIVER, MICH."[^6] LighthouseFriends, describing the Bulletin's account, gives the new structure a concrete base one story high and a steel tower about 33 feet (10 m) above it.[^1]

The hospital has since been demolished, and the lighthouse is now the only historic structure left on the parcel. The area around it is known as Mariner Park.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light. The grounds are open, and the tower is closed.[^1] The archive's place record marks the station as not open to the public, while LighthouseFriends says the grounds are open.[^1][^3] The paved shoreline east of the tower is a popular fishing spot.[^1]
