Cape Henlopen Light stood on the Great Dune at the southern mouth of Delaware Bay, near Lewes, Delaware: a stone tower built in the 1760s with money raised in part by a Philadelphia lottery.[^3][^7][^8] It was partly burned in the Revolution, relit, and fell into the sea on the afternoon of 13 April 1926.[^1][^2][^3] When the Lighthouse Service reviewed the sixteen colonial light stations in 1939, every one was still working except Cape Henlopen, which the sea had undermined and destroyed (and one light of each of the twin pairs at Newburyport and Cape Ann).[^4]

## The place

Philadelphia lay far inland, and a ship bound for it had to come in through Delaware Bay and up the river.[^7] The Lighthouse Service's 1937 review of the district found "a progressive need for aids to mark the shoals and outline the crooked channels" of the river and bay from 1609, when, as the review has it, Henry Hudson discovered the Delaware, and counted 426 square-rigged vessels and 370 sloops and schooners sailing from Philadelphia in 1773.[^5] Writing in 1895, the Light-House Board described the bay's entrance as "well lighted" and safe to enter "in clear weather," but "difficult and dangerous in thick and stormy weather": shoal on the northern side from Cape May, and "steep to" at Cape Henlopen.[^6]

The earliest mention of the light that George R. Putnam gives, in his 1917 book on the lighthouses of the United States, is an advertisement in the *New York Mercury* of 4 January 1762 for a "Scheme of a Lottery for raising £3000 to be applied to erect a Lighthouse on Cape Henlopen and otherwise to facilitate the Navigation of the Delaware."[^1] As LighthouseFriends describes the scheme, £20,000 in prizes was to be drawn, with 15 per cent held back from the prize pool, and that deduction supplied the £3,000; the drawing had to be postponed in early 1762 because tickets sold slowly.[^7] The same site says the lottery was combined with loans and, later, tonnage duties, and that a duty of sixpence a ton on vessels entering Pennsylvania ports was imposed to repay the cost and maintain the light.[^7] The land came from the Penns, though it lay in what is now Delaware: the Lewes History Archive says Thomas and William Penn gave it, and Putnam notes a patent signed by John Penn granting 200 acres to commissioners of the Province of Pennsylvania for the lighthouse, "although it was within the limits of Delaware."[^1][^3]

## Building the light

The sources do not agree on when the light began. The Lighthouse Service said in 1926 that the tower "was built in the years 1765 to 1767."[^2] Putnam gives completion "about 1767" and notes that the cost of the work included "expense of oil from the year 1765," which he takes as evidence that a light was shown earlier.[^1] LighthouseFriends says officials announced by the end of 1765 that the light was working.[^7] The US Lighthouse Society archive lists the station as established in 1764, and the Lighthouse Service's 1937 review also says "built by the Colony in 1764."[^8][^5] The Lewes History Archive and Wikipedia give 1769 as the year it was first lit.[^3][^9]

The tower was an octagon of stone.[^8] LighthouseFriends describes walls of dressed stone (ashlar) filled with rubble, a "birdcage" lantern, and a wooden staircase with landings.[^7] The Lewes History Archive, drawing on a Cape Gazette article that cites the Lewes Historical Society's journal, says the granite was quarried near the Brandywine north of Wilmington, shipped to a dock on Lewes Creek, moved by shallow barge to within about two miles of the site and hauled the rest of the way by ox team.[^3] Its height is given three ways: 69 feet 3 inches (21 m) by the Lewes archive, 82 feet (25 m) from base to the centre of the lantern by the Lighthouse Society's Light List entry, and about 93 feet (28 m) by LighthouseFriends and by a 1926 newspaper.[^3][^8][^7][^10] The cost differs too: about £11,395 in LighthouseFriends, £7,674 in the Lewes archive.[^7][^3] Putnam judged it built on the same plans as the tower at Sandy Hook but "more imposing," standing on "a great sand ridge about forty feet above the sea" (12 m).[^1]

The Revolution damaged it. Putnam says it was "partially burned in 1777" and relit in 1784 after the damage was repaired.[^1] LighthouseFriends says British forces burned the stairs, lantern and lighting apparatus after an encounter with the keeper; the local writer behind Secrets of the Eastern Shore doubts the story of redcoats setting the fire.[^7][^11]

Alexander Hamilton's report to President Washington of 18 June 1790, reprinted in the Lighthouse Service Bulletin, lists "a lighthouse at Cape Henlopen, and several buoys, beacons, and piers" on the Delaware, then looked after by the board of wardens of the port of Philadelphia at an estimated $4,133 a year.[^12] According to LighthouseFriends, ownership passed from Pennsylvania to the federal government in 1792.[^7] That year President Washington approved a contract to "erect, sink and build a well for water" at the station for $278, and in 1795 the keeper wrote asking for "an augmentation" of his salary because "the Necessaries of Life are one third higher than they were two years ago."[^1]

By 1817 the light was described as fixed and 100 feet (30 m) above sea level, a new keeper's house was built in 1819, and by 1850 the tower burned eighteen lamps with reflectors.[^7] The Lighthouse Service's 1937 review says the tower was damaged in the War of 1812 and its interior steps burned, and was rebuilt with cast-iron stairs, a plate-glass lantern and eighteen 21-inch Argand sperm-oil reflector lamps.[^5] A first-order Fresnel lens replaced the reflectors: in 1856 according to the Lighthouse Society archive, in 1855 according to LighthouseFriends.[^8][^7] The roster first lists a first assistant in 1855 and a second assistant in 1856, so from 1856 it generally shows a head keeper and two assistants.[^8]

The keepers' dwelling had to be replaced in the early 1860s because of the sand: Putnam, quoting an unnamed source, says the old house was "threatened with speedy destruction by the steady progress in that direction of a remarkable sand hill," and dates the new one to 1863, where the Lighthouse Society archive says 1862.[^1][^8] LighthouseFriends records work in 1866 that put a brick lining inside the tower and fitted an iron stairway, iron window frames and an iron door.[^7] In the year to 30 June 1895 the Light-House Board had the porches of the keepers' house torn down and rebuilt and an elevated walk built from the tower to the dwelling, and in the year to 30 June 1899 a telephone was put in.[^6][^13] A 49-foot (15 m) wooden skeleton day beacon stood two-thirds of a mile from the tower.[^6] A second light, the Cape Henlopen Beacon, was built a mile to the north in 1824.[^9]

## The keepers

The first keeper the Lighthouse Society archive names is John Hitchcock, listed from at least 1772 to 1776, followed by Eleanor (or Elizabeth) Hitchcock, listed as principal keeper in 1776 and 1777.[^14][^15] On 12 November 1778 Pennsylvania's Supreme Executive Council heard an account for expenses and pay for attending the lighthouse, laid before it by Eleanor Hitchcock, widow of John Hitchcock, deceased, for £46 12s. 6d. The council recommended that the officer collecting the tonnage duty for the light's support settle the account and pay her the balance.[^16] That entry says no more about her.

After the war the roster gives Abraham Hargis as keeper from 1784 to 1813, twenty-nine years.[^8] The roster also shows that from 1856 to 1861 every post at the station, head keeper and both assistants, was held by a man named Wiltbank (Cornelius R. as head keeper, with John C. and then George B. as first assistant and Charles C. as second). The sources read for this story do not say whether they were related.[^8]

Dagworthy D. Joseph took charge in 1869 and stayed until 1910, forty-one years and the longest head-keeper term on the roster.[^8] The Lighthouse Society archive's transcription of his obituary says he was born in Indian River Hundred on 19 March 1839, served in the 3rd Delaware Infantry, which was at Appomattox Court House at Lee's surrender, and was mustered out a captain; he married Mary Hester Sherman in 1868, and she died on 29 January 1903.[^17] The archive's record gives a salary of $760 and notes that his services were "discontinued on May 4, 1910 on account of disability"; he died at Georgetown, Delaware, on 10 December 1915.[^17] The Rehoboth Beach Historical Society describes him as raising seven children on the dunes of the cape.[^18]

Under the rules of the time, a keeper's day turned on sunset. That is general context, not a record from this station: the 1881 *Instructions to Light-Keepers* said that lights "must be lighted punctually at sunset, and must be kept burning at full intensity until sunrise."[^19]

Charles E. Marshall, who appears as second assistant from 1885 and first assistant from 1903, became head keeper in 1910 and served until 1919; his first assistant, Harry H. Palmer, then took the post (LighthouseFriends dates Palmer's promotion to 1920).[^8][^7] The *Lighthouse Service Bulletin* of May 1920 lists Cape Henlopen and its keeper, Harry H. Palmer, as the fourth district's efficiency-flag station for 1919, the light station showing the district's highest general efficiency that year, and the Bulletin of June 1924 lists him again for 1923.[^20][^21] John C. Gray, who came as second assistant in 1919 and was first assistant from 1920, became the last head keeper in 1924.[^8] The Secrets of the Eastern Shore website quotes a July 1924 report in the *Wilmington Evening Journal* (not read directly) in which a keeper identified only as Captain Gray (no first name is given) spoke of the engineers' efforts to save the tower: "We have given up hope, and all we can do now is to wait until she sinks into the water."[^11]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The ground under the station kept shifting. In 1788 a committee of wardens told Benjamin Franklin, then president of Pennsylvania's council, that near the lighthouse "within these few years, where there have been deep ponds, there are now moles considerably high," and the next year the master warden wrote that "nor have we been without our fears on that account for the foundation of that Edifice."[^1] Putnam, writing in 1917, adds that the wind-driven sand cuts deeply into the wood of the keepers' dwellings and grinds the window glass until it is no longer transparent, "but the lantern of the light is too high to be so affected."[^1]

LighthouseFriends dates the turn from sand piling up to the shore wearing away to the late nineteenth century, and mentions, citing unnamed later reports and giving no date, sand banked against a keeper's dwelling high enough for children to get out of an upper window and slide to the ground.[^7] The Light-House Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1897 says the water tank and other fittings "covered by drifting sand" were dug out and moved, a rough detached oil shed was built until a fireproof one could be provided, and "the high sand dune surrounding this station is steadily blowing away."[^22] In the year to 30 June 1899 the day beacon's mark was moved back because the old one had been "undermined by the encroaching sea."[^13] The Lewes History Archive reports that the Lighthouse Board counted the sand blowing away at three to five feet (0.9-1.5 m) a year by 1897, and that tons of brush were laid around the tower and oil house in 1905.[^3]

The Lighthouse Service began protective works in 1914.[^2] After a storm on 1 March it built four low timber groynes and 700 feet (213 m) of timber bulkhead, with three more groynes under way, for a total cost of about $10,000; the Bulletin of September 1914 reported that "the beach has been steadily making up, and no further erosion is expected."[^23] It did not hold. In the northeast storm of 10-12 April 1918 the long bulkhead built in 1914 was destroyed except for a small section at the north end, and the sea "washed away a considerable portion of the base of the hill" on which the tower stood.[^24] After the early-February storm of 1920 the Bulletin reported that "considerable beach was lost" at Cape Henlopen but that the tower and buildings were not damaged, protected to some extent by brushwork recently put in place.[^25]

The keepers also had to deal with quicksand around the station. LighthouseFriends reports that on 24 January 1921 Palmer and Gray pulled William Watson's horse out of quicksand near the station with ropes.[^7] In March 1923, according to the *Bulletin*, Gray, while getting the light ready, saw a four-mule team sinking rapidly in the quicksand near the station; Palmer and second assistant Henry F. G. Bryant went to help and rescued the team.[^26] LighthouseFriends names the owner as Joseph Lynch and gives the date as 26 March; the Bulletin's printed date is damaged in the scan.[^7][^26]

## After the keepers

In the Commissioner of Lighthouses' estimates for improving the aids at the bay's entrance, from the report for fiscal 1915 through those of 1919, the same note appeared: "in consequence of the continued erosion of the shore line in the vicinity of Cape Henlopen Light, Del., the early destruction of that light is anticipated, measures taken for the preservation of the shore line having proved unavailing."[^27] By the 1921 report it read that "the early abandonment of this station is probable," and proposed that in that case a 60-foot (18 m) steel tower with an automatic light be placed on the dune "to take the place of the present station and keepers," with $4,000 budgeted for the tower.[^28]

The light was changed on 1 October 1924 to an automatic light at a safer location farther inshore.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the replacement was an automated beacon on a 67-foot (20 m) skeleton tower, moved in 1927 to the mouth of the Mispillion River.[^7] In December 1924 the Department of Commerce, with the State of Delaware, turned the old tower over to the Henlopen Lighthouse Preservation Commission, appointed by the governor; after engineering examinations the commission reported that in its judgment the lighthouse "was beyond saving at an economic cost."[^2] The *Smyrna Times* of 14 April 1926 said jetties, buttresses, brushwood and other obstacles had cost the government about $30,000 without success.[^10] The Secrets of the Eastern Shore article lists schemes of the 1920s that included a curved breakwater of obsolete World War I ships, a steel casing along the eastern side of the tower, and even moving it to the Dover Green or the University of Delaware.[^11] LighthouseFriends says the commission, whose secretary was Everett C. Johnson, a former Secretary of State, got no further than brushwood on the face of the dune and a plan, never carried out, to sink eleven old ships and barges offshore; the Secrets of the Eastern Shore article says Johnson headed the commission.[^7][^11]

Easter Monday was a holiday in Lewes, when townspeople climbed the dune by the tower for egg rolls and races down the sand.[^3][^11] The same article quotes a 1925 *Wilmington Evening Journal* piece (again not read directly) saying that for Lewes the day meant what a camp meeting meant to many other communities.[^11]

The tower fell shortly after noon on Tuesday, 13 April 1926.[^29][^2] Two Delaware papers carried near-identical accounts that week.[^10][^29] All night, the *Smyrna Times* said on 14 April, great waves had eaten into the sand foundation at the foot of the tower, and "harassed by the gale" the water "pounded higher and higher" until it beat away the last supports and the tower tumbled over.[^10] Nobody was on the narrow tongue of land to see the end, though the tower had been leaning 18 feet (5.5 m) out of perpendicular for several months; shortly before 1 o'clock a member of the Lewes Coast Guard "heard a roaring sound," turned his glasses on the cape and saw the lighthouse go over into the water.[^10][^29] The paper added that skippers who had often been guided to a safe harbor by the tower's 13,000-candle-power light had looked toward the cape each day "to see if the 'old man' was still there."[^10] The *Milford Chronicle* of 16 April named a northeast gale and an unusually high tide.[^29] The Lewes History Archive tells it differently: a clear spring day, about 12:45 p.m., and, in John W. Beach's account, a northeast breeze and a Western Union operator named Roland Webster eating lunch on a nearby bridge who saw the tower go.[^3] The newspapers said the detached old keeper's house was being cut away by the waves; LighthouseFriends says it followed six months later.[^10][^29][^7] People came for the stone: the Secrets of the Eastern Shore article quotes the Wilmington *News Journal* of 14 April (not read directly) as reporting souvenir hunters taking pieces of the rock and glass from the windows.[^11]

## Today

Nothing of the tower stands, and the site is now offshore, so there is no tower to visit.[^7] The cape is Cape Henlopen State Park.[^3] A replica of the lighthouse stands in the middle of a traffic roundabout at the Rehoboth Avenue entrance to Rehoboth Beach; Wikipedia and the Lewes archive say it was built in 1924 and moved there in 2004, while LighthouseFriends says it was built in 2004.[^9][^3][^7] Pieces of the tower's granite are said to survive in Lewes buildings.[^3]
