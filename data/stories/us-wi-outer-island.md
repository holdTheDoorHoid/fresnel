Outer Island Light stands on a clay bluff at the northern tip of the easternmost of the Apostle Islands, in Lake Superior off Wisconsin's Bayfield Peninsula. It was built in 1874 to mark the outside route to the new ports at the head of the lake, and it is still the most remote of the six Apostle Islands light stations.[^1][^2][^4]

## The place

The Apostle Islands lie off the Bayfield Peninsula. The National Register nomination puts Outer Island at the eastern end of the group, about 25 miles (40 km) from the Sand Island light at the western end, and calls its lighthouse the northernmost of the five it describes.[^2] The National Park Service today gives the distance from Bayfield as 24 miles (39 km).[^5]

An earlier light in the group, on Michigan Island, was commissioned in 1857 to guide vessels to La Pointe.[^3] By 1871 the Light-House Board was arguing that the traffic to and from the head of the lake was bypassing the islands altogether. In the words LighthouseFriends quotes from its request, that commerce "all passes outside of the Apostle Islands" and needed a light "on the northern end of Outer Island".[^3] The nomination adds that much of the push for a light there appears to have come from land speculators.[^2]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated the money on 3 March 1873, and work began that August under Louis Lederle. Autumn weather drove the crew off the island in early October, after the boat landing and the foundations of the dwelling and tower were in.[^3] When work resumed in May 1874 it was discovered that the station was being built on the wrong site, and about four new acres, some 1,300 feet (400 m) away, had to be cleared before building could start again.[^3]

The result was a brick conical tower joined by a covered passage to a two-and-a-half-story brick dwelling, with a cut-brownstone foundation and ornate brackets under the gallery.[^3] The USLHS Digital Archive describes the tower as brick with Italianate bracketing on a stone foundation, with its light 130 feet (40 m) above the water.[^1] The tower's own height is given differently: 90 feet (27 m) by the Park Service, just over 86 feet (26 m) by LighthouseFriends.[^4][^3] Army Quartermaster General Montgomery Meigs, as LighthouseFriends reports, praised the Board for giving its newer lighthouses some architectural design.[^3]

The lantern held a third-order Fresnel lens with six bull's-eye panels, made by L. Sautter and Company, which revolved on a clockwork mechanism to give a white flash every ninety seconds.[^3][^4] The light was first shown on 20 October 1874. Keeper Orator K. Hall wrote in the station log, "It gives a splendid light."[^3]

The fog signal came at once, and went just as fast. A steam whistle at the foot of the bluff began work on 5 November 1874, the first fog signal in the Apostle Islands, and was destroyed a few weeks later when part of the bluff collapsed onto the building.[^3] The Park Service adds that the station dock washed away in the first year and that northeast gales made the tower sway so much that Hall feared for the clockwork.[^4] A replacement went up at the top of the cliff in 1875, which made it hard to keep the whistle supplied with water, and a near-twin was added in 1878.[^4] A steam injector to pump lake water was installed in 1886, and a steam hoist for coal in 1887.[^3] The two fog-signal houses were joined in 1900, according to the Park Service;[^4] LighthouseFriends says 1901.[^3] The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 1901 (July 1900 to June 1901) records fog-signal house No. 2 being built "on the east side of building No. 1" and the two connected.[^10]

The reports give a sense of how much work the whistle was. It ran about 478 hours in the fiscal year ended June 1895, 416 hours in the year ended June 1899 and 213 hours in the year ended June 1900.[^7][^8][^9] An oil house of brick with an iron roof, 60 feet (18 m) southwest of the dwelling, was added in 1894-95.[^7] The Lighthouse Service changed the light to incandescent oil vapor in 1913.[^11] LighthouseFriends dates the electrification to May 1942, but the Park Service puts it in the late 1930s.[^3][^4] The Park Service says the steam whistle was converted to an air diaphone in 1925; LighthouseFriends says 1929.[^4][^3]

## The keepers

The USLHS Digital Archive, working from the keeper registers, lists fifteen head-keeper entries between 1874 and 1961, and well over a hundred keeper entries in all, including a first and second assistant at most periods.[^1] The first was Orator K. Hall, listed as "Orator K. Hall (1874-1875)". The USLHS Pages from the Past article says Hall opened the station with an assistant he had appointed, John Drouillard; the archive's roster lists the first assistant of those years as John Dullard. The names differ, and the sources do not say whether they are one man.[^6][^1] The article prints letters sent in the spring of 1875 and infers from them that something had soured the relationship over the winter.[^6]

Most keepers served a few years. John Leonard stayed from 1887 to 1896, and John Irvine from 1898 to 1908.[^1] Otto Olson was an assistant from 1905 and head keeper from 1908 to 1914.[^1] His daughter Elna Olson later wrote what a month between mail days meant: "Father went to Bayfield once a month for provisions, mail and newspapers." In between, she wrote, the family had no idea what was happening in the world.[^4] Walter Daniels was head keeper for eighteen years, 1917 to 1935, the longest head-keeper term in the roster.[^1]

Anna Palmgren Carlson came to the island in 1891 as the new wife of second assistant Robert Carlson. Years later she told a reporter about the isolation, as LighthouseFriends quotes her: "Oh! The loneliness of those days on Outer Island!" She remembered three people to talk to, her husband, the one-eyed head keeper, John Leonard, and a fisherman living in a shack down the shore. Their daughter was born in October 1892, and the family moved to Michigan Island the next year.[^3] The roster shows Robert Carlson as second assistant in 1891-92 and first assistant in 1892-93.[^1]

The nomination describes what it calls perhaps the most gruesome, yet telling, example of isolation. In the 1930s the keeper of the Outer Island light died near the end of the navigation season. Nobody outside knew until the tender came to close the station; the assistant keeper, who could not signal a passing vessel, had stayed alone for twelve days.[^2] The nomination does not name the keeper.

LighthouseFriends says the Coast Guard took over responsibility for the station in 1939. The USLHS roster has one entry under its USCG heading, Mart Allwardt (1943-1945), and lists Theodore J. Schelvan as head keeper from 1948 to 1961.[^3][^1]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 2 September 1905 the schooner-barge *Pretoria*, loaded with iron ore, was under tow in a storm that LighthouseFriends calls the worst on Lake Superior in over thirty years; it disabled her steering gear and parted her tow line. LighthouseFriends notes that when she was built in 1900 she was the largest wooden vessel in the world.[^3] The Omaha *Daily Bee* and the *San Francisco Call* of 5 September reported that the *Pretoria*, of Bay City, Michigan, was lost off Outer Island and that five of her crew of ten drowned when their yawl capsized.[^14][^15] Keeper John Irvine's log, quoted by LighthouseFriends, records the afternoon: he saw a small boat leave the schooner, hurried down the beach with a white flag and a rope, and "helped to pull five men ashore." The papers credit Irvine and his assistant with the rescue and say he launched his own boat.[^14][^15] Irvine housed and fed the survivors for two days until the steamer *Venezuela* returned for them.[^3]

Lights could also be seen a very long way. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin printed a report in 1922 that on the night of 7 May both assistants at Outer Island had seen the Rock of Ages light flash, some 76 miles (122 km) away.[^12] In 1923, officers crossing Lake Superior on the tender *Amaranth* recorded Outer Island's light at 38 statute miles (61 km).[^13] The Bulletin's 1924-1929 index also lists an item headed "Outer Island light station keepers stranded by ice", citing page 225 of the series.[^16]

## After the keepers

The station was automated on 5 October 1961, when the Fresnel lens was removed and a solar-powered optic took its place.[^3] The nomination, written in 1976, says the lights were automated in the 1940s, which does not match the 1961 date above; it also records that the National Park Service began stabilization work at the Michigan, Raspberry, Sand and Outer Island sites in the summer of 1975.[^2] The Apostle Islands National Lakeshore was created in 1970, and the Park Service took over the island's lighthouse.[^3] The Outer Island Light is part of the Apostle Islands Lighthouses listing on the National Register of Historic Places, entered in 1977.[^2]

The bluff had been a threat since the first fog signal fell with it. In 2004 and 2005 the Park Service armored the foot of the cliff with a stone wall and improved the drainage; planned plantings were cut back when funds ran out.[^3]

## Today

The Outer Island Light is part of the Apostle Islands National Lakeshore, and the light is an active aid to navigation.[^1][^5] The Park Service calls it the most remote of the six Apostle Islands light stations. There are no scheduled cruises, though a cruise service runs special trips in September for the lighthouse celebration. The Park Service advises that only very experienced private boaters and sea kayakers should try the trip, given the distance and exposure, and that the exposed dock often makes landing impossible even when the lake allows an approach.[^4][^5] The island has a campsite on the southern sand spit and a 7.5-mile (12 km) trail from there to the light station.[^5]
