Brant Point Light stands at the end of a spit of sand on the west side of the entrance to Nantucket Harbor, Massachusetts.[^2][^4] The town's own page calls it the second-oldest lighthouse in the country, after Boston Light.[^1] The town voted for a light on the point in January 1746.[^3][^2] The tower there now is a wooden one of 1901. The sources disagree about how many lights have stood on the point.[^4][^6]

## The place

The National Register nomination says the light serves both as a daymark and as a night aid.[^2] Shoals lie off the point. In 1888 a sloop-yacht grounded on one, a shoal off Brant Point, while running into the harbor in a strong southwest breeze.[^5]

The town built the first light itself. Putnam quotes the town's vote as building the light "in supposition that the owners of, or others concerned in, shipping will maintain a light therein," but he adds that the town paid the expenses until 1774.[^6] From 1774, the National Register form says, "the keeper at Brant Point collected six shillings from each vessel" to help pay for its maintenance.[^2] The Commonwealth of Massachusetts ceded the light to the United States in 1795, by a grant dated 23 June, the year the town took the name Nantucket.[^2][^6]

## Building the light

The first tower burned.[^3][^4] A second, also wooden, was blown down on 9 March 1774.[^4] The Bulletin of March 1939, a history of the point printed by the Service, prints a newspaper item of 1774 which reported "a most violent gust of wind" that blew the second tower down.[^3]

The sources are thinner after that. The Bulletin says that when the federal government took over in 1789 there was no lighthouse at Brant Point, although the light "may have been displayed from some temporary erection."[^3] The Bulletin's date for that takeover differs from the 1795 cession in Putnam and the National Register form.[^3][^6][^2] The Bulletin says Congress appropriated $1,600 for a keeper's residence with a tower and lantern, and LighthouseFriends dates that appropriation to 3 March 1825.[^3][^4] In 1854 Congress appropriated $15,000 to rebuild the light. The National Register form describes the new lantern as "of the fourth order, commonly known as the Fresnel Light."[^2] LighthouseFriends gives 10 December 1856 as the date the brick tower's Fresnel lens first shone.[^4] Putnam counts seven successive lighthouses on the point, in 1746, 1758, 1774, 1786, 1825, 1856 and 1901.[^6]

In 1895 the Light-House Board recorded that the light had been "changed from a fourth-order to a lens-lantern light" on the nights of 24 and 25 June.[^7] Five years later the Board reported that "a fixed red lens-lantern light was installed at the extremity of the point, 600 feet from the fourth-order light."[^8] The present wooden tower went up at the tip of the point in 1901. The Board's report for that year gives the light station as "moved 596 feet" on 31 January.[^9] LighthouseFriends says shifting sands made the new tower necessary.[^4]

The sources disagree about how many lights the point has had. The National Register form calls the 1901 tower the tenth light, counting beacons.[^2] LighthouseFriends calls it the ninth.[^4] Putnam's list of seven successive lighthouses ends with it.[^6] The Bulletin and the U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive record both give the tower a fifth-order lens.[^3][^10]

Two wooden range lights went up in 1908. The National Register form says that when lined up, they "represented the center of the existing 300' wide dredged channel" into the harbor.[^2][^4] The fog signal came in 1919. The Commissioner's report says "the fog signal went into operation November 1, 1919," and the Bulletin lists the Brant Point and East Breakwater signals as "each a bell operated by electricity."[^11][^12]

Erosion followed. The Commissioner's estimates asked $15,000 to protect the site from erosion, with the note that "owing to the excessive tide at this point the reservation is being rapidly eroded."[^13] Riprap went down in September 1924 at a cost of about $1,500, and the Bulletin says it "has stopped the erosion at those points."[^14] New dwellings for Brant Point and Wings Neck were also planned. The Commissioner's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1921 says the project "has been indefinitely postponed."[^15][^4]

## The keepers

In 1746, LighthouseFriends reports, the town designated Ebenezer Calef, Obed Hussey and Jabez Bunker to care for the light. The USLHS roster lists the three only as contractors.[^4][^10] The roster proper begins in 1794 with a run of Coffins: Tristram Coffin from 1794 to 1796, Jonathan Coffin from 1796 to 1831, and David Coffin II from 1831 to 1849.[^10] LighthouseFriends, citing accounts of a land dispute in 1887, says that Jonathan's granddaughter Mary E. Coffin went to live at the light with her father in 1829, when her grandfather grew old and ill.[^4]

Inspectors checked the lamps. LighthouseFriends prints an 1838 critique by Lieutenant Edward W. Carpender. He found the keeper away until sundown and, calling in the afternoon, the lamps untrimmed and the reflectors so dirty that they looked as if they had not been cleaned for weeks or months.[^4] The Bulletin's history of the point says the lamps of the period were a problem: "the lamps then available left much to be desired."[^3]

After the Coffins came a run of keepers on short terms: Alexander D. Bunker in 1849, Joseph Allen to at least 1851, James F. Chase from at least 1853 to 1861, then Thomas A. Folger, William H. Swain, William S. Chase, Benjamin C. Sayer, Francis B. Smith, Joseph G. Remsen and Andrew J. Sandsbury, each for a few years.[^10] John M. Chapman kept the light from 1898 to 1907. The roster then lists Mary A. Chapman as head keeper in 1907, followed that year by Marcus E. Howes. LighthouseFriends says Keeper Howes and his family lived in the keeper's dwelling that year, according to a 1907 newspaper article it cites.[^10][^4] The roster gives no reason for the changes that year.

Richard F. Dixon kept the light from 1911 to 1926. In February 1926 the Bulletin reports that he "rendered assistance to two men" whose motor boat "was damaged by ice."[^16][^10] Gerald M. Reed followed him. LighthouseFriends prints a 1928 account by Reed of his duties. Two men were stationed at the light, and by his reckoning about 60 per cent of his time went to the lights, 30 per cent to the fog signal, and 10 per cent to going for mail and supplies. The clockwork bell, he said, needed its weight raised to the top of the tower every five hours while it ran.[^4] Reed retired for disability in 1947.[^4][^10]

The assistants came and went. Frank W. Craig was assistant from 1926 to 1940, and Richard A. Fricke from 1941 to 1944.[^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The earliest storm in the record is the 1774 gust that blew down the second light. The Life-Saving Service's report for 1888 describes a yacht grounding near the point. The sloop-yacht *Onicard*, of New York, was running into Nantucket Harbor in a strong southwest breeze when she grounded on a shoal off Brant Point, about six miles from the Coskata station. The station's crew had been disbanded for the summer, but Surfman Howard, who was nearby, "went on board and offered his services." Under his direction the yacht was floated and taken to a snug berth. She had a crew of four men and one passenger, and she sustained no injury.[^5]

The Bulletin records Dixon's aid to two men in a motorboat damaged by ice in February 1926, and LighthouseFriends says the boat sank.[^16][^4] In 1928 Reed put out a fire in a neighbor's car, which had caught fire when a tire blew, using the station's extinguisher, according to LighthouseFriends.[^4]

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the point's light changed from white to red in 1933, so that it would not be mistaken for the growing number of residential lights near the harbor.[^4] A lifesaving station on the point came from Coskata. Its barracks had been built at Coskata Lifeboat Station, and the station was moved to Brant Point from Coskata about 1950.[^2]

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive record gives 1965 as the year the light was automated, and LighthouseFriends agrees.[^10][^4] In 1975 Brant Point was nominated as a National Landmark as part of the Nantucket National Historic Landmark district. The National Register form gives the tower "13,000 candlepower" in 1981.[^2] The station is part of a Massachusetts lighthouse group listed on the National Register.[^2] The tower was renovated again in 2000, when the Coast Guard and a contractor spent six weeks removing lead paint, replacing the lantern-room glass, reshingling the tower and repainting it.[^4]

## Today

The point is Coast Guard property. The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive record names the U.S. Coast Guard as owner and lists the station as not open to the public.[^10] The 1856 tower is now a Coast Guard office, and the town's page says that building "is not open to the public."[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds are open and the tower closed.[^4] Visitors should not try to climb the tower or go into the Coast Guard buildings. The fourth-order lens from the station is on display at the Nantucket Life-Saving Museum, not on the point.[^4]
