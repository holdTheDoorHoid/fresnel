A round cast-iron tower stands on a stone-filled timber crib at the end of a breakwater in Harbor Beach, Michigan, on the Lake Huron shore of Huron County. It was built in 1885 to mark an entrance to what Lighthouse Friends describes as the only safe refuge on that stretch of coast.[^1]

## The place

In 1872 a board of Army engineers chose Sand Beach, as the town was then called, as the site of an artificial harbour for ships caught in a storm on the southern part of Lake Huron. Work on a breakwater of stone-filled timber cribs began in 1873. Lighthouse Friends gives its cost on completion in 1885 as $975,000 and says it sheltered about 650 acres, the only safe refuge between Tawas Bay and the St. Clair River, 115 miles apart.[^1] The town changed its name to Harbor Beach in 1899, and the light took the new name with it.[^1]

## Building the light

The first light here was a pier light. Lighthouse Friends reports that it was lit on 25 October 1875 at the angle of the unfinished breakwater: a square open-frame tower with an octagonal lantern, showing a fixed white light from a fourth-order lens.[^1] The 1875 date belongs to that pier light and 1885 to the present tower.[^1]

The present tower was built in two seasons. The timber crib for the main light, in 28 feet (8.5 m) of water on the north side of the east entrance, was begun on 19 May 1884 and finished on 25 June. The superstructure waited until 1885: a concrete pier on the crib, then a circular cast-iron tower 44 feet 4 inches (13.5 m) high, with brick lining in its lower stories.[^1] The main light was first shown on 1 October 1885, and the steam fog signal a week later.[^1] The old frame tower was moved to the east side of the northern entrance and given a fifth-order lens.[^1] A gale on 4 and 5 December 1885 broke in the doors of the fog-signal house and washed away the bridge between the lighthouse pier and the breakwater; repairs were finished in June 1886.[^1]

## The keepers

At first nobody lived at the light. Thomas M. Wallace was appointed head keeper in January 1876, with Willis P. Graves as assistant; no dwelling was provided, so the keepers had to find lodgings ashore and go out to the light by boat.[^1][^2]

Loren J. Trescott took over in 1878 and held the post until 1 January 1919, retiring at 74 according to Lighthouse Friends. He had come to Sand Beach with his parents at twelve, sailed on the lakes, and served in the Life-Saving Service on Lake Superior before returning.[^1] His brother Alva J. Trescott was assistant from 1885 until 1910, when, Lighthouse Friends reports, a fall from his barn roof led to his death a few weeks later.[^1][^2] Otto H. Both followed Loren Trescott as head keeper in 1919 and stayed until 1940.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The fog signal's running hours show how foggy a season was. Lighthouse Friends says the whistle typically ran about 175 hours a year but ran 636 hours in 1886.[^1] The Lighthouse Board reported the 10-inch steam whistle at Sand Beach running some 313 hours and burning about 27 tons of coal in its report for the fiscal year ended June 1895, and some 87 hours and 9 tons in the report for fiscal 1900.[^3][^4]

The keepers also pulled people from the water. The Lighthouse Service Bulletin's index for 1924 to 1929 lists a rescue at Harbor Beach by Harold C. Fraser, there called keeper;[^5] Lighthouse Friends dates it to May 1927 and says he saved three people from drowning,[^1] when the archive's roster has him as second assistant.[^2] The Bulletin reported that on 28 August 1935 keeper Otto Both and assistant Lawrence E. Lane rescued three men from drowning when their sailboat capsized near the station.[^6]

## After the keepers

The tower was painted from brown to white; Lighthouse Friends dates the change to 12 April 1900, and the Lighthouse Board's report for fiscal 1900 records it.[^1][^4] Commercial power reached the station in 1934, along with a radiobeacon. The last Coast Guard crew left after the 1967 season, and the fog-signal building was removed soon afterwards. The fourth-order Fresnel lens came out in 1986 and went to the Grice House Museum in town.[^1] The Coast Guard declared the light excess, and the City of Harbor Beach received it in a ceremony in 2010, after a dispute over a state bottomlands lease; the Harbor Beach Lighthouse Preservation Society, caretakers since the early 1980s, continued to manage it. A replica fourth-order lens was installed in 2015.[^1]

## Today

The lighthouse is owned by the City of Harbor Beach.[^1] It stands offshore and can be reached only by the preservation society's boat tours, which the city describes as running on summer Saturdays and including the Grice House Museum.[^7][^1]
