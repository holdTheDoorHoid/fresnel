Mission Point Light stands at the tip of Old Mission Peninsula, at the north end of Grand Traverse Bay in Michigan.[^1][^2] Its keeper's dwelling and short tower went up in 1870 to mark shoals off the peninsula.[^1] The lighthouse was staffed until 1933 and replaced by an offshore light in 1938.[^2] The old lighthouse is township property by LighthouseFriends' account, though another source disagrees (see Today), and visitors can stay in the keepers' quarters.[^2][^4]

## The place

Shoals reach about two miles out from the northern tip of the peninsula.[^1] Terry Pepper's history says mariners heading south into Grand Traverse Bay "had to exercise great caution" around them.[^1]

The peninsula became known for a mission. Under the Treaty of 1836, the federal government agreed to provide the natives "with both a mission and schools."[^1] The Reverend Peter Dougherty, sent by the Presbyterian Board of Missions, arrived in the harbor in 1838 to set up the mission the peninsula would come to be known for.[^1]

Congress appropriated $6,000 for a light here on 3 March 1859.[^1][^2] According to LighthouseFriends, the land was still not bought a decade later, partly because of the Civil War.[^2] In 1863 a Lighthouse Board committee visited potential sites, and Colonel James D. Graham of the Army Corps of Engineers, who evaluated the Mission Point site, reported that "there is an extensive trade in the two arms of this bay."[^1][^2] In 1868 the Board said the need for the light had been "never having been at all urgent."[^2]

## Building the light

LighthouseFriends says the land was purchased in 1869.[^2] Work began at the start of the 1870 navigation season.[^1] The station was a one-and-a-half-story timber-framed dwelling with a short wooden tower built into its lakeward gable end.[^1] The tower stood 36 feet (11 m) from its foundation to the top of its ventilator ball and carried a fixed white fifth-order Fresnel lens.[^1] LighthouseFriends puts the center of the lantern 35 feet (11 m) above the ground.[^2]

Jerome M. Pratt, who had served as first assistant at Skillagalee Island since 1861, was the first keeper.[^1] The light was exhibited for the first time on the night of 10 September 1870.[^1][^2] The sources give his name two ways. The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster prints Jerome M. Pratt, Jr. Terry Pepper prints Jerome M. Pratt Sr.[^3][^1]

Pratt kept a journal. LighthouseFriends says he recorded the weather, the wind direction and the number of ships passing the point, every day.[^2] In October 1870 he noted sixty-nine sailing ships and fifty-one steamers passing, and five years later 101 steamers and seventy-five sailing craft.[^2] He wrote in the journal only once outside official business, on 8 October 1876, when a margin note reads "Eddie Died: 7:00 p.m." LighthouseFriends identifies Eddie as the adopted infant son of Jerome and Araminta Pratt.[^2]

Life at the station appears to have been fairly uneventful, Terry Pepper says, with minor repairs almost every year until 1880.[^1] That year a crew dug a cellar under the dwelling and repainted the station inside and out.[^1] Erosion of the sand bank led to a timber revetment 200 feet (61 m) long in 1889.[^1] Materials for an oil storage building were ordered from the lighthouse depot in 1897.[^1]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster has seven keepers from 1870 to 1933.[^3] Terry Pepper dates Pratt's removal to 30 January 1877 and says he was replaced by John M. McHaney.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the removal was reportedly for political reasons, and names the replacement as John M. McHarry, then 71.[^2][^3] The spelling of the name differs between the sources.

The two accounts disagree about the second keeper's death. LighthouseFriends says McHarry drowned after going over the side of the steamer City of Traverse on 1 August 1881.[^2] Terry Pepper says McHaney died on 15 August 1881 and gives no cause.[^1]

John W. Lane came to Mission Point from the St. Clair Flats South Canal Range Lights, where he had been first assistant, and was first made acting keeper.[^1] Terry Pepper says he was given full keeper status on 19 June 1883.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends says Lane had served on a whaling ship and as captain of a Great Lakes steamer before he joined the service.[^2]

Lane died on 12 December 1906, and his wife Sarah was appointed acting keeper in his place.[^1][^2] Terry Pepper says she resigned the following March. The roster prints her as Sarah E. Lane, for 1906 to 1907.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends says she ran the light for at least eight years before her husband's death, because of his failing health, and calls her the only woman to keep the light.[^2]

Terry Pepper describes James Davenport as sixty years old when he was appointed on 13 March 1907.[^1][^3] Pepper says Davenport had entered the lighthouse service in 1871, had served at Waugoshance and Little Sable, and had been keeper at McGulpin's Point Light from 1879 until that station closed in 1906, when he transferred here.[^1] William Franklin Green came from South Fox Island in 1919.[^1][^3] Emil C. Johnson, who had been first assistant at Grand Traverse Light since 1916, was promoted to the station and kept it from 1924.[^1][^3]

## After the keepers

In 1911 a bell buoy was placed off Mission Point to mark the northern end of the shoals.[^2] In March 1931 all but 5.38 acres of the 142-acre reservation went to the State of Michigan for a public park. The remaining piece, which included the lighthouse, was declared surplus on 24 September 1938.[^2]

Emil C. Johnson, the last keeper, left on 30 June 1933, and an automated acetylene light was installed in the lantern room.[^2] Vandals removed the lens between 1933 and 1948.[^2]

In 1938 a new light on a pier replaced the bell buoy.[^2] Work began that year on the pier, in 19 feet (6 m) of water about 1 7/8 miles northwest of Mission Point.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends says the new light took over the old light's function, which was then deactivated.[^2]

Residents of Peninsula Township raised the money and bought the lighthouse and the remaining land for $1,001 in 1948.[^2] Caretakers lived there until 2008, when the interior was opened to the public.[^2] As part of the 150th-anniversary work in 2020, fifteen sets of green shutters were installed.[^2]

## Today

LighthouseFriends says the township owns the grounds.[^2] Terry Pepper's older page says instead that the State of Michigan bought the old property after the Second World War, and that the dwelling and grounds were then closed to the public.[^1] The two accounts differ on who owns the property and when it changed hands. This story follows LighthouseFriends for the 1931 and 1948 dates. Check with the township or the operator before a visit.[^1][^2]

The U.S. Lighthouse Society lists the lighthouse as a keeper stay.[^4] The Keepers' Quarters has one bedroom. In season, keepers work the retail shop from 10 to 5 and must be able to "climb the 37 steps to the tower."[^4] LighthouseFriends says visitors can now stay at the lighthouse for a small fee.[^2]
