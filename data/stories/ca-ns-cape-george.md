Cape George light stands on Cape George Point, at the northwest entrance to St. George's Bay in Antigonish County, on the Northumberland Strait.[^2][^4] A light has been shown from the headland since 1861, when a lantern was set on a keeper's dwelling.[^2][^4] The octagonal concrete tower standing now dates from 1968 and is the third light on the site.[^2][^4]

## The place

Cape George is a lofty headland, nearly 183 metres high, that defines the northwest entrance to St. George's Bay. The bay lies between the Nova Scotia mainland and Cape Breton Island.[^2] Parks Canada says a light was first established there in 1861 to aid mariners navigating the Northumberland Strait and entering St. George's Bay.[^1]

A light on the cape was petitioned for in 1840, more than twenty years before the first one was lit. Lighthouse Friends says the House of Assembly's lighthouse committee recommended in that year that a petition from Robert McDonald be granted, but only after a survey of the coast. The survey led to a light at North Canso, on the Strait of Canso, instead of Cape George. Petitions from Pictou and Arichat followed in 1860.[^2]

## Building the light

The first station was a dwelling topped by an octagonal iron lantern room.[^2] The Nova Scotia Lighthouse Preservation Society's list describes the first light instead as a square white wooden tower.[^4] The Sessional Papers of 1875, as the provincial site quotes them, describe the lantern as ten feet across, with eight sides.[^3] David Condon was appointed keeper in 1861.[^3] The light was shown from a revolving frame with two triangular faces, each carrying three lamps and twelve-inch reflectors.[^2][^3]

In 1866 the province's superintendent of lighthouses, Thomas P. Jose, proposed a fixed light, because the tower shook so much in gales that the keeper found it hard to turn the machinery. Supports were added instead, "for security against the storms which blow over the Cape in fearful gusts." In 1885 wire-rope stays were fitted to the lantern against its vibration in storms.[^2]

The sources disagree about what happened to the first light. The provincial site's timeline gives 1907 as the year the lighthouse was "destroyed by fire."[^3] Lighthouse Friends does not mention a fire.[^2] The Department's report for the year 1906-07 says a third order double flashing light had been received for a "Cape George" and was being erected.[^7] The report does not give a county. The same reports list a separate Cape George on the Great Bras d'Or Lake, so that entry may refer to that station instead.[^9] The Department's entry does not describe a fire.

A new wooden tower and keeper's dwelling were erected in 1908.[^5] The tower was octagonal, with sloping sides and an iron lantern, and stood 16.6 m (54 ft 6 in) to the top of the lantern. The NSLPS list gives its height as 44 ft.[^4] E. F. Munro of Westville built the tower and dwelling for $3,097.[^2][^3] By the Department's report for 1908-09, the catoptric revolving white light had been replaced by a third-order dioptric triple flashing light.[^6] Electrical power reached the station in 1954.[^2]

## The keepers

David Condon wrote a letter to Sir John Thompson, the MP for Antigonish, in 1881, asking for an extension of his years served for superannuation purposes. In it he described the gales: "Sometimes in heavy gales the machinery will not work owing to the shaking of the building, and I am then obliged to sit on the metal floor of the Lantern and turn the machinery by hand."[^2]

The two sources differ on the succession. Lighthouse Friends has Condon retiring in 1882, his son Joseph taking over, Hugh McDonald following in 1889 and Alex McEachern in 1898.[^2] The provincial site gives David Condon's service as 1861 to 1898, and says the Condon family ran the light for 29 years, with W. J. Condon among them.[^3] This story gives both accounts.[^2][^3]

Alex MacEachern was appointed on 8 September 1898 at a salary of $450 a year.[^3] A Department keepers' table of 1912 lists "Cape George Alex. McEachern."[^8] Lighthouse Friends has him serving until 1919.[^2]

Charles Albert Falkenham followed him. Lighthouse Friends gives his service as 1919 to 1951, and the provincial site as 1919 to 1952.[^2][^3] William W. Clark took over in the early 1950s and retired in 1968, when the light was automated.[^2][^3]

## After the keepers

The concrete tower was contracted in October 1967 and placed in operation on 6 June 1968.[^2] The Parks Canada statement places it within the Department of Transport's mid-twentieth-century programme to automate and de-staff lighthouses, and calls it one of many automated lighthouses erected within that project.[^1] The NSLPS gives the date of automation as by 1993, where Lighthouse Friends ties it to 1968.[^4][^2]

The old wooden tower and dwelling were bulldozed on 7 September 1978. The lantern and the lens had been removed and taken away earlier.[^2] The lantern and a third-order lens later went on display at the Gateway Centre at the Prince Edward Island end of the Confederation Bridge, and were removed some time after 2008.[^2]

Lighthouse Friends says the light was transferred to the local community in 2016, but its visitor note still says the Canadian Coast Guard owns the light.[^2] The Parks Canada statement notes that the provincial government acquired the land around the light to keep it open to the public.[^1] The NSLPS names the Antigonish North Shore Development Association as owner.[^4]

## Today

To reach the light, turn onto Lighthouse Road, a 0.8 km (half-mile) road that ends at the lighthouse.[^2] Lighthouse Friends says the grounds are open.[^2] The Parks Canada statement describes the light as one of the trailheads for the Cape George Trail.[^1]

The tower is 14 m (45 ft) high and stands on a high cliff above the strait. Its light is a group of white flashes every 12 seconds.[^4] The Parks Canada statement says the lighthouse sits on a wooded bluff high above St. George's Bay, and that it receives large numbers of visitors every year.[^1]
