The Bois Blanc Island Lighthouse is a limestone tower on the southern tip of Bois Blanc Island, known locally as Boblo Island, at the place where Lake Erie narrows into the Detroit River off Amherstburg, Ontario. It was lit in the autumn of 1836 and kept by members of one family, the Hacketts, until 1924.[^1][^2][^10]

## The place

Bois Blanc is French for "white woods", a name given for the white-barked birch and beech trees that grew on the island.[^1] In April 1835 two commissioners were appointed to oversee the building of a lighthouse at the entrance to the Detroit River. They asked several experienced captains, British and American, where it should go, and the captains were unanimous that it belonged on the island's southern end rather than on Bar Point, a hazardous shoal reaching into Lake Erie from the Canadian shore.[^1] The island site also allowed a shorter tower, a cheaper foundation, and ground the government already owned.[^1]

## Building the light

The Parliament of Upper Canada had provided 800 pounds, but the lowest tender for the tower and a keeper's cottage was 275 pounds higher; a further 300 pounds was voted in April 1836, and John Cook, the lowest bidder, did the work that year.[^1] The tower is built of limestone, a rarity at the time, since stone cost far more than wood.[^2] It stood 13.7 m (45 ft) from its base to the centre of the lantern room, with a diameter of 5.5 m (18 ft), tapered sides, three windows and a cornice of stepped corbelling.[^1] Parks Canada's heritage statement calls it a good example of an "Imperial tower", a common lighthouse type of the 1830 to 1860 period.[^3]

Merchants and ship-masters pressed for the light to be put into service as soon as the tower was finished. James Hackett volunteered to tend it before any official appointment, and the commissioners supplied oil so that it burned from 7 November 1836 until navigation closed in mid-December.[^1] In 1861 it used six lamps and reflectors to show a fixed white light; coal oil replaced whale oil in 1864.[^1] A fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the lamps and reflectors in 1908.[^1]

## The keepers

James Hackett had started his family in Scotland before emigrating to Upper Canada, where he served nearly twenty years as a captain on the Great Lakes before taking charge of the light.[^1] Two stories survive about how he got the post. In one, his wife Mary visited Lieutenant-Governor Francis Bond Head with twin babies in her arms and won it by petition; in the other, she offered to trade the family's Newfoundland dog, Sailor, for the job. Lighthouse Friends says neither may be true, but the couple did name a son born at the lighthouse in 1842 Francis Bond Head Hackett.[^1]

The family held the light for three generations. Lighthouse Friends lists James (1836 to 1870), his son Andrew (1870 to 1901), Andrew's widow Agnes (1901 to 1910) and her son Charles R. Hackett (1910 to 1924).[^1] Sources disagree on some of the handovers. A local history gives James's death as 24 September 1872 and says he served until his death, yet in another passage says Andrew succeeded him in 1870; it also says Andrew died in 1901 and was succeeded by his son Charles, leaving Agnes out.[^4] The Department of Marine's keeper lists name "Mrs. A. Hackett" at Bois Blanc in its reports for 1902 and 1905, and "C. R. Hackett" in 1914, which supports the Lighthouse Friends sequence.[^7][^8][^9] Agnes was appointed on condition that she keep a competent male assistant.[^1]

The keeper had work beyond the lamp. The Department of Marine reported in 1900 that the Bois Blanc keeper maintained all of its Detroit River buoys, a system it said, while perhaps costing somewhat more than contract work, was prompt at replacing losses.[^6]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 8 January 1838, during the Upper Canada Rebellion, about sixty Canadian "Patriots" and American sympathisers landed from the schooner *Anne* and forced the small military guard, and the Hacketts, to leave the island. The invasion ended the next day when the rebels' vessel ran aground near Amherstburg and all aboard were captured.[^1][^2] A wooden blockhouse was built on the island in 1839, after the rebels' invasion.[^1]

In October 1913 Grace Hackett, a daughter of Agnes, was living at the lighthouse when a glass jar she was warming in boiling water burst and cut both her eyes; she was taken to Grace Hospital in Detroit.[^1]

## After the keepers

A pair of range lights was placed on the island's northeast point in October 1875, and the wooden front tower burned in 1891; iron skeleton towers replaced it, and the Department of Marine's report recorded the range lights discontinued and their towers taken down, in 1907 according to Lighthouse Friends.[^1][^5] In 1924 the Department of Marine converted Bois Blanc to an unwatched light using the Aga gas system, ending resident keeping.[^10] The light was discontinued in 1959 and the tower passed to Parks Canada in 1961.[^1][^3] In the 1970s Parks Canada restored the entrance door and its fanlight; after vandals damaged them, the doorway was sealed with rubble stone.[^1]

## Today

The tower and the nearby blockhouse were designated a National Historic Site of Canada in 1955.[^1] The lighthouse is owned by Parks Canada.[^1] Parks Canada says it is not open to the general public, that public access to the site is limited, and that it can be seen from the Amherstburg shore of the Detroit River.[^11] Lighthouse Friends describes Boblo Island as home to a planned residential community reached by ferry, and lists the grounds as open and the tower closed; Parks Canada's wording on access is more restrictive.[^1][^11] The tower should be viewed from outside.
