McInnes Island is a small rocky island off the southern end of Price Island on British Columbia's Central Coast, and its light marks the northern side of the way into Milbanke Sound. A light first shone here in 1921 as an unattended lamp on a concrete base; the present lighthouse, built for the traffic bound for the new aluminum smelter at Kitimat, has had keepers since the winter of 1953.[^1][^2]

## The place

Most vessels heading north along the British Columbia coast take the sheltered Inside Passage, but the route has a few gaps where it is open to Pacific swell. Milbanke Sound is one of them, with Price Island to the north and the Bardswell Group of islands to the south, and McInnes Island sits on its northern entrance.[^1] The Canadian Coast Guard's List of Lights places the light "on bluff, S. side of island, N. entrance to Milbanke Sound."[^3] The island is named for Thomas Robert McInnes, a physician who sat in the House of Commons and the Senate and was Lieutenant Governor of British Columbia from 1897 to 1900.[^1]

## Building the light

The first light was established in 1921. It was an unwatched light, described as a concrete base carrying a staff topped by a white slat-work ball and a red lantern.[^1] The register entry for the station calls it "first established as an automated light in 1921," and the 1921 engine building survives among the station's heritage buildings.[^2]

The second, larger station came with the aluminum industry. Alcan, building up the Kitimat smelter site about 200 kilometres north of the island, put up a reservoir, a hydroelectric generating station, a deepwater port and a townsite between 1951 and 1954, and the Department of Transport expected more ships to pass through Milbanke Sound as a result. It built a major light station on McInnes Island in 1953 and 1954: three dwellings, a lighthouse, a fog alarm building, a boathouse, a landing platform and an aerial cable for hauling supplies.[^1]

Ken Stewart, whose father co-founded the contractor Stewart & Slade Construction, worked on the job as a labourer and returned in 2011 to see it. According to Lighthouse Friends' account of his story, the crew excavated the cellars and the tower foundation by hand, worked six nine-hour days a week, and had the houses and lighthouse framed in by 15 December 1953, when the men broke for the winter. By then the first keeper, Larry Reid, had arrived. A smaller crew came back in March and spent about six weeks finishing.[^1] (That page gives the crew's arrival as "August 1852," which is evidently a slip for 1953; the work is dated 1953-54 elsewhere.[^1][^2])

The lighthouse is a white rectangular concrete building, 8.5 metres (28 ft) high, with the light tower built into one corner. It originally showed a group of two white flashes every 24 seconds, and a pitched roof was added to the lighthouse later. The fog alarm was a diaphone giving three blasts a minute, synchronised with a radiobeacon so that a navigator with a radio could work out his distance from the island by timing the gap between the radio signal and the blast.[^1][^2] The Parks Canada register gives the date as 1954 and says it is the second lighthouse at this location.[^2] Today the light flashes white every 5 seconds from a focal height of 29.5 m.[^3]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists the head keepers as Larry L. Reid from 1953, Dave McLeod in 1955, Bruce W. Rogers 1960 to 1964, Edward Carson 1965 to 1966, William F. McIlroy 1966 to 1971, Maurice Collette 1971 to 1977, John Coldwell 1977 to 2001, Robert Desmanche 2002 to 2005, and Colin Toner from 2006.[^1]

John Coldwell, who arrived as head keeper in August 1977, stayed for more than two decades. One morning in spring 1978 he woke to find the sea around the island milky white instead of blue, radioed the Coast Guard at Bull Harbour to report pollution, and then learned, to his embarrassment, that it was a massive herring spawn.[^1] A blog retelling, with no date given, tells of him heading his boat towards a bait ball of herring while fishing, when the other keeper radioed that orcas were heading his way; he hurried to a kelp bed at the water's edge, sea lions scurried toward him, and the whales went for the herring instead.[^4]

## Today

The station is still staffed. The Parks Canada register describes the light as providing weather reports for marine and air traffic and taking part in search and rescue.[^2] The Coast Guard's 2024 announcement that keeper functions would end at Carmanah Point and Pachena Point said that twenty-five other staffed light stations on the British Columbia coast would carry on as before; Lighthouse Friends lists Mark Quiring as head keeper from 2022 to at least 2024.[^1][^5] Canada recognised the lighthouse as a heritage lighthouse on 29 May 2015, with six surrounding buildings counted as contributing to its character.[^2]

The lighthouse is owned by the Canadian Coast Guard. It is best seen from the water, about eleven miles west of the Inside Passage ferry route between Prince Rupert and Port Hardy; the grounds are listed as open and the tower is closed, but this is a staffed station best seen from the water, and visitors should not land without arrangement.[^1]
