Algoma Pierhead Light stands at the end of the north pier at the mouth of the Ahnapee River, in the city of Algoma, Kewaunee County, Wisconsin.[^1][^2] Range lights first shone at the pier in March 1893, and the present red steel tower has stood there since 1932.[^5][^2] Work on the pier that began in May 2026 has started removing the catwalk, and the sources disagree on whether the lighthouse itself is already off the pier (see Today).[^10][^2]

## The place

Algoma began as a settlement called Wolf River and later took the name Ahnapee.[^2] In early October 1871 torrential rains doused a great fire just as it neared the town.[^2] "A few years later, the city was renamed Algoma," LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The water at the mouth of the Ahnapee River was only three feet (0.9 m) deep, which limited commercial traffic on the river.[^2] A project adopted in 1875 aimed to make a small artificial harbor, using parallel piers.[^2]

The Board's reports of the early 1890s used the old name. The archive lists "Ahnapee Pierhead" as an alternate name for the station.[^1][^4][^5] The appropriations list for 1891 names "Ahnapee range or pier lights," but it gives the state as Michigan. The station is in Wisconsin, so the list is wrong on that point.[^3]

## Building the light

The city had kept its own light at Algoma for several years.[^2] In 1890 the Lighthouse Board noted this and asked for money for a pierhead tower and a post light to work as range lights. LighthouseFriends gives the request as $2,500.[^2] Congress funded the project in March 1891.[^2][^3]

The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1892 says the station "was ready for operation at the end of the fiscal year," with only the illuminating apparatus still to arrive.[^4] The range lights went into service on 1 March 1893, with a lens lantern on the rear tower and a tubular lantern on the post light.[^5][^2] LighthouseFriends gives the rear tower as 35 feet (11 m) from base to ventilator ball, with its light at a focal plane of 37 feet (11 m).[^2]

The first keeper was Ole Hansen, who came from the Chicago Harbor light.[^2][^1] The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1893 says a bridge was built to connect the detached cribs that carry the lights with the shore cribs.[^6] LighthouseFriends dates the bridge to 1892.[^2] A wooden oil house was built at the rear light in 1894.[^7] In June 1895 a fifth-order lens replaced the lens lantern on the rear light. The Board's station entry gives 5 June, and its list of changes gives 6 June.[^8][^11] The Board's report for the year ended 30 June 1897 says an elevated walk 400 feet (122 m) long was built at the rear range.[^9] LighthouseFriends says the bridge was abandoned in 1900, after more than 300 feet (91 m) of elevated walkway had been installed on the pier and the detached pier.[^2]

Congress provided money for a keeper's dwelling in 1907 and 1908. LighthouseFriends describes the house as "a red brick, six-room dwelling with green shingles on its second story and gables," built in May and June 1909.[^2]

## The keepers

The archive's roster lists fourteen head keepers, beginning with Ole Hansen (1893-1895), and runs on into the Coast Guard years.[^1]

Gustavus Umberham was head keeper from 1901 to 1913. LighthouseFriends says he was "transferred to Algoma from Michigan's Cedar River Lighthouse" with his five children after his wife had died there.[^2][^1] Eugene V. Kimball followed from 1913 to 1923, and Carl Joseph Graan took over in 1923 and stayed until 1944.[^1]

Louis Braemer, hired days after the fog signal went into service in 1910, was the first assistant. LighthouseFriends says he "never applied for a promotion" and was an assistant at Algoma for at least thirty years.[^2][^1]

LighthouseFriends says Graan held the head keeper's post longer than any other man. To placate residents annoyed by the fog signal, he devised a curved, metal-lined reflector that "reflected most of the sound seaward." LighthouseFriends says similar reflectors went to other stations.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On the night of 3 February 1913, according to LighthouseFriends, Gustavus Umberham was pitched overboard from a friend's boat, the Reliance, about six miles (10 km) from Algoma. He had left that morning with his friend for Kewaunee. A heavy swell struck the boat, pitching him into the cabin door and then overboard. After an hour of circling, the men aboard "returned to Algoma and trudged up the hill" to tell his family.[^2]

## After the keepers

On 10 April 1910 an electrically operated siren fog signal was set up in the wooden tower on the pier. It sounded a ten-second blast followed by ten seconds of silence when needed.[^2]

The steel tower came in 1932, as part of a $100,000 harbor improvement that also capped the north and south piers with concrete. Its diaphone fog signal could be run by remote control.[^2][^1] The light was automated in 1973.[^2]

In May 2014 the Coast Guard deemed the light excess and made it available to eligible groups. In early 2015 the National Park Service told the City of Algoma that it was the sole applicant.[^2] In 2019 the Coast Guard removed the historic Fresnel lens and put in an LED beacon. After restoration, the lens went on display at Milwaukee's North Point Lighthouse.[^2]

## Today

On 18 May 2026 Roen Salvage Company of Sturgeon Bay began removing the catwalk "in preparation for north pier reconstruction this year."[^10] The Kewaunee County Star-News says the lighthouse "will be taken off, repainted and returned when work is complete, possibly late next year."[^10] LighthouseFriends says instead that "the historic steel catwalk and lighthouse were removed as part of the project," and that the plan is to return the lighthouse at the end of a two-year undertaking.[^2] The two accounts disagree on whether the lighthouse is already off the pier. A citizen-led committee, Save the Algoma Catwalk, is raising funds to restore the catwalk.[^10]

The lighthouse is owned by the City of Algoma. LighthouseFriends reports that the grounds are open and the tower is closed.[^2] The tower stands on a detached breakwater, so visitors can walk the piers on either side of the river but, in LighthouseFriends's words, "you can not get right up to it."[^2]
