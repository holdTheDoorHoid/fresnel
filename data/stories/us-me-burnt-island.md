Burnt Island Light stands on a small island at the western entrance to Boothbay Harbor, in Lincoln County, Maine.[^1] The station was built in 1821, and LighthouseFriends calls its tower the second-oldest original lighthouse structure in Maine, after Portland Head.[^2][^3] Since 1998 the state has owned the station, and it now shows visitors what the keepers' work was like.[^3]

## The place

The harbor was the reason for the light.[^3] In 1837 Captain Joseph Smith of the US Navy called Boothbay Harbor "one of the most important harbors upon the whole coast," and wrote that more than three hundred sail had anchored there at one time.[^3]

The island's name came from sheep farmers, who regularly set the island afire to improve the grazing.[^3]

The hazard was the Cuckolds, rocks just off Southport Island. LighthouseFriends says that before 1888 ships often turned for Boothbay Harbor as soon as they saw the light on Burnt Island, and ended up on the Cuckolds.[^3] The 1976 National Register nomination says a "dead angle" was put into the lantern in 1888 "to prevent ships from being wrecked on the rocks called the Cuckolds."[^1]

## Building the light

On 21 May 1821 officials of Boothbay Harbor and the owners of several coasting vessels asked the customs collector for a lighthouse on the island. Within days the government bought the five-acre island for $150 from Jacob Auld and Joseph McCobb, using money from a $10,500 appropriation for three Maine lights.[^3] LighthouseFriends says the light was complete four months after the island was acquired.[^3]

The sources disagree about the masonry. LighthouseFriends describes a conical granite tower, likely quarried on the island, lined with brick and 30 feet (9.1 m) tall.[^3] The US Lighthouse Society record says rubble stone.[^2] The nomination describes fieldstone, brick and cement.[^1] The nomination also dates the dwelling to 1821.[^1] LighthouseFriends and the Society both date the present keeper's house to 1857, when a wood-framed house replaced the stone cottage.[^1][^2][^3]

The first light was a ring of ten Lewis patent reflector lamps burning whale oil.[^3] In 1857 a new lantern room took a fourth-order Fresnel lens.[^3][^2]

The light's color changed more than once. The nomination says that about 1890 it was changed from fixed white to fixed red, with two white sectors covering the fairways into the harbor. Two years later, it says, the light was made flashing red every five seconds.[^1] LighthouseFriends dates the fixed red to 1889, and dates the flashing to 1901, when a square lens with four bull's-eye panels and clockwork rotation went into the lantern.[^3] The two accounts cannot both be right.

The fog signal came later. A new bell tower was built in 1895, with a 1,000-pound bell and a striking machine.[^4] The nomination describes the machine as a Gamewell fog bell striking machine, made in 1895.[^1] The Board's 1896 report says a new bell was hung, with an automatic striker.[^5] A brick oil house was built in 1899.[^6]

## The keepers

Joshua B. Cushing was the first keeper, from 1821 to 1825.[^2] LighthouseFriends says that thirty-one keepers served at the station between 1821 and 1988.[^3]

James A. McCobb served from 1868 to 1880. LighthouseFriends says he kept detailed records of the work, the weather, passing vessels and his family, and it prints several entries from his logs. On 1 December 1873 he wrote: "Vessels are much iced up as they pass the station."[^3] In July he noted that "Much company around visiting the station," and some days visitors took up most of his time.[^3] His log records the death of his wife in March 1877.[^3] McCobb was a nephew of one of the men who sold the island to the government.[^3]

Joseph Muise and his family lived on the island from 1936 to 1951, the years when the light passed from the Lighthouse Service to the Coast Guard. The state's living-history program now plays them for visitors.[^3][^2]

Henry Sieg was the last keeper, from 1983 to 1988.[^2][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

McCobb's log for 29 November 1875 records a heavy gale from the northwest, with snow squalls. The schooner James Somes of Portland tried to work into the harbor under short sail and was capsized between the light and Tumbler Island. She sank in ten fathoms (18 m). The captain drowned, and four others were saved by boats from the windward. The keeper could not board her from the station because of the gale.[^3] No other account of this wreck is cited in this story.

## After the keepers

The square lens was removed in 1961, when the station was electrified, and the bell tower was dismantled in 1962.[^3] In 1988 the station was automated. LighthouseFriends says it was then one of just eleven staffed lighthouses in the country.[^3]

In 1998, under the Maine Lights Program, ownership passed from the Coast Guard to the Maine Department of Marine Resources. Once funding was found, the station was restored, mostly with volunteer labor.[^3] A Keepers of Burnt Island Light group was formed in 2009 to support the restoration.[^3] In 2020 the tower was restored and the 1857 keeper's house was returned to its 1950s appearance. In 2021 a bronze plaque was put on the tower for its bicentennial.[^3]

## Today

The Maine Department of Marine Resources owns the station. The US Lighthouse Society lists it as open to the public, and LighthouseFriends says the grounds, dwelling and tower are open.[^2][^3]

The island is reached by boat.[^3] LighthouseFriends lists tours that land visitors on the island, offered by the Maine Lighthouse Museum, and public cruises from Bath and Boothbay Harbor that pass the light. The light can also be seen from the shore near Boothbay Harbor.[^3] The public is invited to the Living Lighthouse in July and August, and in spring and fall students come to learn about a keeper's family.[^3]

The square lens removed in 1961 is at the Maine Maritime Museum. The fourth-order lens from the tower is on display at the Maine Lighthouse Museum in Rockland.[^3] The station is on the National Register of Historic Places as Burnt Island Light Station.[^7]
