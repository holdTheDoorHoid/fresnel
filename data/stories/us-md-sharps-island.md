Sharps Island Light stands on a caisson in Chesapeake Bay, in Talbot County, Maryland.[^1][^4] It is the third light built to mark the shoals off Poplar Island and Black Walnut Point.[^1][^4] The first was a wooden house on an island that was washing away.[^1] The second, on screw piles, was swept off its foundations by ice in February 1881.[^1][^4][^5]

## The place

The island was disappearing before the first light was built.[^1] The nomination says it "diminished from 700 acres in 1675 to 480 acres in 1838 when the first light was built."[^1] LighthouseFriends says that the island at one time comprised about 900 acres, and the Chesapeake Chapter also gives 900 acres for an earlier island, so the early acreage figures do not agree.[^4][^5] LighthouseFriends also says the name of Peter Sharp, a Quaker physician and one of the island's three seventeenth-century owners, was applied to it.[^4]

The light was needed for a reason the nomination states plainly: "The Sharps Island Light was required to guide mariners away from the shoals off Poplar Island and Black Walnut Point."[^1] The site was bought from its owners, Joseph A. Reynolds and his wife Ann, for $600 in August 1837.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $5,000 in 1837 to build "an integrated and portable station," according to LighthouseFriends.[^4] The first structure was a small wooden house with a lamp on top, 30 feet (9 m) above the ground on the northwest tip of the island.[^1] The nomination says the structure built by Thomas Evans "was designed so it could be easily moved."[^1]

The nomination says the erosion did threaten the light, and "plans were made to relocate the light."[^1] In 1848 the government acquired 10.2 acres by condemnation, and the light was moved farther inland.[^1][^4] The nomination quotes an 1864 report by the Lighthouse Board to Congress: "the lighthouse at Sharps Island in Chesapeake Bay is in imminent danger of being destroyed by the washing away of the bank on which it stands."[^1]

The second light was a screw-pile structure, and the sources place it differently.[^1][^4] The nomination says it was erected "approximately one (1) mile east of the previous position."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the Board built it at a site "four miles off Tilghman Island."[^4] The nomination says that "In 1866 a second structure was built but it too was ill-fated."[^1] LighthouseFriends says Congress appropriated $15,000 on 7 April 1866 and that the screw-pile light was completed that year.[^4] The Chesapeake Chapter says it was "completed late in 1865."[^5] The two accounts differ on the year, and this story does not choose between them. LighthouseFriends also reports that in 1879 drift ice carried away two of its cast-iron braces, and that a detached ice-breaker of three screw piles and riprap was placed about 200 feet (61 m) south of it.[^4]

The present tower followed the 1881 loss.[^4][^5] LighthouseFriends says Congress provided $35,000, less than a month after the loss, for a cast-iron, concrete-filled caisson.[^4] The Chesapeake Chapter calls it "a cast iron caisson filled with concrete" and says Congress "approved $35,000 for the new current caisson light in 1881," and that it was erected during that summer and fall.[^5] The nomination describes the caisson as steel: "a 30' diameter steel caisson."[^1] Both sources say the light was first shown on 1 February 1882.[^4][^5]

Their construction dates do not fit together.[^4][^5] LighthouseFriends says the tender Tulip "arrived at the site for the third Sharps Island Lighthouse on August 28, 1882," but it also says the lens was "displayed for the first time on February 1, 1882," almost seven months earlier.[^4] The Chesapeake Chapter places the building in 1881–82.[^5] The sources do not settle the months.

The tower's height is also given two ways.[^1] The nomination calls it "The 54 foot high brown tower."[^1] The Chesapeake Chapter says the tower is 37 feet (11 m) tall and that this puts the light 54 feet (16 m) above mean water, and LighthouseFriends also describes a thirty-seven-foot (11 m) iron tower.[^4][^5]

A fog-bell room is dated two ways as well. LighthouseFriends says a small room was built on the lower gallery in 1890 to house the bell's striking apparatus.[^4] The Board's report for 1891–92 describes "a room, about 5 feet 6 inches by 8 feet in plan," built on the gallery "in November" to give the fog-bell machinery shelter from spray, but the passage gives no year.[^7]

One carpenter who worked on the district's lights, this one among them, left a record.[^14] A Lighthouse Service obituary of 1926 says that William H. Davis "entered the Lighthouse Service in 1881" as a carpenter in the field working party of the fifth district, and that he "assisted in the construction of" a list of lights that includes Sharps Island.[^14]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster gives 59 keeper stints for the station between 1838 and 1951: 22 as head keeper and 37 as first assistant.[^2] The first name on the roster is Isaac Sanner, for 1838 only. The roster lists both Benjamin Horney (1838–1841) and Benjamin Harney (1838 to at least 1839) for the same early years, and it does not say whether they are one man.[^2]

Jeremiah Valiant is listed as head keeper from 1841 to 1843 and again from 1849 to 1851.[^2] The roster also lists Harriet Valiant as head keeper from 1851 to 1856, and LighthouseFriends names her in the same post.[^2][^4] The sources read do not say how she came to the post or whether she and Jeremiah Valiant were related.

James Sinclair is listed as head keeper from 1856 to 1866, the years in which the Board's warnings about erosion and the 1866 structure fall.[^1][^2]

Elisha T. Shockley is listed as head keeper from 1897 to 1904, and Jesse M. Shockley from 1904 to 1937.[^2] LighthouseFriends calls Jesse's tenure the longest of any head keeper here, and says he replaced his brother as keeper in 1904.[^4] The Commissioner of Lighthouses prints his name as "Jesse M. W. Shockley."[^10] The roster lists Thomas L. Wallace as head keeper from 1937 to 1951.[^2]

Shockley's work at sea shows up in the Service's own reports. The Commissioner's annual report for fiscal 1921 says that he "towed the disabled motor boat Edna" to Black Walnut Harbor.[^10] The Service's bulletin dates that assistance to 4 April 1921 and says the boat was in distress near the station.[^12] The Commissioner's report for fiscal 1922 says that he "towed to Cooks Point the hydroplane Lady Baltimore, with two occupants."[^11] The bulletin dates that tow to 31 July 1921.[^13] LighthouseFriends prints Shockley's own account of the Lady Baltimore rescue, and describes the craft as a "hydroairplane" of the Eastern Air Line.[^4] In 1928 the Service's bulletin reported that he helped a motor boat with four men aboard, which had broken down near the station, on 27 June.[^15]

The roster and the sources do not agree on every name. LighthouseFriends lists "Columbus Butler" as head keeper from 1880 to 1890, while the roster lists "Christopher C. Butler" for 1880 to 1881 and again for 1882 to 1890.[^2][^4]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 10 February 1881, during a winter thaw of the bay, moving ice swept the screw-pile house away.[^5] The nomination says the house was lifted from its foundation and "carried away by the heavy moving ice floes."[^1] The Chesapeake Chapter says the ice carried the building five miles down the bay with the keepers still inside.[^5]

Two keepers were in the house.[^3][^4] LighthouseFriends says they stayed aboard for sixteen and a half hours, until the house grounded on an island five miles away.[^4] The Board's report says the house grounded full of water at high tide, and that the keepers got ashore once they were satisfied it would not float off again.[^3] When the tide had fallen they went back and took the lens, oil and library ashore; the library had been much damaged by water.[^3][^4]

The Board's report for the fiscal year ended 30 June 1893 recalled the wreck as an example of heroism. It says the house was "thrown over on its side, and carried away by ice." The keepers "drifted in the bay" without fire or food, and the floating ice "piled up against and threatened" the house.[^3] The same passage says the keepers reported the facts "through their inspector to the Board," and that they were "highly complimented by a letter direct from the Board itself."[^3] In the same passage the Board records that the keepers of another light had deserted their post when the ice threatened, and that they were dismissed.[^3]

Newspapers reported the loss too. A Snow Hill paper printed a short item on 12 March 1881, under a heading of news "condensed from" other papers. It said the light had been "dragged by Ice from its foundations" and had drifted ashore at Paw-Paw.[^6] LighthouseFriends says the house grounded on an island, and so does the Board, but the newspaper places it at Paw-Paw, so the sources disagree about where the house came to rest.[^3][^4][^6]

Ice also tilted the tower after it was built.[^5] In the winter of 1976–77 large ice flows "pushed against the tower and tipped it to the south at about a 15 degree angle."[^5] The nomination says the light "started to lean as early as 1973" and that heavy ice in the winter of 1976 leaned it further.[^1]

## After the keepers

The Board's reports from 1891 to 1901 record small works on the station.[^7][^8][^9] In 1899 a new smoke pipe was put up and new model fourth-order lamps were installed.[^8] In January 1901 a scaffold was erected on the outside of the caisson and holes were drilled in its ironwork.[^9]

The island kept shrinking. LighthouseFriends says that Miller R. Creighton, a Baltimore boot and shoe manufacturer, bought the island in 1895 with two partners and began a hotel that overlooked a boardwalk and a steamboat landing.[^4] By 1900 the steamboat pier was gone, and the hotel, like the island's old lighthouse, became "a victim of the island's eroding shore."[^4] A Coast Guard Survey Board visited on 3 August 1955, and the nomination says its report stated that the island "had diminished to about 300 yards."[^1] The nomination also says the island "has completely disappeared."[^1]

The light was automated in August 1951, and the last resident keepers left that year.[^1][^4] On 1 October 1957 the light was converted from battery to electricity.[^1] Maryland took back the acreage the federal government had acquired, by a deed dated 8 October 1957.[^1]

The fourth-order lens was removed on 3 May 1977 and replaced with a 250 mm plastic lens.[^1][^4] LighthouseFriends reports that in 1996 the Coast Guard contemplated demolition, partly because of the precarious lean of the fifty-four-foot (16 m) tower.[^4] A Coast Guard lighthouse official in the Fifth District believed there was "no immediate danger" of the tower tumbling, but another official was concerned that it "could possibly fall while personnel were inside."[^4] The light was listed in the National Register of Historic Places on 22 July 1982.[^1][^16]

## Today

In 2006 the General Services Administration posted a notice that the light was "in excess to the needs of the Federal Government."[^4] No qualified owner was found, and the GSA sold the light at an online auction in 2008.[^4] LighthouseFriends says the winning bid, submitted on 23 September, was $80,000, and that the new owner has been identified as AFB, Inc., of Bear, Delaware.[^4] LighthouseFriends reports that the GSA's invitation for bids called the lighthouse "physically inaccessible," and noted that the iron plates in the caisson were cracked and corroding and that the wooden floor on the first level was unstable.[^4]

LighthouseFriends says the light last appeared on the Coast Guard's Light List in 2009.[^4] It says the Coast Guard deactivated the light in 2009 or 2010, "after deciding the structure was too hazardous for its personnel."[^4] When that page was written, the tower had "neither been demolished nor fallen on its own."[^4]

LighthouseFriends describes the lighthouse as privately owned, with the tower closed.[^4] The sources read for this story do not say who owns it now or what has happened to the tower since. Do not land on the island or try to climb the tower.[^4]
