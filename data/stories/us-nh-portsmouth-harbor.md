Portsmouth Harbor Light stands on the rocks at Fort Point, where the Piscataqua River turns toward the port of Portsmouth, New Hampshire.[^1][^2] The colony lit a tower on the point in 1771.[^1] The cast-iron tower standing today went up in 1877 or 1878, on a masonry base built in 1804, and it is still an active aid to navigation.[^1][^2][^3][^9]

## The place

Fort Point sits at the south side of the mouth of the harbor, in the town of New Castle.[^1] The National Register nomination calls Portsmouth's harbor New Hampshire's only deepwater port.[^1] Merchants and shipping interests in the Portsmouth area first asked the colonial government for a light here around 1721.[^1] Their requests went nowhere until the early 1770s, when Royal Governor John Wentworth persuaded the Provincial Assembly to appropriate the money.[^1] In April 1771 Wentworth argued that "Every future expiring cry of (a) drowning mariner upon our coast will bitterly accuse the unfeeling Recusant that wastes life to save a paltry unblessed shilling."[^1]

A fort had stood on the point since 1632, and by 1692 it had become Fort William and Mary, later renamed Fort Constitution.[^1] The light was officially established in July 1771, the tenth of eleven lights built in Britain's North American colonies before the Revolution.[^1]

## Building the light

The first tower was wood, about 50 feet (15 m) tall, and stood about 100 yards (91 m) west of the present one.[^1] Its signal came from three copper oil lamps.[^1] The nomination says it is unclear whether the light was used as a lighted aid during the Revolution, or served only as an observation post for patriot lookouts.[^1]

New Hampshire gave the fort and the light to the federal government in 1791, and in 1793 President George Washington ordered that a resident keeper be assigned so the light would be kept at all times.[^1] In 1804 the government built an octagonal wooden tower, 80 feet (24 m) tall, about 100 yards (91 m) east of the first, at the water's edge on an outcrop called Pollock Rock. It carried oil lamps with reflectors and no lens.[^1]

Isaiah W. P. Lewis, a lighthouse engineer, visited in 1842.[^1] As LighthouseFriends quotes his report, he called the wooden tower "an excellent piece of carpentry."[^3] He also wrote that Whaleback Light, about 1.2 miles (1.9 km) south near the mouth of the Piscataqua River, had reduced Portsmouth's light "to the grade of a simple harbor beacon," so the tower could be cut down and its lamps reduced.[^3][^1] The nomination dates Whaleback to 1830 and LighthouseFriends to 1831.[^1][^3] The tower was shortened to 55 feet (17 m) in 1851.[^1] In December 1854 a fourth-order Fresnel lens replaced the thirteen lamps and reflectors.[^1][^3] LighthouseFriends reports that oil use fell from 302.21 gallons in six months to 39.93 over the same period.[^3]

An inspector who came in 1851 found the keeper, William Vennard, a former farmer, had been given "no previous instructions." He also found the lantern dome black inside and oil spilled on the floor and steps.[^3]

The present cast-iron tower was designed by Lieutenant Colonel James Chatham Duane, who ran military and lighthouse construction in northern New England from 1868 to 1879.[^1] It was built of curved cast-iron plates that bolted together, and it was assembled inside the 1804 wooden tower. When the new tower was finished, the wooden one was taken down.[^1] The nomination gives 1878 for the new tower and LighthouseFriends gives 1877.[^1][^3] The nomination also says the 1878 lighthouse was the first in the United States with a kerosene-fueled lamp installed as original equipment.[^1]

A bell house went up at the foot of the tower in 1896, and the Board's entry for that year sets a fog bell of 1,000 pounds there.[^4] LighthouseFriends gives the bell as 1,048 pounds, tolled once every ten seconds.[^3] The nomination says that in foggy weather the bell was struck with a single blow every ten minutes, so it disagrees with LighthouseFriends on the rhythm.[^3][^1] A brick oil house was added in 1903.[^1]

The keeper's house was rebuilt on the old foundation in 1871 or 1872; the sources differ.[^3][^1] It was moved about 200 feet (61 m) in 1897 to make room for an artillery battery, and again in 1906 to its present place inside the fort's wall. LighthouseFriends gives 1905 for the second move.[^5][^1][^3]

## The keepers

The first keeper, Captain John Cochran, was the fort's British commander, and his troops tended the light alongside their garrison duties.[^1] David Duncan kept the light from 1793 to 1820, Allen Porter from 1820 to 1839, and William Vennard from 1849 to 1853.[^2][^3]

Joshua K. Card was keeper for 35 years, from 1874 to 1909, the longest tenure on the roster.[^2][^3] Leander White took the light in 1909.[^2][^3] His son-in-law, Henry M. Cuskley, came from lighthouses in Maine in 1915 to replace him.[^3] Cuskley served 26 years at Portsmouth Harbor and was chairman of the school board when he retired in 1941.[^3]

In 1942 the light passed to Arnold B. White, who kept it until 1946. Elson L. Small followed from 1946 to 1948 as the last full-time keeper.[^2][^3] LighthouseFriends says Small's wife, Connie, wrote a book about their years at lighthouses, published in 1986.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The sources cited for this station tie no wreck or rescue to the light itself. The damage they do record came from winter storms in recent years.[^8][^3] In 2022 a winter storm damaged the wooden footbridge that ran from Fort Constitution to the tower, and tours stopped.[^8] In January 2024, back-to-back storms destroyed what remained of the footbridge and harmed the stone piers that held it.[^8] LighthouseFriends says the same January 2024 storms damaged the oil house and broke up much of the concrete base supporting the lighthouse.[^3] The Friends of Portsmouth Harbor Lighthouses later said that additional storms had badly damaged the base, which has since been repaired.[^6]

## After the keepers

Until automation in 1960, a watchstander tended the light.[^2][^3] The watch turned it on half an hour before sunset and off the next morning, and had to crank the fog-bell mechanism every two hours in poor visibility.[^3][^2] In 1948 Coast Guard personnel from the discontinued life-saving station on Wood Island were transferred to Fort Point.[^3]

A foghorn replaced the bell in 1972, sounding the same single blast every ten seconds.[^3] The Coast Guard repainted the tower in 1998, and the American Lighthouse Foundation leased it in 2000. The Friends of Portsmouth Harbor Lighthouse, a chapter of the foundation, was formed in 2001 to care for the light.[^9][^3] The oil house was restored in 2004, the walkway was rebuilt in 2006, and a small museum exhibit went into the restored oil house in 2017.[^9] Fort Constitution was deactivated in 1961 and handed to the state of New Hampshire, and it was listed on the National Register in 1973.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard owns the light, which stands on an active Coast Guard station beside Fort Constitution State Historic Site.[^2][^9] The Friends of Portsmouth Harbor Lighthouses, a chapter of the American Lighthouse Foundation, manage the tower.[^9]

Tours stopped after the 2022 storm and did not resume for three years.[^8][^6] Temporary staircases were installed on July 23, 2026.[^8] The American Lighthouse Foundation's account says two went in; the Friends and LighthouseFriends say three.[^8][^6][^3] The Friends held a ribbon-cutting on August 7, 2026, National Lighthouse Day, when tours resumed.[^6][^3] Tours are by reservation and last about an hour. They cost $25 per person, or $20 for members.[^7] Children must be 42 inches (107 cm) tall to climb.[^7] The visit includes two short stairways of seven steps each and a walk of about 60 feet (18 m) across a flat, stony beach.[^7] Proper footwear is required, and sandals, flip-flops and high heels are not allowed.[^7] Visitors must observe the posted signs and stay away from Coast Guard buildings and boats.[^7] Guides meet visitors at Coast Guard Station Portsmouth Harbor and walk them to the light.[^7]

The light is a fixed green light, visible for 12 miles in clear weather (the nomination does not say whether nautical or statute miles), from a fourth-order Fresnel lens inside a green acrylic cover.[^1] The fog signal sounds one second every ten seconds.[^1] The keeper's house of 1872 was moved to its present place inside the fort's wall in 1906, and the brick oil house of 1903 remains beside the tower.[^1]
