East Point Light stands where the Maurice River reaches Delaware Bay, near Heislerville in Cumberland County, New Jersey. It was placed in service in 1849 as the Maurice River Lighthouse.[^2] In 2026 the state's environmental commissioner called it one of the state's few surviving lighthouses from the 19th century.[^3]

## The place

Oystering was the river's business. In 1895 the Light-House Board repeated a claim that some 500 sailing vessels were engaged in the oyster trade on the Maurice River during the season, and it recommended range lights at the river mouth.[^4]

LighthouseFriends suggests the lighthouse owed more to one man's persistence than to trade or a community.[^2] Around the start of 1849 Joshua Brick wrote to his congressman, Charles Brown, asking for a lighthouse at the river. A letter of 10 March 1849 reported that Congress had allocated $5,000 for one at East Point.[^2]

The site was marsh. The National Register nomination describes it in 1849 as "all salt marsh except a knoll or sand island, about a half acre in extent and known locally as Haystack Island, upon which the lighthouse was erected."[^1] The government bought the knoll for $250 from its owner, who was Brick himself.[^2]

## Building the light

A description of the station from 1878, which LighthouseFriends reproduces, gives the light as 48 feet (15 m) above ordinary sea level. It was shown from a lantern on top of the keeper's brick dwelling, with a sixth-order fixed white light and Franklin lamps. The cellar was wet in very high tides.[^2]

The original nine lamps gave way in 1856 to a sixth-order Fresnel lens.[^2] The nomination describes that lens as made by Henry LePaute of Paris, and gives the height of its light as about 45 feet (14 m) above sea level, against the 48 feet (15 m) of 1878.[^1][^2] In 1870 a fourth-order lantern was placed atop the tower.[^2]

The paint changed over the years. The nomination says the tower was white with a red lantern in 1875, and the lantern was painted black from at least 1879 through 1907.[^1]

The range lights were a separate project. The Board surveyed a site for them in April 1897, and its 1899 list of light-stations includes a Maurice River range light-station with two lantern lights from 15 October 1898.[^5][^6] The same list records that the older Maurice River light-station had its characteristic changed "by inserting a red sector covering the entrance to the river" on the same day.[^9]

The Board's report for 1900 says the erection of a brick oil house at the station was begun under contract. The nomination dates the building to between 1900 and 1901.[^7][^1]

## The keepers

The first keeper, William W. Yarrington, was appointed on 10 September 1849. Two weeks later he was replaced by Francis Elberson, who served until 1853.[^2]

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's roster lists the principal keepers after Elberson: Peter Souder (1853 to 1861), William Tice (1861 to 1867), Benjamin Tice (1867 to 1876), Gilbert Compton (1876 to 1887), Thomas Foster (1887 to 1891), Charles W. Norton (1891 to 1894), William Harrington (1894 to 1905), and Linwood Spicer (1905 to 1911).[^8] LighthouseFriends counts ten keepers in all, the last being Spicer.[^2]

In 1911 an acetylene light was fitted. It could run night and day for a couple of weeks on fuel from tanks in the lighthouse, so full-time keepers were no longer needed. Custodians were allowed to live at the lighthouse for a token salary of $1 a year.[^2] Samuel Flick was the first custodian, from 1911.[^2][^8]

A local inspector proposed a $120 salary, because a capable custodian would be hard to attract to the remote station. The proposal was denied, but capable custodians were still found.[^2]

One custodian ran a boat rental business from the lighthouse to supplement his $1 salary. The business was sold to later custodians, and the last custodian left the lighthouse in 1945.[^2]

## After the keepers

At the outset of the Second World War the light was darkened, and it was later discontinued. The nomination gives 1941 as the year the Coast Guard discontinued it.[^2][^1]

In 1955 the federal government put the lighthouse up for auction. New Jersey's Division of Fish, Game and Wildlife objected that it had not been offered the lighthouse first. The sale was rescinded and the station passed to the state, which wanted the access to the water more than the lighthouse.[^2] The nomination gives the date of the deed to the state as 10 April 1956.[^1]

A fire set by trespassers on 15 July 1971 destroyed the roof and lantern room and caused serious damage to the interior.[^1] By February 1971 the structure had deteriorated so far that concerned local citizens formed the Maurice River Historical Society to save it.[^1][^2] The society raised money to rebuild the roof and lantern, and the Coast Guard reactivated the light on 2 July 1980.[^2][^1]

Restoration came in stages. LighthouseFriends reports about $400,000 of exterior work in the 1990s, an $852,000 project in 2016 for the roof, interior and brickwork, and a rededication on 10 September 2017, 168 years after the light entered service.[^2]

Sources differ on when the light last closed. LighthouseFriends dates the first closure to March 2021, when the society's lease ran out, and says the light reopened in August 2021 under a temporary license. It also says the society left in September 2024, after the society and the state could not agree on a new lease. The Inquirer says the lighthouse closed in 2024.[^2][^3]

The period furnishings are no longer in place. The society had put period-replica pieces in various rooms, and the Inquirer reports those have been removed.[^3] LighthouseFriends described five rooms furnished with period antiques after the 2017 rededication.[^2]

## Today

The lighthouse is state land inside the Heislerville Wildlife Management Area. The Inquirer reports that the state's Department of Environmental Protection will maintain it and that Cumberland County will lease it at little to no cost.[^3]

The Inquirer reported on 9 October 2026 that the lighthouse will reopen to the public on a limited basis on 16 October. More days and hours are expected once a full plan is developed by early 2027.[^3] The department is working with the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers to design protection against the encroaching bay and rising sea levels, which could be a bulkhead, dunes or another method.[^3]

A state senator quoted by the Inquirer said the light remains an active aid to navigation for the Coast Guard.[^3]
