Jupiter Inlet Light is a brick tower on a low mound in Palm Beach County, Florida, where the Loxahatchee River meets Jupiter Sound. It took the better part of six years to build, went dark through the Civil War, and has shown a light again since 1866.[^1][^2]

## The place

In its report of 30 January 1852 the Light-House Board recommended a first-order light near Jupiter Inlet, and Congress authorised one on 3 March 1853 as a seacoast light that would also "guard mariners in approaching the dangerous shoal lying off that point."[^1] The same act admitted that the point was "inaccessible for all useful purposes by land" and hard to reach by sea for want of harbours.[^1] The site chosen was part of the Fort Jupiter reservation left over from the Seminole wars, and 61.5 acres were reserved by President Pierce's executive order of 22 October 1854.[^1] A survey in 1925 showed the reservation had been placed wrongly, and a new order of 12 June 1925 fixed it at 121.95 acres.[^1]

In the 1890s the Light-House Board, pressing year after year for a light at Hillsboro Inlet to the south, noted that steamers bound south, "after making Jupiter Inlet light, hug the reef very closely to avoid the current."[^3]

## Building the light

Lieutenant George G. Meade of the Topographical Engineers ran the district at the start. The National Register nomination says the design went to the Board over his name but was drawn under his instruction by John W. Nystrom, and that Meade administered two lighthouse districts from an office in Philadelphia, so whether he ever visited the site during construction is not documented.[^1] LighthouseFriends, by contrast, says Meade soon visited the site.[^2] A museum history of the light credits Meade with the site and original design and his successor, Lieutenant William F. Raynolds, with adding height and a double wall; it names a civilian, Edward A. Yorke, as overseer of the 1859 and 1860 work.[^4] LighthouseFriends, which spells the successor "Raynold", says Meade's design was modified by him.[^2]

The inlet itself was the first obstacle. The nomination says it was "sanded shut in 1854", so materials had to come down the Indian River and Jupiter Narrows, with some overland carriage and lightering, about five hundred tons in all.[^1] Congress added $19,522.90 in 1856 to continue and complete the light, but little was spent in 1857 and 1858 because of the Third Seminole War, and in 1859 the work stopped again over heat, moisture and insects.[^1] The first work crew, the museum history says, was sent in late 1855 and went home at once when war broke out; work began in earnest in January 1859 and finished in five months of the 1860 season.[^4] The nomination puts the total spent from 1854 to 1859 at $60,858.98, with $6,336.08 moved in from balances of other lighthouse appropriations; LighthouseFriends prints the figure as $60,859.98.[^1][^2]

The light was first lit on 10 July 1860.[^1][^4] The sources disagree on the tower's height: the nomination calls it 105 feet (32 m), noting that the specification grew from 90 feet before it was finished, while the museum history and LighthouseFriends say 108 feet (33 m).[^1][^4][^2] The nomination describes a conical brick tower with walls tapering from 31 and a half inches at the base to 18 inches under the lantern, a ten-sided gallery on brackets, and a south doorway under a brick arch; the brick was left bare at first and is painted red now.[^1] LighthouseFriends says the lens is a first-order Fresnel made by Henry-Lepaute of Paris, showing two flashes then a dark period, and that a two-storey coquina dwelling with a well inside was built in 1859.[^2]

## The keepers

The USLHS archive's roster, drawn from the National Archives' keeper registers, lists Walter F. Wolfkiel as principal keeper in 1860, followed by Thomas Turner and Joseph F. Papy.[^5] In August 1861, the nomination says, men from the area removed the lighting apparatus so Union blockaders could not use the light; it quotes the Board's report that "lawless persons visited the Jupiter Inlet Lighthouse" and took the apparatus away.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes a letter the men sent to the governor of Florida, and says at least one was an assistant at Jupiter.[^2] The museum history names assistant keeper Augustus Lang as one of those who disabled the light, and says it stayed dark through the war.[^4] The light came back on 28 June 1866.[^2][^4]

James A. Armour, who the museum history says had helped the Union Navy recover parts removed from the lighthouse, was among the new keepers; LighthouseFriends says the missing parts were recovered by him in a palmetto hammock near Lake Worth Creek, and that he was appointed assistant under William Davis.[^2][^4] The USLHS roster shows Davis as principal keeper 1866 to 1869, and Armour as first assistant from 1866 and head keeper from 1869 to 1906.[^5] The museum history also has him retiring in 1906, after what it calls 40 years of service as keeper, while LighthouseFriends says he retired in 1908.[^2][^4] He married Almeda Carlile in 1867, according to LighthouseFriends, and the couple had eight children at the station; their eldest daughter Katherine married Joseph A. Wells, an assistant who later succeeded Armour.[^2] The roster lists Wells as head keeper 1906 to 1919.[^5] In December 1895 Armour was one of the keepers detailed to Atlanta to look after the Light-House Board's exhibit at the Cotton States and International Exposition; the Board's report says such details were made "as a mark of appreciation of faithful service."[^6]

Charles Seabrook was head keeper from 1919; the roster gives 1946, and LighthouseFriends says ill health ended his service in 1947.[^5][^2] The museum history says he and his assistants joined the Coast Guard when the Lighthouse Service merged into it in 1939, and that military crews kept the light until it was fully automated in 1987.[^4] The roster's last principal keeper is Mitchel W. Oakland, 1984 to 1987.[^5]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

LighthouseFriends tells of the steamer Victor, which it says broke a shaft and was driven ashore south of the light on 20 October 1872; it reports the keepers salvaged a sewing machine and three dogs, named Vic, Storm and Wreck.[^2] That account rests on LighthouseFriends alone.

After the hurricane of mid-September 1926, the Lighthouse Service Bulletin's preliminary telegraphic reports recorded that damage was done to the quarters at Jupiter Inlet.[^7] LighthouseFriends says a fire on 29 February 1927 destroyed the original keepers' dwelling and that a new double dwelling for the assistants was built in 1929.[^2]

The light was electrified in 1928, and the Bulletin reported that the first-order lens, a flashing lens with the space for two double panels, 180 degrees apart, filled with sheet metal, was turned by electric motors once a minute to give a double flash every 30 seconds.[^8][^9] A radiobeacon was established at the station on 6 January 1928, which the Bulletin called a primary station for its position at the northern entrance to the Straits of Florida.[^10] The keeper reported a severe storm on 7 August 1928: the alternating current failed at 6 p.m., the light and radiobeacon ran all night on direct current, and the power house had to be braced from inside with four braces.[^9]

The September 1928 hurricane hit the same coast hard. The Bulletin says it struck the Florida coast at Palm Beach on 17 September, put the damage in the Sixth District at about $25,000, "most of which was at Jupiter Inlet Light Station," where one building was completely destroyed and the radiobeacon was out of commission until the afternoon of the 18th.[^11] LighthouseFriends, which dates the warnings to 16 September, tells the keepers' side of it: it says the power failed and the generator would not start, Seabrook put the old lamps back in the lens, and his sixteen-year-old son Franklin turned the lens by hand for four hours while the tower swayed an estimated seventeen inches.[^2] The details of the lamps and the hand-turned lens come from LighthouseFriends alone; the Bulletin gives only the damage totals above.

## After the keepers

LighthouseFriends says the light became semi-automated in 1966, with Coast Guard personnel on site until 1987, and that the Loxahatchee Historical Society began public tours in 1994.[^2] An $850,000 restoration followed in 1999 and 2000; the tower was repainted a more subdued brick red.[^2]

## Today

The tower is an active aid to navigation and sits on the Jupiter Inlet Lighthouse Outstanding Natural Area, designated by Congress in 2008.[^4] Ownership passed from the Coast Guard to the Bureau of Land Management in 2019; the lens remains Coast Guard property.[^4] A nonprofit, the Loxahatchee River Historical Society, has looked after the tower under an agreement since 1994 and provides visitor access and interpretation.[^4] Check the society's current schedule before visiting. It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1973.[^1]
