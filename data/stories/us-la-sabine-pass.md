Sabine Pass Light is an octagonal brick tower with heavy buttresses at its base. It stands in the marsh on the Louisiana side of the Sabine Pass, about 75 feet (23 m) from its base to the lantern.[^2] It was completed in 1856, went dark during the Civil War, and kept its light until 1952. A preservation group formed in 1999 to save it is its recorded owner, and the interior is closed to visitors.[^1][^2][^3][^8]

## The place

The lighthouse stands on the east side of the pass, on Lighthouse Bayou, in marshy ground. When the National Register nomination was written in 1981 there was no road to it.[^1] The nomination says the tower lies in Cameron Parish, Louisiana. It also says the lighthouse has traditionally been considered part of the Sabine Pass and Port Arthur community, and that the easiest way to reach it "is by boat from the town of Sabine Pass, Texas."[^1]

Congress first appropriated $7,500 for a light at Sabine Pass in 1849.[^3] LighthouseFriends says a navy commander, Henry A. Adams, came out on horseback to look into the need, and advised that a light-house was "not necessary there at this time." All but $116.80, which LighthouseFriends says was most likely used for the surveyor's expenses, went back to the surplus fund.[^3] Texas citizens near the pass disagreed with that finding, and politicians kept petitioning Congress for a light at the pass.[^3] LighthouseFriends dates the next appropriation, $30,000 for a first-class light at the mouth of the Sabine River, to 1853.[^3] The site lay within a large federal military reservation set aside in 1838.[^3]

## Building the light

Work on the eighty-foot brick tower and a wooden dwelling beside it began in late 1855 under the direction of John Wilson, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] The tower stood on soft marshland, so the foundation used closely spaced piles, and eight buttresses extended from its base to spread its weight over a larger area.[^3] The nomination calls the tower "one of very few extant brick structures of its type in Louisiana." It notes that the federal government had tried to build brick lighthouses on the state's shoreline during the nineteenth century, and that they "invariably sank into the marshy soil."[^1] The tower and dwelling were painted white, LighthouseFriends says.[^3]

The sources do not agree on when the light was first shown. The nomination says the lighthouse "was completed in 1856 for a reported cost of $30,000."[^1] The US Lighthouse Society's record gives 1856 as the year the station was established and as the year its original third-order Fresnel lens was installed.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the light went into service in late spring or early summer of 1857, and that its lens gave a fixed white light with a flash.[^3] This story cannot settle which date is right.

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's roster lists 27 principal keepers and 94 assistants at Sabine Pass between 1857 and 1952.[^2]

Benjamin F. Granger was the first keeper. The roster has him from 1857 to 1859, and LighthouseFriends says he was hired as the first keeper.[^2][^3] Gowen W. Plummer followed him, from 1859 to 1861. LighthouseFriends calls the keeper in post when the light went dark "George Plummer," and later in the same account "Keeper Gowen." The roster gives only Gowen W. Plummer for those years, and the sources do not explain the difference.[^2][^3]

The light went dark on August 16, 1861, and LighthouseFriends says Confederates had stolen the oil. The keeper put his wife and children aboard one of Admiral Farragut's ships and returned to Maine. Before he left, he gave his livestock, three houses, and all of his fencing and firewood to Union soldiers.[^3]

Women also served as assistant keepers. Louisa Crossman was first assistant in 1867 and 1868, and Ricka (Fredericka) Crossman held the post from 1874 to 1881. The roster shares a surname with the principal keeper Charles Crossman, but it does not say how they were related.[^2]

Gustav Hummeland went from first assistant (1882 to 1886) to principal keeper (1886 to 1898). He was keeper when the hurricane of 1886 struck.[^2][^3] In 1915 William B. Thompson was principal keeper, with Charles C. Sapp as his assistant.[^2][^6] Thompson was transferred to Pensacola in 1917, and Kristian Andersen became keeper.[^3] The last principal keeper was Stephen F. Purgley, from 1946 to 1952. LighthouseFriends says he put the light out for the last time on May 21, 1952.[^2][^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On April 16, 1863, Confederate soldiers hid in the lighthouse and waited for a Union party that came ashore to spy on the fort being built across the river. LighthouseFriends says three Union men surrendered at once, the rest retreated to their boats under fire, and four Northerners were killed. The Confederates lost one soldier.[^3] Five months later the Battle of Sabine Pass was fought close to the tower, the same account says.[^3]

Union forces had shipped the light's lens to New York. The light was back in service on December 23, 1865, after the lens returned.[^3]

In October 1886 a storm struck Sabine Pass, and the sources give different nights. LighthouseFriends dates it to October 12.[^3] A letter from the Beaumont relief committee, printed in the *Austin Weekly Statesman* on 21 October, refers to "the night of the 13th" and calls the storm "one of the severest cyclones ever known on this coast."[^7] The same page prints a Beaumont dispatch describing a wave breaking against the lighthouse and tearing out the windows at the very top of the structure.[^7] LighthouseFriends says Keeper Gustav Hummeland, a woman it calls his lady friend, and Assistant Keeper Henry Plummer with his wife, sought refuge in the upper part of the tower after the dwelling was torn apart.[^3]

By the end of the following summer a new double dwelling had been built on piles above the marsh, linked to the tower by an elevated walk.[^3]

In the hurricane of August 16–17, 1915, the wharf, walk and outbuildings at Sabine Pass were carried away. The two entrance range lights, the East Jetty Light, the inner range lights and the channel light were all washed away.[^5] The Lighthouse Service printed a list of employees "commended by the Secretary of Commerce for performing their duties under hazardous and trying conditions" during the storm. It named William B. Thompson, keeper, and Charles C. Sapp, assistant keeper, of the Sabine Pass Light Station.[^6] The Service also reported that at Sabine Pass the keepers kept the light's characteristic going by hand "when the vibration of the tower put the clockwork out of order."[^6] LighthouseFriends gives the same account.[^3]

## After the keepers

The Light-House Board's report for the fiscal year ended June 30, 1899 recommended a "jetty light and fog-signal station" for the east jetty at Sabine Pass. The Board said the jetty's protecting mat extends out about four miles. The nearest light, a small beacon about a mile and a half inside the end, was "too far inside" to guide ships to its entrance. The Board also noted that "Sabine Pass as a port of entry has grown rapidly."[^4]

In 1917 the keepers also took responsibility for the range and jetty lights.[^3] LighthouseFriends dates a light and fog signal at the end of the eastern jetty to 1924, which made the old land-based light less important.[^3] Some people recommended abandoning the lighthouse, but others argued it was a convenient base for tending the jetty light, and the decision was made to keep it active.[^3] The 1899 report makes the jetty light a recommendation, and LighthouseFriends dates its installation to 1924, so this story cannot say when the jetty light first showed.[^3][^4]

LighthouseFriends says electricity and a radio beacon were installed at the lighthouse in 1929. In 1932 two black bands were painted on the tower to make it more visible on hazy days.[^3]

The land-based light was discontinued in 1952.[^3] In 1953 the federal government turned the property over to the Louisiana Wildlife and Fisheries Commission, which planned to use the buildings as a field station for its game wardens.[^3] The station was underused and was eventually returned to the General Services Administration. In 1971 the property went to Lamar State College at Beaumont, which returned it to the federal government after two years.[^1][^3] In 1974 vandals cut away copper roofing from the lantern room, and the State of Louisiana took possession that year.[^1][^3]

On September 7, 1976, a marsh fire destroyed the keepers' dwelling and the boathouse. LighthouseFriends says only the brick tower and an oil house survived.[^3] The 1981 nomination, prepared by Louisiana's Office of State Parks, describes a small brick storage house still standing and the ruins of the keeper's house.[^1] The two accounts name different surviving buildings.

The nomination was signed in November 1981 and judged to have state significance. It described the tower as "deteriorating," with its copper roof destroyed and its spiral staircase rusting.[^1]

In 1986 a federal auction sold the lighthouse and 45 acres of marsh to two private owners for $55,000, LighthouseFriends says.[^3] The Cameron Preservation Alliance, formed in 1999, received the property on May 5, 2001.[^3] In 2019 stainless-steel bands were fitted around the tower to stabilize it, and in 2023 volunteers found original steps buried in the sand at the base.[^3]

## Today

The US Lighthouse Society lists the Cameron Preservation Alliance as owner and manager, and says the lighthouse is not open to the public.[^2] LighthouseFriends' visitor notes say the parish owns it, which conflicts with its own account of the 2001 transfer.[^3]

Cameron Parish Tourism says the lighthouse is "closed to interior tours" but can be seen from outside. It is reached off LA-82, about a mile east of the Causeway Bridge that links Louisiana and Texas.[^8] LighthouseFriends says a four-mile road, built with help from Cheniere Energy, made the lighthouse reachable by land for the first time. That road runs through Cheniere property, is gated, and is passable only in dry conditions.[^3]

The tower is closed, and its spiral staircase came down in Hurricane Ike in 2008. That year two men who climbed to the top were stranded when a ladder broke. Firefighters rescued them with a rope harness, and the local sheriff's department cited them for trespassing and property damage, LighthouseFriends reports.[^3] Visitors should stay outside and off the closed tower.
