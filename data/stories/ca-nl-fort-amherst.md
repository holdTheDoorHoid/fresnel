Fort Amherst Lighthouse stands on South Head, one side of the Narrows, the channel into St. John's harbour in Newfoundland and Labrador. A light has shone from this spot since the early nineteenth century, and it is usually named as Newfoundland's first.[^1][^2]

## The place

The light was built inside a fort. After the British retook St. John's from the French in 1762, a battery was laid out on South Head. Lighthouse Friends says the fort was named for Jeffrey Amherst, brother of the Lieutenant Colonel William Amherst who drove the French out, while Hidden Newfoundland says it was named for William himself.[^1][^4] Lighthouse Friends gives 1763 for the plans and 1777 for completion, while Hidden Newfoundland has a masonry tower and battery built between 1773 and 1777.[^1][^4] The lighthouse was placed within it at the urging of fishing and commercial interests.[^1]

## Building the light

The sources do not agree on when the first light was built. Lighthouse Friends and the local history site Hidden Newfoundland say 1813; Parks Canada says "ca 1813"; Lighthouse Digest says 1810, and describes it as built on top of a barrack.[^1][^4][^3][^2] Until Newfoundland gained representative government in 1832 the light was kept up by contributions, and the new legislature's Commissioners of Lighthouses had repairs made and showed an improved light on 7 July 1834.[^1][^2]

By January 1849 the Superintendent of Lighthouses, Patrick Kough, reported that the lantern was "quite a flimsy concern" and the whole structure so decayed and worn out that it was hard to say where to begin repairs.[^1] A replacement was built next to the old one and was ready in the spring of 1852 or 1853; the account that gives the fuller detail says it was built by Kerr and Moody, with a lantern ordered from Stevenson and Co. of Scotland, and, unlike its predecessor, it also showed into the harbour.[^1][^2] Lighthouse Digest calls it the first lighthouse in Newfoundland to receive a Fresnel lens; Lighthouse Friends dates a fourth-order annular lens to 1855.[^2][^1]

## The keepers

Philip Roach was appointed the first civilian keeper in 1834, though Royal Artillery soldiers kept the duty of firing a fog gun. In 1843 the commissioners noted that the firing shook the building and that the keeper had only a single room for all his purposes.[^1] When Roach died in 1852, his widow Honora petitioned for a small yearly grant.[^1]

John Sheppard took over and stayed until 1887. The new lens needed attending all night, which Lighthouse Friends says damaged his health, so his son Austin became his assistant; Austin later returned from Cape Spear to succeed him.[^1] Lighthouse Digest says Austin was head keeper from 1887 to 1924.[^2] On 8 July 1892 Austin took the station boat to St. John's, and that afternoon the fire that destroyed most of the city burned the boat to its waterline at Queen's Wharf.[^1]

Austin's grandson, Robert Carl Sheppard, a master mariner wounded in the First World War, followed him in 1924, leaving in 1938 or 1939 depending on the source.[^1][^2] He and his wife Sadie ran a tea room at the station, with Thursday night dances according to Lighthouse Digest; the Second World War ended it when soldiers needed the space.[^1][^2] Ben Dalton then lived at the station with his wife and five children. On 3 March 1941, Lighthouse Friends says, a U-boat torpedo aimed at a steamer entering the Narrows hit the cliff beneath the lighthouse; Lighthouse Digest puts a U-boat attack in March 1942, so the year is uncertain.[^1][^2]

## After 1949

After Newfoundland joined Canada, the Department of Transport built two dwellings and the present wooden tower in 1951; the older stone lighthouse and barracks were taken down afterwards; Lighthouse Digest dates the lighthouse building's destruction to 1954 and the barracks' removal to 1959.[^1][^2][^3] The Parks Canada heritage statement describes a square, tapered wooden tower of a standard type, and the third on the site.[^3]

Leo Power came as assistant keeper in 1943 and became head keeper in 1952 or 1953 (the sources differ) until his retirement on his 65th birthday in 1972. He and his wife Nellie raised eleven children there.[^1][^2] Nellie acted as an unofficial assistant keeper: "I’d have to watch to see if the light was still going or if the horn was stopped," she said.[^2] Ed Brake kept the light to 1975 and Con Bennett to 1982, when the station was de-staffed.[^1]

## Today

In 1991 Jack Power, one of Leo's sons, found signs on the dwellings reading "For sale and removal" and with his wife Rosalind set out to save them. The City of St. John's took over the property in 1995, and the Powers restored the dwellings and opened a museum and tea room in 1998.[^1][^2] The museum and tea room closed around 2006, when a grandson of Leo Power took over the property; the two dwellings have since been offered as vacation rentals.[^1] Parks Canada's heritage lighthouse statement for the tower is dated October 2013.[^3] Remains of the Second World War gun batteries lie below the tower.[^4] Lighthouse Friends reports the lighthouse owned by the Canadian Coast Guard, with the grounds open and the tower closed.[^1] The tower should be treated as closed to climbing.
