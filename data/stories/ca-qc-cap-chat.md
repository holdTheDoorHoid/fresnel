Cap-Chat Lighthouse (*phare de Cap-Chat*) is a square concrete tower on a cliff above the village of Cap-Chat, on the north shore of the Gaspé Peninsula in Quebec.[^1][^2] A light has shown from the cape since 1871. The present tower, finished in 1909, is one of the first reinforced-concrete lighthouses in Canada, according to the Quebec heritage register (our translation).[^1]

## The place

The cape takes its name from a large rock formation shaped like a sitting cat, LighthouseFriends says, and it is where the Gulf of St. Lawrence narrows into the estuary.[^2] LighthouseFriends says the narrowing is defined by Cap Chat on the south shore and Pointe des Monts on the north shore, and that it "was a dangerous area for ships, especially at night or in times of poor visibility."[^2]

In 1869 a select committee on fisheries and navigation asked mariners where new lights or fog alarms were needed. One answer, from a Mr. Gibb, said that between Cape Rosier and Point des Monts "the navigator has no means of knowing his whereabouts, except in the vicinity of Mont Louis river," and it called for a light "certainly at Cape Chatte."[^2]

The pressure for a light came from business and politics. The heritage register says that in 1870 Hugh Allan, the owner of a major shipping company, pleaded with the federal minister of marine, Peter Mitchell, for a light at Cap-Chat to guide shipping (our translation). Governor General Lord Dufferin also referred to the site.[^1] LighthouseFriends says that local lore has a private light on the spot from about 1811, but the first official light came in 1871.[^2]

## Building the light

The first tower was a square wooden structure with the dwelling built into it. LighthouseFriends gives its height as 11 m (37 ft) to the vane, on a cliff 30.5 m (100 ft) high.[^2] The register describes its light as a catoptric optic fed by oil lamps (our translation).[^1] LighthouseFriends says the optic had six faces, "each with a No. 1 circular burner lamp and a twenty-inch reflector," and gave a white flash every thirty seconds.[^2] A. Gingras built it under contract for $3,895.49, and Joseph Roy was appointed its first keeper at $300 a year.[^2]

In 1875 a second square wooden tower went up, 440 m (480 yards) east-north-east of the first. The light moved into it and began on 5 August 1875. In 1884 the tower was moved back from the cliff edge.[^2]

Fog was the next problem. From 15 August 1891 an explosive fog signal of cotton-powder cartridges was fired from a derrick on the cliff edge.[^2] LighthouseFriends says a cartridge went off every twenty minutes in low visibility, and that the keeper's pay was raised $200 for the extra work.[^2] LighthouseFriends says a shed went up in 1893 to shelter the firing of the gun cotton, and the register describes a powder magazine of the same year, built to hold it (our translation).[^2][^1] LighthouseFriends also quotes the Chief Engineer, William P. Anderson, on the benefits of firing bombs out over deep water from the cliff: "Bombs could be projected out over deep water to a sufficient distance from the cliff to make the sound effective."[^2] In 1901 the Department's report describes the firing at Cape Chatte as having been increased "every 15 minutes, instead of every 20 minutes as theretofore."[^8] In the year to March 1908 the Department built a bomb shed at the station and added four rooms to the dwelling.[^7]

The concrete tower came in 1909. According to the register, after the Lighthouse Board's recommendation at a meeting in May 1908, a new tower was built in 1909 to carry a third-order Fresnel dioptric lens. It was designed by H. de Miffonis, an assistant engineer of the Marine Department (our translation).[^1] LighthouseFriends describes it as a square, reinforced-concrete tower of about 10 m (31 ft) with a round red lantern, finished in 1909, with a lens made in France by Barbier, Bénard et Turenne.[^2] The Department's report for the year to 31 March 1910 says that "A new lighthouse tower having been erected, the revolving white catoptric light has been discontinued," and that the new light showed "one bright flash every three seconds."[^6] The lighthouse-route association Les amis des phares du Saint-Laurent gives 1911 for the present tower.[^4] The sources disagree, and the Department's 1910 report supports the earlier date.

The diaphone replaced the explosive signal in 1918, LighthouseFriends says.[^2] The register dates a new fog-signal shelter to 1919 (our translation).[^1] A fire destroyed the keeper's residence, and LighthouseFriends says a new dwelling was built in 1923.[^2] The Department's report for the year to 31 March 1924 lists "Erection of dwelling to replace residence destroyed by fire" under Cape Chat.[^9]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends gives the keepers and their years, and little else about them.[^2] Joseph Roy was the first keeper and died in 1874. He was succeeded by Louis Treflé Côté, who kept the light until 1901. Luc Côté followed to 1927, then J.A. Aurèle Côté to 1931. Jean-François Roy kept the light for thirty-three years, from 1931 to 1964. Joseph Marcel Duguay served from 1964 to 1973 and Yves Duguay for about a year after him. The list ends with Charles Hector Fraser, who is shown as keeper from 1974 to 1988.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

On 23 July 1914 the Department's annual report for the year to 31 March 1915 lists, among the salvage services of the subsidized Quebec Salvage and Wrecking Company, "Saved the Canadian ss. Canada, which steamer was hard ashore off cape Chat."[^10]

On 15 May 1917 the steamer *Cape Corso*, listed with Greenock against her name, was "stranded in vicinity of cape Chat, gulf St. Lawrence," according to the Department's casualty list for the year to 31 March 1918.[^11] The same report's list of assistance to vessels records the steamer being towed from Cap Chat to Montreal between 15 and 23 May.[^12]

## After the keepers

The site was electrified in 1952, according to the register and a lighthouse association.[^1][^4] The register says the first tower, built in 1871, was demolished in 1961, and that its 1919 fog-signal shelter gave way to a new building with Lister engines made in Sherbrooke (our translation).[^1] LighthouseFriends says instead that a second wooden tower replaced the first in 1875. The register's "first" tower may therefore be that second one; that is our inference, and the sources do not settle which tower came down in 1961.[^2]

LighthouseFriends says the Fresnel lens was deactivated in 2007, and that "a modern emergency light is privately maintained on the tower."[^2] The Coast Guard's *List of Lights* still gives the main light as "Fl W 6s," operating 24 hours and marked "Private aid," with an emergency light, "Fl(6) W 15s."[^3] The register says the light "is still operated by the Canadian Coast Guard" (our translation), and in another passage that the municipality keeps the lamp lit, but that the site "ne sert plus comme aide à la navigation": it no longer serves as a navigation aid (our translation).[^1] LighthouseFriends and the Coast Guard's list disagree on whether the main light still shows. The register also says the site is now run by the Musée Germain-Lemieux.[^1] LighthouseFriends says "the station is managed by" the Parc récréotouristique du rocher Cap-Chat.[^2] A non-profit, Les gardiens du Phare Cap-Chat, was formed to preserve the tower's structure and operation and to promote its history and that of its keepers (our translation).[^5]

## Today

The tower is closed to visitors.[^1][^2] The Jardin des Brumes at its foot is open to the public, the register says (our translation).[^1] LighthouseFriends lists the grounds as open and the tower as closed.[^2]

The sources differ on the keeper's house. The register says it became a tea room with marine murals (our translation), while LighthouseFriends says it was "formerly used as a café" and "can now be rented for overnight stays."[^1][^2] The access route is by car from the village: LighthouseFriends gives 4.2 km west on Route 132, then north on Rue du Phare, and a walk of roughly 0.3 km to the tower.[^2]
