Cape Charles Light is a 191-foot (58 m) iron skeleton tower on Smith Island, Virginia, on the northern side of the entrance to Chesapeake Bay.[^1][^2] It is the third tower the station has had; the foundations of the first two now lie under the water, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^6]

## The place

The station sits on Smith Island, in Northampton County, at the mouth of Chesapeake Bay; the U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive gives its location as "SMITHS ISLAND/CHESAPEAKE BAY ENTRANCE."[^1] Congress voted $10,000 on 18 May 1826 for a lighthouse on the island, and the following year a rubblestone tower, built for $7,399, was standing off the cape.[^6] The sources disagree on the date and the height. The Light-House Board's 1895 report and the archive say the light was established in 1827, while the Chesapeake Chapter of the U.S. Lighthouse Society says the tower was completed in 1828 and calls it a 60-foot masonry tower, where Lighthouse Friends gives 55 feet (17 m).[^1][^2][^6][^10]

When the Light-House Board took over the nation's lights in 1852, it surveyed this one and found it wanting. As Lighthouse Friends quotes the Board, it had "only ten lamps" and a tower with "an elevation of only 55 feet."[^6]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $35,000 on 18 August 1856 to rebuild "upon a proper site," and a first-order Fresnel lens went into the old tower in 1858; the Board dates the rebuilding from 1858, while the Chesapeake Chapter says work started in 1860.[^2][^6][^10] The war interrupted the work. Confederates removed the lens from the 1827 tower, and a lightship marked the entrance until the light was re-established on 12 December 1861.[^6] On 3 August 1863, in the Board's words as quoted by Lighthouse Friends, "a party of guerrillas" visited the station and "completely destroyed the light." The unfinished new tower had by then reached 83 feet.[^6] Congress added $20,000 on 14 March 1864, and the second tower, a 150-foot (46 m) brick tower a little over a mile southwest of the first and 600 feet (180 m) from the shore, was put into operation on 7 September 1864.[^6]

The sea became the next threat to the second tower. The Light-House Board's engineer reported in 1895 that between 1883 and the late 1880s the water line advanced 240 feet, about 40 feet a year, and that in easterly storms the water "invaded the quarters of the light keepers."[^2] A stone-and-brush protection wall and a jetty 130 feet (40 m) long were built in 1887, and pile-and-stone jetties followed; at unusually high tides the sea washed over the wall.[^2] The Board had asked in its reports for 1889 and 1890 for $150,000 to build a new station, and Congress appropriated it on 30 August 1890.[^2]

The last jetty was finished in January 1891 and the protection wall on 11 March.[^3] In June 1892 a red band 25 feet (7.6 m) wide was painted around the 1864 tower, about 60 feet (18 m) above its base, so that mariners could tell it by day.[^4]

The new site was nearly a mile to the west of the old one, by the engineer's account in 1895; the Board's 1892 report puts it about three-quarters of a mile from the old light.[^2][^4] The district engineer proposed on 13 February 1891 an iron tower like the one at Cape Henry, but on 11 April 1892 the Board chose a skeleton design like those at Waackaack, New Jersey and Southwest Pass, Louisiana, one "so different in appearance from the Cape Henry tower as to prevent any chance of its being mistaken by mariners."[^2] The land had to be taken by condemnation, because one of the owners had died and left a minor among his heirs; five commissioners were appointed to meet at the site on 12 July 1892 to condemn the land.[^4] The Board ordered identical towers for Cape Charles and Hog Island under one contract. Ironwork began at the contractor's shops in June 1893, each section being built up, inspected, taken apart and shipped.[^2] A 1,345-foot (410 m) wharf had to be built so boats could reach deep water, and the island crew was sent home on 30 June 1894 because swarms of mosquitoes breeding in the swampy ground had made work there too difficult to keep up.[^6] The crew returned in November. The engineer reported that the winter was one of "extreme severity," and the structure was complete on 21 December 1894.[^2]

The first-order lens, about 12 feet (3.7 m) high and 6 feet (1.8 m) across, was hoisted into the lantern in June 1895.[^2] It was the first first-order light to use the "Mahan system": four quick flashes, a dark interval of about three seconds, five quick flashes, then about sixteen seconds of dark, so that "the number 45" was spelled out in light, "as in some places the number of a fire-alarm box is designated by strokes of a bell."[^2] The earlier light had flashed white every 45 seconds.[^5] To give mariners notice, the new characteristic was not exhibited until 15 August 1895.[^2] The old tower's lens was taken out that October, and on 1 June 1896 the interval between flashes was doubled, to a four-and-five group every minute.[^5]

The Board's report said the finished station "may be regarded as a model light-station," with a lawn of Bermuda grass set out to hold the sand, dwellings for the keepers and a telephone line to Cape Charles City. It also noted that the place was "especially disagreeable" for its mosquitoes, sand flies and fleas, which made life "almost unbearable for nearly half the year."[^2] The tower is a central iron tube with a spiral stair of 216 treads, surrounded by eight legs.[^6]

## The keepers

The U.S. Lighthouse Society's archive lists 99 keeper stints at Cape Charles between 1827 and 1940.[^1] James Powell was the first head keeper, from 1827 to 1832.[^1] Thomas S. Brickhouse followed, and then Obadiah Goffigan (1846 to 1856).[^1] The Chesapeake Chapter's roster has Brickhouse leaving in 1841, Goffigan serving 1843 to 1845 and Brickhouse returning until 1846, so the exact order in the 1840s is not settled.[^10] The roster carries five men named Goffigan and four named Fitchett across the nineteenth century; the sources do not say how they were related.[^1] John Goffigan was head keeper from 1877 to 1890, and Joseph R. Diggs from 1894 to 1909, which spans the move into the iron tower.[^1]

John S. Robins was head keeper from 1856 to 1862.[^1] Lighthouse Friends quotes a contemporary account (it does not name the report) describing him as a man "represented as a consistent Union man throughout the rebellion" who was "again placed in charge" after taking the oath of allegiance.[^6]

One keeper family is documented in some detail, the Pottses. Jean G. Potts, from Germany, became principal keeper in 1864, and his wife, Nancy B. Potts, joined the staff as first assistant in 1865.[^1][^8][^9] Early in 1868 Jean Potts was killed. The Alexandria Gazette of 29 February 1868 reported that Thomas L. Kendall, then at Point of Shoals light-house in the James River, "takes the place of Mr. Potts, who was killed a few weeks ago by a man named King," and that Mrs. Potts was appointed one of the assistant keepers.[^7] The Chesapeake Chapter, which lists the Richmond Dispatch of 1 February 1868 among its sources, describes the death as the result of a disagreement with a man carrying a shotgun.[^9] Nancy Potts served as an assistant until 1872.[^1][^8]

Later names include Eldred J. Cherrix, assistant from 1922 and head keeper from 1929 to 1938, and Homer T. Austin, the last head keeper the archive lists, from 1938 to 1940.[^1] The Chapter's roster differs in places on dates and first names, for example giving Thomas S. Hudgins where the archive has Walter S. Hudgins.[^1][^10]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In April 1889, Lighthouse Friends reports, a northeasterly gale carried away 75 feet (23 m) of the jetty and left the station surrounded by water.[^6] The jetty work of 1890 and 1891 had its own troubles. The Board's 1891 report says that mosquitoes were so numerous and troublesome that work was practically suspended, that the contractors' landing wharf was washed away three times in heavy storms, and that one vessel chartered to carry stone was wrecked and another badly damaged.[^3] The tower of 1864 outlived those efforts by years. After serving as an observation tower in the First World War, it fell into the ocean on 2 July 1927, according to Lighthouse Friends.[^6]

## After the keepers

Lighthouse Friends reports that the lights at Cape Charles and Hog Island were electrified in 1933, and a 1934 Bulletin item on the Fifth District's electrification names the two stations.[^6][^12] The station was automated in 1963 and the Fresnel lens removed; the lens is now on display at The Mariners' Museum in Newport News.[^1][^6] The assistants' dwellings were torn down around 1960, and the head keeper's house, oil house and generator building passed to a hunting club and then, in 1995, to The Nature Conservancy.[^6]

The sources disagree about the fire that took the keeper's quarters. The Chesapeake Chapter says a brush fire on 13 July 2000 destroyed them, while Lighthouse Friends dates a fire that burned the head keeper's dwelling to July 2013.[^6][^10]

The light itself failed in 2013 and the Coast Guard never repaired it; in 2019 it dropped off the official light list.[^10][^11] Lighthouse Digest put the tower on its Doomsday List of Endangered Lighthouses in 2020, noting that it is surrounded by land owned by The Nature Conservancy.[^11]

## Today

The tower stands on Smith Island and is closed. The archive records it as not open to the public, Lighthouse Friends gives the Coast Guard as its owner, and the island is owned by The Nature Conservancy.[^1][^6][^10] The Chesapeake Chapter says it is one of the lights that cannot be reached by car, and Lighthouse Friends calls a visit an undertaking that depends on tides and shifting sandbars.[^10][^6] The Chesapeake Chapter notes that the wildlife refuge parking area just north of the Chesapeake Bay Bridge-Tunnel offers a view from more than three miles (5 km) away.[^10] The first-order lens can be seen at The Mariners' Museum.[^6]
