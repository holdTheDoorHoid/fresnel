Point Abino Light Tower stands on the rocky north shore of Lake Erie, at the south end of Point Abino in the Town of Fort Erie, Ontario. It is a reinforced concrete tower joined to a fog alarm building, built by the Canadian government in 1917 and 1918 for a point that lake men had been asking to have lit since at least 1898.[^1][^2][^8][^9][^10]

## The place

Point Abino sits near the eastern end of Lake Erie, just 16 km (ten miles) from Buffalo, New York.[^2] The Lake Carriers' Association wrote in its 1917 annual report that there were "outlying reefs, which in thick and smoky weather make shipping to and from Buffalo extremely hazardous," and that mariners had long urged a light and fog signal at the point.[^2][^10] In December 1898 a captain told the *Buffalo Courier* that lake men needed "a lighthouse and fog horn on Point Abino" more than anything else. Without one, he said, he would rather spend the night at sea before the wind than try for the harbour in a snowstorm.[^9]

The point was private land long before it was a lighthouse site. In 1892 a Buffalo developer subdivided it and sold the whole property to twenty-one Buffalo businessmen, who formed the Point Abino Association. The enclave is still a gated community of Canadian and American residents, and the road to the lighthouse runs through it.[^2][^7]

The Shipmasters' Association petitioned the United States government in 1907 to place a lightship in American waters off the point. A bill of that year, which would have appropriated $150,000 for one, failed to pass. A lighthouse bill approved on 17 June 1910 authorized a lightship to be stationed between Sturgeon, New York, and Point Abino.[^2]

## Building the light

Before there was a tower, there was a lightship. By LighthouseFriends' account, the United States placed Lightship 82 on station off Point Abino on 3 August 1912.[^2] The Lighthouse Service's bulletin for 1912 does not name the point. It says the new vessel was "placed on station about 13 miles southwest of Buffalo Harbor," and the 1914 bulletin calls her Buffalo Light Vessel No. 82.[^11][^3] The Lighthouse Service's index for 1912 to 1917 lists "Point Abino, Canada, light station to be established."[^4]

In 1915 William P. Anderson, chief engineer of the Department of Marine and Fisheries, prepared plans for a reinforced concrete lighthouse. The Canadian Register credits him with the design and the Department with the construction.[^1][^2] Delays in buying the site pushed the work back.[^2] Summer residents on the point protested a fog alarm in the lighthouse, but the aid was considered essential for mariners.[^2] The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1917 records tenders advertised for a "new lighthouse and fog alarm station."[^5]

The fittings were a third-order Fresnel lens and a three-inch diaphone fog alarm, driven by two 12-horsepower units that burned natural gas.[^2][^10] The keeper's dwelling was designed to fit the summer houses nearby.[^1][^2] The whole job, dwelling, lighthouse, lens and fog alarm together, came to $37,770.65.[^2][^6]

The sources differ on the tower's height. The Lake Carriers' Association described a 22 m (72 ft) reinforced concrete lighthouse, with the light 26.5 m (87 ft) above the water, and said the building was completed and went into operation about 1 October 1917.[^2][^10] A *Fort Erie Observer* feature calls it a 26.5 m (87 ft) tower.[^7] The 26.5 m (87 ft) in the 1917 notice is the height of the light above the water, not the height of the tower. The Department's report for the fiscal year ended 31 March 1918 still lists the erection of "a reinforced concrete lighthouse tower" among that year's works, with the lens and diaphone.[^6] The Canadian Register dates construction to 1917 to 1918.[^1]

## The keepers

LighthouseFriends gives the head keepers in order: Patrick Augustine, 1918 to 1953; Wesley Earl Thomas, 1953 to 1960; Milton Shaw, listed for 1960; and Lewis W. Anderson, 1960 to 1989.[^2] The *Observer* calls Anderson "the last keeper."[^7]

A tour guide, quoted in the *Observer*, describes the work before power came to the light. The keeper had to climb to the top every four hours to wind the weights that turned the light, until power was installed in 1952.[^7]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

Lightship 82 was lost in the storm of 8 to 10 November 1913. The Lighthouse Service reported the next year that Buffalo Light Vessel No. 82 was "lost together with her crew of 6 men" in that severe storm.[^3] LighthouseFriends says she was the only sizeable vessel lost on Lake Erie in the storm, and that all six of her crew died.[^2]

The Lighthouse Service Bulletin says the wreck was located on 9 May 1914, about 2.8 km (1¾ miles) from the Buffalo light-vessel station, by the lake survey steamer *Surveyor*.[^3] LighthouseFriends gives 13 May, and says a diver from the *Surveyor* identified her in 19 m (63 ft) of water, nearly 3 km (two miles) off station.[^2] The Reid Wrecking Company of Port Huron raised her in September 1915, and she later served as a relief lightship.[^2] Lightship LV 96 was stationed off the point in 1914, and her sister LV 98 carried on from 1915 to 1918.[^2]

## After the keepers

The light was automated in 1989. LighthouseFriends says Lewis Anderson stayed on as caretaker afterwards.[^2]

The light was decommissioned in 1995.[^2][^8] The register's description, drawn from the Historic Sites and Monuments Board's July 1998 minutes, says the light "has operated continuously since it was built."[^1] The later accounts, which date the light's end to 1995, do not agree with that wording.[^2][^7][^8] The place was recognised as a National Historic Site of Canada in 1998.[^2][^8]

In April 2003 the Town of Fort Erie bought the lighthouse and its keeper's dwelling from Public Works Canada.[^2] In 2009 the federal government offered $425,000 through its cost-sharing programme toward about $1.3 million of work. To find the rest, the town sold the keeper's dwelling, and the buyer was the president of the firm that did the restoration.[^2] Phoenix Restoration Services began on the tower in May 2011 and had it substantially completed by that October. The corroded concrete was patched. The lantern room, access ports and fog horn were restored, and replica steel windows and bronze handrails were installed.[^2]

The light was relit on 1 May 2017, in time for the tower's hundredth year. The historic Fresnel lens sits in the lantern room, though it no longer turns. The relighting used an isophase switch, so that the light showed two seconds on and two seconds off.[^2]

## Today

The Town of Fort Erie owns the lighthouse, which is a National Historic Site and a classified heritage building.[^2][^8] It stands inside the gated Point Abino enclave, and the road in is private.[^7] The Town's page describes walk-in visits, which are ticketed and cover the exterior only, from 21 June to 6 September. Parking is at 1010 Point Abino Road South, and the walk to the lighthouse is about 1.5 km each way. No vehicles or pets may pass the gate, and visitors must be back outside it before 6 p.m. The Town says its 2026 tours are on hold.[^8] The *Observer* quotes the president of the Point Abino Lighthouse Preservation Society saying that the Town's insurer deemed the stairs too dangerous to go up.[^7] Keep to the gate rules, and do not try to climb the tower.
