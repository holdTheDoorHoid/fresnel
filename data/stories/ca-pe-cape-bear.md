Cape Bear Lighthouse is a three-storey wooden tower on the southeastern tip of Prince Edward Island, in Kings County. It was built in 1881 to serve the fishing industry on the island's southeast shore,[^1][^3] and in 1912 a wireless station beside it is said to have picked up Canada's first distress call from the sinking *Titanic*.[^1]

## Building the light

Parliament appropriated $2,000 in 1880 and tenders were called for a lighthouse at Cape Bear. According to Lighthouse Friends, John Whalen was paid $1,374 for finishing it the next year, on land bought for $75 from Thomas Munn; the lantern and lighting apparatus cost $1,044 and came to the Island in seventeen cases.[^1] The Prince Edward Island Lighthouse Society says Munn, a carpenter, was by some accounts the person who began the process of getting a light built at the cape.[^2]

The tower is square and tapered, shingled in wood, and Lighthouse Friends measures it at 12.4 m (40.7 ft) from base to vane.[^1][^3] It first showed a revolving red catoptric light, a flash every thirty seconds, visible from about twelve miles.[^1] In its report for the year ended 31 March 1910 the Department of Marine and Fisheries recorded that the red light had been changed to a revolving white one, with its brightest flash every 30 seconds.[^7]

## The keepers

Thomas Hugh Munn, born at West River in 1823, was the first keeper, at $300 a year.[^1][^2] Family history says he fell from the lantern and lay bedridden for three years before dying in 1893, and that his son John tended the light meanwhile.[^2] Lighthouse Friends lists a Mrs. M. Munn for the last months of 1893 and John T. Munn from 1894.[^1] The Department's 1902 list of keepers shows William Harris at Cape Bear, appointed 11 November 1896.[^4] The Lighthouse Society says he married Annie Jordan and, after his death at 57, she had a boarding house built near the station where Marconi operator Elwood Champion lodged with his family.[^2]

Martin Luther Jordan was appointed in April 1905 at $400 a year, according to the Department's 1910 list.[^5] He and his wife Ada, the Society writes, bought Munn's old farm and raised ten children; one daughter, Florence, married the later keeper Ewart Keeping.[^2] Hiram Hyde of Murray Harbour followed. He died in post, and the sources disagree about where he lay: the Society says in the parlour on the tower's first floor, Lighthouse Friends says in the base of the tower.[^2][^1] Clarence White, a veteran of the First World War, was next; he died of tuberculosis in 1925, and his wife Peg ran the light alone through the winter until Ewart Keeping arrived in 1926.[^2]

Keeping stayed longest. Both accounts give him thirty-odd years, from 1926 until automation, and say his daughters Inez and Eileen sometimes helped with the work.[^2][^1] Sources disagree on when it ended: the Society and the provincial heritage record say 1959, while Lighthouse Friends gives 1959 in its keeper list but says elsewhere that he retired in 1960, the year electricity arrived; Lighthouse Digest also dates the automation to 1960.[^2][^3][^1][^8] Lighthouse Friends lists Clarence White as keeper to 1926, though the Society has him dying in 1925.[^1][^2] The keeper lists differ too: the provincial record names an Abraham Daly from 1887 to 1896, whom the other accounts do not mention.[^3]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

The Department of Marine and Fisheries built a Marconi wireless station next to the light. Its 1905 report says a station "has also been erected at Cape Bear" to keep in touch with the government steamers *Minto* and *Stanley* on the winter crossing to the mainland.[^6] Lighthouse Friends gives the year as 1905 and the provincial record 1906.[^1][^3] Lighthouse Friends says Thomas Bartlett kept the station until it moved to Charlottetown in 1922, and that on the night of 14 April 1912 he received the first distress signal in Canada from the *Titanic*.[^1] That claim rests on these modern accounts; no document of the time is cited here. Lighthouse Digest even dates the call to 1914, an evident slip.[^8]

In the Second World War, according to Lighthouse Friends, the tower served for spotting German U-boats, several of which were seen along the shore.[^1]

## After the keepers

The attached keeper's dwelling was removed from the tower in 1960 and, according to Lighthouse Friends, moved a quarter of a mile up the road.[^1][^3] The wooden Marconi building was sold in 1929 and now serves as a family home.[^1]

The cliff kept retreating. The tower was moved back from the edge in 1947 by house-hauler Wesley Coles, says Lighthouse Friends; the Society puts that move in 1946.[^1][^2] By 2009 the shoreline was being lost at roughly a metre a year.[^1] In May 2011 the Coast Guard put a new skeletal tower beside the old one as the official Cape Bear Light.[^1] The old tower, leased since 1998 to a local development corporation that restored it and added a Marconi museum, was recognized as a provincial heritage place on 3 October 2012.[^1] On 22 December 2014 it was moved 100 feet from the bluff,[^1][^8] and on 21 April 2015, 400 feet more to its present site.[^1]

## Today

The 1881 tower stands at its new site, run as a Marconi and lighthouse museum by a community group. Lighthouse Friends gives the season as mid-June to mid-September, 10 a.m. to 6 p.m., and lists the tower as open in season; check before travelling, since that listing may be out of date.[^1] The working light is the 2011 skeletal tower.[^1]
