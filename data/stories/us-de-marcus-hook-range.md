The Marcus Hook Range was a pair of lights that guided ships coming down the Delaware River from Philadelphia.[^1] The name comes from Marcus Hook, Pennsylvania, across the state line, but the lights stand in Delaware.[^10] The rear light is a square concrete tower near the crest of a ridge, close to Bellefonte.[^1] It was established on 27 December 1919.[^2] In June 2019 the Coast Guard rebuilt the range in the river, and the old rear light was discontinued.[^10][^11]

## The place

The Army Corps of Engineers approved the deepening of the channel below Philadelphia in 1910.[^1] The new 35-foot (11 m) channel made it necessary to abandon the old Schooner Ledge range line, so the Lighthouse Service laid out two new ranges, Chester and Marcus Hook.[^2][^1] The nomination says that, with the front light, the rear light "defines the center line of the channel" along that stretch of river. A river pilot or ship's captain keeps the two lights aligned. "In this way he knows that the ship is in the proper channel."[^1]

The 1920 report places the rear site at Gordon Heights. The 1988 nomination places the tower in a residential neighborhood in Bellefonte, and LighthouseFriends gives the site as Gordon Heights (Edgemoor).[^5][^1][^10]

## Building the light

Congress provided the money in 1916.[^10] The design of the rear tower and its dwelling was complete by 1917.[^3] The rear tower was built of reinforced concrete.[^1] The nomination says the usual practice was "iron or steel skeleton towers for rear range lights," and that "Marcus Hook's companion, the 1917 Chester Rear Range Light, was such a tower."[^1] The nomination calls the Marcus Hook rear light one of a half-dozen historic range lights on the Delaware, and says it "is the only one constructed of concrete."[^1]

The front light stood on a concrete block in two feet (0.6 m) of water. Its steel tower was "a 72-foot structural steel tower erected thereon" (about 22 m), and the Service established the light on 11 May 1918.[^5][^4][^2]

The rear site had to be taken by condemnation.[^10] The Bulletin reported that the condemnation "has resulted in an award of $2,050 to owners."[^7]

The 1920 report describes the rear light as a "fixed incandescent oil vapor light shown from a 100-foot detached concrete tower with brick dwelling alongside."[^6] The nomination says the original light was a fourth-order range lens, "focused by a dish-shaped fourthorder Fresnel range lens," and that the original light source was acetylene gas.[^1] The two sources disagree on the illuminant. The 1920 report gives the focal plane as "278 feet above mean high water" (about 85 m).[^5]

Until the land was bought, a temporary light stood at the site.[^10] LighthouseFriends describes it as "a temporary light, consisting of a locomotive headlight shown from a short tower."[^10]

## The keepers

The US Lighthouse Society's archive begins its list for the rear light with Harold P. Keen, principal keeper from 1915 to 1918.[^12] The roster begins before the rear light was established, so Keen's place at this station is unclear. LighthouseFriends names Linwood Spicer as the first keeper.[^10]

Linwood Spicer had earlier served at Fourteen Foot Bank, Ship John Shoal and Bellevue Range Rear.[^8][^10] He "died on November 12, completing nearly 35 years of service in the fourth lighthouse district," the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported in 1930.[^8] His father was also a keeper: "He was the son of William E. Spicer, also a lighthouse keeper."[^8]

After Spicer, the archive lists Julian Bacon from 1931 to 1933, then Leslie V.S. Millar from 1933 until at least 1948.[^12] LighthouseFriends says Millar was responsible for "maintenance of all the aids to navigation from Philadelphia to the mouth of Delaware Bay." Leaving family members to look after the light, he was "absent for weeks at a time."[^10] The last keepers on the archive's list are James O'Connell, from 1951, and James P. Kelly, from at least 1953.[^12]

In 1933 the fourth lighthouse district laid a cable to the front light. A scow was "placed alongside the submarine foundation for the light," with the reel of cable supported on a rack.[^9]

## After the keepers

Coast Guard personnel lived in the keeper's house until about 2004. The house was vacated and boarded up. It was listed on 9 March 2005 "as being available under the provisions of the National Historic Lighthouse Preservation Act," and "No applicant was recommended for transfer of ownership."[^10] The Coast Guard awarded $37,500 in 2008 to refurbish the porch. In 2010 it sold the property outside the transfer process, and the keeper's dwelling "is now a private residence."[^10]

## Today

The range was rebuilt in June 2019. Two new offshore structures were built in the river, north of Wilmington, and the rear light was disestablished.[^11][^10] The lighthouse is privately owned, and its grounds, dwelling and tower are closed.[^10][^12]

The 1988 nomination describes the tower as now illuminated "by a powerful fixed searchlight with a red lens." It says the original lens "has been donated to the Philadelphia Maritime Museum."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the lens is on display at Independence Seaport Museum at Penn's Landing in Philadelphia.[^10] The sources do not say whether the Philadelphia Maritime Museum named in the nomination is the Independence Seaport Museum. The house sits in a residential area, and LighthouseFriends says that made it "not well suited to become a tourist destination."[^10]
