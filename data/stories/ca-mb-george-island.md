George Island Light is a Canadian Coast Guard light on the eastern end of George Island, in the north basin of Lake Winnipeg, between the shore communities of Poplar River and Grand Rapids.[^1] The Department of Marine and Fisheries established it in the year ended 30 June 1906, and it still shows a light from a red steel tower that the Coast Guard's list marks as seasonal.[^2][^3] The sources name its keepers from 1906 to 1981, and they describe two emergencies involving keepers, in 1930 and 1951.

## The place

Lake Winnipeg is shallow for a lake of its size, and Lighthouse Friends says that this lets large waves build quickly, which made harbours of refuge important to fishermen and mariners.[^1] George Island offers some natural shelter. In the 1920s the government dredged a swamp near its eastern end and built a rock breakwater to make a harbour.[^1]

The Department's 1906 description says the light could not be seen in the line of islands and shoals extending north-west from the island.[^2]

Ships were in these waters before there was a light. In the report for the year ended 30 June 1900, the Department recorded that the steamer *Red River* of Winnipeg encountered a heavy gale on Lake Winnipeg and sprang a leak. She was towed by the steamer *Lady of the Lake* to "Georges Island Harbour," where she grounded, and the report says she "still lies" there. The report gives no date and says the amount of loss was not yet known. It does not say whether that is the harbour the light later guarded.[^4]

## Building the light

The Department first called for tenders. The lowest was $4,900, which it judged too high, so the work went ahead by day labour under the direction of M. J. Egan, at a cost of $3,128.10. Steelwork of $600 was built at the government shipyard at Sorel.[^2]

The tower was an open steel skeleton frame, square in plan with sloping sides, painted red, with a wooden watchroom and an octagonal wooden lantern. The report gives its height as 19.5 m (64 ft) from the base to the top of the ventilator. It stands on land 1.8 m (6 ft) above the lake and 30 m (100 ft) back from the water, and the keeper's dwelling stood 46 m (150 ft) to the west.[^2]

The light was a fixed white dioptric light of the seventh order, shown 20 m (66 ft) above the lake and visible 13 miles from all points of approach by water, except in the line of islands and shoals.[^2] The sources do not say the night it was first lit.

The Coast Guard's list gives the present tower as 15.5 m (51 ft) high. The 1906 height and the present height do not match, and the sources do not explain the difference, [^2][^3]

## The keepers

Lighthouse Friends lists the keepers from 1906, and the Department's own tables support several of the names.[^1][^5] Charles T. Whiteway kept the light from 1906 to 1909. The Department's 1910 table lists "Chas. T. Whiteway" at George's Island, Lake Winnipeg.[^5]

A. A. T. McKay followed him. The 1912 report's table gives his appointment there as 6 December 1909.[^6] A footnote in the same report says that "A. A. T. McKay, George Island, has been appointed in his stead" after Wm. Dore died on 13 December 1909. Dore was the Cox Reef keeper, so read literally, the footnote describes a move from George Island to Cox Reef. The sources do not say outright that he moved. By the 1913 table McKay is listed at Cox Reef.[^6][^7]

Lighthouse Friends gives John Thumser as keeper from 1910. The 1912 footnote calls him John Tumster.[^6] The footnote dates his appointment to 31 March 1910.[^6] The 1913 table gives 31 March 1911 instead, and the sources disagree by a year.[^7]

E. Arrason took over after him. The 1915 table gives his appointment as 26 August 1912.[^8] Lighthouse Friends gives his service as 1912 to 1924.[^1]

Ingwald Thordarson served from 1924 until 1930.[^1] W. L. Curran served from 1931 to at least 1937.[^1] Willard Olson came in 1951, kept the light until 1981, and was the longest serving keeper at George Island.[^1] The sources name no keeper for the years between 1937 and 1951.

The work followed a fixed routine. Before the light was electrified it burned kerosene, and a keeper had to climb the tower four times a day.[^1] Olson began each morning at 5:45 a.m. to prepare the first weather report, which was due by radio at 7 a.m. Regular reports then went to the Winnipeg weather officer every three hours until 10 p.m.[^1]

The Department's list of fog signals in its report for the year ended 31 March 1911 includes a hand fog horn at George Island.[^9] The keepers' tables mark George Island with an asterisk. The footnote to the 1912 table reads "$25 for Fog Horn," and the 1913 table's footnote reads "For blowing hand fog horn."[^6][^7] The 1915 table's footnote gives the payment as "$25 for operating fog horn or bell."[^8]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In October 1930 Thordarson picked up his mail and supplies from the northbound *Wolverine* and started to row back to the island. A brisk breeze was blowing from the north-west. He broke an oar and drifted away with a teenage companion. Thirty hours later the *Wolverine* saw a distress signal flying from the island and stopped to help. The news went to the CGS *Bradbury* at Berens River, and a search began. The keeper's boat was found bottom up, and no bodies were ever recovered.[^1]

In November 1951 an early freeze-up left Olson stranded on the island. Food was dropped to him by parachute. A bush pilot landed his Tiger Moth on shaky ice near the island and took Olson off on 7 December. Lighthouse Friends says that the rescue caught the keeper by surprise.[^1]

## After the keepers

A new keeper's dwelling was built on the island in 1953.[^1]

Lighthouse Friends gives two dates for automation. In one passage it says the light was electrified and automated in 1968. In another it says that Olson left in 1981 "when the light was automated." The sources do not settle the year. Lighthouse Friends calls George Island the final lighthouse on Lake Winnipeg to be automated and Olson the lake's last lighthouse keeper, so resident keeping on the lake ended with his time there.[^1]

## Today

The Coast Guard's list gives the light as a flash every five seconds: a one-second flash and a four-second eclipse. Its focal height is 20.1 m and its nominal range is nine nautical miles. The tower is a red rectangular skeleton tower, 15.5 m high, and the light is marked seasonal. It is visible from all points of marine approach, except in the line of islands and shoals to the north-west.[^3]

Lighthouse Friends says the modern light is best viewed by boat.[^1] No source describes a public landing, a tour or a visiting arrangement for the island. The sources do not say the tower may be entered, so treat it as a working light and do not climb it.
