Crossover Island Light stands on a small rock in the St. Lawrence River, about 13 miles (21 km) northeast of Alexandria Bay, New York, close to the Canadian border.[^1][^2] Congress paid for it in 1847 as one of three lights marking the Thousand Islands.[^1] The first keeper was appointed in 1848, and the light was discontinued in 1941.[^1][^2] The island is privately owned today.[^1][^2]

## The place

Lieutenant C. T. Platt, who reported on the site in 1838, described the island as "a solid rock, with a few trees, that have taken root through the crevices of the rocks," about 132 feet (40 m) long and 82.5 feet (25 m) wide.[^1] The name refers to the crossing point: before the St. Lawrence Seaway was built, vessels following the shipping channel crossed the international boundary between the American and Canadian channels near the island.[^1]

Platt urged a light there.[^1] "This island is situated between the two channels, and will be a guide for either," he wrote, in the middle of a cluster of islands and shoals.[^1] He estimated the work at just over $2,000.[^1]

## Building the light

Congress appropriated $6,000 on 3 March 1847 for Crossover Island and two other lights in the Thousand Islands, at Sunken Rock and Rock Island.[^1]

The design followed Rock Island's: a one-and-a-half-story brick dwelling with a wooden lantern on a pitched roof.[^1] Six oil lamps in 14-inch reflectors gave a fixed white light 37 feet above the river, according to LighthouseFriends.[^1] The Lighthouse Society's record gives the height as 34 feet.[^1][^2]

Obed Robeson was appointed the first keeper on 15 May 1848 at $350 a year.[^1] The Society's roster calls him Obediah Robeson.[^2]

In 1868 an inspector found the lantern room leaking badly and the chimneys in need of repair.[^1] The lighthouse had been built of "soft brick," and the station had no boathouse.[^1] The next year a boathouse was added, shutters went on the dwelling's windows, and boards covered the outer walls.[^1]

In 1872 the Lighthouse Board asked for $11,000 for a new tower and dwelling.[^1] LighthouseFriends quotes the request as calling the tower and dwelling "are both in very bad condition, and are not worth repair," and as describing decayed timber and brick walls that "are now unserviceable."[^1] LighthouseFriends says the money came only after a decade of requests. In 1882 a new six-room, two-story keeper's dwelling and a detached iron tower of about 30 feet went up.[^1] The tower was lined with brick salvaged from the old lighthouse.[^1] A sixth-order Fresnel lens by L. Sautter & Co. went into it, and the fourth-order lens from the old light went to the outer tower at Presque Isle, Pennsylvania.[^1] The Society's record gives 1848 as the year the tower was established, but LighthouseFriends dates the present iron tower to 1882.[^2][^1]

The Board's reports record the work that followed.[^3][^6][^8] The Board's report for 1895 records new boat ways and a crib, built to secure the outer end of the ways.[^3] Its report for 1896 to 1897 records a wooden 70-barrel cistern built in the cellar of the keeper's dwelling to replace a brick one, and the ice house and boat ways rebuilt.[^6] Its 1900 report records some 300 cubic yards of earth delivered to fill hollows in the lighthouse lot.[^8] LighthouseFriends adds that a well was sunk through granite in 1884, that a 232-foot seawall went up on the south side in 1903, and that an iron oil house was added in 1904.[^1]

On the opening of navigation in 1899 the tower was changed from brown to white.[^1][^7]

## The keepers

The Society's roster has 13 keepers from 1848 to 1940.[^2] James C. Hill served from 1861 to 1885, and Daniel D. Hill from 1909 to 1931.[^2] Both stayed more than twenty years.[^2][^1]

Daniel D. Hill took over in March 1909 and held the post for over twenty-two years.[^1] He and his wife raised seven children on the island.[^1]

One of those children, Ralph E. Hill, later wrote about growing up there.[^1] LighthouseFriends, which cites his writing, says the island had no telephone, radio, electricity, running water or central heating.[^1]

After a winter on the exposed island, Hill bought land at Oak Point and built a cottage where the family could winter more comfortably.[^1] A furnace reached the keeper's dwelling about two years before he left for Ogdensburg at the end of 1931.[^1]

Edward J. Sweet Jr. served from 1932 to 1937, and Frank K. Ward from 1937 to 1940.[^2]

## Storms, wrecks and rescues

In October 1913 the Lighthouse Service Bulletin reported that on 10 October Keeper Daniel D. Hill had brought the master of the steamer Lambert to shore in the lighthouse launch.[^4] The steamer had run onto a shoal about a mile from the station.[^4]

On 21 June 1932 Keeper Edward J. Sweet Jr. rescued two boys in the St. Lawrence after their canoe overturned.[^5][^1] LighthouseFriends says the boys were Canadian residents who had put a sail on the canoe to save paddling, which made it less stable.[^1] Sweet took the station boat after them when he saw the canoe overturn.[^1]

## After the keepers

The light was discontinued on 10 April 1941 and replaced with a lighted buoy.[^1][^2]

The government sold the lighthouse as surplus property in 1960, for about $6,000, to three brothers.[^1] The island has changed hands privately since.[^1] LighthouseFriends reports that it sold for $465,000 in 2002 and for $350,000 in 2013, after an asking price of $539,900 in 2011.[^1]

## Today

The island is privately owned.[^1][^2] LighthouseFriends lists the grounds, dwelling and tower as closed to visitors.[^1] The light can be seen from River Ledge Road, which parallels Highway 12 just over two miles east of Chippewa Bay.[^1] LighthouseFriends says a view from a boat is much better.[^1] The Society's record lists the light as not an active aid to navigation.[^2]
