About Fresnel

Every lighthouse, on one map

Fresnel puts every lighthouse in the United States (its territories included) and Canada on one map, standing or lost, together with the lightships, the pier, harbor and range lights, and the life-saving stations that stood beside them. Each station gets a page: practical information first (can you visit, climb, stay), then a sourced history of the place and the people who kept the light.

It is named for the Fresnel lens, the stack of glass prisms at the heart of nearly every lighthouse, which bends the light of a single lamp into a beam that can be seen twenty miles out to sea.

What is on the map

Lighthouses
Light stations, standing or lost: a light on a tower or on its building, usually with a keeper’s house. A station keeps one entry when its tower was rebuilt at the same place; its towers are listed on its page.
Range, pier and harbor lights
Smaller lights with a real structure and a history of their own: range lights (a front and a rear light that line up to mark a channel), pierhead and breakwater lights, and harbor and river lights.
Lightships
Each place at sea where lightships were anchored, and each surviving lightship at its berth today.
Life-saving stations
The stations of the US Life-Saving Service and its Canadian counterparts, whose crews rowed out to wrecks, and the historic Coast Guard boathouses that took them over.

Not on the map: modern automated beacons (steel poles, skeleton towers, buoys) with no keeper history, except as the successor of a light, named on its page; and decorative “lighthouses” that never guided a ship.

Reading the map

Each marker’s shape says the station’s status and its silhouette what it is. Colour only repeats what the shape already says. The same key is one click away on the map: the Key button.

Access. Fresnel shows the exact location of every station. Private homes, Coast Guard property closed to the public, stations reached only by boat, tribal land and working lightstations where keepers live are labelled prominently on each page and in the map’s details panel. Please respect those labels.

Units. Pages for stations in the United States and its territories give heights in feet first, with metres alongside; pages for stations in Canada give metres first, with feet alongside. Ranges and distances at sea are in nautical miles everywhere, with kilometres alongside, because that is how lights are charted.

The light’s pattern. A page gives a light’s characteristic as it is charted (“Fl W 4s”) and says it in words (“one white flash every 4 seconds”).

How sure are we?

Every entry carries one of four labels, shown on its page:

Unverified
From a single source and not yet checked. Shown with a visible Unverified badge. Details may be wrong.
Facts agree
Two or more independent sources agree on where it is and its status.
Researched
Its history has been researched and written up, with footnoted sources.
Verified
Researched, then checked independently.

When sources disagree, for example on the year a light was first lit, the page says so plainly (“Sources disagree on…”) and lists what each says, instead of quietly picking one.

How the histories are written

Each history is researched from the record: the Lighthouse Board and Commissioner of Lighthouses reports, the Life-Saving Service reports, keeper registers, heritage nominations and old newspapers. Every factual sentence is footnoted. Its length follows the evidence: a short honest history beats a padded one.

  • Every detail of a scene (the weather that night, the sound of the fog signal, a keeper’s words) comes from a source.
  • Passages about the period in general (what trimming a wick involved, life aboard a lightship) are framed as general (“On most stations of the period…”) and cited to a history of the service. They are never presented as having happened at that station.
  • Nothing is invented: no imagined scenes, no guessed feelings, no composite characters.
  • The keepers roster on each page comes from the research, line by line, each with its sources. Two keepers of the same name are never joined into one person without a source that connects them.

Fresnel takes facts from community sites (dates, names, numbers) with credit and a link, and writes its own words. A station without a history yet says so, with at most a one-sentence summary built from its record and labelled as such.

Your checklist

You can mark stations as Visited, Climbed, Stayed or Want to go, on the map or on any station’s page, and filter the map to show only those. The checklist is saved only in this browser. There are no accounts and nothing is sent anywhere.

Because it lives in one browser, clearing your browsing data deletes it. Download a copy now and then; you can import it on another device.

Takedown requests

Fresnel shows photos and facts gathered from many people’s work, always with credit. If you own a photo or text shown here and want it credited differently, or removed, just ask. We will do it promptly, with no questions asked and nothing to prove beyond telling us which item and what you would like.

  1. Open a takedown request on GitHub. Its title starts with “Takedown:” followed by the station’s name. On a station’s page, the link under its photos fills this in for you.
  2. Say which photo or text it is (a link to the page is enough) and whether you want a different credit line or removal.

Open a takedown request

GitHub issues are public, so please do not include private details such as your address or phone number.

Corrections and additions

Every station page has a Suggest an edit button. It opens a short form on GitHub with the station already filled in: tell us what is wrong, what is right, and how you know (a link, a book, a report, or your own visit with its date). If you have photos you took yourself and are happy to share, say so in the form.

Privacy

No accounts, no cookies, no analytics. Your checklist, the map key’s open or closed state and your colour-theme choice stay in your browser. Like any web map, the map images are downloaded from OpenFreeMap’s servers, which see your IP address as part of normal web traffic. Photos on station pages are loaded from the sites that publish them (Wikimedia Commons and others), which see the same. “Show my location” on the map uses your device’s location only in your browser, and only after you allow it.

Keepers, owners and volunteers who may be alive are named only as published sources name them, in their public role. Private residents of converted lighthouses are not named unless they present themselves publicly.

Sister site: Firefinder

Firefinder does the same for the fire lookouts of the United States and Canada: every lookout, standing or gone, how to visit or rent one, and its history. The two sites share their approach and their code base, and link to each other.
